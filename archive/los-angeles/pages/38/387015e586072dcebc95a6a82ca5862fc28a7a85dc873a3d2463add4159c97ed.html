DEPARTMENT M LAW AND MOTION RULINGS



Case Number: 20SMCV00839    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Ashworth, et al., v. J. Arthur Greenfield & Co. LLP, et al.

CASE NO.:                20SMCV00839

MOTION:                  Motion for Summary Judgment/Adjudication

HEARING DATE:   6/26/2026

 

Legal Standard

 

A party may move for summary judgment in any action or proceeding if it is contended the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding. (CCP, § 437c(a).) “The purpose of the law of summary judgment is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties' pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.)  

 

“A party may move for summary adjudication as to one or more causes of action within an action, one or more affirmative defenses, one or more claims for damages, or one or more issues of duty, if the party contends that the cause of action has no merit, that there is no affirmative defense to the cause of action, that there is no merit to an affirmative defense as to any cause of action, that there is no merit to a claim for damages, as specified in¿Section 3294 of the Civil Code, or that one or more defendants either owed or did not owe a duty to the plaintiff or plaintiffs.”¿(CCP,¿§ 437c(f)(1).)¿If a party seeks summary adjudication as an alternative to a request for summary judgment, the request must be clearly made in the notice of the motion. (Gonzales v. Superior Court¿(1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 1542, 1544.)¿ “[A] party may move for summary adjudication of a legal issue or a claim for damages other than punitive damages that does not completely dispose of a cause of action, affirmative defense, or issue of duty pursuant to” subdivision (t). (CCP,¿§ 437c(t).)¿ 

 

To prevail, the evidence submitted must show there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.¿(CCP, §¿437c(c).)¿The motion cannot succeed unless the evidence leaves no room for conflicting inferences as to material facts; the court has no power to weigh one inference against another or against other evidence. (Murillo v. Rite Stuff Food Inc. (1998) 65 Cal.App.4th 833, 841.) In determining whether the facts give rise to a triable issue of material fact, “[a]ll doubts as to whether any material, triable, issues of fact exist are to be resolved in favor of the party opposing summary judgment…” (Gold v. Weissman (2004) 114 Cal.App.4th 1195, 1198-99.) “In other words, the facts alleged in the evidence of the party opposing summary judgment and the reasonable inferences there from must be accepted as true.” (Jackson v. County of Los Angeles (1997) 60 Cal.App.4th 171, 179.) However, if adjudication is otherwise proper the motion “may not be denied on grounds of credibility,” except when¿a material fact is the witness’s¿state of mind and “that fact is sought to be established solely by the [witness’s] affirmation thereof.” (CCP, § 437c(e).)¿ 

 

Once the moving party has met their burden, the burden shifts to the opposing party “to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto.” (CCP § 437c(p)(1).) “[T]here¿is no obligation on the opposing party... to establish anything by affidavit unless and until the moving party has by affidavit stated facts establishing every element... necessary to sustain a judgment in his favor.”¿(Consumer Cause, Inc. v.¿SmileCare¿(2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 454, 468.)¿ 

¿ 

“The pleadings play a key role in a summary judgment motion. The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues and to¿frame¿the outer measure of materiality in a summary judgment proceeding.” (Hutton v. Fidelity National Title Co.¿(2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 486, 493, quotations and citations omitted.) “Accordingly, the burden of a defendant moving for summary judgment only requires that he or she negate plaintiff's theories of liability¿as alleged in the complaint; that is, a moving party need not refute liability on some theoretical possibility not included in the pleadings.” (Ibid.)¿ 

 

Analysis

 

Defendant J. Arthur Greenfield & Co. LLP (“JAG” or Defendant) moves for summary judgment or adjudication under CCP section 437c(f)(2). JAG notices the following three issues:

 

Issue no. 1: “MS. DERIAN’S CO-TRUSTEE ROLE AND JAG’S TAX PREPARATION SERVICES WERE DISTINCT, AND THEREFORE NO CONFLICT OF INTEREST EXISTED”;

 

Issue no. 2: “PLAINTIFFS WERE NOT JAG CLIENTS AND MS. DERIAN’S CO-TRUSTEE ROLE DOES NOT IMPOSE A FIDUCIARY DUTY FOR TAX PREPARATION SERVICES”;

 

Issue no. 3: “EVEN IF LIABILITY WERE IMPUTED TO JAG FROM MS. DERIAN, WHICH IT IS NOT, EXPERT ANALYSIS ESTABLISHES THAT PLAINTIFFS’ ALLEGED DAMAGES ARE LEGALLY UNSUPPORTED.”

 

Evidentiary Issues

 

Plaintiffs’ objections are OVERRULED.  Initially, the Court was considering sustaining objection nos. 2-4 and 7-17, based upon Mr. Dellinger’s deposition testimony in which he claimed not to be giving any opinions on Derian’s performance as trustee, and that his opinion was limited to area of expertise regarding the standard of care applicable to CPAs. In these paragraphs, Mr. Dellinger opines as to Derian’s performance of her role as Co-Trustee, generally claiming that she exercised independent and reasonable fiduciary judgment and met the standard of care of trustees. Some portions of those paragraphs also pertain to JAG’s CPA issues, e.g., no. 16 [¶ 69], but the opinion is objectionable to any opinion he renders re: Derian’s performance as co-trustee based on the presented deposition testimony. In reply, Defendant argues that these statements were taken out of context, and that he repeatedly affirmed his ability to opine in line with MSJ declaration. Based upon the evidence submitted in Reply (Noon Decl., ¶¶ 3-4), the Court concurs that Dellinger affirmed his ability at his deposition to render these at-issue opinions.

 

Defendant’s objections to the Ashworth, C. Coats, N. Coats, and Cheadle declarations are SUSTAINED as to nos. 7 [as to hearsay statements] and 13 [as to improper expert opinion as to what “should have been done” re: notice to the beneficiaries], and OVERRULED as to the remainder. Defendant’s objections to the Gabrielson and Kahrs declarations are OVERRULED.

 

Successive Motions

 

CCP section 437c(f)(2) states in relevant part: “A party shall not move for summary judgment based on issues asserted in a prior motion for summary adjudication and denied by the court unless that party establishes, to the satisfaction of the court, newly discovered facts or circumstances or a change of law supporting the issues reasserted in the summary judgment motion.”

 

Each of the issues in this motion were already decided at a prior MSJ. The prior MSJ/MSA litigated issues concerning JAG’s duties to Plaintiffs and breach of said duties under the professional negligence and fiduciary duty theories. The Court found that Plaintiffs did not lack standing to sue, even though they were not direct clients of JAG, since they were third party beneficiaries. (See Saks v. Damon Raike & Co. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 419, 427 [beneficiary of a trust, having no legal title or ownership interest in the trust assets, is not the real party in interest and may not sue in the name of the trust]; Wolf v. Mitchell, Silberberg & Knupp (1999) 76 Cal. App. 4th 1030, 1036-1040 [recognized an exception to this general rule, where a beneficiary of a trust may bring an action against a third party to recover property transferred to the third party by the trustee in breach of trust, where the trustee has failed to bring suit against a third party on behalf of a trust]; see also Est. of Bowles, (2008) 169 Cal. App. 4th 684, 692-694 [holding beneficiaries may pursue independent claims against third parties that “actively participate” in or “knowingly benefits” from a trustee's breach of trust; remaindermen beneficiary stated a claim against other beneficiary and third party that induced the trustee to sell trust property at less than fair market value to third party, while knowing that the transactions breached the trustee’s duties].) The Court further noted that Plaintiffs alleged that JAG intentionally engaged in a conflict of interest and promotion of their own and the trustee-beneficiary’s interests over Plaintiffs’ interest. (Compl., ¶¶ 21, 26, 28, 29, 34.) JAG failed to present competent expert testimony concerning the applicable standard of care for accountants to negate the alleged breach. JAG also alleged that the damages were uncertain, but that argument largely relied on the same duty and breach analysis.

 

JAG again challenges its alleged duty, breach, and resulting damages with the instant three issues. First, JAG argues that there was no breach, because there was no conflict of interest and JAG only provided tax services distinct from Derian’s actions as co-trustee. Second, JAG argues there was no duty, because Plaintiffs were not JAG clients and Derian’s role did not impose fiduciary duties for JAG’s tax preparation services. Third, JAG argues that it caused Plaintiffs no damages, as established by new expert testimony. There have been no significant change in circumstances or law. Instead, JAG belatedly secured an expert declaration to support their MSJ. JAG could have timely brought this evidence at the prior hearing if it exercised reasonable diligence. Despite these issues, the Court will tentatively exercise its discretion and consider the merits based on the new expert evidence presented.

 

Issue no. 1: JAG’s Distinct Tax Services Posed No Conflict of Interest

 

JAG explains the background of the case. On June 15, 1978, Roy Coats, a wealthy businessman, with his wife, Laura Coats, created and executed the Roy Coats Family Trust, a Qualified Terminable Interest Property Trust, QTIP Trust, (the “Trust”), for the benefit of surviving spouse (Laura Coats), with remainderman beneficiaries as Roy Coats’ adult children (Plaintiffs Virginia Coats Ashworth, Nancy Coats Routh, and Christy Lynn Coats). (UMF 1.) On January 19, 1987, the Trust was restated in its entirety, which established an irrevocable QTIP Trust in the event Roy predeceased Laura. (Id.) The QTIP Trust was to provide for Laura’s financial needs by giving her a lifetime income interest in the QTIP Trust assets and to pass the QTIP Trust assets to Plaintiffs upon Laura’s death. (Id.)

 

JAG highlights some of the Trust’s relevant terms. “During the lifetime of the Surviving Spouse, all income of the QTIP Trust shall be distributed to him or her in quarter-annual or

more frequent installments… In this respect, only the needs of the Surviving Spouse shall be of importance and the rights of the remaindermen shall be considered to be without significance.” (UMF 2.) “Trustee has the right to “invest the funds of the Trust Estate including the proceeds from the disposition thereof…as Trustee shall deem to be for the best interests of the Trust Estate and the beneficiaries thereof, without being limited at any time to the class of investments… In balancing the portfolio of investments hereunder, the needs of the current beneficiary or beneficiaries shall be of primary importance and the rights of the remaindermen shall be considered of secondary significance.” (UMF 3.) “No successor Trustee shall be under any duty to examine the books and records of its predecessor Trustee. Any successor Trustee may accept as full trust corpus any properties which may be turned over to it… Except as expressly provided herein, Trustee hereunder shall not be liable for any mistake or error in judgment in the administration of any trust hereunder which results in loss to such trust by reason of investment or otherwise, save only for willful misconduct or fraud.” (UMF 4.)

 

On February 15, 1988, Roy died, resulting in the establishment of the QTIP Trust with Plaintiffs as remainderman beneficiaries, Laura as trustee of the QTIP Trust, and Wilmington Trust as corporate co-trustee. (UMF 5.) Notably, in 2005, the Delaware Court of Chancery removed the requirement of a corporate co-trustee and permitted the role be filled by a qualified disinterested co-trustee who is an attorney or an accountant. (UMF 6.) On December 12, 2009, Laura appointed Nancy Derian as co-trustee of the QTIP Trust, as Qualified Disinterested Trustee. (UMF 7.) JAG was retained for the QTIP Trust’s annual preparation of its income tax returns. (UMF 8.)

 

On March 19, 2015, Derian mailed letters to Plaintiffs (on JAG letterhead) disclosing that she is co-trustee of the QTIP Trust and that she had worked with Laura for many years, assisting primarily with tax planning, compliance and consulting on tax and general financial matters. (UMF 9.) Plaintiffs did not raise any concern at the time about a potential conflict after receiving these letters, or object to JAG’s tax preparation services. (UMF 10-11.)

 

JAG presents evidence that Derian complied with the standard of care regarding conflicts of interest. Derian researched, spoke with partners, advisors, and attorneys, and reviewed applicable AICPA Rules prior to accepting the appointment of co- trustee, and concluded there was no conflict. (UMF 12.) JAG contends via expert declaration that Derian was not conflicted in her role as Co-Trustee with her employment at JAG. (UMF 13.) Derian charged reasonable trustee fees. (UMF 15.) Derian did not perform any audits, reviews, or compilations for the Trust. (UMF 16.) Derian as successor co-trustee, continued with the same methodology the previous institutional and professional trustees employed, and had no reason to believe they intentionally or negligently chose poor investment strategies or allowed improper allocations or elections. (UMF 28.)

 

JAG argues that it only performed tax preparation service on behalf of the trust and was hired by the trustees to perform tax preparation services. (UMF 8.) JAG charged the QTIP Trust its standard rates for tax preparation services, and billed their time based upon hourly rates. (UMF 14.) JAG did not perform any audits, reviews, or compilations for the Trust. (UMF 16.) JAG never provided any business management services, never provided any financial forecast projections, never provided business projection planning, never provided any investment review, and never provided estate planning or tax planning services in connection with the QTIP Trust. (UMF 17.) JAG only prepared the QTIP Trust tax returns. (UMF 18.) Critically, JAG disclaims assisting, intentionally participating in, or aiding and abetting Derian or any other Defendant, or colluding with any trustee to intentionally cause harm to the beneficiaries. (UMF 25.) JAG was not negligent in its tax preparation services. (UMF 26.) JAG submits expert testimony that it was not conflicted by Derian’s appointment of Qualified Disinterested Trustee. (UMF 27.)

 

JAG also notes that from 2009 to 2018, REC Management (owned by Laura Coats and managed by Thomas Schulz) provided investment advice and management services to the QTIP Trust in exchange for management fees paid by the QTIP Trust, determined by predecessor co-trustee Wilmington Trust to be considered as discretionary distributions from the Trust. (UMF 19.) After Laura’s death in 2017, no further payments were made to REC Management. (UMF 20.) In December 2019, Plaintiffs became successor co-trustees of the Trust. (UMF 21.) At the time, Plaintiffs did not object to the investment strategies under the Trust, raise any issue with Derian or anyone else regarding investments, loans, administration costs, etc., or raise any issues about JAG’s services. (UMF 22-24.)

 

The Court is not persuaded that the undisputed material facts establish JAG’s services were entirely “distinct” from Derian’s services as the disinterested co-trustee. While JAG contends that it did not control, direct, or participate in actions taken by Derian in her capacity as Co-Trustee, it presents no UMF as to this point. Derian allegedly was the agent of JAG when performing her services as co-trustee. (Compl., ¶ 9.) Derian is admittedly a partner of JAG. (Derian Decl., ¶ 1; Compl., ¶ 21.) Thus, JAG fails to establish its initial burden on its alleged vicarious liability of Derian. There is also conflicting evidence whether Derian was acting on behalf of JAG while acting as the disinterested co-trustee of the Trust. For example, Derian sent Plaintiffs letters on JAG letterhead when she disclosed her professional relationship with Laura. (UMF 9.) Derian testified at her deposition that when she acted as co-trustee of the QTIP Trust, she did so through JAG and not as an individual. (AMF 70; Cheadle Decl., Ex. 9 at 51:23-52:4.) Therefore, a dispute of fact exists as to vicarious liability. Accordingly, if Derian is liable for her role as trustee, then JAG may be vicariously liable.

 

            Moreover, Plaintiffs present a dispute of fact as to whether Derian’s conduct fell below the standard of care. Plaintiffs’ trust expert expressly opines that Derian’s conduct fell below the standard of care of a trustee and as a fiduciary. (UMF 28; AMF 148-178.) Kronvall opines that

Derian was not a disinterested Co-Trustee of the QTIP Trust because both Derian and JAG provided tax services to multiple parties with competing interests which created a structural conflict of interest. (Kronvall Decl., ¶13.) Derian confirmed JAG prepared Laura Coats’ personal income tax returns from approximately 1993 through 2017 or 2018; prepared returns for Laura Coats and Thomas Schulz as a married couple over a similar period; performed tax work for REC Management; provided professional services to Cadmus beginning in 2011; and identified the ‘Coats group of accounts’ at JAG as including Laura Coats and Thomas Schulz, REC Management, the QTIP Trust, and the Coats Foundation. (Kronvall Decl., ¶19.) Those overlapping engagements created a structural conflict of interest rooted in prior knowledge and divided loyalties, not merely overlapping roles. (Id.) Derian never provided a full written disclosure of conflicts and had them provide written consent to the same. (Kronvall Decl., ¶¶13, 18-20, 42-44; Gabrielson Decl., ¶¶1-33.) The March 19, 2015, letter did not give Plaintiffs any meaningful ability to evaluate conflicts of interest or consent thereto. (Kronvall Decl., ¶¶13, 18-20, 42-44; Gabrielson Decl., ¶¶1-33; Ashworth Decl., ¶¶7-25; N. Coats Decl., ¶¶7-21; C. Coats Decl., ¶¶7-25.) Plaintiffs never received any information relating to potential or actual conflicts of interest nor were they provided any information relating to the administration of the QTIP Trust and therefore had no factual basis to raise any concerns about potential conflicts with Derian. (Ashworth Decl., ¶¶7-25; N. Coats Decl. ¶¶7-21; C. Coats Decl., ¶¶7-25.) Kronvall also opines that Derian, as Co-Trustee of the Trust, had a fiduciary duty to exercise independent judgment and review and analyze the administration of the QTIP Trust and her admitted failure to do so was a breach of her fiduciary duties and the standard of care. (Kronvall Decl., ¶¶16-17, 21-27, 39-44.) A fact finder could conclude from Derian’s disregard and abdication of any independent judgment and review of the trust administration, while benefitting from trust assets and other potentially conflicted relationships with the trustees and beneficiary, would be actively participating in or knowingly benefiting from a trustee's breach of trust. (Est. of Bowles, supra, 169 Cal. App. 4th at 692-694.)

 

As there is a dispute of material fact on multiple points of issue no. 1, the motion is DENIED as to issue no. 1.

 

Remaining Issues

 

Issue nos. 2-3 rely on the same UMFs and the same basic legal analysis concerning Derian’s breach of the standard of care. Those issues are also subject to the same disputes of material fact discussed above, among other issues. For example, there are no UMFs establishing that Derian’s alleged breaches could not have caused damages. Accepting Dellinger’s conclusion that there is no causation for certain items of damage (see Dellinger Decl., ¶¶ 57-64), Plaintiffs still present conflicting expert testimony. Ratifying improper monthly payments of $6,000 per month to Laura Coats’ related family business, REC Management, Inc., resulted in approximately $4.4 Million in damages to Plaintiffs. (Kronvall Decl., ¶¶13-14, 28-30, 39-44; Kahrs Decl., ¶¶18, 21.) Ratifying an improper stock to bond ratio of QTIP Trust Assets that favored Laura Coats resulting in approximately $11 Million in damages to Plaintiffs. (Kronvall Decl., ¶¶13-14, 24-26, 39-44; Kahrs Decl., ¶¶15 and 21.) Ratifying yearly distributions of QTIP Trust assets in excess of net distributable income allowed to be distributed to Laura Coats pursuant to the terms of the QTIP Trust resulted in approximately $2.6 Million in damages to Plaintiffs. (Kronvall Decl., ¶¶13-14, 26, 39-44; Kahrs Decl., ¶¶12-13, 21.) Ratifying the allocation of bond premiums to the Plaintiffs to the benefit of Laura Coats resulted in approximately $5.9 Million in damages to Plaintiffs. (Kronvall Decl., ¶¶13-14, 25-26, 39-44; Kahrs Decl., ¶16.) Ratifying the $2 Million in loans from the QTIP Trust to Laura Coats resulted in approximately $1.6 Million in damages to Plaintiffs. (Kronvall Decl., ¶¶13-14, 31-33; 39-44; Kahrs Decl., ¶¶19, 21.) Derian’s receipt of a $150,000 gift from the Estate of Laura Coats resulted in approximately $210,000 in damages to Plaintiffs (Gabrielson Decl., ¶26; Kahrs Decl., ¶20.)

 

Accordingly, the motion is DENIED.


Case Number: 24SMCV04697    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Creative Artists Agency, LLC, v. Range Media Partners, LLC

CASE NO.:                24SMCV04697

MOTION:                  Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

HEARING DATE:   6/26/2026

 

 

Legal Standard

 

A defendant’s motion for judgment on the pleadings may be made after the time to demur has expired and an answer has been filed. (CCP § 438(f).) A motion by a defendant may be made on the grounds that (1) the court “lacks jurisdiction of the subject of one or more of the causes of action alleged” or (2) the complaint or cross-complaint “does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant.” (CCP § 438(c).) 

 

A motion for judgment on the pleadings has the same function as a general demurrer but is made after the time for demurrer has expired. Except as provided by statute, the rules governing demurrers apply. (Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 999.) “A motion for judgment on the pleadings is akin to a general demurrer; it tests the sufficiency of the complaint to state a cause of action. The court must assume the truth of all factual allegations in the complaint, along with matters subject to judicial notice.” (Wise v. Pacific Gas and Elec. Co. (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 725, 738, citations omitted.) Further, like a general demurrer, a motion for judgment on the pleadings “does not lie as to a portion of a cause of action, and if any part of a cause of action is properly pleaded, the [motion] will be overruled.” (Fire Ins. Exchange v. Superior Court (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 446, 452.)  

 

ANALYSIS

 

Defendant/Cross-Complainant Range Media Partners, LLC (“Range”) moves for judgment on the pleadings on Plaintiff Creative Artists Agency LLC’s (“CAA”) cause of action under Business and Professions Code section 17200, predicated on Range’s alleged violation of the Talent Agencies Act (“TAA”). (SAC ¶ 168(3).)

 

Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice is GRANTED.

 

As the court observed at the prior demurrer on this subject, the alleged violations of the UCL may be broken down into distinct categories. The Court commented that the sub-claims would not be subject to a demurrer (or, in this instance, a motion for judgment on the pleadings), as the motions attack only a portion of the UCL cause of action, rather than the entire cause of action. Thus, the instant motion would be better suited as a motion to strike, which may attack specific allegations of a pleading. Despite this procedural issue, the Court will turn to the merits of Defendant’s arguments and newly cited case law.

 

In sum, Defendant argues that the TAA subclaim is barred because the TAA provides the exclusive means for resolving any dispute under the TAA and Plaintiff has not exhausted administrative remedies with the Labor Commissioner. Defendant further reasons that Plaintiff “cannot use the Unfair Competition Law to ‘plead around’ an ‘absolute bar to relief.’” (In re Vaccine Cases (2005) 134 Cal.App.4th 438, 458 (quoting Cal-Tech Communications, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Co. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 163, 182).) In support of this argument, Defendant cites a new case, Loeffler v. Target Corp. (2014) 58 Cal.4th 1081. That case held, in part, that an action may not lie under the UCL when another statutory scheme provides the exclusive means for resolving disputes. (Id., at 1126.) Ignoring that this case could have been brought to the Court’s attention at the initial hearing, Loeffler does not support dismissing the UCL subclaim arising from TAA violations.

 

The exclusive jurisdiction and administrative remedies discussed in Loeffler are materially different from the scheme of the TAA and the jurisdiction of the Labor Commissioner. In Loeffler, consumers alleged that Target had collected a sales tax reimbursement on takeout sales of coffee allegedly not subject to a sales tax. (Loeffler, supra, 56 Cal.4th at 1093-1094.) The UCL claim specifically sought injunctive relief against Target from charging sales tax to consumers for to-go coffee, and restitution of the wrongfully acquired tax reimbursement. (Id.) The Supreme Court reviewed the history and development of California law on the sales tax issues. “[U]nder California's sales tax law, the taxpayer is the retailer, not the consumer. In addition, the taxability question, whether a particular sale is subject to or is exempt from sales tax, is exceedingly closely regulated, complex, and highly technical. A comprehensive administrative scheme is provided to resolve these and other tax questions and to govern disputes between the taxpayer and the Board. Under these administrative procedures, it is for the Board in the first instance to interpret and administer an intensely detailed and fact-specific sales tax system governing an enormous universe of transactions. Administrative procedures must be exhausted before the taxpayer may resort to court. ... [T]his comprehensive statutory scheme is inconsistent with consumer claims such as plaintiffs’ by which a party other than the taxpayer would seek to litigate whether a sale is taxable or exempt.” (Id., at 1103.) The Court held that an action may not lie under the UCL because another statutory scheme provides the exclusive means for resolving disputes, analogizing the tax scheme to the exclusive remedy provisions of the workers' compensation law, which would encompass even collateral or derivative claims. (Id. at 1126-1127.) For those reasons, the Court rejected UCL claims because resolution of the claims would require the court to determine the taxability of the transactions prior to any determination by the tax authorities. Loeffler also discussed whether the claims violated Cal. Const. art. XII, § 32, which forbids any legal proceedings which “prevent or enjoin the collection of any tax” and only permits actions to recover taxes after payment in a manner provided by the Legislature. While the Court avoided directly addressing this constitutional issue, this article still informed the Court’s interpretation of the relevant statutes. (Id. at 1102-1103.)

 

The TAA materially differs from the comprehensive tax scheme discussed above. Generally, it regulates the activities of a “talent agency,” i.e., “a person or corporation who engages in the occupation of procuring, offering, promising, or attempting to procure employment or engagements for an artist or artists . . ..” (Civ. Code § 1700.4(a).) “Artists” include stage and screen actors; radio and musical artists; musical organizations; theater, movie, and radio directors; writers; cinematographers; composers; lyricists; arrangers; models; “and other artists and persons rendering professional services in motion picture, theatrical, radio, television and other entertainment enterprises.” (Civ. Code § 1700.4(b).) The Act’s remedial purpose is to protect artists seeking professional employment from the abuse of talent agencies. (Waisbren v. Peppercorn Productions, Inc. (1995) 41 Cal.App.4th 246, 254.) The Labor Commissioner has jurisdiction over controversies arising under the TAA, and all such matters shall be referred to the Labor Commissioner. (Lab. Code, § 1700.44(a); Styne v. Stevens (2001) 26 Cal.4th 42, 54.) The Labor Commissioner must hear all such disputes, and all remedies before the Labor Commissioner must be exhausted before the parties can proceed to the superior court. (REO Broadcasting Consultants v. Martin (1999) 69 Cal.App.4th 489, 494-495.) Even when a defendant raises a defense based on a violation of the TAA, the court cannot consider the merits of that issue until the Labor Commissioner has first examined it. (Styne, supra, 26 Cal.4th at 58-59.) Under such circumstances, the court would stay the proceedings and permit the filing of a petition to determine the controversy with the Labor Commissioner. (Id. at 58.)

 

The TAA is not analogous to the tax scheme discussed in Loeffler, since the TAA does not provide any means for resolving a dispute between competitors before the Labor Commissioner. As discussed in the previous demurrer, the caselaw on exhaustion only involves contract disputes between artists and agents. (Blanks v. Seyfarth Shaw LLP (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 336, 365.) The term “parties” is not expressly defined by the TAA, but TAA uses “party” or “parties” in reference to parties under an Artist-Agent contract. (Civ. Code §§ 1700.37, 1700.44; see also 8 CCR § 12022.) In fact, unpublished petition decisions by the Labor Commissioner reflect the TAA provides the Labor Commissioner original jurisdiction over “controversies between ‘artists’ and ‘agents.’” (Dokoza v. Lenhoff Enterprises, Inc., No. TAC41756, 2016 WL 10981702 (Cal. Lab. Comm’r Dec. 4, 2016) [dismissing petition by Dokoza because she was not an “artist” as defined by the TAA, and as a result her petition is not subject to the original jurisdiction of the Labor Commissioner]; accord Patchett v. DLM LA, Inc., No. TAG 47367, 2018 WL 11662398 (Cal. Lab. Comm’r Oct. 18, 2018); Turtle Rock Studios, Inc. v. Digital Dev. Mgmt., Inc., No. TAC-41987, 2017 WL 8161184 (Cal. Lab. Comm’r Mar. 20, 2017); Monacella v. Int’l Creative Mgmt., Inc., No. TAC 7-95, 1995 WL 18241646 (Cal. Lab. Comm’r Sept. 27, 1995).) Plaintiff and Defendant do not have an artist-agent relationship. Furthermore, unlike the instant claims between competitors, Loeffler noted an exclusive administrative scheme for consumers to demand repayment before the tax authorities. (Loeffler, supra, 58 Cal.4th at 1122–1123 [a so-called Javor claim].) The TAA reveals no analogous administrative procedure for competitors to bring an action before the Labor Commissioner.

 

The administrative exhaustion principles of the TAA would also be subject to exceptions. Courts recognize several exceptions to the exhaustion of administrative remedies defense, including: “(1) when the administrative agency cannot provide an adequate remedy, and (2) when the subject of controversy lies outside the agency’s jurisdiction.” (Campbell v. Regents of University of California (2005) 35 Cal.4th 311, 322.) “[T]he rule requiring exhaustion of administrative remedies does not apply where an administrative remedy is unavailable.”  (Tiernan v. Trustees of Cal. State University & Colleges¿(1982) 33 Cal.3d 211, 217.)  “The doctrine of exhaustion of administrative remedies does not require a litigant to present his or her claim to an administrative body powerless to grant relief.”  (Id. at 218.)  “There must be clearly defined machinery for the submission, evaluation and resolution of complaints by aggrieved parties.”  (Life Care Centers of America v. CalOptima¿(2005) 133 Cal.App.4th 1169, 1177, internal quotes and alterations omitted.) As discussed, there is no apparent procedure for competitors to bring an action before the Labor Commissioner. As such, the administrative remedies of the TAA are unavailable to Plaintiff. This further distinguishes Loeffler, which likely could not consider any exceptions due to the constitutional forbearance of such judicial proceedings, as well as the availability of remedies for consumers within the tax scheme.

 

Accordingly, the motion is DENIED.


Case Number: 25SMCV01924    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Khorsandi, v. Frankel, et al. 

CASE NO.:                25SMCV01924

MOTION:                  Demurrer to the First Amended Complaint  

HEARING DATE:   6/26/2026

 

LEGAL STANDARD 

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (CCP §§ 430.30, 430.70.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or coKhnclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732, internal citations omitted.) 

 

A special demurrer for uncertainty is disfavored and will only be sustained where the pleading is so bad that defendant cannot reasonably respond—i.e., cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him/her. (CCP § 430.10(f); Khoury v. Maly’s of Calif., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Moreover, even if the pleading is somewhat vague, “ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Ibid.)  

 

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof. (CCP § 435(b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1322(b).) The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (CCP §§ 436(a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].) 

 

“Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given.” (Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227.) It is an abuse of discretion for the court to deny leave to amend where there is any reasonable possibility that plaintiff can state a good cause of action. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) The burden is on plaintiff to show¿in what manner¿plaintiff can amend the complaint, and¿how¿that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading.¿(Id.) 

  

ANALYSIS 

             

Defendants Andrew Frankel M.D. and Lasky Clinic Surgical Center Inc. demur and move to strike Plaintiff Lida Khorsandi’s First Amended Complaint (“FAC”).

 

Second Cause of Action for Fraud

 

The elements of fraud are: “(a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or

nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce

reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Charnay v. Cobert (2006) 145

Cal.App.4th 170, 184.) To advance a cognizable fraud claim, "every element of the cause of action . . . must be alleged in full, factually and specifically, and the policy of liberal construction of pleading will not usually be invoked to sustain a fraud claim deficient in any material respect." (Wilhelm v. Pray, Price, Williams & Russell (1986) 186 Cal.App.3d 1324, 1331.) The heightened particularity requirement necessitates pleading facts that "show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered." (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.) When fraud is alleged against a corporate defendant, the plaintiff must specifically allege the names of the persons who allegedly made the representation, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written. (Tarmann v. State Farm Mutual Auto Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157.)

 

The FAC fails to allege fraud with the requisite specificity. The FAC generally alleges that “Defendant knowingly made false representations about being a double board-certified plastic surgeon and ENT.” (FAC at 2:9-10.) While presumably “Defendant” means Dr. Frankel, and he presumably made these representations to Plaintiff (though this is also unclear), the FAC does not plead how, when, where, and by what means Dr. Frankel made this representation. Thus, further facts are required to meet the heightened pleading standard.

 

Further, the FAC fails to establish the falsity of that representation. The FAC admits that Dr. Frankel is a certified plastic surgeon. Instead, when alleging falsity, the FAC relies on an entirely different material fact, that Dr. Frankel did not disclose that he acquired his license after 2001 and was not subject to continual education. “Defendant did not specify that his license was acquired before 2001, which means he does not have to undergo continual education like his colleagues who may have gotten their licenses to practice well after him. Plastic surgeons who have recently become certified must undergo continual education frequently to maintain their knowledge and ensure that their abilities are to date.” (FAC, at 2:11-15.) Thus, the falsity of the above pled representation is not established. Additionally, Plaintiff has not pled that Dr. Frankel misrepresented when he got his license or whether he was subject to continuing education. The FAC also alleges that Defendant “misled Plaintiff into thinking that because he is double board-certified, he must follow all the rules required of his profession.” (FAC at 2:21-22.) Not only is this misrepresentation not established with specificity, the FAC concedes that Dr. Frankel was following the rules of his profession, since he was not required to undergo continual education. Thus, the new alleged misrepresentations do not support the cause of action.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is SUSTAINED with 20 days’ leave to amend.

 

Third Cause of Action for IIED

 

A cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress (“IIED”) exists when there is: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff's suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant's outrageous conduct. (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1050- 1051.) A defendant’s conduct is “outrageous” when it is so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community. (Ibid.) The defendant must either intend his or her conduct to inflict injury or engaged in it with the realization that injury will result. (Ibid.) Liability for intentional infliction of emotional distress does not extend to mere insults, indignities, threats, annoyances, petty oppressions, or other trivialities. (Ibid.) Generally, the question of whether conduct is in fact outrageous is a question of fact to be determined beyond the pleading stage. (So v. Shin (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 652.) Nonetheless, “[w]hile those cases say that it is ‘usually’ a question of fact, several cases have dismissed intentional infliction of emotional distress claims on demurrer, concluding that the facts allege did not amount to outrageous conduct as a matter of law.” (Barker v. Fox & Associates (2015) 240 Cal.App.4th 333, 355-356.) “Severe emotional distress means emotional distress of such substantial quality or enduring quality that no reasonable [person] in civilized society should be expected to endure it.” (Potter v. Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 965, 1004.)

 

The FAC fails to establish extreme and outrageous conduct and intent of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress. The FAC only alleges a conclusion that Defendant displayed “reckless disregard of the probability of causing emotional distress” because “as a double board-certified ENT and Plastic Surgeon, he should have been more careful with his conduct during the procedure.” (FAC at 3:23-24.) The fact that Dr. Frankel “should have been more careful” sounds in medical negligence, and presents no facts suggesting that he recklessly disregarded any probability of causing emotional distress. Further facts are required to establish his reckless disregard.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is SUSTAINED with 20 days’ leave to amend.

 

Motion to Strike

 

The FAC’s punitive damages request is improper, as the claims arise out of professional negligence. (CCP § 425.13.) Accordingly, the motion to strike is GRANTED without leave to amend.

 

Plaintiff has 20 days to file an amended complaint.

Legal Standard

 

            In the absence of contrary court order, a civil litigant’s right to discovery is broad. “[A]ny party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action . . . if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” (CCP § 2017.010; see Davies v. Superior Court (1984) 36 Cal.3d 291, 301.) Section 2017.020(a) vests the Court with authority to limit the scope of discovery if the burden, expense, or intrusiveness of the discovery sought “clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.”  CCP § 2019.030 directs the Court to consider the needs of the case, amount in controversy, and the importance of the issues at stake in the litigation, and to consider whether the discovery being sought is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative, or is obtainable by a more convenient or less expensive or less burdensome way, when deciding whether to restrict the frequency of extent of use of an authorized discovery method.

 

On receipt of a response to requests for admission the propounding party may move for an order compelling a further response if the propounding party deems that an objection to an RFA is without merit or too general. (CCP § 2033.290(a)(2).)

 

Analysis

 

Plaintiff Michelle Theisen moves for an order compelling Defendant Ridgeline Construction Group to further respond to her Requests for Admission (“RFA”), Set One. Plaintiff seeks monetary sanctions of $1,262.00.

 

            Defendant contends that the motion is moot based on the parties’ meet and confer agreement. In reply, Plaintiff accepts the motion is substantively moot. The only issue remaining is sanctions. The record demonstrates that Plaintiff appropriately brought the instant motion. Defendant does not present any substantial justification for the initial responses or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. Thus, the Court is inclined to impose the modest sanctions noticed.

 

Accordingly, the motion is MOOT. Sanctions are imposed in the noticed amount of $1,262.00 against Defendant and Defendant’s counsel of record, jointly and severally. Sanctions are to be paid within 30 days.
