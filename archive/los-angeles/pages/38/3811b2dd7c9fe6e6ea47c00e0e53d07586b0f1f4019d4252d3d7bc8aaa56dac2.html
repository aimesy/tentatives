SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, American Honda Motor Co., Inc.

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, Esdras Cristobal Hernandez

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Summary Adjudication as to Second and Third Causes of Action

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

This is a lemon law action. Plaintiff Esdras Cristobal Hernandez sued Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. alleging Defendant failed to promptly replace his car or make restitution after it was unable to conform the car to its express warranty after a reasonable number of repair attempts. Plaintiff also asserts claims for breach of implied warranty and violation of section 1793.2, subdivision (b), of the Song-Beverly Act (SBA).

 

RULING: Denied.

Request for Judicial Notice: Granted.

The Court takes judicial notice of the existence of the superior court order and the truth of the results reached therein. (Lockley v. Law Office of Cantrell, Green, Pekich, Cruz & McCort (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 875, 885-86.)

 

Defendant moves for summary adjudication as to Plaintiff’s second and third causes of action for breach of implied warranty and violation of section 1793.2, subdivision (b) of the SBA. It argues that Plaintiff’s claims fail as a matter of law. Plaintiff opposes the motion, maintaining the existence of triable issues of material facts. Defendant’s motion is denied.

 

The pleadings frame the issues for motions, “since it is those allegations to which the motion must respond. (Citation.)” (Scolinos v. Kolts (1995) 37 Cal. App. 4th 635, 640-641; FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 382-83; Jordan-Lyon Prods., LTD. v. Cineplex Odeon Corp. (1994) 29 Cal.App.4th 1459, 1472.) The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atl. Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) A defendant moving for summary judgment “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) “Once the defendant . . . has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Ibid.)

 

“When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) “An issue of fact can only be created by a conflict in the evidence. It is not created by speculation, conjecture, imagination or guesswork.” (Lyons v. Security Pacific National Bank (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 1001, 1041 (citation omitted).)

 

Second Cause of Action: Breach of Implied Warranty

Defendant has not met its burden as to Plaintiff’s breach of implied warranty cause of action. It argues that Plaintiff’s claim fails because the car did not contain defects during the implied warranty period. The duration of the implied warranty of merchantability is coextensive with an express warranty but is not to exceed “one year following the sale of new consumer goods to a retail buyer.” (Civ. Code, § 1791.1, subd. (c).) Here, the car was leased on October 30, 2019 and therefore the implied warranty expired a year later on October 30, 2020. [Compendium Ex. 1: Motor Vehicle Lease Agreement (HERNANDEZ V. AHM – 000008-000010).] The car was presented once during the implied warranty period on June 23, 2020 for routine maintenance. [Id. Ex. 2: Plaintiff’s Depo. pp. 113:6-114:17; id. Ex. 3: June 23, 2020 Repair Order (AHM002248-002251).] However, this is immaterial because it does not show that the car was merchantable during the implied warranty period. At most, Defendant’s evidence shows that the defect may have been discovered after the implied warranty period expired, which is distinct from the existence of a defect making a good unmerchantable. (Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1297, 1308.) Defendant is therefore not entitled to summary adjudication on this claim.    

 

Third Cause of Action: Violation of SBA section 1793.2, subdivision (b)

Defendant also fails to meet its burden as to Plaintiff’s claim for breach of section 1793.2, subdivision (b) of the SBA. Defendant contends that this claim fails because there is no evidence that any repair presentation was not commenced within a reasonable time or lasted more than 30 days. The SBA imposes certain obligations on manufacturers of consumer goods (i.e., new goods) who expressly warrant those goods. (Civ. Code, §§ 1791, subd. (a), 1793.2, subd. (a).) Among these obligations is a requirement that the manufacturer commence service and repair within a “reasonable time,” and “[u]nless the buyer agrees in writing to the contrary,” to service or repair the goods “so as to conform to the applicable warranties within 30 days.” (Id. § 1793.2, subd. (b).) The plain meaning of the statute indicates that goods must be conformed to warranty within 30 days, not that any repair attempt must not exceed 30 days. Thus, Defendant’s argument and supporting evidence that the longest time Plaintiff’s car was out for repairs was 8 days is immaterial. [Compendium Ex. 1: Repair Orders (HERNANDEZ V. AHM – 000011-000033).] If Defendant was unable to conform the car to warranty within 30 days, it was not in compliance with the provision. (See Cummins, Inc. v. Superior Court (2005) 36 Cal.4th 478, 485 [“Goods must be repaired to comply with the warranty within 30 days, unless delay is caused by conditions beyond the control of the manufacturer or its representative”].)

 

Defendant’s authority to the contrary is unavailing. Defendant cites to an unpublished Ninth Circuit decision, where the court held that “under any reasonable reading of the statute, § 1793.2(b) requires only that BMW complete any single repair attempt within 30 days.” (Schick v. BMW of North America, LLC (9th Cir. 2020) 801 Fed.Appx. 519, 521) But the court’s conclusion is unpersuasive because it does not explain why its interpretation is correct. If the legislature intended that goods be out of service due to repair for more than 30 days, it would have said so. (See Civ. Code, § 1793.22, subd. (b)(3) [presumption that reasonable number of repair attempts made if the “vehicle is out of service by reason of repair of nonconformities by the manufacturer or its agents for a cumulative total of more than 30 calendar days since delivery of the vehicle to the buyer”].) Defendant is therefore not entitled to summary adjudication on the section 1793.2, subdivision (b) claim.

 

Defendant’s motion is therefore denied.

BIFURCATE

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant City of Los Angeles acting by and through the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiffs, William Stan Spry and Heidi Spry

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion to Bifurcate

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiffs William Stan Spry and Heidi Spry sued Defendant Los Angeles Department of Water and Power after improvements to the road and drainage leading to the Hollywood Reservoir allegedly caused rainwater to be directed, redirected, and to flood toward and into Plaintiffs’ property. Plaintiffs assert claims for inverse condemnation, private nuisance, and negligence.

 

RULING: Granted.

Request for Judicial Notice: Denied.

The Court does not take judicial notice of the appellate court decisions because they are immaterial to disposition of the motion.

 

Defendant moves to bifurcate the trial on the issue of causation for the inverse condemnation cause of action. It argues that bifurcation promotes judicial economy because a ruling on causation would narrow the issues for the jury trial. Plaintiffs oppose the motion, contending that Defendant’s causation theory is actually one for comparative fault, the issue involves disputed facts that should be heard by the jury, and bifurcation does not support economy here.[1] Defendant’s motion is granted.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 598 states in part: “The court may, when the convenience of witnesses, the ends of justice, or the economy and efficiency of handling the litigation would be promoted thereby, on motion of a party, after notice and hearing, make an order, no later than the close of pretrial conference in cases in which such pretrial conference is to be held, or, in other cases, no later than 30 days before the trial date, that the trial of any issue or any part thereof shall precede the trial of any other issue or any part thereof in the case, except for special defenses which may be tried first pursuant to Sections 597 and 597.5.” Code of Civil Procedure, section 1048, subdivision (b), additionally adds: “The court, in furtherance of convenience or to avoid prejudice, or when separate trials will be conducive to expedition and economy, may order a separate trial of any cause of action, including a cause of action asserted in a cross-complaint, or of any separate issue or of any number of causes of action or issues, preserving the right of trial by jury required by the Constitution or a statute of this state or of the United States.”

 

Trial is bifurcated. In an inverse condemnation proceeding, a plaintiff does not have a right to a jury trial on the issue of liability. (Marshall v. Dept. of Water & Power (1990) 219 Cal.App.3d 1124, 1140-41.) Bifurcation would support judicial economy whether Defendant or Plaintiffs prevail on the issue of cause-in-fact. Should Defendant prevail, the remaining claims may be subject to dismissal. If Plaintiffs prevail, the focus shifts to the other elements of their claims. (Bookout v. State of Cal. ex rel. Dept. of Transportation (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 1478, 1488 [“[I]f the defendant did not cause harm, there is no causation no matter what the cause of action”].) The Court declines to consider the merits of the causation issue here. Defendant’s motion is therefore granted.

 

The issue of causation for the inverse condemnation cause of action is bifurcated.   

 

Defendant to give notice.

 

 

 

 

 


[1] Plaintiffs also claim the motion is procedurally improper because the notice of motion does not cite the authority it is based upon. This challenge is without merit as the notice of motion clearly states that Defendant is moving for bifurcation of the issue of causation for the inverse condemnation claim. The points and authorities also specifies the legal authorities relied upon. (See Luxury Asset Lending, LLC v. Philadelphia Television Network, Inc. (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 894, 909 [“An omission in the notice may be overlooked if the supporting papers make clear the grounds for the relief sought”].)

DEMURRER

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiffs, Liliana Rodriguez, Samuel Alaniz, Alexis Alaniz, and Adriana Alaniz, by and through her Guardian Ad Litem Liliana Rodriguez

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendants, Paola Hernandez, individually and as Trustee of the Hernandez Paola 2007 Trust

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Demurrer to Answer

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiffs Liliana Rodriguez, Samuel Alaniz, Alexis Alaniz, and Adriana Alaniz, by and through her Guardian Ad Litem Liliana Rodriguez sued their landlord Paola Hernandez, alleging unlawful rent increases during the COVID-19 pandemic and uninhabitable conditions on the property. Hernandez filed a cross-complaint against Plaintiffs, seeking damages and an offset on any liability based on Plaintiffs’ alleged refusal to provide access for repairs and causing waste at the property. She asserts claims for: (1) Declaratory Relief; (2) Offset and Equitable Indemnity; (3) Waste; (4) Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing; (5) Abatement of Rent/Unjust Enrichment; and (6) Intentional Interference with Repairs and Code Compliance.   

 

RULING: Sustained in part.

Plaintiffs demur to each of the 43 affirmative defenses in Defendants’ answer. They argue that the affirmative defenses fail to state facts sufficient to constitute a defense, are uncertain, and are not directed at specific causes of action. Defendants oppose the demurrer, maintaining the sufficiency of their defenses. Plaintiffs concede that affirmative defenses 19, 24-25, 32, and 40 are not affirmative defenses, but denials. They are thus not at issue. Plaintiffs’ demurrer is sustained with leave to amend as to defenses 2-22, 24-38, and 40-42. The demurrer is sustained without leave to amend as to defenses 23 and 43. The demurrer is overruled as to defenses 1 and 39.

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.” (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.) “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law . . . .” (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.) In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated. (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.) “If the complaint states a cause of action under any theory, regardless of the title under which the factual basis for relief is stated, that aspect of the complaint is good against a demurrer.” (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.)

 

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of Cal., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616; Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2 [“[U]nder our liberal pleading rules, where the complaint contains substantive factual allegations sufficiently apprising defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet, a demurrer for uncertainty should be overruled or plaintiff given leave to amend”].)

 

An “answer to a complaint must include ‘[a] statement of any new matter constituting a defense.’ The phrase ‘new matter’ refers to something relied on by a defendant which is not put in issue by the plaintiff. [Citation.] Thus, where matters are not responsive to essential allegations of the complaint, they must be raised in the answer as ‘new matter.’ [Citation.] Where, however, the answer sets forth facts showing some essential allegation of the complaint is not true, such facts are not ‘new matter,’ but only a traverse.” (Walsh v. West Valley Mission Community College Dist. (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1532, 1546.) Affirmative defenses must aver facts “as carefully and with as much detail as the facts which constitute the cause of action and are alleged in the complaint.” (FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 384.)

Plaintiffs’ demurrer to affirmative defenses 1 and 39 is overruled. Plaintiffs do not address the first affirmative defense in their memorandum of points and authorities and their argument in support of the demurrer to the thirty-ninth affirmative defense is actually directed to the thirty-eighth affirmative defense for ratification. [Demurrer at p. 14.] The demurrer to these defenses is thus overruled.

The demurrer to the remaining defenses is sustained because they are new matters supported by “terse legal conclusions” or conclusions of fact. (FPI Development, Inc., supra, 231 Cal.App.3d at p. 384.) Defendants are granted leave to amend these defenses, except for affirmative defenses 23 for indemnification and 43 for reservation of further defenses. Defendants and Plaintiffs are not joint tortfeasors. (Gem Developers v. Hallcraft Homes of San Diego, Inc. (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 419, 430.) An answer can be amended to assert additional affirmative defenses via noticed motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (a). The Court declines to consider additional attacks on the answer raised for the first time in reply. (Maleti v. Wickers (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 181, 227-28.)

Defendants are granted 30 days’ leave to amend. Defenses conceded by Plaintiffs may be amended as well.

Plaintiffs to give notice.

DEMURRER

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiffs, Liliana Rodriguez, Samuel Alaniz, Alexis Alaniz, and Adriana Alaniz, by and through her Guardian Ad Litem Liliana Rodriguez

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendants, Paola Hernandez, individually and as Trustee of the Hernandez Paola 2007 Trust

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Demurrer to Cross-Complaint

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiffs Liliana Rodriguez, Samuel Alaniz, Alexis Alaniz, and Adriana Alaniz, by and through her Guardian Ad Litem Liliana Rodriguez sued their landlord Paola Hernandez, alleging unlawful rent increases during the COVID-19 pandemic and uninhabitable conditions on the property. Hernandez filed a cross-complaint against Plaintiffs, seeking damages and an offset on any liability based on Plaintiffs’ alleged refusal to provide access for repairs and causing waste at the property. She asserts claims for: (1) Declaratory Relief; (2) Offset and Equitable Indemnity; (3) Waste; (4) Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing; (5) Abatement of Rent/Unjust Enrichment; and (6) Intentional Interference with Repairs and Code Compliance.   

 

RULING: Sustained.

Plaintiffs demur to each cause of action in Defendants’ cross-complaint. They argue that Defendants fail to sufficiently plead their counterclaims. Defendants oppose the demurrer, maintaining the sufficiency of their allegations. Plaintiffs’ demurrer is sustained without leave to amend as to the declaratory relief, offset and equitable indemnity, implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and abatement of rent/unjust enrichment causes of action. The demurrer is sustained with leave to amend as to the waste and intentional interference with repairs and code compliance claims.

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.” (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.) “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law . . . .” (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.) In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated. (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.) “If the complaint states a cause of action under any theory, regardless of the title under which the factual basis for relief is stated, that aspect of the complaint is good against a demurrer.” (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.)

 

First Cause of Action: Declaratory Relief

Plaintiffs argue that Defendants’ declaratory relief claim fails because it seeks a judicial declaration of issues that will be resolved by the main action.

 

“Generally, an action in declaratory relief will not lie to determine an issue which can be determined in the underlying tort action.” (Cal. Ins. Guarantee Assn. v. Superior Court (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1617, 1623.) “The relief statute should not be used for the purpose of anticipating and determining an issue which can be determined in the main action. The object of the statute is to afford a new form of relief where needed and not to furnish a litigant with a second cause of action for the determination of identical issues.” (Id. at pp. 1623-24.)

 

The demurrer to the declaratory relief cause of action is sustained without leave to amend. Defendants seek a judicial declaration that rent increases during periods not governed by local COVID-19 moratorium were lawful, Plaintiffs interfered with access to the property, “delaying code remediation,” and any monetary liability is offset by unpaid rent and Plaintiffs’ damage to the property. [Cross-Complaint ¶ 8.] All of these issues are raised by the complaint and answer and will necessarily be resolved independent of Defendants’ cross-complaint. Defendants cannot seek declaratory relief for identical issues. Plaintiffs’ demurrer to this claim is thus sustained without leave to amend.

 

Second Cause of Action: Offset and Equitable Indemnity

Plaintiffs contend that Defendants’ cause of action for offset and equitable indemnity fails because offset is not a cause of action and indemnity is inapplicable.

 

The demurrer to the offset and equitable indemnity cause of action is sustained without leave to amend. First, a setoff claim “may only be used defensively.” (Constr. Protective Services, Inc. v. TIG Specialty Ins. Co. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 189, 197-98.) Second, “[t]here can be no indemnity without joint and several liability by the prospective indemnitor and indemnitee....” (Gem Developers v. Hallcraft Homes of San Diego, Inc. (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 419, 430.) Defendants and Plaintiffs are not joint tortfeasors. The demurrer to this claim is therefore sustained without leave to amend.

 

Third Cause of Action: Waste

Plaintiffs argue that Defendants’ waste claim fails because their allegations are conclusory.

 

The demurrer to the waste cause of action is sustained with leave to amend. “The hirer of a thing must repair all deteriorations or injuries thereto occasioned by his want of ordinary care.” (Civ. Code, § 1929.) Defendants allege that Plaintiffs caused or permitted waste at the property, including but not limited to uncontrolled pest infestation, saturated and deteriorated subflooring, and willful neglect of fixtures and common areas. [Cross-Complaint ¶ 11.] Absent, however, is the specific action or inaction that caused the waste. Because there is a reasonable possibility that Defendants can cure this defect, they are granted leave to amend.

 

Fourth Cause of Action: Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

Plaintiffs contend that Defendants’ breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing claim fails because it is barred by the litigation privilege and Defendants’ allegations are conclusory.

 

The good faith and fair dealing cause of action is barred by the litigation privilege. The litigation privilege bars a civil action for damages for communications made in any legislative or judicial proceeding or “in any other official proceeding authorized by law . . . .” (Civ. Code, § 47, subd. (b).) This extends to “a communication intended to prompt an administrative agency charged with enforcing the law to investigate or remedy a wrongdoing.” (Hagberg v. Cal. Federal Bank (2004) 32 Cal.4th 350, 362.) Contrary to Defendants’ assertion, their claim is premised on Plaintiffs’ reports to the Los Angeles Housing Department regarding the conditions of Plaintiffs’ tenancy. [Cross-Complaint ¶ 13.] The cause of action is therefore barred by the litigation privilege and the demurrer to this claim is sustained without leave to amend.

 

Fifth Cause of Action: Abatement of Rent/Unjust Enrichment

Plaintiffs argue that Defendants’ cause of action for abatement of rent/unjust enrichment fails because neither offset nor unjust enrichment are causes of action.

 

The demurrer to the abatement of rent/unjust enrichment cause of action is also sustained without leave to amend. Defendants allege that Plaintiffs were unjustly enriched during periods where they withheld rent while allegedly obstructing Defendants’ compliance efforts. [Cross-Complaint ¶¶ 15-16.] They seek a disgorgement of the wrongfully withheld rent in equity or via “abatement setoff.” [Id. ¶ 16.] As explained above, a setoff is not a cause of action. Neither is unjust enrichment. (Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 221, 231.) The cause of action also cannot be construed or amended as a restitution claim because an express contract governs Plaintiffs’ rent obligations. (Id. at p. 231.) The demurrer to this claim is therefore sustained without leave to amend.

 

Sixth Cause of Action: Intentional Interference with Repairs and Code Compliance

The demurrer to the intentional interference with repairs and code compliance cause of action is sustained with leave to amend. As pled, this claim does not state a cognizable cause of action. However, there is a reasonable possibility that Defendants can amend their pleading to state a viable claim (e.g., Civil Code section 1954). Defendants are thus granted leave to amend.

 

Defendants are granted 30 days’ leave to amend. Defendants may only add facts in support of the successfully challenged claims. (Harris v. Wachovia Mortgage, FSB (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1018, 1023.) If Defendants decline or fail to file an amended cross-complaint, Plaintiffs may move for dismissal. (Code Civ. Proc., § 581, subd. (f)(2).) Any new causes of action outside the scope of the order may be addressed via a motion to strike.

 

Plaintiffs to give notice.

MOTION TO STRIKE

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendants, 1015 West Garvey West Covina, LP and MIG Management, Inc.

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiffs, Sean McClure and Sandra McClure

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion to Strike

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiffs Sean McClure and Sandra McClure brought the instant habitability action against Defendants 1015 West Garvey West Covina, LP and MIG Management, Inc. alleging an unremedied cockroach infestation, faulty weather protection, and defective plumbing, among other issues.

 

RULING: Denied.

Defendants move to strike Plaintiffs’ prayer for punitive damages and allegations supporting their punitive damages claim. They argue that Plaintiffs do not sufficiently plead their entitlement to punitive damages. Plaintiffs oppose the motion, maintaining the sufficiency of their allegations. Defendants’ motion is denied.

 

To support punitive damages, the plaintiff’s complaint “must allege ultimate facts of the defendant’s oppression, fraud, or malice.” (Cyrus v. Haveson (1976) 65 Cal.App.3d 306, 316-17; Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) “Malice” is “conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1).) “Oppression” is “despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.” (Id. § 3294, subd. (c)(2).) “To support an award of punitive damages on the basis of conscious disregard of the safety of others, a plaintiff ‘must establish that the defendant was aware of the probable dangerous consequences of his conduct, and that he willfully and deliberately failed to avoid those consequences.’” (Penner v. Falk (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 858, 867.) “Pleading in the language of the statute is not objectionable when sufficient facts are alleged to support the allegation.” (Perkins v. Superior Court (1981) 117 Cal.App.3d 1, 6-7.)

 

Plaintiffs have sufficiently pled their punitive damages claim. They allege numerous defective conditions spanning multiple years and across two different units. This includes a cockroach infestation, faulty weather protection, defective plumbing, and dilapidation. [Compl. ¶¶ 22-56.] Plaintiffs further allege that these issues persisted despite notice to Defendants and that Defendants’ failure to remedy the hazards was done “intentionally and knowingly” and to prioritize their own profits. [Id. ¶¶ 81, 85.] This is sufficient to support their punitive damages request. (See Stoiber v. Honeychuck (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 903, 920 [prayer for punitive damages sufficiently supported by allegations of defective conditions creating a nuisance and defendants’ “full knowledge of the consequences thereof and the damage being caused to plaintiff”].) Defendants’ motion is therefore denied.

 

Defendants to answer the operative complaint within 10 days.
