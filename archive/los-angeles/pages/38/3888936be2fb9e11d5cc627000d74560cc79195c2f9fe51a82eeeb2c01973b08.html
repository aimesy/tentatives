Case Number: 21STCV00939    Hearing Date: September 14, 2026    Dept: 406

 

HOLLYWOOD & VINE RESIDENCES ASSOCIATION,

                       

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

HEI/GC HOLLYWOOD & VINE CONDOMINIUMS LLC, et al.,

                       

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  21STCV00939

  Hearing Date:  September 14, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

cross-defendant pan pacific mechanical, llc’s demurrer to cross-complaint  

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On January 11, 2021, Plaintiff Hollywood & Vine Residences Association (the Association) filed this action against Defendants HEI/GC Hollywood & Vine Condominiums LLC (HEI) and Webcor Construction L.P. (Webcor). The complaint stems from alleged construction defects. HEI was the developer on the project, and Webcor was the general contractor.

            On May 7, 2024, HEI and Webcor filed a cross-complaint for indemnity against Pan Pacific Mechanical, LLC (Pan Pacific), a subcontractor on the project.

            On May 9, 2025, Indian Harbor Insurance Company (Indian Harbor) filed a complaint-in-intervention against Peter Kraut (Kraut) and South Coast Engineering Group, Inc. (South Coast) for indemnity and contribution. Indian Harbor filed an amended complaint-in-intervention on June 25, 2025. Indian Harbor is the insurer for Pan Pacific. Indian Harbor alleges that Kraut and South Coast are responsible for the defective plumbing system which led to the cross-claims against Pan Pacific.

            On June 4, 2025, pursuant to a settlement, the Court entered the Association’s request for dismissal of the complaint against HEI and Webcor.

            On June 23, 2025, pursuant to that same settlement, the Court entered HEI’s and Webcor’s request for dismissal of the cross-complaint against Pan Pacific.

            On April 1, 2026, Kraut and South Coast filed a cross-complaint for indemnity against Pan Pacific.

            Effectively, the only claims left are between Pan Pacific and its insurer (Indian Harbor) on the one hand, and Kraut and South Coast on the other. Indian Harbor, having paid the settlement on behalf of Pan Pacific, presently seeks indemnity from Kraut and South Coast. Kraut and South Coast, in turn, seek indemnity from Pan Pacific. 

            On August 7, 2026, Pan Pacific filed the instant demurrer to Kraut’s and South Coast’s cross-complaint. Kraut and South Coast filed an opposition on August 31, 2026. Pan Pacific filed a reply on September 4, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or by proper judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30(a).) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) “California law emphasizes ultimate fact pleading (with some exceptions, notably for fraud and related torts) ‘in ordinary and concise language,’ and the test for adequacy is not absolute but ‘whether the pleading as a whole apprises the adversary of the factual basis of the claim.’” (Lim v. The.TV Corp. Internat. (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 684, 690.)

MEET AND CONFER

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, 435.5.) The Court finds that Cross-Defendant has satisfied the meet and confer requirement. (See Prountzos Decl.)

DISCUSSION

“Subrogation is defined as the substitution of another person in place of the creditor or claimant to whose rights he or she succeeds in relation to the debt or claim.” (American States Ins. Co. v. National Fire Ins. Co. (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 692, 701.) “In the case of insurance, subrogation takes the form of an insurer's right to be put in the position of the insured in order to pursue recovery from third parties legally responsible to the insured for a loss which the insurer has both insured and paid.” (Ibid.) “The subrogated insurer is said to ‘stand in the shoes’ of its insured, because it has no greater rights than the insured and is subject to the same defenses assertable against the insured.” (Ibid.)

Kraut and South Coast seek indemnity from Pan Pacific to the extent they are held liable to Indian Harbor. However, Indian Harbor, as Pan Pacific’s insurer, stands in the shoes of Pan Pacific in the complaint-in-intervention. Thus, Indian Harbor’s claims against Kraut and South Coast are not true third-party claims for which Kraut and South Coast may seek indemnity from Pan Pacific. There is no “joint legal obligation to another for damages.” (See C.W. Howe Partners Inc. v. Mooradian (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 688, 700, emphasis added.) An indemnity agreement does not cover first-party claims unless the parties expressly agree to do so. (Alki Partners, LP v. DB Fund Services, LLC (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 574, 600-01.) The contract in this case provides as follows:

 

The client agrees, to the fullest extent permitted by law, to indemnify and hold harmless South Coast Engineering Group, Inc., it's officers, directors, employees, and subconsultants against all damages, liabilities or costs including reasonable attorney's fees and defense costs, arising out of or in any way connected with this project or the performance by any of the parties above named of the services under this agreement, excepting only those damages, liabilities or costs attributable to the sole negligence of South Coast Engineering Group, Inc.

(Cross-Compl., Ex. A.)

            This does not expressly cover first-party claims. Kraut and South Coast cite to Hot Rods, LLC v. Northrop Grumman Systems Corp. (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 1166 for the proposition that a broadly worded indemnity clause may be interpreted to cover first-party claims. In Hot Rods, the indemnity clause covered “any claims, demands, penalties, fees, fines, liability, damages, costs, losses, or other expenses” and defined “claim” as “any claim or demand by any Person for any alleged liabilities.” (Id. at p. 1181.) The clause then defined “person” as “any person, employee, individual, corporation, unincorporated association, partnership, trust, federal, state or local governmental agency, authority or other private or public entity.” (Ibid.) The court found that “the parties expressly adopted a broad definition of ‘claim’ and ‘person’ that encompasses ‘any alleged liabilities,’ and covers both first and third party claims.” (Ibid.)

            By contrast, the indemnity clause in this case contains no such language. The court in Alki distinguished Hot Rods for this precise reason, finding that “the indemnity provision in Hot Rods, LLC is significantly different” and that “[n]o such language appears in the Alki Partners-Hedgeworks indemnity provision.” (Alki, supra, 4 Cal.App.5th at p. 605.) The indemnity clause in Alki provided that “Alki Partners will ‘indemnify’ Hedgeworks for all losses, including attorney fees ‘resulting in any way from the performance or non-performance of [Hedgeworks's] duties hereunder.’” (Id. at p. 602.) The court in Alki found that this clause was “indistinguishable from the provision considered in Carr, where the indemnitor promised to indemnify ‘against all claims, damages, losses and expenses including attorney fees arising out of the performance of the work described herein.’” (Ibid., quoting Carr Business Enterprises, Inc. v. City of Chowchilla (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 14, 19.) The clause in Alki was also “indistinguishable from the indemnity provision in Myers, which indemnified ‘every claim’ arising ‘out of the performance of the contract.’” (Ibid., quoting Myers Building Industries, Ltd. v. Interface Technology, Inc. (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 949, 974.) The indemnity clause in this case is also indistinguishable from the ones in Alki, Carr, and Meyers, which only covered third-party claims. Because the claims asserted in Indian Harbor’s complaint-in-intervention are essentially first-party claims between Pan Pacific and Kraut/South Coast, the agreement does not require Pan Pacific to indemnify Kraut/South Coast for those claims.

            Interstate Fire & Casualty Ins. Co. v. Cleveland Wrecking Co. (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 23, cited by Kraut and South Coast, does not compel a different conclusion. In that case, the subcontractor breached its agreement with the general contractor by failing to defend and indemnify the general contractor against a personal injury claim. The general contractor’s insurer funded the general contractor’s settlement with the injured individual. The insurer then filed a subrogation action against the subcontractor to recover the amounts it incurred in defending and settling the underlying claims. (Id. at pp. 28-31.) The issue was whether the insurer could maintain a subrogation action against the subcontractor. (Ibid.) The subcontractor argued that the insurer “was not in a superior equitable position” and that the “alleged breach of contract had not caused any damage.” (Ibid.) The court disagreed with the subcontractor and concluded that the insurer had stated a valid subrogation claim. (Id. at pp. 32-50.)

            Interstate Fire is inapposite because the issue here is not whether Indian Harbor has a valid subrogation claim against Kraut and South Coast. Rather, the issue is whether Kraut and South Coast may seek indemnity from Pan Pacific for the subrogation claims asserted by Indian Harbor. Interstate Fire did not contend with this issue and does not support the proposition asserted by Kraut and South Coast.

In sum, Kraut and South Coast cannot seek indemnity from Pan Pacific for the claims asserted by Pan Pacific’s own insurer. Indian Harbor stands in the shoes of Pan Pacific and is enforcing Pan Pacific’s indemnity rights under the agreement with Kraut and South Coast. Kraut and South Coast cannot make Pan Pacific indemnify them for their own indemnification obligation to Pan Pacific. This is a circular claim which would effectively nullify Kraut’s and South Coast’s indemnity obligations under the agreement, a result not supported by the law.

The remaining claims for contribution, apportionment, and declaratory relief are derivative and fail for the same reasons. Moreover, to the extent Kraut and South Coast contend their liability should be reduced or negated because Pan Pacific shares some or all of the fault, that should be asserted as a defense rather than an affirmative claim against Pan Pacific.

CONCLUSION

            Cross-Defendant Pan Pacific Mechanical, LLC’s demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.


Case Number: 25STCV08294    Hearing Date: September 14, 2026    Dept: 406

 

AURORA GUERRA RODRIGUEZ,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

HECTOR JESUS CANO, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV08294

  Hearing Date:  September 14, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendants’ motion for summary judgment or adjudication

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On March 21, 2025, Plaintiff Aurora Guerra Rodriguez filed this action against Defendants Hector Jesus Cano and Etelvina de Casas. The complaint stems from alleged refusal to accept rent, uninhabitable conditions, and tenant harassment.

            On August 7, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion for summary judgment or adjudication. Plaintiff filed an opposition on August 27, 2026. Defendants filed a reply on September 3, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” (Leyva v. Garcia (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1095, 1101.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce “substantial responsive evidence.” (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)

DISCUSSION

            “Notice of the motion and supporting papers shall be served on all other parties to the action at least 81 days before the time appointed for hearing.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(a)(2).) “If the notice is served by facsimile transmission, express mail, or another method of delivery providing for overnight delivery, the required 81-day period of notice shall be increased by two court days.” (Ibid.) “[T]rial courts do not have authority to shorten the minimum notice period for summary judgment hearings.” (McMahon v. Superior Court (2003) 106 Cal.App.4th 112, 118.)

Here, Defendants served the motion electronically on August 4, 2026, but set the hearing for September 14, 2026. This fails to account for the 81-day notice period plus 2 court days for electronic service. The Court has no discretion to hear the motion on a shortened notice period.

Defendants argue that Plaintiff waived the notice defect by filing a substantive opposition, citing Carlton v. Quint (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 690. Quint reiterated the principle that “the appearance of a party at the hearing of a motion and his or her opposition to the motion on its merits is a waiver of any defects or irregularities in the notice of motion.” (Id. at p. 697.) In Quint, the plaintiff challenged the propriety of service under Code of Civil Procedure section 1011. (Ibid.) However, the court found that the plaintiff received the then-required 28 days of notice prior to the summary judgment hearing. (Id. at p. 696.)

Unlike Quint, this case does not involve an irregularity in service. Instead, the issue is the shortening of the mandatory notice period. Subsequent cases have distinguished Quint on this ground. (See Urshan v. Musicians' Credit Union (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 758, 768 [“the present case does not involve an alleged defect with the movant's service of notice, as was the case in Quint. It instead involves an unauthorized order by the trial court shortening time to notice the summary judgment hearing”]; Robinson v. Woods (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1264 [same]; Boyle v. CertainTeed Corp. (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 645, 651 [same].) Quint is further distinguishable because the plaintiff in that case did not “request a continuance of the summary judgment hearing or contend he was prejudiced by inadequate notice or service.” (Quint, supra, 77 Cal.App.4th at p. 697.) Here, Plaintiff does both.

Courts have repeatedly held that a trial court may not shorten the notice period absent the parties’ express, affirmative consent. (See McMahon, supra, 106 Cal.App.4th at p. 118; Robinson, supra, 168 Cal.App.4th at p. 1264; Urshan, supra, 120 Cal.App.4th at p. 768.) Waiver of the notice period cannot be inferred from silence. (Ibid.) Nor can waiver be implied where a plaintiff objects to the shortened notice period and then defends on the merits. (See Boyle, supra, 137 Cal.App.4th at p. 650 [“No waiver may be implied where, as here, a party alleging error has made its objection and then acted defensively to lessen the impact of the error”].) Here, Plaintiff expressly objects to the shortened notice period. This objection is not waived just because Plaintiff also addresses the merits in case the Court rejects his notice argument.

In sum, the motion must be denied because Defendants failed to comply with the mandatory notice period.

CONCLUSION

            Defendants’ motion for summary judgment or adjudication is DENIED.

           

 


Case Number: 26STCV09505    Hearing Date: September 14, 2026    Dept: 406

 

FARHAD ARDESHIRPOUR-ZARTOSHTI, M.D., et al.,

                        Plaintiffs,

            v.

 

MELISSA PENUELA, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  26STCV09505

  Hearing Date:  September 14, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendants’ special motions to strike (CRS# 5268, 1363)

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On March 24, 2026, Plaintiffs Farhad Ardeshirpour-Zartoshti, M.D. (Dr. Ardesh) and Farhad Ardesh, M.D., Inc. filed this action against Defendants Melissa Penuela (Melissa) and Roy Penuela (Roy). The complaint asserts (1) breach of written contract, (2) intentional infliction of emotional distress, (3) defamation, and (4) false light.

            The dispute stems from a cosmetic surgery that Plaintiffs performed on Melissa in March 2023. The complaint makes the following allegations. The procedure was an elective surgery fully negotiated and agreed upon while Melissa was represented by Roy, her husband and attorney. Among other things, the contract provided that Plaintiffs would perform the surgery at a discount in exchange for Defendants posting a positive review within 30 days. Defendants expressly acknowledged the risks of the procedure, including scarring. The procedure was performed within the standard of care, and the outcome was reasonable and satisfied Defendants’ expectations.

However, Defendants failed to post a positive review within 30 days or at any time. Instead, Defendants raised concerns about scarring, and Plaintiffs performed some revisional procedures in October 2023 and February 2024, free of charge. When Defendants returned to Plaintiffs in September 2024, demanding additional surgical services for free, Plaintiffs declined. Then, from March to July 2025, Melissa posted multiple online reviews with the following statement: “Dr. Farhad Ardesh (‘SCAR-DESH’) left me with ugly visible scarring from a facelift he did when I was only 39 years old. I am not happy with my results.” (Compl., Ex. E-I.)

On July 23, 2026, Defendants filed the instant two special motions to strike. Plaintiffs filed an omnibus opposition on August 31, 2026. Defendants filed their respective replies on September 4, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

A special motion to strike under Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16 allows a defendant to seek early dismissal of a lawsuit that qualifies as a strategic lawsuit against public participation. A SLAPP is “a cause of action against a person arising from any act of that person in furtherance of the person’s right of petition or free speech under the United States Constitution or the California Constitution in connection.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(b)(1).)

Such acts include (1) any written or oral statement or writing made before a judicial proceeding, (2) any written or oral statement or writing made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a judicial body, (3) any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest, or (4) any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(e).)

Evaluation of an anti-SLAPP motion requires a two-prong process. First, the court decides whether the defendant has made a threshold showing that the challenged cause of action is one arising from protected activity. Second, if the court finds such a showing has been made, the court must then consider whether the plaintiff has demonstrated a probability of prevailing on the claim. (Nygard, Inc. v. Uusi-Kerttula (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 1027, 1035.) If both prongs are met—(1) the complaint targets protected activity, and (2) the plaintiff has no reasonable probability of success—then the lawsuit is subject to being stricken under the statute. (Dwight R. v. Christy B. (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 697, 709-10.)

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

            The Court does not address Defendants’ objections as they are not pertinent to the Court’s ruling on the motion.

DISCUSSION

I. Protected Activity

To satisfy the first prong, a moving defendant must identify the activity each challenged claim rests on and demonstrate that that activity is protected by the anti-SLAPP statute. (Wilson v. Cable News Network, Inc. (2019) 7 Cal.5th 871, 884.) A “claim may be struck only if the speech or petitioning activity itself is the wrong complained of, and not just evidence of liability or a step leading to some different act for which liability is asserted.” (Park v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1060.)

a. Online Reviews

The anti-SLAPP statute protects “any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(e)(3).) A website is a public forum. (Cross v. Facebook, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 190, 199.) Courts broadly construe what is considered a matter of public interest. (Brodeur v. Atlas Entertainment, Inc. (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 665, 674.) “[T]he issue need not be ‘significant’ to be protected by the anti-SLAPP statute—it is enough that it is one in which the public takes an interest.” (Ibid.)

At the same time, “‘not every Web site post involves a public issue.’” (Wong v. Jing (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 1354, 1366, quoting D.C. v. R.R. (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 1190, 1226.) A post involves a public issue if it constitutes “consumer information that goes beyond a particular interaction between the parties and implicates matters of public concern that can affect many people.” (Ibid.) By contrast, publications which only cover “parochial issues concerning a private dispute about particular [medical] appointments” would not implicate the public interest. (Id. at p. 1367.)

Courts reject a “broad and amorphous” construction of public interest. (Workman v. Colichman (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 1039, 1052.) “Defendants cannot merely offer a ‘synecdoche theory’ of public interest, defining their narrow dispute by its slight reference to the broader public issue.” (FilmOn.com Inc. v. DoubleVerify Inc. (2019) 7 Cal.5th 133, 152.) “At a sufficiently high level of generalization, any conduct can appear rationally related to a broader issue of public importance.” (Rand Resources, LLC v. City of Carson (2019) 6 Cal.5th 610, 625.)

Melissa argues that the online reviews implicate the first prong because “consumer information about the qualifications and performance of professionals — above all, physicians performing elective surgery on the public — is a paradigmatic issue of public interest.” (Melissa Mtn. 7:18-20.) Roy similarly argues that “reviews of a physician’s services are statements on an issue of public interest.” (Roy Mtn. 6:1-2.)[1] However, the actual posts at issue do not constitute consumer information and do not discuss the qualifications and performance of medical professionals. Rather, the posts solely concern Melissa’s dissatisfaction with her surgical experience. The fact that Melissa publicly aired her grievances does not transform the substance of the publications into matters of public importance. (See Wilbanks v. Wolk (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 883, 900 [“a publication does not become connected with an issue in the public interest simply because it is widely disseminated, or because it can be used as an example of bad practices or of how to combat bad practices”].)

This conclusion is bolstered by Defendants’ own argument that the posts contained Melissa’s personal opinions. Melissa confirms that her review merely “reports the speaker’s own state of mind” or her “aesthetic judgment” about her own face. (Melissa Mtn. 12:25-26; Melissa Reply 3:21-23.) Melissa additionally contends that the insult “SCAR-DESH” constitutes “classic rhetorical hyperbole and name-calling that no reasonable reader would understand as a verifiable assertion of fact,” or “a pun.” (Melissa Mtn. 13:1-3; Melissa Reply 3:22-23.) Roy argues the same. (Roy Mtn. 8:10-22.) This further confirms that Melissa’s posts provided no information to aid consumers. Melissa’s review about “ugly visible scarring” reflects her personal opinion about a particular experience. It does not constitute protected consumer information for purposes of the anti-SLAPP statute. Therefore, the posts did not implicate any matter in which the public has an interest.

This case is distinguishable from the cases cited by Defendants. In Wong, the posts “dealt with the more general issues of the use of nitrous oxide and silver amalgam, implied that those substances should not be used in treating children, and informed readers that other dentists do not use them.” (Wong, supra, 189 Cal.App.4th at p. 1367.) “Thus, the review was not just a highly critical opinion of Wong's performance on particular occasions; it was also part of a public discussion and dissemination of information on issues of public interest.” (Ibid.) By contrast, Melissa’s online reviews were “just a highly critical opinion of [Dr. Ardesh’s] performance on particular occasions.” Melissa’s posts did not discuss any issue of public importance in relation to cosmetic surgery.

In Carver v. Bonds (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 328, 344, the challenged article “warned readers not to rely on doctors' ostensible experience treating professional athletes, and told what it described as ‘a cautionary tale’ of plaintiff exaggerating that experience to market his practice.” The statements involved a matter of public concern because they “served as a warning against plaintiff's method of self-promotion, and were provided along with other information to assist patients in choosing doctors.” (Ibid.) By contrast, Melissa’s posts did not warn readers about Dr. Ardesh’s business practices and provided no information to assist patients in choosing doctors. Instead, the posts were purely personal grievances about Melissa’s own experience: “I am not happy with my results.” (Compl., Ex. E-I.)

In Wilbanks, “[t]he statements made by Wolk were not simply a report of one broker's business practices, of interest only to that broker and to those who had been affected by those practices. Wolk's statements were a warning not to use plaintiffs' services. In the context of information ostensibly provided to aid consumers choosing among brokers, the statements, therefore, were directly connected to an issue of public concern.” (Wilbanks, supra, 121 Cal.App.4th at p. 900.) Again, Melissa’s posts solely concerned her own experience and provided no information to aid consumers choosing among cosmetic surgeons.

In Chaker v. Mateo (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1138, 1146, the online posts implicated the plaintiff’s “character and business practices” and “were intended to serve as a warning to consumers about his trustworthiness.” Once again, Melissa’s posts do not serve a similar purpose. They contain no information about Dr. Ardesh’s character or business practices and cannot be interpreted as warning about his trustworthiness. Rather, according to Melissa herself, the posts contained her subjective opinion about her own appearance and a personal insult against Dr. Ardesh which no reasonable reader would take seriously. Melissa specifically denies that her review imputed Dr. Ardesh with any fraudulent or predatory intent, or accused him of being incompetent and reckless. (See Melissa Reply 2:22-26 [“the review says none of that”].) This further confirms that the review provided no consumer information about Dr. Ardesh’s character, practices, or trustworthiness.  

Ultimately, Defendants’ attempt to elevate their personal grievance against Dr. Ardesh into a matter of public importance is not well-taken. The purported public interest—which shifts from “the qualifications and performance of professionals” (Melissa Mtn. 7:18-20) to “the quality and risks of elective facial cosmetic surgery” (Melissa Reply 5:18-21)—is precisely the type of “broad and amorphous” formulation that is routinely rejected by the courts. (See Workman, supra, 33 Cal.App.5th at p. 1052.) Melissa’s online posts, airing her personal grievances through opinions and personal insults, do not contribute to a matter of public importance under either formulation. “[W]hen the context and content of the specific, allegedly wrongful statements at issue here are considered, the degree of connection between those statements and that topic of public interest is insufficient to warrant protection under section 425.16.” (See Musero v. Creative Artists Agency, LLC (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 802, 821.)

Accordingly, Defendants have failed to satisfy the first prong in relation to the allegations targeting the online reviews.

b. Lawsuit

            The anti-SLAPP statute protects (1) any written or oral statement or writing made before a judicial proceeding or (2) any written or oral statement or writing made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a judicial body. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(e).) Defendants’ filing of the lawsuit, Melissa Penuela v. Farhad Ardeshirpour, M.D., et al. (25STCV29227), is a written statement made before or in connection with a judicial proceeding. So too is Defendants’ response to Plaintiffs’ retraction demand. The complaint partially targets this protected activity. (See Compl. ¶¶ 28-29.)

Where a cause of action arises from a mix of protected and unprotected activity, a court may strike those portions that pertain to protected activity. (Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 393-94.) Therefore, the burden shifts to Plaintiffs to show a probability of success on the claims to the extent they relate to Defendants’ filing of the prior lawsuit and their pre-litigation communications.

II. Probability of Success

Civil Code section 47(b) makes privileged a publication made “[i]n any (1) legislative proceeding, (2) judicial proceeding, (3) in any other official proceeding authorized by law, or (4) in the initiation or course of any other proceeding authorized by law.” (Civ. Code, § 47(b).) The privilege “applies to any publication required or permitted by law in the course of a judicial proceeding to achieve the objects of the litigation, even though the publication is made outside the courtroom and no function of the court or its officers is involved.” (Rusheen v. Cohen (2006) 37 Cal.4th 1048, 1057.) The privilege covers prelitigation communications that are rationally related to litigation. (Action Apartment Assn., Inc. v. City of Santa Monica (2007) 41 Cal.4th 1232, 1251.)

Here, Defendants’ filing of the prior lawsuit and their pre-litigation communications constitute privileged publications. Therefore, Plaintiffs have no probability of success to the extent the claims are based on that activity. Accordingly, paragraphs 28 and 29 are stricken from the complaint.

CONCLUSION

            Defendants’ special motions to strike are GRANTED as to paragraphs 28 and 29 of the complaint and DENIED in all other respects.

 


[1] Roy also denies making the posts at all, but for purposes of this motion, the Court finds that the posts do not implicate matters of public interest regardless of who made them.
