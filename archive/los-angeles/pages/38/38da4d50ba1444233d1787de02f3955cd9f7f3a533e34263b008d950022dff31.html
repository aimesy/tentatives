I.            INTRODUCTION

Plaintiff Thomas Tran (“Tran”) moves to strike or tax the memorandum of costs filed by defendant Li Lin(“Lin”) on July 30, 2026. The memorandum claims costs of $5,324.18, consisting of three line items: (1) $2,212.67 for “court-ordered transcripts”, (2) $1,070.94 in filing and motion fees, and (3) $2,040.57 for “Other.”

Li filed an opposition brief on September 10, 2026.

Tran filed a reply brief on September 14, 2026.

II.          LEGAL STANDARD

The memorandum of costs is a verified statement by the party, attorney, or agent that the costs are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).) If the items on a verified cost bill appear to be proper charges, they are prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses, and services listed therein were necessarily incurred. (Oak Grove School Dist. v. City Title Insurance Co. (1963) 217 Cal.App.2d 678, 698.) “[T]he trial court’s first determination is whether the statute expressly allows the particular item and whether it appears proper on its face; if so, the burden is on the objecting party to show the costs to be unnecessary or unreasonable.” (Foothill-DeAnza Community College Dist. v. Emerich (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 11, 29 (Foothill-DeAnza).)

III.        DISCUSSION

Tran argues that Lin’s memorandum of costs improperly claims $2,212.67 for transcripts that were not ordered by the Court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (b)(5).

In opposition, Lin argues that the Court ordered specific transcripts to be submitted along with the Closing trial binder and submits a copy of said Order. The Order required the parties to submit the page of the reporter’s transcript as a numbered appendix to their written closing arguments if their arguments referred to any trial testimony or exhibits. The practical effect of this Order directed the parties to order transcripts. Accordingly, the cost for the transcripts is allowable.

Next, Tran challenges Lin’s claim for $2,040.57 in “Other” charges. Lin’s opposition includes a collection of unauthenticated receipts, without any supporting declaration, along with a document entitled “Cost Calculation.” (Opp., Ex. E.) The first line of the second page of Exhibit E identifies “Other costs” as “binder, driving, trial costs, lodging fees $2,040.57”, but without any accompanying declaration showing that these expenses were necessary to the litigation or reasonably incurred, the evidence is inadmissible.  

Last, while Tran’s moving papers sought to tax Lin’s claimed costs for filing fees, the reply brief omits any discussion of this and the Court considers this issue is withdrawn.

IV.        CONCLUSION

In light of the foregoing, the Court GRANTS Tran’s motion to tax in part and taxes Lin’s memorandum of costs in the amount of $2,040.57, leaving an award of costs in the amount of $4,253.24.

I.            INTRODUCTION

On April 16, 2026, plaintiffs German Perez Garcia and Andrea Martinez Negrete (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed this motion for an attorneys’ fee award in the amount of $77,364.38, consisting of $58,691.50 in fees for Quill & Arrow, LLP (“QA”), a 1.25 multiplier enhancement of $14,672.88, and an additional $4,000 for Plaintiffs’ counsel to review the opposition brief, prepare the reply, and attend the hearing on this motion.

Defendant Ford Motor Company (“Defendant”) filed an opposition brief on September 10, 2026.

Plaintiffs filed a reply brief on September 17, 2026.

II.          DISCUSSION

Plaintiffs’ attorneys and paralegals billed at hourly rates ranging from $175 to $950, with most billing around $350 to $500. In the Court’s experience, these rates are reasonable. Defendant argues these rates should be reduced to a range of $250 to $400 for attorneys and a maximum of $100 for law clerks or paralegals, and refers the Court to a spreadsheet attached to the Declaration of Saed Atallah for proposed reductions. Notably, Defendant does not argue that any of the billing entries were duplicative or excessive; rather, the spreadsheet simply notes that the hourly rate of each timekeeper should be reduced. However, Defendant does not include any evidence supporting its proposed rates, such as declarations from attorneys in the community or its own defense counsel.

In the Court’s experience, the hourly rates charged by QA are reasonable and the amount of time spent on this case, 142.40 hours, is reasonable considering that litigation lasted over two years, involved a dispositive motion, and required trial briefs and motions in limine to be filed. The Court therefore grants a base lodestar of $58,691.50, along with an additional $2,425.50 for time spent to review Defendant’s opposition brief and prepare the reply brief. The Court declines to award a multiplier because there is no showing that the case required any exceptional skill, beyond that possessed by counsel and reflected in the hourly billing rate, or that it required Plaintiffs’ counsel to turn down other work.

III.        CONCLUSION

Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ motion is GRANTED in part and the Court issues an award of $61,117 in fees.

Dept. 3

I.            INTRODUCTION

This action was filed on May 16, 2025, by plaintiff Anna Ayvazyan (“Plaintiff”). Plaintiff alleges she was involved in an automobile collision with defendant Anush Tsaturyan (“Defendant”) on or about August 9, 2023. Defendant allegedly failed to stop at a posted stop sign while Plaintiff was traveling through a controlled intersection, thus colliding with Plaintiff’s vehicle and causing Plaintiff serious injuries.

On August 25, 2026, Plaintiff filed this motion for an order compelling Defendant to serve further written responses to Plaintiff’s Requests for Production of Documents, Set One, Nos. 23 through 27 and produce all responsive documents. Plaintiff also requests the Court impose monetary sanctions against Defendant and/or her counsel of record in the amount of $4,500.

Defendant filed an opposition brief on September 10, 2026.

Plaintiff filed a reply brief on September 14, 2026.

II.          DISCUSSION

The document requests at issue, Request Nos. 23, 24, 25, 26, and 27, all seek photo and video surveillance footage of Plaintiff, as well as written reports and billing documentation relating to any said surveillance performed from the date of the incident to the present.

Both parties discuss the application of Suezaki v. Superior Court (1962) 58 Cal.2d 166, in which the California Supreme Court held that surveillance footage does not constitute a privileged attorney-client communication, but may possibly be entitled to qualified attorney work-product protection. (Id., p. 178.) Plaintiff contends that review of the surveillance discovery is necessary to prepare for Defendant’s use of it as impeachment material. (See, e.g., Plaintiff’s Separate Statement, p. 7.) Plaintiff also argues that billing documentation is directly related to the “existence, scope, timing, and extent of any surveillance conducted” on Defendant’s behalf. (Separate Statement, p. 15.) Plaintiff cites to Suezaki for the proposition that surveillance evidence was discoverable in order to protect against surprise and prepare for the examination of the person who conducted the surveillance. (Motion, 8; Reply, p. 4.)

Defendant points out that the Suezaki court remanded the matter back to the trial court in order to consider “all of the relevant factors involved and then determine whether, under all the circumstances, discovery would or would not be fair or equitable.” (Suezaki, supra, 58 Cal.2d at p. 178.)

The work product doctrine exists to “[p]reserve the rights of attorneys to prepare cases for trial with that degree of privacy necessary to encourage them to prepare their cases thoroughly and to investigate not only the favorable but the unfavorable aspects of those cases.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.020, subd. (a).) It also exists to “[p]revent attorneys from taking undue advantage of their adversary’s industry and efforts.” (Id., subd. (b).) The work product of an attorney, other than a writing reflecting an attorney’s impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal research, “is not discoverable unless the court determines that denial of discovery will unfairly prejudice the party seeking discovery in preparing that party's claim or defense or will result in an injustice.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.030, subd. (b).)

Both parties acknowledge that the purpose of this surveillance footage is to impeach Plaintiff’s claims regarding the nature and extent of her injuries; Furthermore, the extent of any surveillance conducted reflects defense counsel’s mental impressions, thoughts, opinions, and strategy. The impeachment value of such evidence is lost if the footage is produced before Plaintiff has been deposed and, conversely, the impeachment value of the surveillance footage has been preserved if Plaintiff’s deposition has already taken place. Therefore, at this point, ordering Defendant to produce the surveillance footage would allow Plaintiff to take advantage of defense counsel’s industry and efforts, while one of the purposes of the work product doctrine is to prevent that from happening.

Furthermore, written reports exchanged between attorney and investigator are attorney-client privileged. (Fireman’s Fund Ins. Co. v. Sup. Ct. (Front Gate Plaza, LLC) (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1263, 1274.) Therefore, they are not discoverable under any circumstance absent a showing of waiver. Accordingly, the motion to compel further is DENIED with respect to RFP Nos. 23 through 27.

III.        CONCLUSION

Plaintiff’s motion is DENIED. Plaintiff’s request for sanctions is DENIED.

Defendant to give notice.

Dept. 3

I.            INTRODUCTION

On August 5, 2025, plaintiff Aaron Flowers (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against defendant Lyft, Inc. (“Defendant”) and Michael Andrew Fernandez (“Fernandez”). Plaintiff alleges that Fernandez was operating a vehicle for Lyft when Fernandez’s vehicle collided with Plaintiff’s vehicle, causing severe harm and other personal injuries. (Compl., ¶ 26.) The accident occurred on October 2, 2023, near the intersection of Orange Grove Blvd. and Madeline Dr. in Pasadena, CA. (Compl., ¶ 26.)

On April 29, 2026, Defendant filed this motion to compel arbitration. Plaintiff filed an opposition brief on September 10, 2026. Defendant filed a reply brief on September 16, 2026.

II.          LEGAL STANDARD

“The burden of persuasion is always on the moving party to prove the existence of an arbitration agreement with the opposing party by a preponderance of the evidence.” (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 164.) The moving party “can meet its initial burden by attaching to the [motion or] petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party's] signature.” (Bannister v. Marinidence Opco, LLC (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 541.) The moving party can also meet its burden by setting forth the agreement's provisions in the motion. (Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 219; see also Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330 [“The provisions must be stated verbatim or a copy must be physically or electronically attached to the petition and incorporated by reference.”].) For this step, “it is not necessary to follow the normal procedures of document authentication.” (Condee, at p. 218; Iyere v. Wise Auto Group (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 755, review denied (Apr. 26, 2023).)

If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then in the second step, the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement. (Condee, supra, 88 Cal.App.4th at p. 219.) If the opposing party meets its burden of producing evidence, then in the third step, the moving party must establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties. (Iyere, supra, 87 Cal.App.5th at p. 755.) The burden of proving the agreement by a preponderance of the evidence remains with the moving party. (Ibid.)

III.        DISCUSSION

Defendant contends that Plaintiff must be compelled to arbitrate his claims because he signed an arbitration agreement when he created a Lyft user account on June 27, 2013, and agreed to the Terms of Service (“Terms”) on October 4, 2016. Defendant points out that Plaintiff affirmatively accepted Defendant’s Terms through the Lyft App on eight separate occasions before and after the alleged accident, from October 4, 2016, to January 3, 2025. Defendant also contends that Plaintiff was logged into the Lyft Platform and was using the platform to provide Rideshare Services at the time of the incident.

The Terms include an arbitration provision stating that “ALL DISPUTES AND CLAIMS” between Plaintiff and Defendant “SHALL BE EXCLUSIVELY RESOLVED BY BINDING ARBITRATION”, including claims arising out of the “Lyft Platform,” “Rideshare Services,” and “all other federal and state statutory and common law claims.” The Terms also include a delegation clause which states that “All disputes concerning the arbitrability of a Claim (including disputes about the scope, applicability, enforceability, revocability or validity of the Arbitration Agreement) shall be decided by the arbitrator,” except in very limited circumstances that do not apply here. (COE, Ex. 7 at pp. 2, 27-28, §§ 17(a), (g), emphasis added.)

Plaintiff argues that the arbitration agreement is unconscionable and cannot be enforced. However, since the arbitration agreement has a delegation clause, Plaintiff’s claims of unconscionability must be specific to the delegation clause; questions regarding the enforceability of the agreement are reserved for the arbitrator. (Tiri v. Lucky Chances, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 231, 244 [emphasis in original]; accord, Aanderud v. Superior Court (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 880, 895.) Since Plaintiff’s opposition brief only addresses whether the entire agreement as a whole is unconscionable, Plaintiff fails to show that the delegation clause in particular is unconscionable. Moreover, since the arbitration agreement is governed by the FAA, Plaintiff’s invocation of the third-party litigation exception codified at Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2(c) is inapplicable.

IV.        CONCLUSION

Accordingly, the motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED. The Court stays the action and sets a status conference for _____________ at 8:30 a.m. in Department 3 of the Alhambra Courthouse.

On April 30, 2026, plaintiff Gevork Mkhitaryan (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendant Fitness International, LLC (“Defendant”). Defendant moves to strike portions of Plaintiff’s Second Cause of Action for Premises Liability: (1) Count Two – Willful Failure to Warn (Civil Code section 846) and (2) Count Three – Dangerous Condition of Public Property.

Plaintiff generally alleges that he slipped and fell while visiting property owned by Defendant. Plaintiff does not state any facts identifying the dangerous condition that Defendant failed to warn of; therefore, the Second Count for Willful Failure to Warn is inadequately pleaded. Also, it is undisputed that Count Three for Dangerous Condition of Public Property is inappropriate because Defendant is not a public entity.

Accordingly, the unopposed motion to strike is GRANTED without leave to amend.
