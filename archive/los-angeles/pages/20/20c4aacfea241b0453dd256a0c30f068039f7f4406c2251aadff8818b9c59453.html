# 21   TENTATIVE RULING                9:15 a.m., Wednesday, June 24, 2026

 

CHRISTINA BALDWIN, et al. v. AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR CO., et al. [25STCV17670]

 

DEMURRER TO THE THIRD AMENDED COMPLAINT OF DEFENDANT ALLSTATE NORTHBROOK INDEMNITY COMPANY, ERRONEOUSLY SUED AS ALLSTATE INSURANCE COMPANY

 

MEET AND CONFER: [UNCLEAR] Counsel for Allstate fails to specify whether the parties met and conferred in-person, telephonically, or by video conference in compliance with CCP 430.41.

 

TIMELINE: Insurance dispute arising out of motor vehicle accident

 

6/23/2023: While Plaintiff Christina Baldwin (“Mrs. Baldwin”) was driving her vehicle, a 2022 Honda Accord (the “Vehicle”), she was in a car accident with Defendant Matthew Zeigler (“Zeigler”), who ran a red light and crashed into Mrs. Baldwin’s Vehicle. Mrs. Baldwin sustained multiple traumatic injuries because of the accident. Mrs. Baldwin’s husband, Plaintiff Bruce Baldwin (“Mr. Baldwin”) (collectively with Mrs. Baldwin, “Plaintiffs”) is alleging loss of consortium.

 

7/24/2023: Plaintiffs’ counsel formally notified Mrs. Baldwin’s property damage insurer— Defendant Allstate Northbrook Indemnity Company (“Allstate”)— that it needed to preserve the Vehicle as evidence in connection with potential product liability claims. Allstate’s adjuster acknowledged counsel’s communication. In connection with the settlement of Mrs. Baldwin’s property damage claim, Allstate acquired title to the Vehicle after it was deemed a “total loss.”

 

12/13/2024: Allstate’s claims representatives reiterated in writing that the Vehicle would not be released or disposed of without notice and would be preserved for litigation purposes.

 

12/18/2024: Allstate called Plaintiffs’ counsel asking if the Vehicle could be disposed of and Plaintiffs’ counsel advised Allstate to continue storing the Vehicle.

 

2/2025: Allstate unilaterally authorizes the release and sale of the Vehicle for auction, without providing notice to Plaintiffs or Plaintiffs’ counsel.

 

6/18/2025: Plaintiffs file the Complaint. The operative Third Amended Complaint (“TAC”), filed 1/6/2026, alleges causes of action for:

1.   Strict Product Liability – Manufacturing Defect

2.   Strict Product Liability – Design Defect

3.   Negligence

4.   Negligence – Failure to Recall

5.   Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability

6.   Negligence

7.   Loss of Consortium

8.   Promissory Estoppel

9.   Negligent Misrepresentation

10.        Negligence

 

5/19/2026: Allstate files this Demurrer, followed by Plaintiffs’ Opposition (6/10/2026) and Allstate’s Reply (6/16/2026).

 

TENTATIVE RULING: DEMURRER OF DEFENDANT ALLSTATE NORTHBROOK INDEMNITY COMPANY is SUSTAINED without leave to amend

 

Allstate demurs to the eighth, ninth, and tenth causes of action in the TAC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e). This is Plaintiff’s third attempt to state a claim against Allstate. For the reasons discussed below, Allstate’s demurrer is SUSTAINED in its entirety without leave to amend.

 

A.   8th Cause of Action: Promissory Estoppel — SUSTAINED without leave to amend

 

Plaintiff’s promissory estoppel claim is insufficiently pled. The elements of a promissory estoppel claim are the same as a breach of contract claim, absent consideration. (US Ecology, Inc. v. State of Cal.¿(2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 887, 903.) “‘[A] promise is an indispensable element of the doctrine of promissory estoppel. The cases are uniform in holding that this doctrine cannot be invoked and must be held inapplicable in the absence of a showing that a promise had been made upon which the complaining party relied to his prejudice....’ ... The promise must, in addition, be ‘clear and unambiguous in its terms.’” (Aceves v. U.S. Bank, N.A. (2011) 192 Cal.App.4th 218, 226 (Aceves), quoting Garcia v. World Savings, FSB (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1031, 1044 (Garcia).) “To be enforceable, a promise need only be ‘definite enough that a court can determine the scope of the duty[,] and the limits of performance must be sufficiently defined to provide a rational basis for the assessment of damages.’” (Aceves, supra, 192 Cal.App.4th at p. 226, quoting Garcia, supra, 183 Cal.App.4th at p. 1045.) 

 

In the TAC, Plaintiff alleges that Allstate promised that the Vehicle would remain subject to the litigation hold and would not be released or disposed of while Plaintiff’s claims were pending or until further authorization from Plaintiff. (TAC, ¶ 90.) Plaintiff alleges the promise was expressly evidenced by Allstate’s communications and impliedly demonstrated by its implementation and ongoing maintenance of the litigation hold over an extended period of time. (Id. ¶¶ 87, 90.)

 

This Court has twice held that Plaintiff failed to plead a “clear and definite” promise by Allstate to indefinitely hold the Vehicle until either the conclusion of litigation or until Plaintiff otherwise gave permission to release the Vehicle. (Minute Order, 12/8/2025; Minute Order 4/1/2026.) In the TAC, Plaintiff has now simply removed the allegations referring to the December 13, 2024 email thread between Plaintiff and Allstate, which the Court found was insufficient to demonstrate the scope and terms of any alleged promise by Allstate. Instead, Plaintiff now relies on Allstate’s internal claim file, which identifies the Vehicle as being on a “Seller Hold Program,” states that the Vehicle was “on hold due to litigation and cannot be released,” and indicates that the Vehicle was “released in error” and sold. (TAC, ¶¶ 88, 96.) These allegations do not cure the previously identified pleading defects. In fact, Plaintiff’s reliance on Allstate’s internal claim notes are even further removed from the direct communications with Plaintiff which the Court previously sought and it is unclear how Plaintiff could have relied on internal claim notes that were not communicated in an outward promise to Plaintiff. At most, the internal claim file appears to describe the now-omitted email exchange with Plaintiff which this Court has already found to not contain any forward-looking commitment to preserve the Vehicle into perpetuity. (Granadino v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 411, 417 [a promise is an assurance that a person will do or will not do something, and not a mere representation of fact].) 

 

Likewise, Plaintiff’s allegations that Allstate’s representations “were communicated to Plaintiff and/or her representatives during the claims process” and were maintained in “follow-ups” remain impermissibly vague. (TAC, ¶¶ 87, 89.) These vague allegations fail to provide the missing factual details related to the extent of Allstate’s direct communications with Plaintiff, the scope of Allstate’s purported duty to Plaintiff, and the terms of the purported promise, such as the limits or conditions on Allstate’s performance, the basis for potential damages, or otherwise. Even if Plaintiff can show that some promise was made by Allstate regarding the preservation of their Vehicle, the issue remains that Plaintiff has not identified any specific promise, whether express or implied, that is “definite enough” such that the Court can determine the scope of the duty, the limits of performance, and a rational basis for the assessment of damages. (Aceves, supra, 192 Cal.App.4th at p. 226; see West v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 780, 804 [to succeed on a promissory estoppel claim, the promise need only be sufficiently clear and definite enough for a court to determine the scope of the duty, the limits of performance and a rational basis for assessing damages].)

 

As this is Plaintiff’s third attempt to cure the defects in her promissory estoppel claim, the Court finds that further opportunity to amend this cause of action would be futile. Accordingly, Allstate’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s eighth cause of action is SUSTAINTED without leave to amend.

 

B.    10th Cause of Action: Negligence — SUSTAINED without leave to amend

 

Plaintiff’s negligence claim is insufficiently pled. In the TAC, Plaintiff alleges that Allstate “undertook responsibility for the handling, management, and disposition of the Vehicle, including placing and maintaining the Vehicle on a litigation-related hold and controlling whether the Vehicle would be released or sold.” (TAC, ¶ 115.) Plaintiff concludes that Allstate owed a duty to exercise reasonable care in preserving the Vehicle and in not releasing or disposing of it in a manner that would foreseeably impair Plaintiff’s ability to investigate and pursue those claims. (Id. ¶ 118.) Plaintiff indicates that this alleged duty is not based on the obligation to preserve evidence, but from Allstate’s assumption of control over the Vehicle and undertaking to maintain it on a litigation hold, thereby inducing Plaintiff’s reliance and creating a foreseeable risk of harm if such undertaking was performed without reasonable care. (Id. ¶ 119.)

 

Although Plaintiff contends that her negligence claim is not based on the preservation of evidence, it is not apparent how this claim is distinct from their previously pled “negligent undertaking” claim which this Court has already rejected. (Minute Order 4/1/2026; see TAC, ¶¶ 118-119.) California does not recognize an independent tort remedy for negligent spoilation of evidence but instead contemplates the existence of such a duty arising from contract (either by mutual agreement or promissory estoppel). (Coprich v. Superior Court (2000) 80 Cal.App.4th 1081, 1091.) As Plaintiff fails to sufficiently plead the elements of promissory estoppel (or an express contractual basis to hold the Vehicle indefinitely during pending litigation), Plaintiff has not established a factual basis to support their conclusory allegations that Allstate owed Plaintiff a duty based on its mere possession of the Vehicle. Once again, Plaintiff’s attempt to reframe Allstate’s duty as arising from a “voluntary undertaking” is an improper circumvention of the rule against negligent spoilation claims.

 

Allstate further argues that California does not recognize a tort cause of action for an insurer’s negligent claim handling, and remedies for the insured arise under bad faith claims (i.e. tortious breach of contract) instead of traditional negligence. (Sanchez v. Lindsey Morden Claims Services, Inc. (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 249, 254 [“negligence is not among the theories of recovery generally available against insurers”]; Benavides v. State Farm General Ins. Co. (2006) 136 Cal.App.4th 1241, 1251 [“The relationship between the parties is contractual.”]; see also Id. at pp. 1253-1254, J. Mosk, concurring [“Plaintiff could not recover for negligence in investigating the insurance claim because ‘[n]egligence is not among the theories of recovery generally available against insurers. Delay or failure to pay policy benefits may be actionable as a breach of contract or bad faith, not negligence.’”].) Plaintiff does not submit any contrary authority.

 

In this case, any action taken by Allstate was taken in the scope of Allstate’s role as Plaintiff’s insurer, pursuant to the alleged property damage insurance policy between the parties. (See TAC, ¶ 22.) Plaintiff’s allegations that Allstate failed to manage and preserve the Vehicle while it was within Allstate’s possession simply reflect a mishandling of Plaintiff’s insurance claim (i.e. an internal processing error) for which liability is limited to a bad faith insurance claim rather than tort liability. Thus, even to the extent that Plaintiff’s negligence claim is not based on a theory of spoilation of evidence, Plaintiff is simply attempting to repackage their breach of the implied covenant claim pled in the First Amended Complaint, which this Court already dismissed, and which Plaintiff did not attempt to replead in the Second Amended Complaint. (Minute Order, 12/8/2025; see Second Amended Complaint, generally.)

 

Accordingly, Allstate’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s tenth cause of action is SUSTAINTED without leave to amend.

 

C.   9th Cause of Action: Negligent Misrepresentation — SUSTAINED without leave to amend

 

Plaintiff’s negligence misrepresentation claim is insufficiently pled. In the TAC, Plaintiff alleges that in the course of handling her insurance claim, Allstate “made representations to Plaintiff, both expressly and through its course of conduct, regarding the status and handling of the Vehicle, including that the Vehicle was currently subject to an active litigation hold and was not eligible for release under that hold.” (TAC, ¶ 101.) Plaintiff relies on the same representations identified in her promissory estoppel claim. (Id. ¶¶ 102-103.)

 

First, Allstate argues that Plaintiff fails to demonstrate that the representations made by Allstate about the status of the Vehicle’s then-existing litigation hold were not false when made. “The law is well established that actionable misrepresentations [whether fraudulent or negligent] must pertain to past or existing material facts.” (Cansino v. Bank of America (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1462, 1469; see CACI 1903.) Thus, at minimum, Allstate’s purported liability for negligently misrepresenting facts regarding the Vehicle’s litigation hold must be limited to misrepresentations regarding the then-current state of the litigation hold and the status of the Vehicle. Here, Plaintiff alleges that Allstate communicated and reinforced the status of the Vehicle through its ongoing monitoring and maintenance of the litigation hold over time, including repeated follow-ups confirming that the hold remained active. (TAC, ¶ 103.) Plaintiff alleges that Allstate subsequently authorized the release of the hold. (Id. ¶ 106.) Taken together, the allegations on the face of the TAC reflect a series of communications to Plaintiff regarding the then-current status of the Vehicle on a litigation hold, which was ostensibly true at the time the communications were made to Plaintiff. Indeed, Plaintiff admits that the Vehicle was erroneously released after Allstate’s communicated with Plaintiff about the status of the Vehicle. Moreover, to the extent that Plaintiff’s Opposition attempts to frame the “misrepresentation” as a representation that the Vehicle “would remain preserved,” such representations about future conduct are not an actionable basis for negligent misrepresentation claims. (See Opp., at p. 6:4.)

 

In the Court’s view, Plaintiff’s negligent misrepresentation claim is essentially duplicative of both her promissory estoppel and negligence theories of liability, which independently fail. The negligent conduct underlying Plaintiff’s negligent misrepresentation claim occurred after the alleged communications about the Vehicle’s status, reflecting an internal error and a mishandling of Plaintiff’s insurance claim that must be asserted as a bad faith insurance claim, rather than a tort claim independent of the insurance policy itself. Further, any alleged “misrepresentations” to Plaintiff are identical to the purported “promise” that Allstate would preserve the Vehicle indefinitely. As the Court discusses above, such allegations are not pled with sufficient factual detail to state a claim for promissory estoppel, let alone to overcome the heightened pleading requirement for negligent misrepresentation.

 

In any event, the Court did not grant Plaintiff leave to add a cause of action for negligent misrepresentation when ruling on Allstate’s prior demurrers. (Minute Order, 12/8/2025; Minute Order 4/1/2026.) “Following an order sustaining a demurrer … with leave to amend, the plaintiff may amend his or her complaint only as authorized by the court's order.” (Harris v. Wachovia Mortgage, FSB (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1018, 1023.) “The plaintiff may not amend the complaint to add a new cause of action without having obtained permission to do so, unless the new cause of action is within the scope of the order granting leave to amend.” (Ibid.) Plaintiff’s theory of negligent misrepresentation does not fall within the scope of the order granting leave to amend Plaintiff’s promissory estoppel/negligent performance of voluntary undertaking theory as pled in the Second Amended Complaint.

 

Accordingly, Allstate’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s ninth cause of action is SUSTAINTED without leave to amend.

JOHNNY GARCIA v. EED MANAGEMENT, LLC, et al. [25STCV24013]

 

DEMURRER TO THE THIRD AMENDED COMPLAINT WITH MOTION TO STRIKE OF DEFENDANT EED MANAGEMENT, LLC

 

MEET AND CONFER: OK. Complies with CCP 430.41 and 435.5.

 

TIMELINE: Real property purchase dispute

 

10/21/2024: Plaintiff Johnny Garcia (“Plaintiff”) enters into a written real estate purchase agreement (the “Agreement”) with Defendant EED Management, LLC (“Defendant”) to purchase the industrial real property located at 4630-4638 & 4646 E. Washington Blvd., Commerce, CA 90040 (the “Property”) for a price of $3,265,000.00. Defendant’s managing member and controlling individual, Defendant Edward E. Diskin (“Diskin”), signs the Agreement on Defendant’s behalf. The Agreement contains multiple contingencies, including that Plaintiff would investigate the Property’s environmental conditions and cancel the Agreement if materially adverse conditions were discovered (with an initial deadline of 11/20/2024).

 

          Diskin, acting on behalf of Defendant, also executes a written Seller’s Mandatory Disclosure Statement in connection with the sale. In doing so, Diskin falsely affirmed that Defendant had no reason to believe that any release of hazardous substance was located on the Property. At the time, Defendant was in possession of a May 2014 Phase II Environmental Site Assessment (the “2014 Report”) that identified hazardous chemicals in soil vapor samples at the Property. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant eventually provided the 2014 Report to Plaintiff. (TAC, ¶¶ 33-34.)

 

Unknown Date: Plaintiff deposits $98,000.00 into escrow as the initial deposit toward the purchase price.

 

11/27/2024: Plaintiff signs amended escrow instructions granting a limited extension to the environmental contingency, which now expired on 12/4/2024, in reliance on Defendant’s assurances that access to the Property would remain available for ongoing testing.

 

2/17/2025: Defendant obstructs Plaintiff’s consultants from inspecting the Property, preventing Plaintiff from discovering vapor intrusion risks on the Property before the real estate transaction closed.

 

2/25/2025: Plaintiff’s Phase I Environmental Site Assessment (the “2025 Report”) reveals multiple environmental conditions, including chemicals, which were hidden by Defendants and which revealed that the value of the Property was lower than the valuation presented by Defendant.

 

4/3/2025: Plaintiff elects to cancel the Agreement and demands the return of Plaintiff’s escrow deposit of $98,000.00.

 

4/14/2025: Defendant refuses to cancel the Agreement, asserting that Plaintiff had waived all environmental contingencies.

 

8/15/2025: Plaintiff files the Complaint. The operative Third Amended Complaint (“TAC”), filed 4/24/2026, alleges causes of action for:

1.   Breach of Contract

2.   Fraud – Intentional Misrepresentation

3.   Fraud – Concealment

4.   Negligent Misrepresentation

 

5/22/2026: Defendant files this Demurrer and Motion to Strike, which is followed by Plaintiff’s Opposition (6/8/2026) and Defendant’s Reply (6/16/2026).

 

TENTATIVE RULING: DEMURRER TO THE THIRD AMENDED COMPLAINT WITH MOTION TO STRIKE OF DEFENDANT EED MANAGEMENT, LLC is OVERRULED in part and SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND in part.

 

I.        DEMURRER

 

Defendant demurs to each cause of action in the TAC on the grounds that Plaintiff fails to plead sufficient facts to state the causes of action against Defendant. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).)

 

A.           2nd and 4th Causes of Action: Fraudulent Misrepresentation and Negligent Misrepresentation — OVERRULED

 

Plaintiff’s fraudulent and negligent misrepresentation claims are sufficiently pled. The elements of a claim for fraudulent misrepresentation are: (1) a false representation by the defendant; (2) knowledge of falsity; (3) an intent to deceive or induce reliance; (4) justifiable reliance by the plaintiff; and (5) resulting damages. (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 974; Service by Medallion, Inc. v. Clorox Co. (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 1807, 1816.) The elements of a claim for negligent misrepresentation are similar, except negligent misrepresentation does not require knowledge of falsity, unlike a cause of action for fraud. (Tindell v. Murphy (2018) 22 Cal. App. 5th 1239, 1252.) “Fraud allegations must be pled with more detail than other causes of action. The facts constituting the fraud, including every element of the cause of action, must be alleged factually and specifically.” (Apollo Cap. Fund, LLC v. Roth Cap. Partners, LLC (2007) 158 Cal. App. 4th 226, 240; Charnay v. Cobert¿(2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 170, 185, fn. 14 [applying heightened pleading standard for fraud to negligent misrepresentation claim].) “This particularity requirement necessitates pleading facts which show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.” (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.) 

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant made false representations to Plaintiff by: (1) certifying in the Seller’s Mandatory Disclosure Statement that Defendant had no knowledge of or reasonable cause to believe that hazardous substances were present at the Property, and (2) certifying in Paragraph 12.1(c) of the Agreement that no hazardous substance conditions existed except as disclosed. (TAC, ¶ 82.) Regarding the former, Dirkin executed the disclosure statement on Defendant’s behalf of October 21, 2024, in writing, through the transaction documents transmitted through escrow. (Id. ¶¶ 12-20.) In the disclosure statement, Dirkin affirmative checked the “Hazardous Substances” box which states the following: “If the property is non-residential property, then as required by California Health and Safety Code § 25359.7 Seller notifies Buyer that: Seller neither knows nor has reasonable cause to believe that any release of hazardous substance (as such terms are defined by California Health and Safety Code §§ 25320 and 25316) has come to be located on or beneath the Property.” (Id., Exh. B, § 7.) Regarding the latter, Dirkin executed the written Agreement on Defendant’s behalf on the same date. (Id. ¶ 61.) Section 12.1(c) of the Agreement contains the “Hazardous Substances/Storage Tanks” provision at issue, which states: “Seller has no knowledge, except as otherwise disclosed to Buyer in writing, of the existence or prior existence on the Property of any Hazardous Substance, nor of the existence or prior existence of any above or below ground storage tank.” (Id., Exh. A, § 12.1(c), italics added.) Plaintiff’s negligent misrepresentation claim is pled in the alternative to Plaintiff’s fraud claims and incorporates the same facts underlying the fraudulent misrepresentations by reference. (Id. ¶¶ 91-92.)

 

Defendant argues that these “misrepresentations” underlying Plaintiff’s second and fourth causes of action do not constitute the type of positive assertions of false fact necessary to state a claim based on a misrepresentation. Civil Code section 1572 “defines ‘fraud’ as, among other things, the ‘positive assertion, in a manner not warranted by the information of the person making it, of that which is not true, though he believes it to be true.’” (Blankenheim v. E. F. Hutton & Co. (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 1463, 1472.) “[C]ase law has long held that negligent misrepresentation is included within the definition of fraud.” (Id. at pp. 1472-1473.) “An ‘implied’ assertion or representation is not enough.” (Wilson v. Century 21 Great Western Realty (1993) 15 Cal.App.4th 298, 306.) In this case, the Court disagrees with Defendant, finding Dirkin’s representations in the Agreement and in the disclosure statement to be clear, affirmative assertions of a verifiable fact (i.e. that Defendant had no knowledge of hazardous substances on the Property at the time the documents was executed). (See TAC, ¶ 82.) Plaintiff alleges that this statement was false when made because Defendant had actual knowledge of unremediated chemicals on the Property. (Id. ¶¶ 17-18.) Whether the statement was actually false when made is a factual question not suitable for resolution on demurrer.

 

Defendant next argues that the above-identified statements cannot be false because Defendant eventually disclosed the presence of hazardous substances on the Property when it provided Plaintiff with the 2014 Report, which disclosed the presence of hazardous chemicals on the Property over a decade earlier. (TAC, ¶¶ 28, 33-34.) The issue with this argument, however, is that it relies on several factual inferences that cannot be drawn from the facts alleged on the face of the pleading. First, the TAC does not identify when the 2014 Report was disclosed to Plaintiff, such that the facts contained within the 2014 Report may not have been timely disclosed to Plaintiff at the time the affirmative factual representations were made. Second, Plaintiff’s own allegations indicate that the 2014 Report was not comprehensive of the information known to Defendant at the time Defendant’s disclosure was made, such as the status of remediation or the outdated models used for measurements in the 2014. (Id. ¶¶ 35-36, 38.) The extent of Defendant’s actual knowledge about the hazardous conditions on the Property at the time the disclosures were made is a factual question that cannot be resolved on demurrer.

 

Defendant further argues that Plaintiff fails to adequately plead reasonable reliance on Defendant’s false representations. In the real estate context, parties “should not be permitted to blindly rely upon statements of interested parties when means of correct information was at hand.” (Kahn v. Lischner (1954) 128 Cal.App.2d 480, 490, citing Konda v. Fay, 22 Cal.App. 722, 726.) Here, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s reliance on Defendant’s representations were unreasonable because the 2014 Report identified unremediated chemicals on the Property. (TAC ¶¶ 21, 34, 38.) Because the 2014 Report revealed chemicals on the Property, Defendant argues that it was unreasonable for Plaintiffs to conclude that the Property lacked contamination. However, this argument ignores Plaintiff’s allegations that the 2014 Report “created a false impression that the Property was in a stable, ‘safe’ condition requiring no further remediation.” (Id. ¶ 35.) By telling Plaintiff that the 2014 Report represented the extent of Defendant’s knowledge about hazards on the Property, Plaintiff was led to believe that the existing report represented “the full extent of the known risk” and refrained from cancelling the Agreement after the conclusions of the 2014 Report were “debunked” by the 2025 Report. (Id. ¶¶ 35, 37.) While Defendant argues that Plaintiff should have investigated the Property before waiving contingencies, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant had superior knowledge of unremediated conditions on the Property and the Property’s condition could not have been discovered because the Property was inaccessible to Plaintiff due to Defendant’s obstruction. (Id. ¶¶ 33, 55-56, 70.) Whether Plaintiff’s due diligence was reasonable under the circumstances— particularly in light of the facts eventually given to Plaintiff in the 2014 Report— is a factual question that cannot be resolved at the pleading stage. For pleading purposes, Plaintiff has alleged sufficient facts to conclude that he reasonably relied on Defendant’s representations about the conditions of the Property.

 

Relatedly, Defendant argues that the alleged misrepresentations could not have caused Plaintiff’s loss of the escrow deposit because this damage was instead caused by the terms of the Agreement itself and Plaintiff’s waiver of contingencies before adequately investigating the Property. Nevertheless, Plaintiff’s theory is that he was wrongfully induced into executing the Agreement and letting the environmental contingency expire in reliance on Defendant’s false representations and failure to disclose the true extent of the facts regarding contamination on the Property. That Defendant’s alleged fraud occurred by means of a contractual relationship does not break the causal relationship between Defendant’s purported false representations and Plaintiff’s alleged damages.

 

Because Plaintiff’s fraud and negligent misrepresentation claims rely on the resolution of factual questions, these claims are not suitable for dismissal at the demurrer stage. The Court finds Plaintiff has adequately pled facts outlining each element of the claims to state causes of action for fraud and negligent misrepresentation against Defendant.

 

B.            3rd Cause of Action: Fraudulent Concealment — OVERRULED

 

Plaintiff’s fraudulent concealment claim is sufficiently pled. “[T]he elements of an action for fraud and deceit based on concealment are: (1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.” (Blickman Turkus, LP v. MF Downtown Sunnyvale, LLC (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 858, 868, internal citation omitted.)¿“California courts apply the same specificity standard to evaluate the factual underpinnings of a fraudulent concealment claim at the pleading stage, even though the focus of inquiry shifts to the unique elements of the claim.” (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 43, emphasis added.) 

 

First, Defendant argues that it did not owe Plaintiff a duty to disclose the “ramifications” of the hazardous substances on the Property. “In transactions which do not involve fiduciary or confidential relations, a cause of action for non-disclosure of material facts may arise in at least three instances: (1) the defendant makes representations but does not disclose facts which materially qualify the facts disclosed, or which render his disclosure likely to mislead; (2) the facts are known or accessible only to defendant, and defendant knows they are not known to or reasonably discoverable by the plaintiff; (3) the defendant actively conceals discovery from the plaintiff.” (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 337, quoting Warner Construction Corp. v. City of Los Angeles (1970) 2 Cal.3d 285, 294.)

 

Here, Plaintiff expressly alleges that Defendant’s duty to disclose arose out of Defendant’s partial disclosures about the Property’s hazards (i.e. the 2014 Report) that were likely to mislead Plaintiff regarding the value of the Property. (TAC, ¶¶ 34-36, 89.) While Defendant frames the undisclosed facts as the “potential ramifications” of contamination on the Property, the Court disagrees with this characterization. Plaintiff is alleging Defendant concealed material facts concerning the environmental condition of the Property, including the: (1) continuing presence and risk of chemical contamination, (2) the absence of regulatory closure or remediation, and (3) the fact that prior environmental conclusions were based on outdated regulatory standards. (Id. ¶ 87.) These facts are not simply the “legal or practical ramifications of disclosed facts,” as Defendants argue, but facts pertaining to the status of the Property itself and the existing hazards thereon at the time of the Agreement. (Opp., at p. 19.) Plaintiff alleges that the true condition of the Property was within the exclusive knowledge of Defendant, but Defendant nevertheless “actively impeded” Plaintiff’s ability to discover such facts by denying access to the Property for further testing. (TAC, ¶¶ 33, 88.) By disclosing the 2014 Report which concluded that the chemicals on the Property fell within an “acceptable risk,” but failing to disclose the remaining material facts regarding the status of the contamination on the Property, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant created a false impression that the Property was in a stable, safe condition and required no further remediation. (Id. ¶¶ 34-35.) Assuming the truth of Plaintiff’s allegations that the 2014 Report was likely to mislead a reasonable property buyer, Defendant was obligated to disclose the remaining information regarding the status of the Property that was not within the 2014 Report.

 

Defendant next argues that Plaintiff fails to plead the reasonable reliance and causation elements of his fraudulent concealment claim for the same reasons discussed above with respect to Plaintiff’s misrepresentation claims. For the same reasons outlined above, including Defendant’s alleged obstruction of Plaintiff’s ability to evaluate the Property and the framework that Defendant’s fraud induced Plaintiff into agreeing to the contract terms, the Court finds these elements of concealment are sufficiently alleged to survive the pleading stage.

 

C.           1st Cause of Action: Breach of Contract — SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s breach of contract claim is insufficiently pled. “The standard elements of a claim for breach of contract are: “(1) the contract, (2) plaintiff's performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant's breach, and (4) damage to plaintiff therefrom.” (Wall Street Network, Ltd. v. New York Times Co. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1171, 1177.) Here, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant materially breached the Agreement by: (1) denying Plaintiff’s environmental consultants access to the Property, (2) falsely certifying that Defendant had no knowledge or reasonable cause to believe that hazardous chemicals were present, (3) failing to disclose the extent of the environmental conditions on the Property and the obsolete nature of the 2014 Report, and (4) interfering with Plaintiff’s right to investigate and cancel based on the hazardous conditions on the Property and the materially different facts uncovered in the 2025 Report. (TAC, ¶ 79.)

 

First, Defendant argues that Plaintiff fails to allege even ultimate facts to demonstrate that he performed the Agreement or was excused from performance. Plaintiff’s breach of contract claim does not even plead the ultimate fact that he adequately performed or was excused from performing the extent of his contractual obligations under the Agreement. At most, Plaintiff alleges he paid the down payment for the transaction into escrow pursuant to Paragraph 4.1 of the Agreement. (TAC, ¶ 9.) Plaintiff also alleges that he proceeded with environmental due diligence by retaining environmental consultants to conduct a site assessment. (Id. ¶ 47.) Plaintiff also generally alleges that he obtained financing and continued performance under the Agreement in reliance on Defendant’s environmental representations. (Id. ¶ 54.) Nevertheless, Plaintiff admits in his Opposition that he was unable to close the transaction but was excused from performance because Defendant obstructed Plaintiff’s access to conduct environmental testing on the Property. (See e.g. Id. ¶ 56.) Nevertheless, Plaintiff does not specifically plead in the TAC that his performance was excused based on Defendant’s conduct. Nor is it apparent that Defendant’s interference with the Property access, in fact, prevented Plaintiff from closing the sale transaction. Accordingly, the Court finds that Plaintiff fails to adequate plead that he was excused from performing his obligations under the Agreement.

 

Second, Defendant argues that the alleged “breaches” of the Agreement did not cause Plaintiff’s contract damages. Instead, Defendant argues that the provisions of the Agreement itself preclude Plaintiff’s contract claim. Specifically, Plaintiff agreed that his escrow deposit would become non-refundable upon the waiver and expiration of all contingencies. (TAC, Exh. A, §§ 4.5, 28.) Plaintiff also agreed to a liquidated damages clause in which Defendant’s damages would be $98,000.00 if Plaintiff breached the Agreement after waiver of the contingencies. (Id. § 21.) Plaintiff admits in the TAC that he allowed the environmental contingency to expire based on Defendant’s conduct. (Id. ¶¶ 45-46, 57-58, 74.) Considered together, Defendant contends that Plaintiff’s loss of the $98,000.00 as the result of the terms of the Agreement itself, not any breach by Defendant.

 

In the Court’s view, Plaintiff’s breach of contract theory relies on the existence of a valid and enforceable Agreement, not one that is voidable due to Defendant’s fraudulent conduct related to the environmental disclosures. Based on the plain language of the Agreement and Plaintiff’s own admissions in the TAC, Plaintiff cannot demonstrate that he is entitled to recover the $98,000.00 escrow deposit on a theory of contract. By the time Plaintiff sought to cancel the sale transaction, the environmental contingency had already expired on December 4, 2024. (TAC, ¶¶ 25-27, 57, 63.) It is not clear how Defendant’s “breach” of Paragraph 14 of the Agreement in February 2025 caused the expiration of the environmental contingency, which had already expired. (Id. ¶¶ 33, 63.) By permitting the waiver of the environmental contingency, Plaintiff obligated himself to pay the escrow deposit under the terms of the Agreement unrelated to Defendant’s alleged breach of Paragraph 14.

 

At best, the Court finds Plaintiff’s allegations related to Defendant’s fraudulent disclosure and misrepresentations relating to the environmental conditions on the Property may be alternatively pled as a breach of the implied covenant claim. While Plaintiff does not point to a specific provision of the Agreement which Defendant purportedly breached through this fraudulent conduct, Plaintiff is essentially arguing that Defendant abused its discretion related to the environmental disclosures for the Property and interfered with Plaintiff’s ability to receive the benefits of the bargain by misleading Plaintiff. To the extent that Plaintiff intends to amend his contract-based claim, the Court grants leave to amend this cause of action as a breach of the implied covenant theory in the alternative to fraud. Therefore, Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s first cause of action is SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

II.       MOTION TO STRIKE – DENIED

 

Defendant moves to strike Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages from the TAC. Plaintiff is seeking punitive damages pursuant to Civil Code section 3294 to the extent that Defendant acted with fraud, oppression, or malice. At minimum, because Plaintiff has sufficiently stated two causes of action based on the fraudulent conduct of Defendant’s managing member, the TAC adequately supports Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages from Defendant. (Civ. Code¿§¿3294, subd. (c)(3).) Diskin’s role as the managing member and controlling individual of Defendant is sufficient to extent liability to the entity Defendant itself. Accordingly, the Court finds that Plaintiff has sufficiently stated a request for punitive damages at the pleading stage.
