Case Number: 23LBCV02181    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: S27

Kellum Trial - 23LBCV02181 Tentative Ruling

 

The court hereby issues its tentative ruling following the trial in this matter.   The complaint has three causes of actions:  1) Partition of Real Property; 2) Accounting/Unjust Enrichment and 3) Declaratory Relief.

The court sees two central issues as follows

1.      Should Dwayne be compensated for acting as the general contractor?  Are there any equitable (or case law) concepts that would allow Dwayne to be reimbursed?   (He's claiming 19 hours a week at 76 weeks, for a total of $85,500.)   

It was not reasonably disputed that all parties agreed that Dwayne would take on the role of overseeing the remodeling due to the fact that Gary had a fulltime job as a Los Angeles Sheriff’s Deputy Sergeant working the county jails, including a lot of overtime and Arthur was a truck driver, and Dwayne working from home and having more capacity and time.  Arthur and Gary did not think that by Dwayne overseeing the renovations it took away from his regular work.  One of the major disputes was whether Dwayne should be compensated for essentially acting as a general contractor for the benefit of all parties.  Arthur and Gary contend that there was no discussion of compensation to Dwayne, whereas Dwayne contends he believes Gary told him he would be compensated, while Arthur never mentioned compensation, and the number he remembers from Gary is $50,000.  There was also testimony that Gary preferred initially to have gone with a contractor rather than with Dwayne. 

There are two equitable theories that could support an award of compensation to Dwayne for acting as general contractor: (1) Quantum Meruit and (2) Equitable Accounting and Credit in a Partition Action.

Quantum Meruit

“Quantum meruit refers to the well-established principle that ‘the law implies a promise to pay for services performed under circumstances disclosing that they were not gratuitously rendered.’ To recover in quantum meruit, a party need not prove the existence of a contract, but it must show the circumstances were such that ‘the services were rendered under some understanding or expectation of both parties that compensation therefor was to be made’ ” (Huskinson & Brown v. Wolf (2004) 32 Cal.4th 453, 458, internal citations omitted.)

“The general rule [is that] when services are rendered by one person, from which another derives a benefit, although there is no express contract or agreement to pay for the services, there is a ‘presumption of law which arises from the proof of services rendered that the person enjoying the benefit of the same is bound to pay what they are reasonably worth.’ ” (Moore v. Spremo (1945) 72 Cal.App.2d 324, 329–30.)

“Ordinarily, where services are rendered and voluntarily accepted, the law will imply a promise upon the part of the recipient to pay for them; but where services are rendered by members of a family, living in one household, to each other, or necessaries are supplied by one near relation to another, the law will presume that they were gratuitous favors merely, prompted by friendship, kindness, and the relationship between them. And in such case, before the person rendering the service can recover, the express promise of the party served must be shown or such facts and circumstances as will authorize the jury to find that the services were rendered in the expectation by one of receiving, and by the other of making compensation therefor.” (Ruble v. Richardson (1922) 188 Cal. 150, 157, 204 P. 572, 575.)

Here, the key inquiry is whether the circumstances at the time the services were rendered were such that both parties understood or expected compensation would be made. There was a clear understanding between the parties that the house would be remodeled and then used as a rental with a rental value of $3,000/month, with the brothers splitting the rental profits equally. Based on Plaintiffs’ reading of the evidence, Dwayne was expected to expend labor and time to carry out the remodel for the benefit of Arthur and Gary, without any compensation. The brothers were to then split the rental income equally. However, that income would arguably be of lesser value without Dwayne’s labor.

Under Moore, there is a legal presumption that Dwayne would be compensated for his services in acting as general contractor while renovating a rental property that all parties would derive equal income from. Further, the family member exception enumerated in Ruble does not appear to defeat that presumption here. The brothers did not live in one household, and the services were not “necessaries” or “gratuitous favors” but rather a laborious undertaking for the monetary benefit of all parties that would ordinarily require paying a professional contractor for their services. Defendant’s construction expert Steve Viani testified that the time Dwayne spent was reasonable, and that the reasonable rate for the work Dwayne performed was approximately $80 per hour. The expert also testified without dispute that a general contractor would have charged $300,000 or more to perform the work Dwayne accomplished for under $200,000. (Defendant’s Closing Brief, p. 4.) It would be unreasonable to expect Dwayne to carry out this work for free.

Plaintiffs’ own brief states that “this was not a casual arrangement, but rather one where the brothers created a business entity and a separate business account to manage the income property.” (Plaintiffs’ Closing brief, p. 3.) As Plaintiffs concede that this was a business arrangement, there is no basis for the family member exception to deprive Dwayne of credit for services rendered. In any other business arrangement, it would be understood or expected by the parties that compensation would be made to Dwayne.

Therefore, Dwayne has a valid claim for recovery through the equitable theory of quantum meruit.

Equitable Accounting and Credit in a Partition Action

“The court may, in all cases, order allowance, accounting, contribution, or other compensatory adjustment among the parties according to the principles of equity.” (CCP § 872.140.)

“Essential principles of equity require that a co-tenant be credited with improvements made in good faith regardless whether the court proceeds by division or sale of the property provided that it can be done without material injury to the rights of the other parties.” (Wallace v. Daley (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 1028, 1038.) “Even though one cotenant does not consent to the making of the improvement, since an action for partition is essentially equitable in its nature, a court of equity is required to take into account the improvements which another cotenant, at his own cost in good faith, placed on the property which enhanced its value....” (Id., 1036.)

“[I]mprovements were not only necessary to the preservation of the common property, but enhanced its rental value, and the resulting profits were shared equally with the plaintiff. To permit the plaintiff to participate in the benefits which must have resulted from improvements necessary to the preservation of the entire property, and at the same time share equally in the increased rentals which followed the making of a part of the improvements, would manifestly be inequitable, unless provision be also made upon partition for suitable compensation to Tiscornia for the cost of such improvements.” (Ventre v. Tiscornia (1913) 23 Cal.App. 598, 607.)

Here, Plaintiffs do not reasonably dispute that the improvements Dwayne facilitated were necessary to the preservation of the common property, enhanced its rental value, and that value was to generate rental income to be shared equally between the parties.  In fact, both Arthur and Gary testified that the work was satisfactory and they had no complaints about the work. 

Dwayne also puts forth substantial evidence that would support equitable compensation for acting as a general contractor. “The evidence showed that the pre-remodel condition of the property required substantial work. The December 31, 2021 inspection report identified numerous issues, including deteriorated framing and sheathing, inadequate drainage, deteriorated eaves and rafter tails, water intrusion, electrical issues, exposed wiring, missing GFCI protection, outdated smoke detectors, galvanized piping beyond its intended service life, leaking drain lines, plumbing deficiencies, deteriorated flooring and walls, moisture damage, foundation cracking, and termite-related concerns. The later inspection showed material improvement, including that the electrical main/service panel had been updated within the prior two years and was in good serviceable condition, and that visible copper supply piping and ABS drain/waste piping appeared serviceable and updated within the prior one to two years. Exhibit 103 was admitted into evidence and summarized Dwayne’s remodel expenses, including vendor, contractor, labor, mortgage, insurance, property tax, utility, storage, hauling, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, fencing, and other payments. Exhibit 103 lists total cash and expense payments of $196,827.67, lists 1,140 hours worked on the remodeling project, lists labor value of $85,500.00, and lists a total remodel cost of $282,327.67. Defendant’s construction expert Steve Viani testified that day laborers commonly do not provide receipts, that day laborers are commonly used to keep construction costs down, that the time Dwayne spent was reasonable, and that the reasonable rate for the work Dwayne performed was approximately $80 per hour. The expert also testified without dispute that a general contractor would have charged $300,000 or more to perform the work Dwayne accomplished for under $200,000.” (Defendant’s Closing Brief, p. 4.)

Plaintiffs do not meaningfully dispute this evidence. Plaintiffs first argue that Dwayne is not entitled to any compensation for his contractor work per Nevarov v. Nevarov (1953) 117 Cal.App.2d 581, 586: “[t]he usual rule is that one partners is not entitled to more compensation than another in the absence of an agreement to the contrary.” (Plaintiffs’ Reply Brief, p. 3.) However, Nevarov discussed only whether one partner is entitled to more salary than another arising out of that partnership. Nevarov is thus unavailing in this case which does not involve a salary dispute arising out of a partnership.

Plaintiffs next argue that “California law recognizes that a cotenant who makes improvements that enhance property value may receive credit in partition, but that credit is for expenditures - money paid - not for the cotenant's own labor.” (Plaintiffs’ Reply Brief, p. 4.) However, Plaintiffs cite no legal authority for this proposition.

Plaintiffs finally argue that “equity does not support a labor credit where the cotenant seeking it simultaneously breached his duties to the other cotenants. Dwayne restricted Plaintiffs' access to the business account, failed to provide documentation, wrote checks to himself without accountability, and occupied the income property without paying rent. A cotenant cannot invoke equity to obtain credit for alleged labor while denying his co-owners the transparency and income to which they were entitled. Cal Code Civ Proc § 872.140.” (Plaintiffs’ Reply Brief, p. 4.) However, Plaintiffs’ cited code section merely states the Court’s general power to order allowance, accounting, contribution, or other compensatory adjustment among the parties according to the principles of equity. Plaintiffs otherwise put forth no case law to support their argument regarding Dwayne’s alleged breach of his duties as cotenant serving as a bar to labor credit. This argument is thus unavailing.

Overall, the Court is empowered to credit Dwayne for his labor under its powers of equitable accounting and credit in a partition action under CCP § 872.140.

However, due to the various conflicting accounts of what Dwayne should be owed, the Court is awarding $50,000.  

2.     Should Dwayne have been paying rent (not just the mortgage)?

Plaintiffs assert that the parties hold title to the Subject Property as tenants in common and concede that the general rule is that each tenant in common has the right to occupy the common property, and that one co-tenant generally cannot recover rent from another. However, Plaintiffs argue that general rule does not apply where, as here, the parties specifically agreed that Dwayne would pay rent for the Subject Property. Plaintiffs contend that the agreement that Dwayne would pay rent controls, and Plaintiffs are entitled to their rent as demanded.

The evidence as to this “agreement” to pay rent is conflicting. Per Plaintiffs’ Closing Brief, Gary testified on this issue as follows: “The parties came to an agreement: Dwayne would pay $1,730.00 per month and then $3,000.00 per month. (See Ex. 6.) Gary testified that Dwayne would start paying $1,730.00 per month within "one to two months" of the February 2023 text message. Gary and Arthur further would require Dwayne to sign a "letter", which Gary explained was essentially a writing explaining the payment of rent. (Id.) Gary and Arthur were going to meet Dwayne at AAA by the airport to review the "letter" and then they would have the writing notarized. (Id.) Gary testified that Dwayne never ended up signing the "letter" despite their asking him to do so. Dwayne and his family were already living in the Subject Property at this point.”

As to Arthur, Plaintiffs’ Closing Brief sets forth that “Arthur testified that he understood that Dwayne would be paying rent for the Subject Property, and he agreed in redirect that it was his understanding that Dwayne would begin paying $1,730.00 per month within one to two months of the text messages dated February 18, 2023 and then the amount would go to $3,000.00 per month. (See Ex. 6.) Arthur confirmed that he and Gary wanted Dwayne to sign the "letter" which would be notarized concerning the payment of rent, but that Dwayne never did.”

As to Dwayne, Plaintiffs’ Closing Brief sets forth that “Dwayne testified that prior to moving into the Subject Property, he was paying $2,300.00 per month for his home in Lakewood, California. He was a renter there. He moved into the Subject Property but never paid Plaintiffs any amount of rent. Except for a few months in which Gary paid part of the loan payments, Dwayne was paying the mortgage payments, but out of the business account. (See Ex. 3.) He did not sign the "letter" and didn't feel that he needed to sign anything because he was a part owner of the Subject Property and had a right to live there. (See also Ex. 6.) However, he did admit that the Subject Property was intended to be an income property for the brothers once it was remodeled. He testified that once his medical tests were over, he would start paying $1,730.00 per month. But, to date, he has never paid $1,730.00 in rent.”

Here, Dwayne does not dispute that he has never paid rent. However, outside of the parties’ conflicting testimony, the only writing on this issue shows that Dwayne claimed he would begin paying $1,730 in rent once he had his medical diagnosis. This is taken from an ambiguous group text message from August 2023 where Dwayne states, “[a]s I explained I m going thru medical tests and I said I will pay the mortgage once the tests are over im going to pay $1730 then $3000.” (Exhibit 6.)

Weighing the evidence, Plaintiffs did not memorialize any rental agreement with Dwayne in writing. Per all parties’ testimony, Dwayne did not agree to sign any formal agreement regarding rent. Dwayne further points out that Plaintiffs testified that they had not even drafted an agreement for Dwayne to sign: “Both Arthur and Gary testified that the "letter" they allegedly wanted Dwayne to sign was never written. Gary specifically testified he never wrote the "Letter" that was allegedly to be signed at AAA.” (Defendant’s Reply Brief.) Based on Plaintiffs’ testimony alone, Plaintiffs have failed to show a meeting of the minds regarding Dwayne’s rental payments which is necessary to overcome the general rule that each tenant in common has the right to occupy the common property, and that one co-tenant generally cannot recover rent from another.

If Plaintiffs are entitled to rent, the only clear evidentiary basis it would arise from is Defendant’s text message agreeing to pay after his diagnosis, which he received in February of 2026. Rent payments would thus only be owed from that date.

Further, Dwayne paid mortgage payments, property taxes, and homeowner’s insurance during this time which otherwise should have been split by all three parties. Defendant's Exhibit 103 details these payments, Exhibit 106 reflects the mortgage loan history, showing repeated payments from July 2022 through April 2024, Exhibit 105 reflects homeowner's insurance premiums for multiple policy years, and Exhibit 107 reflects property tax payments.   The Court is uncertain as to the exact amount of these offsets, and orders the Parties to meet and confer and stipulate to the amount and whether or not these amounts were paid out of the business account or Dwayne’s personal funds.  These amounts should offset any rent Plaintiffs seek.

3.      What offset, if any, should Arthur and Gary get for the $95,589 in checks Dwayne wrote to himself?

Plaintiffs allege that Dwayne made tens of thousands of dollars in checks out to himself from the parties’ joint account, the ADG Kellum Brothers, LLC bank account, made numerous cash withdrawals, and did not provide the receipts, invoices, etc. supporting that the funds were used solely for the remodel of the Subject Property.

In their reply brief, Plaintiffs state that they gave Dwayne credit for all payments supported by admissible evidence such as formal contractor invoices, receipts, and statements, which Plaintiffs stipulated as proper expenditures, and that the $95,589.00 at issue represents only those amounts for which Defendants produced no documentation.

Dwayne claims that these withdrawals were to pay subcontractors who asked to be paid in cash during the renovation.

Anthony Locke testified that he performed construction work at the premises, and he requested payment in cash. He also testified that he did not have any bank statements, receipts, or any other documentary evidence to support any amount of money that he was paid and that even on the stand he was unsure about exactly how much he was paid. Nevertheless, he testified that he was paid $17,680.00 as stated on the spreadsheet provided by Defendants as Exhibit 103.

Exhibit 103 was admitted into evidence and summarized Dwayne’s remodel expenses, including vendor, contractor, labor, mortgage, insurance, property tax, utility, storage, hauling, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, fencing, and other payments. Exhibit 103 lists total cash and expense payments of $196,827.67, lists 1,140 hours worked on the remodeling project, lists labor value of $85,500.00, and lists a total remodel cost of $282,327.67. Defendant’s construction expert Steve Viani testified that day laborers commonly do not provide receipts, that day laborers are commonly used to keep construction costs down, that the time Dwayne spent was reasonable, and that the reasonable rate for the work Dwayne performed was approximately $80 per hour. The expert also testified without dispute that a general contractor would have charged $300,000 or more to perform the work Dwayne accomplished for under $200,000.” (Defendant’s Closing Brief, p. 4.)

Here, based on the evidence before the Court, Arthur and Gary are entitled to an offset for the amounts that Dwayne withdrew from the joint account that he cannot clearly prove were paid to contractors or otherwise used for the remodel.

Per Plaintiffs’ Closing Brief, Plaintiffs did not stipulate to the introduction or authenticity of the printouts prepared by Defendants and "signed" by certain contractors, namely payments to Anthony Locke and Jerimiah Tillman, as they were hearsay and required testimony. Defendants produced Anthony Locke to testify about his payments, however Defendants did not produce Jerimiah Tillman to authenticate the alleged payments to him. The Court agrees with Plaintiffs that the evidence as to payments to Jerimiah Tillman is hearsay and unsubstantiated by testimony, but Anthony Locke’s testimony substantiates the $17,680.00 Dwayne withdrew as stated on the spreadsheet provided by Defendants as Exhibit 103.

While Dwayne repeatedly cites the fact that Plaintiffs did not ask for receipts until after the remodel was done as justification for his failure to keep accurate records, this does not absolve him of his fiduciary responsibility to keep an accounting of withdrawals from their joint account. The Court cannot reasonably rely on Dwayne’s testimony alone to substantiate these withdrawals as Dwayne has a felony conviction for financial fraud and served prison time, which goes directly to his credibility on this issue.

Thus, Gary and Arthur are entitled to a $95,589.00 offset, which is the amount Dwayne withdrew from the joint bank account for which he has produced no documentation.

Therefore, the Court orders the following:

1.      Plaintiffs are entitled to a $95,589.00 offset for checks written by Dwayne to himself

2.     Plaintiffs are entitled to rent in the amount of $1730 per month as of February 2026 to the present.

3.     Defendant is entitled to $50,000 in compensation for the remodeling of his home.

4.     Defendant is entitled to an offset for amounts paid for the mortgage and property tax to the extent those monies did not come out of the business account;

5.     The property located at 2315  Easy Avenue will be  ordered to be sold (Partition by Sale) and proceeds to be split by each party’s 1/3 interest minus offsets as listed above. 

The Court sets a next Court date of September 4, 2026 at 8:30 a.m. to discuss the next steps such as appraisal of the property in the event there is a right of first refusal among the three brothers to purchase the property before a forced sale under CCP 874.317, the setting of an Order to Show Cause re Why the Court Should Not Order the Appointment of a Referee Pursuant to CCP 873.010, etc.

Case Number: 25LBCV01930    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: S27

 

1.     Background Facts

Plaintiff, Timothy Hunter filed this action against Defendant, Long Beach Memorial Medical Center for damages arising out of alleged medical malpractice on 7/01/25. 

 

The Court heard an OSC re: proof of service on 9/16/25, and Plaintiff failed to appear.  The Court continued the matter to 10/29/25 and also set an OSC re: dismissal for failure to prosecute for the same date and time.  Plaintiff failed to appear on 10/29/25, and the Court dismissed the case.    

 

2.     Motion to Vacate Dismissal

a.     Relief Sought by Plaintiff

On 4/14/26, Plaintiff filed this motion to vacate the dismissal, contending it was entered as a result of his attorney’s mistake or excusable neglect in connection with staffing challenges at the law firm. 

 

The dismissal was entered without any statutory or other authority for dismissing the action.  CCP §583.420(a)(1) permits the Court to dismiss an action if the summons and complaint are not served within two years after the action is filed.  §583.410 also permits the Court to dismiss an action if the case is not brought to trial within two years after filing.  There are various other statutes that permit dismissal of an action, none of which apply here.  Pursuant to Lyons v. Wickhorst (1986) 42 Cal.3d 911, 915-917, the Court’s powers to dismiss absent a statutory basis are extremely limited, and can only be applied in sham actions or where a delay of less than the statutory period has been prejudicial to the other party.  Because the dismissal was entered without statutory or other basis, it is void and the Court vacates the dismissal. 

 

b.     Future Hearings

Plaintiff has not, to date, filed proof of service of the summons and complaint on Defendant.  The Court sets an OSC re: proof of service for October 14, 2026, 8:30 a.m.

Case Number: 25LBCV03128    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: S27

 

1.     Background Facts

Plaintiff, American Express National Bank filed this action against Defendant, Peter Guirguis on 11/17/25 for breach of credit card agreement.  Plaintiff filed proof of personal service on 1/07/26, which POS indicates Plaintiff’s registered process server served Defendant with the summons and complaint on 12/19/25.  On 1/26/26, at Plaintiff’s request, the Clerk entered Defendant’s default.  On 1/29/26, at Plaintiff’s request, the Court entered Judgment.    

 

2.     Motion for Relief from Default and Default Judgment

Defendant moves for relief from default pursuant to CCP §473(b).  Defendant supports his motion with his own declaration and his attorney’s declaration.  Defense Counsel declares Defendant tendered the complaint to his office, and his assistant prepared an answer, which was approved and returned for filing, but due to his office’s fault and mistake, the answer was never actually filed. 

 

Whenever an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney’s sworn affidavit of fault attesting to his/her mistake, inadvertence, surprise or neglect, the court “shall” vacate the resulting default, default judgment or dismissal. CCP §473(b).  The only limitation is that the court may deny relief if it finds that the default, default judgment or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise or neglect, but that the attorney is attempting to “cover up” for his/her client. Rogalski v. Nabers Cadillac (1992) 11 Cal.App.4th 816.  If the attorney is willing to take the blame, the court must set aside the entry of default, default judgment or dismissal. 

 

Any opposition to the motion was due on or before 8/27/26.  Plaintiff has not filed timely opposition, and the Court will not consider late-filed opposition papers.  In light of the mandatory nature of relief and the lack of opposition, the motion is granted. 

 

The Court notes that §473(b) requires the moving papers to be accompanied by a copy of the defendant’s proposed responsive pleading.  Defendant did not lodge a proposed answer with his moving papers, but did file an answer on 2/02/26.  The requirement that Defendant file an answer is therefore satisfied.

Case Number: 25LBCV03326    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: S27

 

1.     Background Facts

Plaintiff, James Brown filed this action against Defendant, American Honda Motor Co. for violations of Song-Beverly and related claims.

 

2.     Motion to Compel Arbitration

a.     Legal Standard

The moving party on a motion to compel arbitration “bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence, while a party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense.  The trial court sits as the trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, and any oral testimony the court may receive at its discretion, to reach a final determination.”  (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 842, internal citations omitted.)

 

b.     Warranty Booklet

Here, Plaintiff signed a purchase agreement with Dealership.  At the time of purchase, Plaintiff was provided a warranty booklet, which includes three provisions relevant to this ruling.  They are (see Exhibit A to Plaintiff’s complaint, pages 3 and 6):

Please note that this new vehicle limited warranty contains an arbitration provision that may affect your legal rights, and you agree that, pursuant to the Arbitration Provision contained in this warranty (beginning on page 6) of this booklet, that either you or American Honda Motor Co., Inc. may elect to resolve any dispute by neutral, binding arbitration and not by a court action. See the Arbitration Provision contained (beginning on page 6) of this new vehicle limited warranty for additional information concerning the agreement to arbitrate. 

 

You and we agree that any dispute arising out of or relating to any aspect of the relationship between you and Honda will not be decided by a judge or jury but instead by a single arbitration administered by the American Arbitration Association (AAA) under its Consumer Arbitration Rules in effect on the date of delivery of your vehicle to you. This Agreement to Arbitrate includes all claims, whether based in contract, tort, statute, fraud, misrepresentation or any other legal theory; claims arising out of your warranty; claims arising before or after this Agreement, such as claims related to statements about our products; claims about the performance, design of our products, or manufacturing of our products; and claims that are currently the subject of purported class action litigation in which you are not a member of a certified class. 

 

You may opt out of arbitration within 30 days after the date of delivery of your vehicle to you by sending a letter to: Honda’s Office of the General Counsel, 1919 Torrance Blvd., Mail Stop CHI-5, Torrance, CA 90501, stating your name, Vehicle Identification Number, and intent to opt out of the arbitration provision. If you do not opt out, then this agreement to arbitrate is binding.  

 

c.     Acceptance of Contract

Plaintiff concedes he received the warranty booklet (indeed he is suing for breach of the warranties found therein), and concedes he did not opt out.  He contends, however, that his lack of signature means there was no “agreement” and therefore he cannot be compelled to arbitrate his claims.

 

Plaintiff relies on Norcia v. Samsung Telecommunications America, LLC (9th Cir. 2017) 845 F.3d 1279 to support his position.  In Norcia, the plaintiff filed a putative class action alleging that Samsung had made misrepresentations concerning the performance of its Galaxy phone.  Samsung moved to compel arbitration, relying on an arbitration clause contained in a warranty brochure included in the Galaxy box. 

 

When the plaintiff purchased the phone, he signed a receipt that stated that he was agreeing to settlement of disputes by arbitration and other important terms in the Customer Agreement; there was nothing on the receipt indicating with whom he was making such an agreement (the store, the manufacturer, the service provider, etc.).  The actual agreement was found inside the box in a document called Product Safety & Warranty Information; notably, this document was not entitled Customer Agreement.  Instead, the Customer Agreement apparently included a reference to the Product Safety & Warranty Information and incorporated its terms by reference. 

 

Notably, Honda also relies on Norcia, contending it establishes, “A party who is bound by a contract is bound by all its terms, whether or not the party was aware of them.”  Norcia does so hold, but only in the section discussing general contract law.  Indeed, Norcia goes on, immediately thereafter, to indicate, “As a general rule, ‘silence or inaction does not constitute acceptance of an offer.’”  The Norcia Court went on to cite examples of situations where silence DOES constitute acceptance, such as a situation where an employee signs a one-page clear document with an arbitration provision that is applicable unless the employee opts out, and then gives the employee a mechanism to opt out if he chooses to do so.  Norcia went on to note that, unless the employee in the example above, the purchaser of the Samsung product did not sign anything at all, and merely received the warranty booklet.

 

The Court finds Norcia is directly on point.  While it is a federal court case, it is an appellate level decision, and the parties do not cite on-point state appellate authority.  Defendant cites various federal district court cases holding a provision in a warranty agreement is enforceable, but district court cases are not authorities.  The Court finds placing an arbitration provision in a warranty booklet and then providing the booklet to the purchaser, without requiring the purchaser to sign the booklet or sign any agreement contained therein, does not establish consent to the agreement. 

 

d.     Commencement of Arbitration Proceedings

Defendant also contends it commenced arbitration proceedings, and Plaintiff has participated in the proceedings, such that the agreement must be enforced.  Defendant cites no authority in this section of its brief.  It fails to show that its decision to commence arbitration after Plaintiff has commenced litigation somehow bars the ongoing litigation. 

 

e.     Suing on Warranty

Defendant makes much of the fact that Plaintiff is suing for breach of the warranty in which the arbitration agreement appears.  Defendant cites no authority, however, for the position that this is relevant to the issue of whether Plaintiff consented to the arbitration provision in the warranty.

 

f.      Conclusion

The motion to compel arbitration is denied.  The FSC and trial dates remain on calendar.
