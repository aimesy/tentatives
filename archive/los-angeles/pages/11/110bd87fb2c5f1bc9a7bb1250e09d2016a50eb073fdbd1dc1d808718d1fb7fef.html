Case Number: 24STCV04998    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: 224

Adilene Gomez v. Kia America Inc.

Plaintiff's Ex Parte Application to continue the trial for 90 to 120 days is DENIED.

This case was filed in February 2024. California Rules of Court, rule 3.1332(a), makes clear that trial dates are firm and continuances are disfavored.

Rule 3.1332(c) identifies the circumstances under which a court may grant a continuance. Plaintiff has not established any of those grounds. The application was filed only two days before the Final Status Conference, and only after the Court set an Order to Show Cause re Sanctions based on Plaintiff's failure to comply with the Court's trial readiness requirements.

Plaintiff principally relies on counsel's failure to timely conduct expert discovery. That is not, standing alone, a basis for continuing trial where the record does not demonstrate diligence. As the Court of Appeal recently explained:

"The absence of a reasonable justification meant allowing this tardy [expert] witness to testify was an abuse of discretion. Of course, some late designations can have valid justifications. Long-designated experts may suddenly become unavailable: death, illness, incapacitation, and other serious and uncontrollable events can create an understandable need for replacements. The world's supply of unexpected and unfortunate events is varied and unlimited. McDonald, however, offered no reasonable justification. There was no emergency or serious unexpected development." (McDonald v. Zargaryan (2025) 117 Cal.App.5th 344, 352.)

Here, Plaintiff has not demonstrated diligence in litigating this matter. The application does not explain Plaintiff's failure to comply with Los Angeles County Superior Court Local Rule 3.25(f)(2), which requires motions in limine and other trial preparation motions to be filed with sufficient statutory notice to be heard at the Final Status Conference. (LASC Local Rule 3.25(f)(2).) Nor does Plaintiff address compliance with the remaining trial readiness requirements set forth in Local Rule 3.25 or this Department's Trial Readiness Policies.

This is not a case in which a diligent party encountered a single, unforeseen event that disrupted an otherwise trial-ready case. Rather, the record reflects a broader failure to prepare for trial in accordance with the California Rules of Court, the Los Angeles County Superior Court Local Rules, and this Department's Trial Readiness Policies.

Accordingly, the Final Status Conference and the Order to Show Cause Re Sanctions remain on calendar. All pretrial deadlines remain in effect and will not be continued. The Court intends to continue the trial date, but only a date in August which the Court and parties will discuss at the FSC. The parties are expected to appear in person, as required for the Final Status Conference. The Court expects declarations from both Plaintiff's counsel and Defendant's counsel addressing the Order to Show Cause Re Sanctions for failure to comply with the Los Angeles Superior Court's Local Rules and trial readiness requirements.

Case Number: 24STCV10563    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: 224

Correa v. Thistle Health Inc.

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendants' Motion for Partial Judgment on the Pleadings is DENIED as to the Tenth Cause of Action and GRANTED without leave to amend as to the Ninth Cause of Action.

Introduction

On April 26, 2024, Plaintiff Jason Correa filed a Complaint in this action.

On June 13, 2024, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint.

On October 3, 2025, Defendants filed the instant Motion for Partial Judgment on the Pleadings, seeking dismissal of the Ninth and Tenth Causes of Action.

On April 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.

On July 9, 2026, Defendants filed a reply.

Meet and Confer

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (CCP §§ 430.41)

The Declaration of Nicholas Deming, executed on October 3, 2025, sets forth the meet and confer efforts of counsel for the Defendants, and counsel’s attempts to resolve the disputes informally by written correspondence. The Court finds that the declaration does not satisfy the requirement of Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, that "... the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer." Nonetheless, given how long the Motion has been pending, the Court will exercise its discretion to address the merits.

Request for Judicial Notice

Defendants request judicial notice of the operative complaints in Cornelious, Luckett, and this action, and of the Notice of Entry of Order Granting Plaintiffs’ Motion for Order Approving PAGA Settlement and Entering Judgment dated May 16, 2025. (Defendants’ RJN, Exhs. 1-4.)

Plaintiff requests judicial notice of the parties' respective LWDA PAGA notices, the prior complaints, the Cornelious dismissal, the Cornelious/Luckett FAC, and the May 16, 2025 Judgment. (Plaintiff's RJN, Exhs. 1-10.)

The requests are granted.

Legal Standard

A defendant may move for judgment on the pleadings where¿the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the complaint or¿the complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant.¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 438 subd. (c)(1)(B).)¿ A non-statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings may be made any time before or during trial.¿ (Stoops v.¿Abbassi¿(2002) 100 Cal.App.4th 644, 650.)¿ “Such motion may be made on the same ground as those supporting a general demurrer,¿i.e., that the pleading at issue fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a legally cognizable claim or defense.”¿ (Ibid.)¿¿¿

The standard for ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings is essentially the same as that applicable to a general demurrer, that is, under the state of the pleadings, together with matters that may be judicially noticed, it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (Bezirdjian v. O'Reilly (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 316, 321-322, citing Schabarum v. California Legislature (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1216.)

Like demurrers, motions for judgment on the pleadings challenge the legal sufficiency of the allegations, not their veracity. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) The Court “must accept as true all material facts properly pleaded, but does not consider conclusions of law or fact, opinions, speculation, or allegations contrary to law or facts that are judicially noticed.” (Stevenson Real Estate Services, Inc. v. CB Richard Ellis Real Estate Services, Inc. (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1219-1220.)

Tenth Cause of Action: PAGA

Defendants argue that Judgment on the Pleadings should be granted in their favor because the PAGA cause of action is barred by claim preclusion arising from the PAGA settlement and judgment entered in Carla Cornelious, Michael Luckett v. Thistle Health Inc. (Solano Cty. Super. Ct., Case No. FCS058261) on May 16, 2025, and that Plaintiff lacks standing to pursue it.

Res Judicata

Claim preclusion bars a second action between the same parties or their privies on the same cause of action following a final judgment on the merits. (DKN Holdings LLC v. Faerber (2015) 61 Cal.4th 813, 824.) A judicially approved PAGA settlement operates as a final judgment on the merits, and because the State is the real party in interest, such a judgment binds the State and all aggrieved employees. (Arias v. Superior Court (2009) 46 Cal.4th 969, 985-986; Robinson v. Southern Counties Oil Co. (2020) 53 Cal.App.5th 476, 481-483.)

These principles establish that the Cornelious/Luckett judgment precludes a successive PAGA action to the extent it seeks to relitigate the Labor Code violations that were, or could have been, encompassed within that prior representative action. Thus, the question is whether Plaintiff's PAGA claim for the misappropriation of gratuities under Labor Code § 351 falls within that preclusive scope.

The preclusive reach of a prior PAGA judgment is defined and limited by the scope of the prior plaintiff's LWDA notice. A PAGA plaintiff acts as the State's authorized proxy only as to the specific violations exhausted through the pre-suit notice required by Labor Code § 2699.3, subdivision (a)(1)(A). A plaintiff cannot litigate, settle, or release a PAGA claim she had no authority to pursue. (LaCour v. Marshalls of California, LLC (2023) 94 Cal.App.5th 1172, 1192–1197.) In LaCour, the Court of Appeal held that a prior PAGA judgment "does not extinguish unlisted PAGA claims in litigation brought by other authorized PAGA plaintiffs because such claims do not arise from violations of the same primary rights" the prior plaintiff was authorized to pursue. (Id. at p. 1195.) On the privity element, the court likewise held that a PAGA "statutory proxy acting without authority cannot be said to be in privity with her principal" as to claims outside her LWDA notice. (Id. at p. 1196.)

The judicially noticed records establish that the Cornelious and Luckett PAGA notices and complaints alleged wage-and-hour violations, including unpaid minimum and overtime wages, meal and rest period violations, inaccurate wage statements, unreimbursed expenses, and related claims. However, they did not allege facts supporting a violation of Labor Code § 351. (Plaintiff's RJN, Exhs. 3-6.) Neither the underlying settlement nor the Luckett Approval Order and Judgment references section 351. (Plaintiff's RJN, Exh. 10.) By contrast, Plaintiff's own April 2, 2024 LWDA notice specifically alleged that Defendants violated section 351 by misappropriating tips and gratuities. (Plaintiff's RJN, Exh. 1; FAC ¶¶ 40, 91.)

Because the Cornelious/Luckett plaintiffs never exhausted a section 351 violation before the LWDA, they lacked authority to pursue or release a section 351 PAGA claim on the State's behalf. Under LaCour, the broad release of “any other PAGA violations that could have been alleged” cannot extend to a claim the prior plaintiffs had no authority to assert, and Plaintiff was not in privity with those plaintiffs as to the section 351 claim.

Defendants' reliance on Villacres v. ABM Industries, Inc. (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 562 and Moniz v. Adecco USA, Inc. (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 56 is misplaced. LaCour distinguished Moniz, and Villacres did not involve an unexhausted claim outside the scope of the prior LWDA notice.

Defendants' primary-rights argument that misappropriated tips are merely another form of unpaid compensation does not compel a different result. Section 351 declares that "[e]very gratuity is hereby declared to be the sole property of the employee or employees to whom it was paid, given, or left for." (Lab. Code, § 351.) Our Supreme Court has recognized that an employer's misappropriation of a gratuity is analytically distinct from an employer's withholding of promised wages: a gratuity left by a patron "arguably qualifies as a specific sum of money, belonging to the employee, that is capable of identification and separate from the employer's own funds," and "an employer's misappropriation of gratuities is not the same as an employer's withholding of promised wages." (Voris v. Lampert (2019) 7 Cal.5th 1141, 1155.) The harm alleged here is not the same harm as the nonpayment of earned wages litigated and resolved in Cornelious/Luckett. The section 351 claim thus implicates a primary right distinct from the wage claims resolved in that action.

Because Plaintiff's PAGA cause of action states a legally cognizable claim as to at least the section 351 theory, and a motion for judgment on the pleadings does not lie against part of a cause of action, the motion cannot be granted as to the Tenth Cause of Action. The Court need not reach Plaintiff's alternative argument that the injustice exception bars application of claim preclusion.

Standing

Plaintiff's PAGA notice was filed on March 7, 2024, before June 19, 2024, so the pre-2024 PAGA standing framework applies. (Lab. Code § 2699, subd. (v)(2).) To have standing, a plaintiff must be an "aggrieved employee" who was employed by the alleged violator and against whom at least one alleged violation was committed. (Kim v. Reins International California, Inc. (2020) 9 Cal.5th 73, 83-84; Lab. Code § 2699, subd. (c).)

Plaintiff alleges he was employed by Defendants and that Defendants misappropriated his tips and gratuities in violation of section 351 (FAC ¶¶ 1, 13, 40). Section 351 is among the provisions for which PAGA penalties may be sought. (Lab. Code § 2699.5.) Plaintiff therefore satisfies the statutory standing requirements.

Here, the State's section 351 claim was never exhausted, litigated, or resolved in Cornelious/Luckett. Plaintiff, having exhausted section 351 through his own LWDA notice, retains standing to prosecute that claim on the State's behalf.

The motion is DENIED as to the Tenth Cause of Action.

Ninth Cause of Action: Failure to Provide Gratuities

Defendants further contend the Ninth Cause of Action fails because Labor Code § 351 provides no private right of action.

The Ninth Cause of Action seeks to enforce Labor Code § 351 directly. (FAC ¶¶ 84-86.) The California Supreme Court has squarely held that section 351 does not create a private right of action authorizing an employee to sue an employer directly for misappropriated gratuities. (Lu v. Hawaiian Gardens Casino, Inc., supra, 50 Cal.4th at pp. 603-604.) As pled, the Ninth Cause of Action is not legally cognizable.

Plaintiff does not meaningfully dispute the absence of a private right of action under section 351.

Plaintiff bears the burden of showing a reasonable possibility that amendment would cure the defect and cannot do so. The absence of a private right of action under Labor Code section 351 is a categorical bar that no repleading can cure. (Lu v. Hawaiian Gardens Casino, Inc., supra, 50 Cal.4th at pp. 603-604.)

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED without leave to amend as to the Ninth Cause of Action.

Conclusion

Defendants’ Motion for Partial Judgment on the Pleadings is DENIED as to the Tenth Cause of Action and GRANTED without leave to amend as to the Ninth Cause of Action.

Case Number: 25STCV00591    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: 224

Topanga Beach Holdings, LLC v. Lakeview Skilled Nursing Facility, LLC

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff’s Motion for Relief from Waiver of Jury Trial is GRANTED.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On January 9, 2025, Plaintiff filed the Complaint in this action.

On January 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint.

On August 29, 2025, the initial Case Management Conference occurred in this

action. The Court set trial for June 14, 2027, as a non-jury trial on the stated ground that jury fees had not been timely posted by any party.

On September 12, 2025, the Court issued a nunc pro tunc order correcting its August 29, 2025 minute order, which memorialized that "Jury Fees are not posted by any party in compliance with C.C.P. Section 631," and confirmed the June 14, 2027 trial as a non-jury trial. (Hill Decl. ¶ 4, Ex. B.)

On June 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion.

No opposition has been filed.

Legal Standard

A party must pay jury fees no later than 365 calendar days after filing of the initial complaint. (CCP § 631, subds. (b) and (c).) The failure to post jury fees constitutes a waiver of that right. (CCP § 631(f)(5)&(6).)

California Code of Civil Procedure section¿631, subdivision (g) provides courts with discretion to allow a jury trial despite there being a waiver of trial by jury.¿ “A trial court abuses its discretion as a matter of law when [] relief has been denied where there has been no prejudice¿to the other party or to the court from an inadvertent waiver.”¿ (Tesoro del Valle Master Homeowners Assn. v. Griffin¿(2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 619,¿638.)¿ Policy favors the court using its discretion to proceed with a jury trial. (Bishop v. Anderson (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 821, 823.)

“Where the right to jury is threatened, the crucial focus is whether any prejudice will be suffered by any party or the court if a motion for relief from waiver is granted. [Citations.] A trial court abuses its discretion as a matter of law when ‘…relief has been denied where there has been no prejudice to the other party or to the court from an inadvertent waiver. [Citations.]’” (Wharton v. Superior Court (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 100, 104.) “The mere fact that trial will be by jury is not prejudice per se.” (Johnson-Stovall v. Superior Court (1993) 17 Cal.App.4th 808, 811 [prejudice not shown by need to prepare motions in limine, enlarged exhibits, and jury instructions unless inadequate time also shown].) Rather, the prejudice that must be shown is “prejudice from the granting of relief from waiver not prejudice from the jury trial.” (Winston v. Superior Court (1987) 196 Cal.App.3d 600, 602, 603 [fact that it takes longer to try a jury case is not prejudice from granting relief from waiver].)

Discussion

Although captioned as a "Motion for Order Modifying Case Management Order," the motion is in substance a motion for relief from waiver of jury trial under Code of Civil Procedure section 631, subdivision (g), and the Court treats it as such.

Plaintiff moves the Court for relief from waiver of jury trial pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 631, subdivision (g).

The Court's September 12, 2025 nunc pro tunc order found that “Jury Fees are not posted by any party in compliance with C.C.P. Section 631” and set the matter for a non-jury trial. (Hill Decl. ¶ 4, Exh. B.)

As a threshold matter, Plaintiff contends there was no waiver at all. Section 631, subdivision (c) provides that jury fees are “due on or before the date scheduled for the initial case management conference in the action.” (CCP § 631(c).) The record reflects that the initial Case Management Conference was held on August 29, 2025, and that Plaintiff posted its $150 nonrefundable advance jury fee that same day, with the Court confirming receipt at 4:27 p.m. (Hill Decl. ¶ 3, Exh. A.) Because the deadline to file fees is the date scheduled for the initial Case Management Conference, and Plaintiff tendered payment on that date, Plaintiff's position that its fees were timely is well taken.

No prejudice will result from granting relief. Trial is set for June 14, 2027 – well over a year from the filing and hearing of this Motion – leaving the parties ample time to prepare for a jury trial and eliminating any possibility of unfair surprise. (Hill Decl. ¶ 5.) The mere fact that trial will be by jury is not prejudice per se. (Johnson-Stovall, supra, 17 Cal.App.4th at p. 811.) Rather, the prejudice that must be shown is "prejudice from the granting of relief from waiver not prejudice from the jury trial." (Winston, supra, 196 Cal.App.3d at pp. 602-603.) The Motion is unopposed, and no party has identified any prejudice arising from proceeding to a jury trial. Given the strong policy favoring resolution of doubts in favor of preserving the constitutional right to a jury trial (Bishop, supra, 101 Cal.App.3d at p. 823), relief is warranted.

Accordingly, Plaintiff's Motion for Relief from Waiver of Jury Trial is GRANTED. The trial in this matter, currently set for June 14, 2027, is designated as a jury trial.

Case Number: BC521491    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: 224

Zimny v. Bahk et al.,

RULING

Cross-Defendant Henry M. Lee’s Ex Parte Application for Temporary Stay of Collection is DENIED.

ANALYSIS

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1202(c), requires a moving party to make an affirmative factual showing of irreparable harm, immediate danger, or another statutory basis for ex parte relief. The moving party must also show that the asserted emergency was not created by the party’s own conduct or lack of diligence.

Here, Lee concedes he took no action with respect to the judgment after it was entered in 2018. The asserted urgency to stay enforcement arises only because the Judgment Creditor has now initiated collection efforts. Any exigency is therefore of Lee’s own making and does not support ex parte relief.

Further, Lee’s supplemental brief effectively concedes there is no statutory basis for the stay he seeks. Instead, he asks the Court to exercise its inherent authority under Code of Civil Procedure section 128 to grant an equitable stay. But the application is unsupported by admissible, competent, and relevant evidence establishing harm.

In any event, even if Lee had submitted admissible evidence, the Court lacks authority to grant the requested relief in light of controlling law. A money judgment is enforceable upon entry. (Code Civ. Proc., § 683.010.) Code of Civil Procedure section 918(b) provides that where an undertaking would be required before an judgment could be appealed “a trial court shall not have power, without the consent of the adverse party,” to stay enforcement beyond 10 days after the last date to file a notice of appeal, and section 918(c) makes clear that this limitation applies “whether or not an appeal will be taken.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 918(b), (c).) Lee does not address this statute, or why this judgment would not require the giving of an undertaking before the Court could issue any stay.

Accordingly, the ex parte application is DENIED.
