BACKGROUND

 

Plaintiff brings this action under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (Civil Code § 1790 et seq.) for a vehicle she purchased on 10/22/22, for which Defendant issued the manufacturer’s express warranty. (Compl. ¶¶ 2–3, 8.) Plaintiff alleges that the subject vehicle “was delivered to Plaintiff with serious defects and nonconformities to warranty and developed other serious defects and nonconformities to warranty including, but not limited to, condition causing the driver side half-shaft to disengage from the transmission, causing a loss of propulsion and/or a loss of mechanical park, rear license plate lamp is inoperable, exhaust leak at the flex pipe weld, the front exhaust pipe bracket separates, and causing a hole and increased noise, lacks power and has difficulty accelerating, and leaking oil from the right corner of the front engine cover and causing a burning smell.” (Id. at ¶ 9.)

 

On 2/28/25, Plaintiff filed her complaint, alleging against Defendant the following causes of action: (1) Violation of Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; (2) Breach of Implied Warranties Pursuant to Song-Beverly; (3) Failure to Promptly Repurchase Product Pursuant to Song-Beverly; and (4) Failure to Commence Repairs Within a Reasonable Time and to Complete Them Within 30 Days Pursuant to Song-Beverly. On 4/7/25, Defendant filed its answer.

 

On 10/9/25, the parties executed a settlement agreement, settling entire lawsuit. (Ex. D to Decl. of Ashkan Reza.) On 10/13/25, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Conditional Settlement of the entire case.

 

On 1/20/26, Plaintiff filed the instant motion and her Memorandum of Costs. On 5/15/26, Defendant filed its opposition. On 5/26/28, Plaintiff filed an untimely reply. On 5/28/26, the Court continued the instant motion for supplemental briefing, “as Plaintiff failed to provide the Court with any evidence of the time incurred in the case.” (5/28/26 Min. Order.) On 6/10/26, Plaintiff filed her supplemental brief. On 6/23/26, Defendant filed its supplemental opposition.

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.    Right to Recovery

 

An award of attorney fees is proper when authorized by contract, statute, or law. (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 1032, subd. (b); 1033.5, subd. (a)(10).) Here, the Song-Beverly Act authorizes a buyer “to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” (Civ. Code § 1794, subd. (d).)

 

Moreover, Plaintiff bases the instant motion on the parties’ settlement agreement, wherein the parties agreed that “reasonable attorneys’ fees, costs, and expenses pursuant to subdivision (d) of Section 1794 of the Civil Code shall be determined by the court by way of a noticed motion. Defendant agrees the prevailing party for purposes of said fee motion.” (Ex. D to Reza Decl., ¶ 1.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that the instant motion is authorized both by contract and statute.

A buyer prevailing under the Song-Beverly Act may recover “reasonable” attorney fees and costs as determined by the Court. (Civ. Code § 1794, subd. (d).) In determining a reasonable fee award, the Court begins with the lodestar method of calculation, i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.¿(Karton v. Ari Design & Construction, Inc. (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 734, 744; PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095–1096.)

1.      Counsel’s Hourly Rate

 

When determining a reasonable hourly rate, courts consider whether the stated rates “are within the range of reasonable rates charged by and judicially awarded [to] comparable attorneys for comparable work.” (Children's Hosp. & Med. Ctr. v. Bonta (2002) 97 Cal.App.4th 740, 783.)

 

Here, Plaintiff asserts that the rates charged by her attorneys are commensurate with the experience and skill of the attorneys involved, and have been approved by courts in the past. (Pl.’s Mot. 8:4–9:2; Reza Decl. ¶¶ 22–36.) In opposition, Defendant argues that the Court should apply rates from the “2021 Real Rate Report: The Industry’s Leading Analysis of Law Firm Rates, Trends, and Practices” (the “Real Rate Report”), and reduce counsel’s hourly rates to $252.00 per hour for partners and $231.00 per hour for associate attorneys. (Def.’s Opp. 5:24–28.)

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff has sufficiently shown that her attorneys’ hourly billing rates are commensurate with their experience, and fall within the range of reasonable rates charged by attorneys in similar cases. Accordingly, the Court declines to reduce Plaintiff’s counsel’s hourly billing rates.

 

2.      Hours Expended

 

Plaintiff seeks to recover attorney fees for the 67.4 hours expended by her attorneys on this matter, totaling $22,219.50. Plaintiff proffers an itemized list of time entries detailing their attorneys’ work on this matter. (Ex. A to Supp. Reza Decl.)

 

Defendant challenges the following specific entries in Plaintiff’s attorneys’ proffered time log: (1) clerical tasks; (2) time billed in connection with the instant fee motion; (3) time billed in connection reviewing Defendant’s initial disclosures; (4) time billed in connection with Plaintiff’s deposition; and (4) time billed for mediation.

Billing the entire 4 hours to one matter is not only excessive but effectively asks GM to pay several times over for the same mediation session.” (Id. at 5:5–8.) Based on the foregoing, the Court reduces the time actually incurred for attending the mediation by 2.0 hours for a total reduction of $750.00.

 

 

C.    Multiplier

 

“Once the lodestar has been calculated, the second step is to apply any positive or negative multipliers. The purpose of such adjustment is to fix a fee at the fair market value for the particular action. In effect, the court determines, retrospectively, whether the litigation involved a contingent risk or required extraordinary legal skill justifying augmentation of the unadorned lodestar in order to approximate the fair market rate for such services.” (Santana v. FCA US, LLC (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 334, 351 [internal quotations omitted].) “Perhaps the most common multiplier applied, at least where a plaintiff prevails, is a modifier for the contingent nature of the representation.” (Ibid.) Whether a multiplier is applied to adjust the lodestar amount based on the contingent nature and risk associated with the action and other factors, such as degree of skill required and ultimate success achieved is within the discretion of the trial court.

 

Here, Plaintiff argues that she is entitled to recover a 0.3 multiplier enhancement because “this case required Plaintiff’s Counsel to decipher the various issues that the Vehicle suffered from and plan out Plaintiff’s discovery process. The Vehicle at issue contained several different types of defects.” (Pl.’s Mot. 13:6–8.) Plaintiff further asserts that “Plaintiff’s Counsel achieved an excellent outcome for Plaintiff, which included a favorable settlement amount to Plaintiff and GM’s agreement to pay Plaintiff’s attorney fees and costs by motion. This is by any measure an outstanding result.” (Id. at 13:25–27.)

 

Defendant argues in opposition that the record “reflects that this was a routine lemon-law matter that resolved promptly under California's fee-shifting scheme, presenting little to no contingency risk.” (Def.’s Opp. 6:26–28.) Defendant further argue that Plaintiff’s counsel’s “rates already reflect the contingent nature of work.” (Id. at 7:11.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that a multiplier enhancement is not warranted in this case. While Plaintiff ultimately prevailed, the action appears to have been a relatively straightforward Song-Beverly case. As such, the Court finds the lodestar fee award sufficient to compensate Plaintiff’s counsel.

 

D.    Costs Incurred

 

If the charges listed on a submitted memorandum of costs appear to be proper charges on their face, the verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of their propriety, and the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show they were not reasonable or necessary. (Ladas v. California State Auto. Ass’n (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 774–776.)

 

Here, Plaintiff asserts that she incurred costs and expenses totaling $535.24, which encompasses: (1) $485.24 in filing and motion fees; and (2) $50.00 in process server fees. (1/20/26 Mem. of Costs.) As Defendant does not challenge any of Plaintiff’s claimed costs, the Court finds them proper and reasonably incurred.

Case Number: 25CHCV03564    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: F51

LOS ANGELES SUPERIOR COURT

MOTIONS TO COMPEL FURTHER DISCOVERY RESPONSES

(Form Interrogatories and Special Interrogatories, Set One)

Los Angeles Superior Court Case # 25CHCV03564

 

Motions filed: 3/4/26

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant Mirzakbar Turghan Yueksell (“Defendant”)

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff Ediluce Wooden (“Plaintiff”)

NOTICE: OK

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: Orders compelling Plaintiff’s further responses to the following discovery requests:

·         Defendant’s Form Interrogatories, Set One, No. 6.4; and

·         Defendant’s Special Interrogatories, Set One, Nos. 13, 14, and 27.

Defendant also seeks monetary sanctions in the total amount of $1,123.30 to be imposed against Plaintiff and/or her counsel.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: Defendant’s motion to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to Defendant’s Form Interrogatories, Set One, is DENIED. Defendant’s motion to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to Defendant’s Special Interrogatories, Set One, is GRANTED as to Special Interrogatory No. 13, and DENIED as to Special Interrogatory Nos. 14 and 27. The Court orders Plaintiff to provide further code-compliant responses to Defendant’s Special Interrogatory No. 13 within 30 days. The Court declines to impose monetary sanctions.

 

BACKGROUND

 

This is a personal injury action in which Plaintiff alleges that on 12/15/23, “Defendant failed to maintain, repair, own or operate their vehicle such as to cause or contribute to the collision which injured Plaintiff.” (Compl. p. 5.) On 10/6/25, Plaintiff filed her complaint, alleging against Defendant the following causes of action: (1) Motor Vehicle Negligence; and (2) General Negligence. On 11/25/25, Defendant filed his answer.

 

On 11/25/25, Defendant served his first set of discovery requests on Plaintiff. (Decl. of Manuel Magapian ¶ 6.) On 1/27/26, Plaintiff served her responses thereto. (Id. at ¶ 9.)

 

On 3/4/26, Defendant filed the instant motions to compel further responses to the subject discovery requests.  On 6/15/26, Plaintiff filed her oppositions. No reply memoranda have been filed to date.[1]

 

ANALYSIS

 

“The party to whom interrogatories have been propounded shall respond in writing under oath separately to each interrogatory by any of the following: (1) An answer containing the information sought to be discovered; (2) An exercise of the party’s option to produce writings; or (3) An objection to the particular interrogatory.” (Code Civ Proc. § 2030.210, subd. (a).)

 

“Each answer in a response to interrogatories shall be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits. … If an interrogatory cannot be answered completely, it shall be answered to the extent possible. … If the responding party does not have personal knowledge sufficient to respond fully to an interrogatory, that party shall so state, but shall make a reasonable and good faith effort to obtain the information by inquiry to other natural persons or organizations, except where the information is equally available to the propounding party.” (Code Civ Proc. § 2030.220.)

 

A propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses to interrogatories if any of the following apply: “(1) An answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete; (2) An exercise of the option to produce documents under Section 2030.230 is unwarranted or the required specification of those documents is inadequate; or (3) An objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.300, subd. (a).)

 

A.    Meet and Confer

 

A motion to compel further discovery responses must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration stating “facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 2030.300, subd. (b)(1); 2016.040.) 

 

Here, Defendant’s counsel declares that beginning on 1/28/26, he and Plaintiff’s counsel began the meet and confer process regarding the issues raised in the instant motions, but the parties were unable to come to an informal resolution. (Magpapian Decl. ¶¶ 10–16.) While Plaintiff disputes the good faith nature of the meet and confer efforts, the Court finds that counsel has satisfied the preliminary meet and confer requirements under Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300, subdivision (b)(1).

 

B.     Form Interrogatory No. 6.4

 

Defendant’s Form Interrogatory No. 6.4 asks Plaintiff: “Did you receive any consultation or examination (except from expert witnesses covered by Code of Civil Procedure sections 2034.210–2034.310) or treatment from a HEALTH CARE PROVIDER for any injury you attribute to the INCIDENT? If so, for each HEALTH CARE PROVIDER state: (a) the name, ADDRESS, and telephone number; (b) the type of consultation, examination, or treatment provided; (c) the dates you received consultation, examination, or treatment; and (d) the charges to date.” (Def.’s FROG Sep. Stmt. 2:3–12.) “In lieu of preparing a compilation, abstract, audit, or summary of medical records, Plaintiff refers to her medical records attached to her response to production of document request propounded herewith and incorporates same by reference as though set out fully herein, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 2030.230.” (Id. at 2:14–17.)

 

Defendant argues that further responses are warranted because “Plaintiff is required to provide an address and telephone number for each Health Care Provider for any injury Plaintiff attributes to the INCIDENT and is required to provide the type of consultation, examination, or treatment she received from two providers: Henry Mayo Newhall Memorial Hospital and Corey Michael Wilson MD.” (Id. at 3:21–24.) In opposition, Plaintiff argues that she “expressly invoked [Code of Civil Procedure] section 2030.230 and referred Defendant to the medical records, which contain complete contact information for each provider on billing statements, letterhead, and patient records.” (Pl.’s FROG Opp. 9:10–13.)

 

“If the answer to an interrogatory would necessitate the preparation or the making of a compilation, abstract, audit, or summary of or from the documents of the party to whom the interrogatory is directed, and if the burden or expense of preparing or making it would be substantially the same for the party propounding the interrogatory as for the responding party, it is a sufficient answer to that interrogatory to refer to this section and to specify the writings from which the answer may be derived or ascertained. This specification shall be in sufficient detail to permit the propounding party to locate and to identify, as readily as the responding party can, the documents from which the answer may be ascertained. The responding party shall then afford to the propounding party a reasonable opportunity to examine, audit, or inspect these documents and to make copies, compilations, abstracts, or summaries of them.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.230.)

 

Here, Plaintiff maintains that “the medical records produced by Plaintiff contain detailed descriptions of the treatment provided.” (Pl.’s FROG Opp. 10:20–21.) In invoking section 2030.230, Plaintiff asserts that “the burden of extracting treatment types and appointment dates from the medical records is substantially the same—if not less—for Defendant than for Plaintiff.” (Id. at 11:18–20.)

 

In his response to Plaintiff’s separate statement, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s responses remain code-deficient because “Plaintiff’s RPD responses consisted of merely a link for Defendant to retrieve documents in which Plaintiff only produced a portion of the records from providers that were listed in her form interrogatories responses. Plaintiff’s responses were not sufficient answers because Plaintiff did not correctly and completely (sufficiently) specify the writings.” (Def.’s Response to Pl.’s Sep. Stmt. 2:18–21.) “As Plaintiff’s responses lack the necessary detail, further responses are warranted.” (Id. at 3:12–13.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court agrees with Plaintiff that she has sufficiently provided a response to Defendant’s Form Interrogatory No. 6.4 which complies with Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.230. The Court finds that in listing the names of the medical providers and the charges to date, Plaintiff has sufficiently specified the writings from which the answer to Form Interrogatory No. 6.4 may be derived or ascertained. Accordingly, the Court denies Defendant’s motion to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to Defendant’s Form Interrogatory No. 6.4.

 

C.    Special Interrogatories

 

Defendant argues that “Plaintiff has failed to provide any responses to Special Interrogatories Nos. 13, 14, and 27.” (Def.’s SPROG Mot. 2:12–13; Ex. C to Magpapian Decl.) In opposition, Plaintiff argues that “this assertion is incorrect. Plaintiff served timely objections on December 29, 2025, and verified responses on January 27, 2026. Those responses include responses to interrogatories Nos. 13, 14, and 27.” (Pl.’s SPROG Opp. 9:3–6, citing Ex. A to Decl. of Michael Stone-Molloy.)

 

The Court notes that Defendant has not filed any reply argument disputing that Plaintiff served timely objections to Special Interrogatory Nos. 13, 14, and 27 on 12/29/25. Accordingly, the Court addresses the merits of Plaintiff’s objections as follows.

 

1.      Special Interrogatory No. 13

 

Defendant’s Special Interrogatory No. 13 asks Plaintiff to “identify by name, address, and telephone number of all your primary care/family doctors you have seen in the five (5) years prior to the incident which is the subject of this lawsuit.” (Def.’s SPROG Sep. Stmt. 2:4–6.) In response, Plaintiff asserted objections based on, inter alia, attorney-client privilege, attorney work product, expert opinion, vagueness, and privacy. (Ex. A to Stone-Molloy Decl., 8:23–9:5.)

 

Plaintiff argues that no further responses are warranted because “Defendant can issue a subpoena to Kaiser Permanente requesting Plaintiff’s medical records for the five years preceding the collision. Those records will identify any treating physicians. Requiring Plaintiff to undertake that same investigation imposes a duplicative burden and defeats the statute’s efficiency purpose.” (Pl.’s SPROG Opp. 9:26–10:3.) The Court disagrees. “Each answer in a response to interrogatories shall be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220, subd. (a).) The Court is not persuaded that the burden should be on Defendant to subpoena Plaintiff’s medical records to obtain information that Plaintiff is able to provide.

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court grants Defendant’s motion to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to Defendant’s Special Interrogatory No. 13.

 

2.      Special Interrogatory No. 14

 

Defendant’s Special Interrogatory No. 14 asks Plaintiff to “identify by name, address, and telephone number all individuals, excluding medical providers, with knowledge of any and all physical pain, mental suffering, loss of employment of life, disfigurement, physical impairment, inconvenience, grief, anxiety, humiliation, and/or emotional distress you allege you sustained as a result of the subject incident.” (Def.’s SPROG Sep. Stmt. 2:26–3:2.) In response, Plaintiff asserted objections based on, inter alia, attorney-client privilege, attorney work product, expert opinion, vagueness, and privacy. (Ex. A to Stone-Molloy Decl., 9:13–23.)

 

Plaintiff argues that no further responses are warranted because “under California law, interrogatories seeking the identification of witnesses to support a party’s contentions are premature until the propounding party has conducted sufficient discovery to frame meaningful contention interrogatories. Defendant propounded this interrogatory on November 25, 2025, barely six weeks after Plaintiff filed her Complaint. At that time, neither party had conducted depositions, exchanged expert designations, or completed written discovery. Defendant cannot reasonably claim to need lay-witness identification at such an early stage of litigation.” (Pl.’s SPROG Opp. 10:20–11:2.) “Second, the interrogatory is overbroad. It seeks the identity of ‘all individuals’ with knowledge of Plaintiff’s pain, suffering, impairment, grief, anxiety, or humiliation. This sweeping request encompasses every friend, family member, coworker, or acquaintance who may have observed Plaintiff’s injuries or symptoms. Plaintiff has no duty to conduct an investigation to identify every such person, nor does Plaintiff have a duty to provide contact information for individuals whose observations may be protected by privacy considerations.” (Id. at 11:3–11.)

 

The Court disagrees with Plaintiff’s prematurity argument, particularly where Defendant served the subject discovery requests over seven months ago. However, the Court agrees with Plaintiff that Special Interrogatory No. 14 is facially overbroad. Based on the foregoing, the Court denies Defendant’s motion to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to Defendant’s Special Interrogatory No. 14.

 

3.      Special Interrogatory No. 27

 

Defendant’s Special Interrogatory No. 27 asks Plaintiff to “identify by name, address, and telephone number all medical providers who provided care or treatment to the same areas of your body in the past ten (10) years that you claim were injured in the incident which the subject of this lawsuit.” (Def.’s SPROG Sep. Stmt. 3:22–25.) In response, Plaintiff asserted objections based on, inter alia, attorney-client privilege, attorney work product, expert opinion, vagueness, and privacy. (Ex. A to Stone-Molloy Decl., 16:12–22.)

 

Plaintiff argues that no further responses are warranted because “here, Plaintiff has not alleged that the December 15, 2023 collision aggravated any pre-existing condition.” (Pl.’s SPROG Opp. 12:17–18.) “Plaintiff has consistently reported that she had no physical complaints prior to the collision. Because Plaintiff has not placed pre-existing conditions at issue, Defendant is not entitled to discover Plaintiff’s unrelated medical history for the ten years preceding the collision.” (Id. at 12:24–28.)

 

Plaintiff further argues that the interrogatory is overbroad because “Plaintiff alleges injuries to her head, neck, chest/ribs, left shoulder, low back, left hip, and abdomen. ‘The same areas’ could encompass any treatment to Plaintiff's head, neck, torso, shoulder, back, hip, or abdomen over a ten-year period—virtually her entire body. The interrogatory would require Plaintiff to disclose treatment for unrelated conditions such as routine physical examinations, gynecological care, gastrointestinal issues, respiratory infections, or any other condition affecting those broad anatomical regions.” (Id. at 13:7–15.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court agrees with Plaintiff that Special Interrogatory No. 27 is facially overbroad. Based on the foregoing, the Court denies Defendant’s motion to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to Defendant’s Special Interrogatory No. 27.

 

D.    Monetary Sanctions

 

“The court shall impose a monetary sanction … against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.290, subd. (c).) Additionally, “the court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2023.030, subd. (a).)

 

Here, Defendant requests a total combined sum of $1,123.30 in monetary sanctions to be imposed against Plaintiff and/or her counsel. The Court finds that the imposition of sanctions would be unjust here, as Defendant did not fully succeed on his motions. Accordingly, the Court declines to impose monetary sanctions.

 

CONCLUSION

 

Defendant’s motion to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to Defendant’s Form Interrogatories, Set One, is DENIED. Defendant’s motion to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to Defendant’s Special Interrogatories, Set One, is GRANTED as to Special Interrogatory No. 13, and DENIED as to Special Interrogatory Nos. 14 and 27. The Court orders Plaintiff to provide further code-compliant responses to Defendant’s Special Interrogatory No. 13 within 30 days. The Court declines to impose monetary sanctions.

BACKGROUND¿ 

 

This is a landlord-tenant action in which Plaintiff alleges that on 3/1/24, she took lawful possession of certain residential property located at 13123 Tonopah Street, Arleta, California 91331, pursuant to a written lease agreement entered into with Defendant. (Compl. ¶¶ 1–3.) “This case arises from a sustained campaign of harassment, intimidation, discriminatory treatment, privacy invasion, and retaliatory conduct directed at Plaintiff by her landlord, Delia Avila. Through continuous surveillance, threats, accusations, unwarranted restrictions, hostile confrontations, fabricated allegations, and denials of disability accommodations, Defendant has systematically dismantled Plaintiff’s right to secure, stable, and peaceful housing.” (Id. at ¶ 7.)

 

On 1/7/26, Plaintiff filed her complaint, alleging against Defendant the following causes of action: (1) Violation of Los Angeles Tenant Anti Harassment Ordinance; (2) Breach of the Covenant of Quiet Enjoyment; (3) Nuisance; (4) Negligent Infliction of Emotional Distress; (5) Negligence; (6) Breach of Contract; and (7) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress.

 

On 3/19/26, Defendant filed the instant demurrer and motion to strike. No opposition has been filed to date.

DEMURRER

 

As a general matter, a party may respond to a pleading against it by demurrer on the basis of any single or combination of eight enumerated grounds, including that “the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action” and is uncertain, meaning “ambiguous and unintelligible.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subds. (e) and (f).) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿

 

“A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) 

 

Here, Defendant¿demurs to Plaintiff’s entire complaint on the basis that she fails¿to allege facts sufficient to state any of the causes of action therein, rendering the complaint fatally uncertain.

 

A.    Meet-and-Confer 

 

Before filing its demurrer, “the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41, subd. (a).) “If the parties are not able to meet and confer at least 5 days before the date the responsive pleading is due, the demurring party shall be granted an automatic 30-day extension of time within which to file a responsive pleading, by filing and serving, on or before the date on which a demurrer would be due, a declaration stating under penalty of perjury that a good faith attempt to meet and confer was made and explaining the reasons why the parties could not meet and confer.” (Id. at subd. (a)(2).)

 

Here, Defendant’s counsel declares that her “did not have sufficient time and opportunity to meet and confer with Plaintiff’s counsel over noted issues in her Complaint, including but not limited to multiple failures to state sufficient facts for multiple causes of action, and uncertain, unclear causes of action which cannot be reasonably responded to via an Answer until the Complaint is either amended or the Demurrer is filed and heard by this Court.” (Decl. of Nathan P. Ayala ¶ 5.) Therefore, the Court finds that counsel has satisfied the preliminary meet and confer requirements of Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a).

 

B.     Uncertainty

 

Generally speaking, “demurrers for uncertainty are disfavored and thus are strictly construed because ambiguities can reasonably be clarified under modern rules of discovery. Such demurrers are granted only if the pleading is so incomprehensible that defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (Cal.Jur.3d § 137.) “Where the complaint contains substantive factual allegations sufficiently apprising defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet, a demurrer for uncertainty should be overruled or plaintiff given leave to amend.” (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (2011) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139 fn.2.)

 

Here, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s complaint is uncertain pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (f), because “the mismatched causes of action, between the list on page five [paragraph 19], and the subsequent seven causes of action, create uncertainty within the Complaint. It makes it unclear as to which causes of action are being alleged, as the list on page five contains four more causes of action than the seven that follow. Only four of the eleven causes of action on page five are followed by full pleadings.” (Dem. 6:3–6.) “Because Defendant cannot determine which causes of action are being alleged, nor which facts support a specific claim, Defendant cannot reasonably respond to the Complaint or prepare a defense.” (Id. at 6:10–12.)

 

The Court disagrees, finding that Defendant attempts to cast the list of claims contained in paragraph 19 of the complaint as “causes of action.” However, accepting as true the allegations of the complaint, Plaintiff alleges that this is a list of “conditions” that she, and/or third parties, noticed and documented. (Compl. ¶ 19.) The substantive causes of action Plaintiff brings against Defendant, following the allegations in paragraph 19, are clearly enumerated and identified. Therefore, in applying the stringent standard for demurrers filed on this ground, the Court finds that the complaint is not “so incomprehensible” that Defendant cannot respond. Accordingly, the demurrer is overruled on this ground.

 

C.    Factual Sufficiency

 

Defendant further generally argues that Plaintiff has failed to allege facts sufficient to support any of her seven causes of action. “Within the first four pages of the Complaint there are vague references to date ranges, with no specific event or connection between a date and a cause of action that would trigger the liability of Defendant. In the subsequent seven causes of action (including the four overlapping causes of action), no further reference is made to specific acts, dates, or conduct by Defendant to satisfy the elements of those claims. Instead, Plaintiff relies only on vague and conclusory statements which lack ultimate facts.” (Dem. 7:5–10.)

 

Upon review of the complaint, the Court notes that Plaintiff has alleged that “beginning in 2024 and escalating into 2025, Defendant initiated a series of hostile communications and intrusive conduct designed to intimidate Plaintiff.” (Compl. ¶ 11.) Defendant’s alleged misconduct includes, inter alia, accusing Plaintiff of trespassing into common areas; forbidding Plaintiff’s emotional support animal from entering the backyard; photographing the interior spaces without Plaintiff’s consent; attempting to enter the subject premises without lawful notice; and withholding funds owed to Plaintiff for a washer and dryer she purchased for the premises. (Id. at ¶¶ 11–17.) “Taken together, Defendant’s actions constitute a continuous campaign of harassment, discrimination, intimidation, and interference with Plaintiff’s lawful right to quiet enjoyment.” (Id. at ¶ 18.)

 

Based on the foregoing allegations, the Court rejects Defendant’s argument that Plaintiff has failed to allege facts to support any of her causes of action. The Court notes that Defendant makes no substantive arguments relating to any defects with the individual causes of action. Accordingly, the demurrer is overruled on this ground.

 

MOTION TO STRIKE

 

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Id., § 437.) 

 

Here, Defendant moves to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages, and various allegations contained in paragraph 19, from the complaint.

 

A.    Punitive Damages

 

Punitive damages may be recovered upon a proper showing of malice, fraud, or oppression by clear and convincing evidence. (Civ. Code § 3294, subd. (a).) “Malice” is defined as conduct intended to cause injury to a person or despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard for the rights or safety of others. (Id. at subd. (c); Turman v. Turning Point of Cent. Cal., Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) “Oppression” means despicable conduct subjecting a person to cruel and unjust hardship, in conscious disregard of the person’s rights. (Ibid.) “Fraud” is an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known by defendant, with intent to deprive a person of property, rights or otherwise cause injury. (Ibid.)

 

Here, Defendant argues that “Plaintiff fails to provide specificity to the causes of action (of uncertain number and type) to make a claim for punitive damages.” (MTS 4:10–11.) Defendant therefore asserts that “the insufficient facts fail to constitute a cause of action and certainly do not meet the requirements for punitive damages.” (Id. at 4:13–14.) However, as discussed above, the Court finds that Plaintiff has alleged facts sufficient to support each of her causes of action. The Court further finds that Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged that Defendant acted willfully and in conscious disregard for Plaintiff’s rights. (Compl. ¶ 27.)

 

Accordingly, the Court finds that at this stage, Defendant’s alleged conduct as pled may be considered malicious and oppressive as defined by Civil Code section 3294. Further investigation of the merits of Plaintiff’s allegations may be resolved through the discovery process. Accordingly, the motion to strike these allegations from the complaint is denied.

 

B.     Irrelevant and Improper Matter

 

Defendant further moves to strike items (e) through (k) from paragraph 19 of the complaint, arguing that “of the causes of action listed on page five [paragraph 19], the seven in bold above have no supporting factual allegations and appear to be added either by mistake or part of a boilerplate pleading. In either event, the causes of action being merely listed only create uncertainty as to the actual claims being made in the Complaint.” (MTS 5:15–18.)

 

As discussed above, the Court rejects Defendant’s characterization of these allegations as substantive causes of action. Accordingly, the motion to strike these allegations from the complaint is denied.

 

CONCLUSION¿ 

 

The unopposed demurrer is overruled, and the unopposed motion to strike is denied. Defendant to file her answer to Plaintiff’s complaint within 20 days.
