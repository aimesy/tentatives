Case Name: Akopyan v. Airapetian, et al.

MOTION TO QUASH
[CCP § 418.10]

Moving Party: Specially Appearing Defendant, Anna Airapetian
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Edvard Akopyan [Unopposed]

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Quash service of summons and Complaint as to Anna Airapetian

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint 
1) Fraud
2) Intentional Misrepresentation
3) Negligent Misrepresentation
4) Fraud by Wire in Violation of United States Code Title 18 § 1343
5) Breach of Contract
6) Breach of Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealings
7) Conversion
8) Intentional Interference with Prospective Economic Advantage 
9) Negligent Interference with Prospective Economic Advantage
10) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress
11) Unjust Enrichment
12) Conspiracy 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
Edvard Akopyan (“Plaintiff”) alleges that Anna Airapetian (“Airapetian”) and Tamara S. Chirkalina (“Chirkalina”) (collectively, “Defendants”) became indebted to Plaintiff in the amounts of $17,000 and $25,000 for Plaintiff’s services rendered. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants failed to issue a check for $17,000 and refused to pay $25,000 when requested. 

ANALYSIS:
“Service of process, under longstanding tradition in our system of justice, is fundamental to any procedural imposition on a named defendant.”¿(AO Alfa-Bank v. Yakovlev¿(2018)¿21 Cal.App.5th¿189, 202¿[internal citations omitted].) “To establish personal jurisdiction, compliance with statutory procedures for service of process is essential.” (Kremerman v. White (2021) 71 Cal.App.5th 358, 371.) Defendant’s knowledge of the action does not dispense with statutory requirements for service of summons. (Kappel v. Bartlett¿(1988) 200 Cal.App.3d 1457, 1466.)¿¿¿ 

“A defendant, on or before the last day of his or her time to plead or within any further time that the court may for good cause allow” may move “to quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over him or her” that results from lack of proper service. (CCP, § 418.10, subd. (a)(1).)¿A defendant has 30 days after the service of the summons to file a responsive pleading. (CCP, § 412.20, subd. (a)(3).)¿¿¿¿ 

Once a challenge to a court’s personal¿jurisdiction¿is filed, “the burden of proof is placed upon the plaintiff to¿establish¿the facts of¿jurisdiction¿by a preponderance of the evidence. This may be done through presentation of declarations, with opposing declarations received in response.”¿(CenterPoint Energy, Inc. v. Superior Court¿(2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1101, 1118 (citing¿Aquila, Inc. v. Superior Court¿(2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 556, 568).)¿“When a defendant challenges the court’s personal¿jurisdiction¿on the ground of improper service of process ‘the burden is on the plaintiff to prove the existence of jurisdiction by proving, inter alia, the facts requisite to an effective service.’”¿(Summers v. McClanahan¿(2006) 140¿Cal.App.4th 403, 413.) 

Airapetian argues that the summons and Complaint were not properly served and that the Court lacks jurisdiction over her. 

“A summons may be served by publication if upon affidavit it appears to the satisfaction of the court in which the action is pending that the party to be served cannot with reasonable diligence be served in another manner specified in this article and that either: (1) A cause of action exists against the party upon whom service is to be made or he or she is a necessary or proper party to the action. (2) The party to be served has or claims an interest in real or personal property in this state that is subject to the jurisdiction of the court or the relief demanded in the action consists wholly or in part in excluding the party from any interest in the property.” (CCP § 415.50 subd. (a).) 

“The court shall order the summons to be published in a named newspaper, published in this state, that is most likely to give actual notice to the party to be served. If the party to be served resides or is located out of this state, the court may also order the summons to be published in a named newspaper outside this state that is most likely to give actual notice to that party. The order shall direct that a copy of the summons, the complaint, and the order for publication be forthwith mailed to the party if his or her address is ascertained before expiration of the time prescribed for publication of the summons.” (CCP § 415.50 subd. (b).) 

On January 16, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s application to serve Airapetian by publication in the New York Jewish Press. (1/16/2026 Order.) Plaintiff filed proof of publication on four dates in The Jewish Press. (3/06/2026 Notice of Completion.) 

Airapetian argues that The Jewish Press is not the newspaper most likely to give her actual notice. Airapetian notes that the “The Jewish Press is an independent, Brooklyn-based weekly paper that describes its content as an ‘eclectic mix of Jewish news, political and religious commentary’ that ‘has championed Torah values and ideals from a centrist or Modern Orthodox perspective.’” (Mot., p. 6:18-21.) Airapetian is neither Jewish nor has “any other known reason to regularly read a Brooklyn-based Orthodox Jewish publication.” (Airapetian Decl., ¶ 6; Mot., p. 6:22-23.) Airapetian argues that Plaintiff also failed to comply with the Court’s order for publication because he failed to file a declaration of mailing at the expiration of the time prescribed for publication, or a declaration of the fact that Airapetian’s address was not ascertained. (Mot., p. 13:17-28.) Airapentian further argues that the published summons is not Code-compliant because it does not include all the required elements pursuant to CCP section 412.20. (Mot., pp. 12:9-13:15.) 

In addition, Airapetian argues that the Court lacks jurisdiction over her because she is a resident of New York, has never lived in California, never owned or rented real property in California, and has never worked or conducted any business in California. (Airapetian Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.) The Complaint does not allege any conduct by Airapetian in California. (Compl., ¶ 7.) The Complaint alleges that Airapetian is “an individual, with the place of residence and/or business in the USA.” (Compl., ¶ 2.) 

Lastly, Plaintiff has not filed any timely opposition to this motion. When a defendant challenges the court’s personal¿jurisdiction, it is the plaintiff’s burden to establish proper service. (Dill v. Berquist Const. Co., Inc. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1439-1440.) Plaintiff fails to do so here. 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that it does not have personal jurisdiction over Airapetian based both on improper service and lack of minimum contacts with California. 

Thus, the motion is GRANTED. 

RULING:
Specially Appearing Defendant Anna Airapetian’s Motion to Quash is GRANTED.

The service of summons on Anna Airapetian is QUASHED.

Case Number: 23GDCV02547    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: D

TENTATIVE RULING

Calendar: 2
Date: September 11, 2026
Case No: 23GDCV02547 Trial Date:  n/a
Case Name: Valdes v. KIA America, Inc., et al.

MOTION FOR ATTORNEYS’ FEES
[CCP § 1794 subd. (d)]

Moving Party: Plaintiff, Mirla Valdes  
Responding Party: Defendant, Kia America, Inc.

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Order awarding attorneys’ fees and costs in the amount of $106,958.13 comprised of attorneys’ fees of $86,366.50, and a lodestar multiplier enhancement of $20,591.63. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint 
1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act - Breach of Express Warranty
2) Violation of Song-Beverly Act - Breach of Implied Warranty
3) Violation of Song-Beverly Act – Section 1793.2(b) 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
Mirla Valdes (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on October 20, 2022, Plaintiff purchased a 2023 Kia Sorento for which Kia America, Inc. (“Defendant”) issued express warranties. Plaintiff alleges that the vehicle was delivered with defects and nonconformities to warranties and that other defects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves within the express warranty period. The Complaint alleges that notwithstanding Plaintiff’s entitlement, Defendant has failed to either promptly replace the new motor vehicle or to promptly make restitution in accordance with the Song-Beverly Act. 

ANALYSIS:
“Except as otherwise expressly provided by statute, a prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs in any action or proceeding.” (CCP, § 1032 subd. (b).) Attorney fees are allowed as costs when authorized by contract, statute or law. (CCP, § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10)(B).)

In a lemon law action, costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees, may be recovered by a prevailing buyer under the Song-Beverly Act. Civil Code section 1794 states: 

“If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” 

(Civil Code, § 1794, subd. (d).) 

Entitlement to Attorneys’ Fees
The parties here do not dispute that Plaintiff is the prevailing party pursuant to a February 6, 2026 Settlement Agreement which provides for an award of attorneys’ fees, costs, and expenses to be determined by noticed motion. (Jacobson Decl., ¶ 134.)  Thus, Plaintiff is the prevailing party in this action and is entitled to a reasonable amount of attorneys’ fees. 

Reasonableness of Fees
Plaintiff seeks an award of attorneys’ fees and expenses in the total amount of $106,958.13 consisting of: (1) $86,366.50 in attorneys’ fees (including the $4,000.00 requested to review the opposition, draft the reply, and attend the hearing); and (2) a 1.25 multiplier enhancement on the attorneys’ fees and costs [or $20,591.63]. The motion seeks approximately 158 hours of time spent by attorneys at Quill & Arrow, LLP at billing rates ranging from $175.00 to $950.00 per hour. 

Hourly Rate
In determining a reasonable attorneys’ fee, the trial court begins with the lodestar, i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate. (PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084. 1095.) The lodestar may then be adjusted based on factors specific to the case in order to fix the fee at the fair market value of the legal services provided. (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 36.) These facts include (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award. (Ibid.) 

“The reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work.” (PLCM Group, supra, 22 Cal.4th at p. 1095 [“The experienced trial judge is the best judge of the value of professional services rendered in his court.”].)

In connection with attorneys’ fee awards under the Song-Beverly Act, “The statute “requires the trial court to make an initial determination of the actual time expended; and then to ascertain whether under all the circumstances of the case the amount of actual time expended and the monetary charge being made for the time expended are reasonable. These circumstances may include, but are not limited to, factors such as the complexity of the case and procedural demands, the skill exhibited and the results achieved. If the time expended or the monetary charge being made for the time expended are not reasonable under all the circumstances, then the court must take this into account and award attorney fees in a lesser amount. A prevailing buyer has the burden of ‘showing that the fees incurred were “allowable,” were “reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation,” and were “reasonable in amount.” ’ ” (Goglin v. BMW of North America, LLC (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 462, 470, quoting Nightingale v. Hundai Motor America (1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 99, 104.) 

In the opposition, Defendant argues that the billing rates charged are unreasonable. Defendant asserts that Plaintiffs’ counsels’ rates are unreasonable in light of the non-complex nature of the case. (Opp., pp. 6:27-7:28.) Defendant requests that the Court reduce the rates to a range of $350-$450 per hour. (Opp., p. 7:27.)

The moving papers submit evidence justifying the billing rates claimed, describing the experience and expertise of each attorney who billed on this matter (Jacobson Decl., ¶¶ 3-70.) The rates requested conform with what this Court would expect to be charged in matters of this nature. The Court has reviewed all evidence submitted and is familiar with the customary billing rates in this county and in cases of this nature. Under the circumstances, the Court finds the billing rates reasonable, and the Court will not reduce the billing rates charged. The Court will however, take into account the high hourly rates charged and counsels’ attested levels of expertise when assessing whether the total number of hours expended was reasonable.

Number of Hours
Plaintiff’s fee recovery is based on 158 hours of time spent litigating this case through the instant motion for which Plaintiff has submitted billing records. (Jacobson Decl., Ex. 38.) Defendant objects to Plaintiff’s requested hours on the grounds that several of the billing entries are unreasonable. (Opp., pp. 8:19-12:5.)

“In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence. General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.” (Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc. v. California Ins. Guarantee Ass’n (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 564.)

First, Defendant objects to 9.0 hours billed for tasks aimed at recovering Plaintiff’s own fees and costs, including generating the instant motion for attorneys’ fees. Defendant argues that a reduction of 7.0 hours is warranted. While the billing and supporting evidence may have taken some time to gather, this appears to be substantially a template motion regularly used by Plaintiff’s counsel. The Court therefore reduces the fee by 4.0 hours. 

Accordingly, the Court will reduce the fees by $1,980.00

In addition, the request for an additional $4,000 to review the opposition, draft a reply, and appear at the hearing is excessive. At counsel’s requested rate of $495.00 per hour, this represents more than 8.0 hours of attorney time. The Court finds a reasonable time spent to be no more than 1.0 hour to review the opposition, 3.0 hours to prepare the reply, and 1.0 hour to attend the hearing by video appearance. In total, 5.0 hours of attorney time at $495.00 per hour amounts to $2,475.00. 

Accordingly, the Court will reduce the fees by an additional $1,525.00

Second, Defendant objects to 1.4 hours billed to prepare Plaintiff’s initial written discovery requests. The Court agrees that the hours claimed are overstated. The Court therefore reduces the fee by 0.6 hours.

Accordingly, the Court will reduce the fees by $315.00.

Third, Defendant objects to 2.7 hours billed to draft Plaintiff’s trial brief on the grounds that the document is nearly identical in every case besides Plaintiff’s name, the vehicle information, and the description of the case. The Court agrees that the hours claimed are excessive. Updating the trial brief with the relevant information should take minimal time, especially for counsel with the level of experience and high hourly rate claimed here. The Court therefore reduces the requested time by 1.0 hours.

Accordingly, the Court will reduce the fees by $425.00. 

Fourth, Defendant objects to 3.6 hours billed for reviewing Defendant’s discovery responses and documents produced on grounds that the documents are the same or similar to documents produced to Plaintiff’s counsel in other cases. The Court agrees that the hours claimed are overstated. The Court therefore reduces the requested time by 1.5 hours.

Accordingly, the Court will reduce the fees by $525.00. 

Fifth, Defendant objects to 0.9 hours to review Defendant’s propounded first set of discovery. Upon review of the time entries, the Court does find the time spent on this task to be unreasonable. Accordingly, the Court declines to reduce the fees on this ground. 

Sixth, Defendant objects to 4.0 hours billed to draft Plaintiff’s discovery responses on the grounds that the same discovery is propounded in nearly every lemon law matter. The Court agrees that the hours claimed are excessive. Responding to standard lemon law discovery should take minimal time, especially for counsel with the level of experience and high hourly rate claimed here. The Court therefore reduces the requested time by 2.0 hours. 

Accordingly, the Court will reduce the fees by $800.00. 

Seventh, Defendant objects to several entries as block-billed. After reviewing the challenged billing entries, the Court finds that some reduction in the time billed is warranted. Specifically, the 4.2 hours billed on August 25, 2025, is improperly block-billed. The entry lumps together various distinct tasks, such that the Court has no way to disaggregate the activities to determine whether the time billed for any particular task, or all of the tasks together, was reasonable. The Court therefore reduces the requested time by 1.2 hours. 

Accordingly, the Court will reduce the fees by $1,140.00. 

Eighth, Defendant objects to several entries on the grounds that they are duplicative. The Court agrees that the entries on May 15, 2024 to review Defendant’s objections to the notice of deposition are duplicative. The Court therefore reduces the requested time by 0.2 hours.

Accordingly, the Court will reduce the fees by $70.00. 

Ninth, Defendant objects to 5.9 hours billed to begin drafting the opening statement. Defendant argues that having two attorneys work on a single task is excessive and unnecessary. The Court agrees that the time is duplicative and unnecessary. The Court therefore reduces the requested time by 2.0 hours.

Accordingly, the Court will reduce the fees by $1,900.00. 

Having analyzed the motion and pleadings filed, and having reviewed the billing statements provided, the Court determines that a reasonable lodestar in this case is $77,686.50 representing 145.7 hours of attorney time. In sum, the Court reduced the requested lodestar of $86,366.50 by $8,680.00 for a total of $77,686.50 and the Court reduced the requested hours of 158 by 12.5 for a total award of 145.5 hours. 

Multiplier
While the lodestar reflects the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community, it may be adjusted based on various factors, including “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, and the skill displayed in presenting them; (2) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys; (3) the contingent nature of the fee award” and (4) the success achieved. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49.) 

Nonetheless, the court must not consider extraordinary skill and the other Serrano factors to the extent these are already included with the lodestar. (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal. 4th 1122, 1138-1139.) “[A] trial court should award a multiplier for exceptional representation only when the quality of representation far exceeds the quality of representation that would have been provided by an attorney of comparable skill and experience billing at the hourly rate used in the lodestar calculation. Otherwise, the fee award will result in unfair double counting and be unreasonable.” (Id. at 1139.) 

Plaintiff requests a lodestar multiplier enhancement of 1.25 to account for risk and delay in payment. (Mot., pp. 13:11-15:7.)

The Court finds that under the circumstances of this case, a lodestar multiplier is not appropriate. There is no indication that counsel was prevented from taking on other clients by the representation. While counsel accepted the case on a contingency basis and Plaintiff’s attorneys advanced all litigation costs and expenses, the Court finds that such consideration and risk is already included within the lodestar amount. That conclusion is because the substantial hourly rates allowed for by the Court are hourly rates for lemon law cases done on a contingency basis. 

Based on the foregoing, the Court declines to award a lodestar multiplier.

RULING:
Plaintiffs’ Motion for Attorney Fees, Costs, and Expenses is GRANTED, IN PART.

The Court finds that reasonable attorney fees are: 
Lodestar Adjusted= $77,686.50 [$86,366.50 requested]

The total fee award of $77,686.50 [$106,958.13 sought] is to be awarded to Plaintiff Mirla Valdes against Defendant Kia America, Inc. and added to the judgment. 

Counsel for moving party is ordered to prepare a proposed form of judgment and submit it on eCourt by noon today in accordance with this order.

Date: September 11, 2026
Case No: 24NNCV00504 Trial Date:  July 6, 2027
Case Name: Gallogly v. Huntington Memorial Hospital, et al.

MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO RFPS
[CCP § 2031.310]

Moving Party: Plaintiff, Mary Garripoli Gallogly
Responding Party: Defendant, Huntington Health

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Compel further responses to Requests for Production, No. 1. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint
1) Medical Negligence

CHRONOLOGY: 
Discovery Served:    April 21, 2026 
Responses Served: June 22, 2026 / August 28, 2026
Motion Served: August 7, 2026

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
Mary Garripoli Gallogly (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on September 8, 2022, she was admitted to defendant Huntington Health (“Huntington”) and diagnosed with distal right ureterolithiasis, a condition wherein a stone or stones are present in the ureter, the “tube” connecting the kidney to the bladder. Defendant Shahin Chandrasoma, (“Chandrasoma”) performed a right ureteroscopy, right retrograde pyelogram, right renal washings, laser lithotripsy, basket extraction of stone fragments, and placement of a ureteral stent. During the procedures, Chandrasoma observed a liquid within Plaintiff’s ureteral orifice. A sample of this liquid was sent for laboratory analysis which revealed the presence of E. coli in Plaintiff’s body. Plaintiff alleges that at no point did Chandrasoma disclose to Plaintiff that she had an E. coli infection. Following the procedures, Plaintiff began to develop a number of health issues including severe back pain and symptoms of congestive heart failure. Plaintiff was treated for fluid build-up in her lungs and was hospitalized multiple times. In March of 2023, Plaintiff consulted with an infectious disease doctor which revealed that Plaintiff still had an E. coli infection which was causing her discitis and likely her other health issues. He prescribed medications, and the infection was cured.

ANALYSIS:
A motion to compel a further response is used when a party gives unsatisfactory answers or makes untenable objections to interrogatories, demands to produce, or requests for admission. (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (a); Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 403.)

To request further production, a movant must establish: (1) good cause for the production (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1); Sinaiko, supra, at p. 403); and (2) that a further response is needed because (a) the responding party’s statement of compliance with the demand to produce is incomplete (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (a)(1)), (b) the responding party’s representation that it is unable to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (a)(2)), (c) the responding party’s objection in the response is without merit or is too general (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (a)(3); Catalina Island Yacht Club v. Superior Court (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1127), or (d) if the responding party objected to the production of ESI on the ground that it is not reasonably accessible the movant can show that the (i) ESI is reasonably accessible or (ii) there is good cause for production of the ESI regardless of its accessibility. (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (e).)

Plaintiff’s RFP No. 1 seeks “a complete, unredacted, and unaltered AUDIT TRAIL /AUDIT LOG and all associated METADATA for the Electronic Medical Record (EMR) of Marry Garripoli-Gallogly, specifically covering the period from September 9, 2022 to December 10, 2022.” 

In the opposition, Huntington notes that on August 28, 2026, it served a supplemental response to the request responding that “After diligent search and reasonable inquiry Responding Party is no longer in possession, custody, or control of documents responsive to this Request. Responding Party lost access with no way to retrieve these documents when it made a switch to a new software system in 2022 prior to this case being filed in 2024. As a result of switching to the new software, access to Plaintiffs audit trail for the period from September 9, 2022 to December 10, 2022 was lost and is no longer retrievable.” 

This response is a Code-compliant statement of inability to comply. (CCP, § 2031.230.) Accordingly, the motion to compel further is DENIED. This posture leaves only the issue of sanctions. 

Request for Sanctions
Except in certain circumstances involving electronically stored information, “the court shall impose a monetary sanction … against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further response to a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (h).)

Under CRC Rule 3.1348(a): “The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though no opposition to the motion was filed, or opposition to the motion was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.”
 
The burden is on the party subject to sanctions to show substantial justification or injustice. Mattco Forge, Inc. v. Arthur Young & Co. (1990, 2nd Dist.) 223 Cal.App.3d 1429, 1436.   

Plaintiff requests sanctions in the amount of $2,310.00. The amount requested is high given that the motion pertains to a single discovery request and was ultimately denied. However, the Court finds that sanctions are warranted because Huntington did not provide a Code-compliant response until after this motion was filed. The sanctions are adjusted accordingly. Sanctions are awarded as follows: Total attorneys hours of 3.0 hours at $450.00 per hour for total attorney fees of $1,350.00 

RECOMMENDED RULING:
Plaintiff Mary Garripoli Gallogly’s Motion to Compel Further Responses is DENIED. 

Monetary sanctions sought by the moving party: 
Utilizing a lodestar approach, and in view of the totality of the circumstances, the Court finds that reasonable amount of attorney’s fees and costs incurred for the work performed in connection with the pending motion is $1,350.00 (3.0 hours @ $450/hour)  [5.0 hours requested] plus $60 in filing fees for a total award of $1,410.00 [Amount Requested $2,310.00], which sum is to be awarded in favor of Plaintiff Mary Garripoli Gallogly, and against Defendant Huntington Health and Defendant’s attorney of record, jointly and severally, payable within 30 days. (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (h); CRC Rule 3.1348(a).) 

Counsel for moving party is ordered to prepare an order for sanctions and submit it on eCourt by noon today in accordance with this order.

TENTATIVE RULING

Calendar: 4
Date: September 11, 2026
Case No: 24NNCV04104 Trial Date:  May 10, 2027
Case Name: Ogihara v. Kim, et al.

MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE A SAC
[CCP § 473 subd. (a)(1)]

Moving Party: Plaintiff, Ikuo Ogihara
Responding Party: Defendants, Andrew Kim and HBSM Care Inc. [Unopposed]

RELIEF REQUESTED:
An order granting leave to file a Second Amended Complaint 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from First Amended Complaint 
1) Breach of Contract
2) Breach of Contract
3) Breach of Contract
4) Account Stated
5) Money Had and Received
6) Open Book Account
7) Declaratory Relief 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
Ikuo Ogihara (“Plaintiff”) alleges that in June of 2013, Plaintiff entered into a Revolving Line of Credit Agreement (RLCA), accompanying Promissory Note (Note) and Personal Guaranty (Guaranty) pursuant to which Plaintiff agreed to loan Defendants $500,000 in exchange for Defendants’ repayment of the principal amount in five years with an interest rate of 3% per annum compounding on the original principal of the loan. Plaintiff alleges that defendant Andrew Kim (“Kim”) executed the RLCA in his capacity as owner of Sports Med Care, Inc., which was later amended to name only HBSM Care, Inc (“HBSM”). Kim also executed the Note, and, as an additional term to the RLCA and to provide further assurance to Plaintiff, Kim also executed the Guaranty to the Note in his individual capacity as personal guarantor. 

The FAC alleges that the Note was originally due and payable on July 14, 2018, with Defendants allowed to make only the interest payments during the first 3 years of the loan and then to pay the principal plus accrued interest in the remaining final two years. Defendants, however, failed to make timely interest payments and instead made sporadic interest payments over time. They also failed to make any payments toward the principal loan balance. The sporadic payments continued until 2021, and, despite promises of repayment, Defendants failed to make any additional payments to Plaintiff since May 23, 2021. The FAC alleges that as of the filing of the Complaint, the total outstanding amount owed by Defendants is approximately $609,621.17.

ANALYSIS:
The court may, in its discretion and after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars. (CCP, § 473, subd. (a)(1); Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Association (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) 

As judicial policy favors resolution of all disputed matters between the parties, leave to amend is generally liberally granted. (See Kolani v. Gluska (1998) 64 Cal.App.4th 402, 412.) The court may deny the plaintiff’s leave to amend if there is prejudice to the opposing party, such as delay in trial, loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation. (Ibid.)¿California courts ordinarily will not consider the validity of a proposed amended pleading in ruling on a motion for leave to amend given that any grounds for a demurrer or a motion to strike will be premature at that time. (California Casualty General Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1985) 173 Cal.App.3d 274, 280-281 [citations omitted, overruled on other grounds.) 

A motion to amend a pleading before trial must (1) include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments; (2) state what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and (3) state what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located. (California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a).) A separate supporting declaration specifying (1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) the reason why the request for amendment was not made earlier must accompany the motion. (CRC, rule 3.1324 (b).)¿

Plaintiff seeks leave to file a second amended complaint to add new causes of action, defendants, and allegations. Plaintiff’s motion is made on the grounds that newly discovered evidence has revealed several new facts warranting the proposed additional allegations. (Mot., pp. 16:20-17:4.) The motion includes a copy of the proposed SAC and describes the additional allegations and their location within the pleading. (Sohi Decl., ¶¶ 3; Exs. 1-2.) Plaintiff has also submitted a separate declaration specifying the effect of the amendment (Sohi Decl., ¶ 4), why the additional allegations are necessary and proper (Sohi Decl., ¶¶ 5-7), when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered (Sohi Decl., ¶ 8), and why the request for amendment was not made earlier (Sohi Decl., ¶¶ 9-17.) The Court finds that Plaintiff has complied with CRC, rule 3.1324 subdivisions (a) and (b). 

Defendants have not filed any opposition to this motion, and thus there are no arguments as to why the motion should not be granted. 

Thus, the motion is GRANTED. 

RULING:
Plaintiff Ikuo Ogihara’s Motion for Leave to File a Second Amended Complaint is GRANTED. 

The amended pleading is to be filed within 10 days of this order.

Case Name: Arce v. Granville Management Group, Inc., et al.

MOTION TO COMPEL PHYSICAL EXAMINATION OF PLAINTIFF 
[CCP § 2032.240]

Moving Party: Defendants, Granville Management Group & Granville Restaurant Partners, LLC
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Luis Javier Arce

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Compel Plaintiff to appear for a physical examination. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Form Complaint 
1) General Negligence
2) Premises Liability 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
The Complaint alleges that on March 21, 2023, Luis Javier Arce (“Plaintiff”) was on the premises of defendants Granville Management Group, Inc., Granville Restaurant Partners, LLC, and I C Properties LLC, (collectively, “Defendants”) located at 12345 Ventura Blvd., North Hollywood, CA 91604. Plaintiff alleges that he was ascending the exterior steps within the subject premises when he stepped on an unknown substance causing him to slip and fall, thereby causing Plaintiff to endure severe injury and pain. 

ANALYSIS:
“In any case in which a plaintiff is seeking recovery for personal injuries, any defendant may demand one physical examination of the plaintiff, if both of the following conditions are satisfied: (1) The examination does not include any diagnostic test or procedure that is painful, protracted, or intrusive. (2) The examination is conducted at a location within 75 miles of the residence of the examinee.” (CCP, § 2032.220 subd. (a).)

Defendants bring this motion on the grounds that Plaintiff failed to appear for his physical examination on February 24, 2026. (Schrader Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. D.) Defendants concede that “the parties have reached an agreement on an alternative date for the physical examination, which is set for June 9, 2026” and assert that they “filed this motion in abundance of caution should Plaintiff fail to appear for [the] new examination date.” (Mot., p. 2:9-11.) Defendants argue that they are entitled to repayment of the $750.00 cancellation fee for Plaintiff’s failure to attend the February 24, 2026 examination as well as attorney’s fees associated with bringing this motion. (Mot., pp. 8:1-11:17.) 

In the opposition, Plaintiff asserts that he appeared for and completed the June 9, 2026 medical exam. (Brown Decl., ¶ 13, Ex. A.) Plaintiff further asserts that sanctions are not warranted because notice of the February 24, 2026 exam was sent to an incorrect email address for Plaintiff’s counsel (jacob@downtownlaw.com instead of jacob@downtownlalaw.com) and thus Plaintiff never received notice of the February 24, 2026 appointment. (Schrader Decl., ¶ 3, Exs. A-B.) The email was also sent to three paralegals, who also appear on the electronic service list for Plaintiff. (Schrader Decl., ¶ 2, Exs. A-B.) California’s e-service rules do not explicitly address whether electronic service is defective when a document is transmitted to only some, but not all, of the email addresses that a party has designated for service. (CCP, § 101.6; CRC, rule 2.251.) Generally, the target of proper e-service for a represented party is counsel, not firm staff. As a practical matter, however, notice of the appointment was successfully served on three of the four parties on the service list, including the staff member who originally offered and confirmed Plaintiff’s availability for February 24, 2026. Plaintiff’s failure to appear seems to be a matter of miscommunication by counsel, rather than a genuine lack of notice of the appointment date. 

Nonetheless, filing a motion to compel a physical examination where Plaintiff had already agreed to submit to the examination is premature. Defendants state that they “filed this motion in abundance of caution should Plaintiff fail to appear for [the] new examination date.” (Mot., p. 2:11.) As Plaintiff did in fact appear for the new examination date, the motion is DENIED as MOOT. 

The Court reminds the parties that civil discovery is intended to be a self-executing system with minimal judicial intervention. (See Volkswagenwerk Aktiengesellschaft v. Superior Ct. (1981) 122 Cal.App.3d 326, 331; Clement v. Alegre (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1277, 1291-1292.) 

Request for Sanctions 
“The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel response and compliance with a demand for a physical examination, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (CCP, § 2032.240 subd. (c).)

The Court will GRANT Defendants’ request to recover the $750.00 cancellation fee for Plaintiff’s failure to appear at the original appointment date. Because the Court has otherwise denied the motion as unnecessary and moot, the remaining request for sanctions is DENIED. 

RULING:
Defendants Granville Management Group & Granville Restaurant Partners, LLC’s Motion to Compel Physical Examination of Plaintiff is DENIED as MOOT.  

Plaintiff is ordered to remit the $750.00 cancellation fee to Defendants within 15 days of this order. Counsel for moving party is ordered to prepare an order for sanctions and submit it on eCourt by 3:00 PM today in accordance with this order.

Case Name: Chase v. Mohammad, et al.

MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE A CROSS-COMPLAINT
[CCP § 428]

Moving Party: Defendant, United Independent Taxi Cooperative, Inc. 
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Tom Chase 

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Leave for defendant United Independent Taxi Cooperative, Inc. to file a cross-complaint. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Form Complaint 
1) Motor Vehicle
2) General Negligence 

JUDICIAL NOTICE:
The Court may take judicial notice of “[t]he decisional, constitutional, and statutory law of any state of the United States and the resolutions and private acts of the Congress of the United States and of the Legislature of this state.” (Evidence Code, § 452, subd. (a).) 

Pursuant to Plaintiff’s request, the Court takes judicial notice of California Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1. 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
This is a motor vehicle personal injury action. The Complaint alleges that on June 18, 2023, Tom Chase (“Plaintiff”) was a passenger in a vehicle driving westbound on Ventura Boulevard in Studio City, CA 91604, when Daud Mohammad (“Mohammad”) who was travelling a high rate of speed, collided with Plaintiff. Plaintiff alleges that he has sustained bodily injuries as a result of the collision. 

ANALYSIS:
Under CCP section 428.50, subdivision (a), a party is entitled to “file a cross-complaint against any of the parties who filed the complaint or cross-complaint against him or her before or at the same time as the answer to the complaint or cross-complaint.” Furthermore, “[a]ny other cross-complaint may be filed at any time before the court has set a date for trial.” (CCP, § 428.50, subd. (b).) If a cross-complaint is not filed within these guidelines, it may only be filed with leave of the court, who may grant it “in the interest of justice at any time during the course of the action.” (CCP, § 428.50, subd. (c).)¿ 

A cross-complaint is compulsory when a related cause of action existed at the time of serving the defendant’s answer to the complaint.¿ (CCP § 426.30, subd. (a); see also Crocker Nat. Bank v. Emerald (1990) 221 Cal.App.3d 852, 864.)¿ A “‘[r]elated cause of action’ means a cause of action which arises out of the same transaction, occurrence, or series of transactions or occurrences as the cause of action which the plaintiff alleges in his complaint.”¿ (CCP, § 426.10, subd. (c).)¿ Leave must be granted to file a compulsory cross-complaint when the defendant is acting in good faith.¿(CCP, § 426.50.)¿¿¿ 

Defendant United Independent Taxi Cooperative, Inc. (“United”) seeks leave to file a cross-complaint against proposed cross-defendant Marlena Mont (“Mont”) for equitable indemnity, contribution, and declaratory relief. The proposed cross-complaint arises from the same June 18, 2023 collision that is the subject of Plaintiff’s Complaint. (Aleagha Decl., ¶ 7, Ex. A.) Plaintiff was a passenger in the vehicle operated by Mont when it collided with the vehicle operated by Mohammad. (Aleagha Decl., ¶ 5.) United was only recently brought into this action and did not appear until March 23, 2026. (Aleagha Decl., ¶ 4.) United asserts that “[t]hrough the course of investigation and discovery in this action, including review of dashcam footage from the vehicle operated by Defendant Daud Mohammad, video footage obtained by subpoena depicting the Los Angeles Police Department’s interaction with the parties following the incident, and the deposition testimony of Plaintiff, evidence has been developed indicating that the collision was caused, in whole or in part, by the negligent operation of the vehicle by Marlena Mont, including an unsafe turn and/or lane change across lanes of traffic, a failure to yield, and driving against the flow of traffic.” (Aleagha Decl., ¶ 6.)

Thus, the causes of action asserted in the proposed cross-complaint arise out of the same occurrences set forth in the Complaint. Moreover, there is no indication that United unreasonably delayed in seeking to file a cross-complaint. United filed the instant motion three months after appearing in this action. 

In the opposition, Plaintiff argues that the cross-complaint is barred by the statute of limitations. The deadline for presenting a claim for personal injury is two years. (CCP, § 335.1.) The collision at issue occurred on June 18, 2023. (Opp., p. 3:12.) However, “a tort defendant’s equitable indemnity action is separate and distinct from the plaintiff’s tort action. The indemnity action, unlike the plaintiff’s claim, does not accrue for statute of limitations purposes when the original accident occurs, but instead accrues at the time the tort defendant pays a judgment or settlement as to which he is entitled to indemnity. ’” (Valley Circle Estates v. VTN Consolidated, Inc. (1983) 33 Cal. 3d 604, 611, quoting People ex rel. Department of Transportation v. Superior Court (1980) 26 Cal.3d 744, 748.) Thus, the tort defendant’s statute of limitations does not begin to run “until payment of an underlying claim, payment of a judgement thereon, or payment of a settlement.” (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 285.)

Here, the underlying action is still being litigated. Accordingly, United’s cross-complaint for indemnity and contribution is timely. 

Plaintiff also argues that granting leave to file the cross-complaint would prejudice Plaintiff and that Defendants have not demonstrated a good faith justification for waiting three years after this action was initiated to seek leave to file the cross-complaint. (Opp., pp. 5:25-10:4.) These arguments are unavailing. Trial in this action is not currently set until June 28, 2027, leaving substantial time for the parties to litigate the cross-complaint without disrupting the trial date. Moreover, United did not appear in this action until March 23, 2026. United filed this motion approximately three months after appearing in the action, which is not an unreasonable amount of time to seek leave to file a cross-complaint. 

The motion is GRANTED.    

RULING:
Defendant United Independent Taxi Cooperative, Inc.’s Motion for Leave to File a Cross-Complaint is GRANTED.

The cross-complaint is to be filed within 5 days of this order.

Case No: 25NNCV04886 Trial Date:  October 25, 2027
Case Name: Jin v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc., et al.

MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION
[9 U.S.C. § 1, et seq and CCP § 1281.2]

Moving Party: Defendant, American Honda Motor Co., Inc.
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Xing Jin

RELIEF REQUESTED:
An order compelling Plaintiff to arbitrate this matter and to stay the proceedings pending completion of arbitration. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint 
1) Breach of Express Warranty Obligations Under the Song-Beverly Warranty Act
2) Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability Under the Song Beverly-Warranty Act

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
This is a lemon-law action. Xing Jin (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on November 20, 2022, Plaintiff leased a new 2023 Acura MDX AWD for which defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (“Defendant”) issued express warranties. Plaintiff alleges that at the time the vehicle was delivered to Plaintiff, the vehicle was not in a merchantable condition and did not conform to the quality guidelines reasonably expected of a motor vehicle. Plaintiff alleges that certain defects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves within the express warranty period. The Complaint alleges that notwithstanding Plaintiff’s entitlement, Defendant has failed to either promptly replace the new motor vehicle or to promptly make restitution in accordance with the Song-Beverly Act. 

ANALYSIS:
Under both the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. § 1, et seq. (the “FAA”), and Title 9 of Part III of the California Code of Civil Procedure commencing at section 1281 (known as the California Arbitration Act), arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a contract. (Winter v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943, 947.)

Section 2 of the FAA provides, in pertinent part that “[a] written provision . . . to settle by arbitration a controversy thereafter arising out of such contract . . .  shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable.”  (9 U.S.C. § 2.)  Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2 permits a party to file a motion to request that the Court order the parties to arbitrate a controversy. The Court may grant the motion if the Court determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists, “unless it determines that: (a) The right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or (b) Grounds exist for rescission of the agreement.” (CCP § 1281.2.) 

A court’s inquiry is limited to a determination of (1) whether a valid arbitration agreement exists and (2) whether the arbitration agreement covers the dispute. (9 U.S.C. § 4; Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostics Systems, Inc. (9th Cir. 2000) 207 F.3d 1126, 1130; Howsam v. Dean Witter Reynolds, Inc. (2002) 537 U.S. 79, 84; Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961; Lacayo v. Cataline Restaurant Group Inc. (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 244, 257 [Where moving party meets initial burden, “the party opposing arbitration must prove by a preponderance of the evidence any defense to the petition”].) 

Existence of Valid Arbitration Agreement
The party moving to compel arbitration must establish the existence of a written arbitration agreement between the parties. (CCP, § 1281.2.) The moving party can meet this initial burden by attaching to the petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the respondent’s signature. (Bannister v. Marinidence Opco, LLC (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 541, 541-543.) Alternatively, the moving party can meet its initial burden by setting forth the agreement’s provisions in the motion. (See Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 219; see also Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330.) 

In support of the motion, Defendant attaches a copy of the arbitration clause contained in the lease agreement for the subject vehicle executed by Plaintiff and the dealer on November 20, 2022. (Mossy Decl., Ex. A.) In relevant portion, the arbitration clause states:

“Any claim or dispute, whether in contract, tort, statute or otherwise (including the interpretation and scope of this Arbitration Provision, and the arbitrability of the claim or dispute), between you and us or our parents, subsidiaries, affiliates, employees, officers, agents, representatives, predecessors, successors or assigns (individually and collectively “us” or “our”) which arises out of or relates to your credit application, origination or servicing of this lease, the manufacture, delivery, condition, or performance of this Vehicle, any representations, omissions, or warranties, or any resulting transaction or relationship (including any such relationship with third parties who do not sign this Lease) shall, at your or our election, be resolved by neutral, binding arbitration, and not by a court action.” (Mossy Decl., Ex. A, § 26.) 

The Lease Agreement defines “we,” “us” and “our” to mean Acura of Alhambra as the lessor and Honda Lease Trust as the assignee. (Mossy Decl. ¶ 3, Ex. A, p. 1.) Honda Lease Trust is a wholly-owned subsidiary of American Honda Financial Corporation (“AHFC”).  AHFC is a wholly-owned subsidiary of Defendant. (Mossy Decl., ¶¶ 3-5.) Generally, a non-signatory parent company may compel arbitration of actions arising out of its subsidiary company’s agreement to arbitrate so long as the parent company had sufficient control over the subsidiary’s activities. (See Cohen v. TNP 2008 Participating Notes Program, LLC (2019) 31 Cal.App.5th 840, 864-865.) 

The terms of the arbitration clause are broadly stated to include the signatories’ “parents, subsidiaries, affiliates … or assigns.” (Mossy Decl., Ex. A, § 26.) Honda Lease Trust is designated as an assignee under the Lease.  Defendant is the parent company of Honda Lease Trust’s parent company and is encompassed by the Lease Agreement’s broad language regarding parents and affiliates. Defendant may compel arbitration based on the arbitration provision contained in the Lease Agreement. (See Cohen, supra, 31 Cal.App.5th at pp. 864-865.) 

Thus, the Court finds that a valid arbitration agreement exists between the parties. 

Arbitrability of Dispute 
The Court further finds that the arbitration clause covers Plaintiff’s claims here. The arbitration clause applies to “Any claim or dispute, whether in contract, tort, statute or otherwise […], between you and us or our parents, subsidiaries, affiliates, employees, officers, agents, representatives, predecessors, successors or assigns […] which arises out of or relates to […]  the manufacture, delivery, condition, or performance of this Vehicle, any representations, omissions, or warranties, or any resulting transaction or relationship (including any such relationship with third parties who do not sign this Lease).” (Mossy Decl., Ex. A, § 26.) Plaintiff’s claims here all arise out of or relate to the manufacture, condition and performance of the vehicle as well as the warranties provided by Defendant. (Compl., ¶¶ 6-15.) “[I]n determining whether a matter is subject to arbitration, courts apply the presumption in favor of arbitration [citation].... ‘ “Doubts as to whether an arbitration clause applies to a particular dispute are to be resolved in favor of sending the parties to arbitration. The court should order them to arbitrate unless it is clear that the arbitration clause cannot be interpreted to cover the dispute.” (California Correctional Peace Officers Assn. v. State of California (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 198, 205.) Plaintiff’s claims are thus arbitrable under the clause at issue. 

Thus, Defendant has met its initial burden to establish the existence of a written arbitration agreement between the parties which covers the instant dispute. The burden thus shifts to Plaintiff to establish that a defense exists to enforcement of the agreement.   

Defenses 
Under the FAA, a party may challenge the validity or applicability of the arbitration provision by raising the same defenses available to a party seeking to avoid the enforcement of any contract. (Cox v. Ocean View Hotel Corp. (2008) 553 F.3d 1114, 1121.)

Waiver
Plaintiff argues that Defendant has waived its right to compel arbitration by waiting over a year to bring this motion. “[I]n the absence of legal excuse, a party’s failure to timely demand arbitration results in a contractual forfeiture of the right to compel arbitration.” (Platt Pacific, Inc. v. Andelson (1993) 6 Cal.4th 307, 319.) 
“To establish waiver under generally applicable contract law, the party opposing enforcement of a contractual agreement must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the waiving party knew of the contractual right and intentionally relinquished or abandoned it.” (Quach v. California Commerce Club, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 562, 584.) “Under the clear and convincing evidence standard, the proponent of a fact must show that it is ‘highly probable’ the fact is true.” (Ibid, quoting Conservatorship of O.B. (2020) 9 Cal.5th 989, 995.) “The waiving party’s knowledge of the right may be ‘actual or constructive.’” (Ibid, quoting Outboard Marine Corp. v. Superior Court (1975) 52 Cal.App.3d 30, 41.) “Its intentional relinquishment or abandonment of the right may be proved by evidence of words expressing an intent to relinquish the right or of conduct that is so inconsistent with an intent to enforce the contractual right as to lead a reasonable fact finder to conclude that the party had abandoned it.” (Ibid.) “[T]here is no requirement that the party opposing enforcement of the contractual right demonstrate prejudice or otherwise show harm resulting from the waiving party’s conduct.” (Id. at p. 585.) 

To meet the first element of waiver, a plaintiff must show by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant knew of the arbitration provision. (Quach, supra,16 Cal.5th at p. 584.) Here, the arbitration provision is contained in the Lease Agreement that is attached to the Complaint. There is no dispute that the Defendant was aware of the arbitration provision from the outset of the case. 

Next, a plaintiff must show by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant intentionally relinquished or abandoned his right to compel arbitration. (See Quach, supra,16 Cal.5th at p. 584.) 

In Quach, the court held that the defendant waived its right to arbitration when “fully aware of its right to compel arbitration, [it] chose not to do so for 13 months, affirmatively indicated its intent to pursue a jury trial rather than arbitration, and actively engaged in discovery—words and conduct markedly inconsistent with an intent to arbitrate.” (Id. at p. 587.) “Although [the defendant] asserted in its answer that [the plaintiff] should be compelled to arbitrate, its counsel did not otherwise raise the issue with [plaintiff]’s counsel or with the court. (Id. at p. 586.) On [the [defendant’s] initial case management conference statement, filed about three months after [the plaintiff] filed his complaint, [the defendant] requested a jury trial, left the check box for indicating it was ‘willing to participate’ in arbitration blank, and represented that the only motion itintended to file was a ‘dispositive motion.’”(Quach, supra, 16 Cal.5th at pp. 586-587.) The trial court found that the defendant “instead of moving to compel arbitration, propounded a ‘large amount’ of written discovery, spent ‘significant’ time meeting and conferring ‘over many months,’ and took [the plaintiff]’s deposition, demonstrating ‘a position inconsistent [with the intent] to arbitrate’….” (Id. at p. 571.)  

Here, Plaintiff argues that Defendant waived its right to compel arbitration by filing an Answer, responding to Plaintiff’s written discovery, and attending a case management conference all before first raising the issue of arbitration on April 8, 2026. (Opp., p. 5:5-20.) Plaintiff asserts that the parties then attended a second case management conference and set a mediation date for April 7, 2027 before Defendants filed the instant motion on July 13, 2026. (Opp., p. 5:20-25.) The Court does not find that these actions indicate a clear intent to abandon the right to compel arbitration. Unlike in Quach, Defendant here has not propounded any written discovery, spent any significant time meeting and conferring over substantive issues in this case, taken Plaintiff’s deposition, or otherwise clearly demonstrated a position inconsistent with the intent to arbitrate. (Quach, supra, 16 Cal.5th at p. 571.) In the Song-Beverly Act Joint Case Management Conference Statement Addendum filed on April 15, 2026, Defendant indicated that it intended to compel arbitration. (4/15/2026 Song Beverly Act Joint Case Addendum.)

Accordingly, the Court concludes that Defendant’s conduct is not “so inconsistent with an intent to enforce the contractual right [to arbitration] as to lead a reasonable fact finder to conclude that the party had abandoned it.” (Quach, supra,16 Cal.5th at p. 584.) 

Defendant is a Non-Signatory
Plaintiff further argues the motion should be denied because Defendant is not a signatory to the Lease Agreement. Plaintiff relies on the holding in the Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2025) 17 Cal.5th 1122 for this argument. However, Plaintiff’s reliance on the Ford Motor Warranty Cases is misplaced. There, the Supreme Court declined to permit a manufacturer to compel arbitration under principles of estoppel where the arbitration agreement contained no language authorizing enforcement by a third party in the defendant’s position.   

This case is distinguishable from the Ford Motor Warranty Cases in that the Lease Agreement specifically extends arbitration rights to parents, subsidiaries, and affiliates. (Mossy Decl., Ex. A, § 26.) Because Defendant is the parent company of the Lease’s assignee, the reasoning in Ford Motor Warranty Cases does not apply. Defendant may thus enforce the arbitration provision. 

Thus, based on the foregoing, the motion is GRANTED. 

RULING:
Defendant, American Honda Motor Co., Inc.’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is GRANTED. 

The case is ordered STAYED pending binding arbitration as to the entire action.

DEMURRER WITH MOTION TO STRIKE; MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

Defendants, Burbank Healthcare, LLC and Longwood Management, LLC
Responding Party: Plaintiffs, Bronwen Jones and The Estate of Renata Scharf Jones
RELIEF REQUESTED:
Sustain demurrer to fifth and sixth causes of action alleged in the First Amended Complaint and strike the sixth cause of action and the claims and prayer for punitive damages. 

An order compelling Plaintiff to arbitrate the claims asserted against Burbank Healthcare LLC and Longwood Management LLC and to stay the proceedings pending completion of arbitration. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from First Amended Complaint
1) Professional Negligence 
2) Wrongful Death [CCP § 337.60]
3) Elder and Dependent Adult Abuse [Welfare & Institutions Code § 15600 et seq.]
4) Negligence
5) Fraudulent Concealment
6) Interference with Right of Sepulcher

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE
The Court may take judicial notice of “[r]ecords of (1) any court of this state or (2) any court of record of the United States or of any state of the United States.” (Evidence Code, § 452, subd. (d).) 

The court, however, may not take judicial notice of the truth of the contents of the documents. (Herrera v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1366, 1375.) Documents are only judicially noticeable to show their existence and what orders were made such that the truth of the facts and findings within the documents are not judicially noticeable. (Lockley v. Law Office of Cantrell, Green, Pekich, Cruz & McCort (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 875, 885.)  
Pursuant to Valley of Peace Cremation and Burial Services, Inc.’s request, the Court takes judicial notice of the following: (1) This Court’s May 8, 2026, Minute Order ; and (2) The records within Los Angeles Superior Court case no. 16STPB02409, filed in the probate court on July 18, 2016. 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
The First Amended Complaint (the “FAC”) alleges that Renata Scharf Jones (the “Decedent”) was admitted to defendant Burbank Healthcare LLC (“Burbank Healthcare”) in January 2016 for delirium caused by untreated urinary tract infections. Bronwen Jones (“Plaintiff”) is Decedent’s daughter. The FAC alleges that once admitted, Defendants and their staff, including defendant Dr. Michael Marsh (“Dr. Marsh”) repeatedly used physical restraints, failed to provide required exercises and repositioning, and left Decedent in the same position and soiled diapers for extended periods. The FAC alleges that Burbank Healthcare and defendant Longwood Management, LLC (“Longwood”) failed to provide sufficient staffing levels to ensure basic care needs. The FAC alleges that these ongoing failures substantially contributed to the worsening of Decedent’s condition, including progression from mild to severe global aphasia, bilateral knee contractures, recurrent urinary tract infections, severe pressure ulcers, sepsis, and a stroke in May 2020. Decedent passed away at Burbank Healthcare on February 27, 2025. Plaintiff alleges that no one informed her of her mother’s death and that that the body was transferred to Valley of Peace Cremation and Burial Services, Inc. without family knowledge or consent. Plaintiff alleges that Dr. Marsh, as the physician in charge at the time of death, failed to direct that the body be embalmed or preserved for the autopsy that the family had requested. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants have refused to allow Plaintiff to view her mother’s remains and combined with their failure to embalm the body for an autopsy, Plaintiff has been completely deprived of any meaningful closure or ability to grieve her mother’s death. Plaintiff further alleges that she has been unable to obtain a death certificate for Decedent as the County of Los Angeles Department of Health Services Office has no death certificate on file for Decedent.  

DEMURRER:
A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; CCP, § 430.10, subd. (e).) 

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) “[E]ach evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.) 

In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-67.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) A demurrer, however, “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)  

Fifth Cause of Action, Fraudulent Concealment
The elements of fraudulent concealment are (1) concealment or suppression of a material fact; (2) by a defendant with a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff; (3) intent to defraud the plaintiff by intentionally concealing or suppressing the fact; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he or she did if he or she had known of the concealed or suppressed fact; and (5) the plaintiff sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of fact. (Hambridge v. Healthcare Partners Medical Group, Inc. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 124, 162.) 

In California, fraud must be pled with specificity. (Small v. Fritz Companies, Inc. (2003) 30 Cal.4th 167, 184.) “The particularity demands that a plaintiff plead facts which show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.” (Cansinov. Bank of America (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1462, 1469.) Fraud allegations need not be liberally construed, general pleading of the legal conclusion of fraud is insufficient, and every element of the cause of action for fraud must be alleged fully, factually and specifically. (Wilhelm v. Pray, Price, Williams & Russell (1986) 186 Cal.App.3d 1324, 1331; see also Quelimane Co., Inc. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 47.)

Defendant Valley of Peace Cremation and Burial Services, Inc. (“Valley of Peace”) demurs to the fifth cause of action on the basis that Plaintiff fails to establish all the elements of a fraudulent concealment cause of action. 

The Court previously sustained Valley of Peace’s demurrer to the Complaint on the grounds that Plaintiff did not sufficiently allege what representation was made to Plaintiff which was contrary to the alleged concealment nor did Plaintiff allege the names of the persons who made the allegedly fraudulent representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written. (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn., Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384; Tenet Healthsystem Desert, Inc. v. Blue Cross of California (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 821, 838.) 

The FAC sufficiently cures these defects. The FAC alleges that “[t]hrough an individual identified as “Don” and other staff, [Valley of Peace] provided misleading information, moved the body without proper notification, refused to allow Plaintiff to view her mother’s remains, stored the body unembalmed in improper conditions (preventing an autopsy), demanded storage fees, and refused to produce records in response to Plaintiff’s May 13, 2026 demand, asserting technical and procedural objections while continuing to withhold information.” (FAC, ¶ 29.) The FAC alleges that “[t]hese actions prevented Plaintiff from grieving, viewing her mother, obtaining an autopsy, and fulfilling her mother’s wishes for cremation with ashes placed at the San Francisco War Memorial Opera House.” (FAC, ¶ 29.) The FAC further alleges that on August 20, 2025, “‘Don’ left a voicemail for Plaintiff from a phone number that, on information and belief, belongs to a lawyer’s office in Texas.” (FAC, ¶ 29.)

The FAC sufficiently alleges that Valley of Peace concealed or suppressed a material fact regarding, among other things, the location of the Decedent’s remains (Compl., ¶ 61) and that Valley of Peace had a duty to inform Plaintiff of the location of the remains and dispose of the remains in accordance with Plaintiff’s wishes. (See Christensen v. Superior Court (1991) 54 Cal.3d 868, 882.) The FAC alleges that Valley of Peace intended to defraud Plaintiff (Compl., ¶ 62), that Plaintiff was unaware of the true conditions and location of her mother’s remains (Compl., ¶ 62), and that Plaintiff would have acted differently had she known of the concealed or suppressed fact. (Compl., ¶¶ 62-63.) Finally, Plaintiff alleges that she sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of fact. (Compl., ¶ 64) Thus, for pleading purposes, Plaintiff has sufficiently stated a fraudulent concealment cause of action.

The demurrer to the fifth cause of action is OVERRULED. 

Sixth Cause of Action, Interference with Right of Sepulcher
“The right to control the disposition of the remains of a deceased person, the location and conditions of interment, and arrangements for funeral goods and services to be provided […] vests in, and the duty of disposition and the liability for the reasonable cost of disposition of the remains devolves upon […] The sole surviving competent adult child of the decedent [or] The surviving competent adult person or persons respectively in the next degrees of kinship.” (Health & Safety Code § 7100, subd. (a).) 

Valley of Peace demurs to the sixth cause of action on the ground that the FAC fails to allege that Plaintiff “is, in fact, the successor-in-interest to Renata Scharf Jones, such as that she is the sole surviving competent adult child or that all surviving, competent adult children agree on the disposition of the remains.” (Demurrer, p.p. 14:27-15:1.) The FAC alleges that Plaintiff is Decedent’s “next of kin and sole competent adult child.” (FAC, ¶ 66.) For pleading purposes, nothing more is required. 

The demurrer to the sixth cause of action is OVERRULED. 

MOTION TO STRIKE:
The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (a) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (b) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (CCP § 436, subds. (a), (b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].)

Valley of Peace moves to strike the sixth cause of action on the basis that Plaintiff amended their Complaint to add the sixth cause of action without leave of the Court to do so and the amendment is beyond the scope of the Court’s order granting leave to amend. In the opposition, Plaintiff argues that the right of sepulcher was already a part of the case as the original Complaint had alleged “loss of the right of sepulcher” as an element of damages. (Opp., p. 11:22-25.) 

The Court finds that adding the sepulcher cause of action was beyond the scope of the order granting leave to amend. Sustaining the demurrer to the prior Complaint with leave to amend granted Plaintiff leave to amend only the causes of action in the prior Complaint, not leave to add an entirely new cause of action. (Patrick v. Alacer Corp. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 995, 1015.) “Following an order sustaining a demurrer or a motion for judgment on the pleadings with leave to amend, the plaintiff may amend his or her complaint only as authorized by the court's order. The plaintiff may not amend the complaint to add a new cause of action without having obtained permission to do so, unless the new cause of action is within the scope of the order granting leave to amend.” (Zakk v. Diesel (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 431, 456 [quoting Harris v. Wachovia Mortgage, FSB (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1018, 1023].)

The Court previously sustained the demurrer on the ground that Plaintiff had not pleaded the fraud claim with the requisite specificity. (5/8/2026 Minute Order.) Plaintiff’s new cause of action for interference with the right of sepulcher does not respond to the Court’s reasons for sustaining the earlier demurrer. (Compare Patrick v. Alacer Corp., supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at 1015 [after demurer sustained for plaintiff's apparent lack of standing on shareholder claims, complaint amended to add claim for declaratory relief to establish plaintiff's community property interest in shareholdings].) 

Thus, the motion to strike is GRANTED as to the sixth cause of action for interference with the right of sepulcher. 

Valley of Peace also moves to strike the claims and prayer for punitive damages on the basis that the FAC fails to plead facts to show that Valley of Peace acted with fraud, malice and oppression. 

Punitive damages may be recovered upon a proper showing of malice, fraud, or oppression. (Civil Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) “Malice” is defined as conduct intended to cause injury to a person or despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard for the rights or safety of others. (Turman v. Turning Point of Cent. Cal., Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) “Oppression” means despicable conduct subjecting a person to cruel and unjust hardship, in conscious disregard of the person’s rights. (Id.) “Fraud” is an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known by defendant, with intent to deprive a person of property, rights or otherwise cause injury. (Id.) Conclusory allegations, devoid of any factual assertions, are insufficient to support a conclusion that parties acted with oppression, fraud or malice. (Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1042.)

The FAC alleges that Valley of Peace moved the Decedent’s body to a remote industrial park without notifying Plaintiff, refused to allow Plaintiff to view her mother’s remains or to pay for cremation or related services, provided misleading and inconsistent information about the location and condition of the body, and stored the body in improper conditions that prevented an autopsy. (FAC, ¶ 61.) If these allegations are proven to be true, a reasonable trier of fact could conclude that Valley of Peace acted with malice, oppression or fraud within the meaning of Civil Code section 3294. Thus, the Court declines to strike the requests for punitive damages.

MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION
Defendants Burbank Healthcare, LLC and Longwood Management, LLC’s motion to compel arbitration previously came up for hearing on July 24, 2026. The Court continued the motion to allow Plaintiff to retain a handwriting expert and file supplemental briefing regarding the authenticity of the Decedent’s signature on the arbitration agreement.  

Plaintiff filed supplemental briefing on August 28, 2026. Burbank Healthcare and Longwood Management request a continuance to independently evaluate the new evidence submitted with Plaintiff’s supplemental briefing. Moving Defendants notes that “[a]t the July 24, 2026, hearing on Defendants’ Motion to Compel Arbitration, the parties and the Court discussed the potential need for forensic examination of the signatures appearing on the Admission Agreement and Resident-Facility Arbitration Agreement. It was contemplated and agreed that, following Plaintiff’s submission of their forensic expert’s analysis, Defendants would have an opportunity to retain their own expert, conduct an independent examination, and respond to Plaintiff’s supplemental submission.” (Resp. to Supp. Brief, p. 2:11-16.) 

Burbank Healthcare and Longwood Management assert that “after receipt of the Supplemental Brief and supporting declaration, Defendants retained Forensic Document Examiner, Linton A. Mohammed, Ph.D., D-ABFDE, to independently evaluate the questioned signatures and Plaintiff’s expert analysis.” (Resp. to Supp. Brief, p. 2:18-21.) 

The Court finds that Burbank Healthcare and Longwood Management’s request for a brief continuance so that their retained expert can evaluate the signatures is reasonable. Thus, the request for a continuance is GRANTED. Discovery is STAYED pending a determination on the motion to compel arbitration. 

RULING:
Defendant Valley of Peace Cremation and Burial Services, Inc.’s Demurrer is OVERRULED. The Motion to Strike is GRANTED as to the sixth cause of action and DENIED as to the claims and prayer for punitive damages. 

Defendants Burbank Healthcare, LLC and Longwood Management, LLC’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is CONTINUED to October 23, 2026 at 8:30 am. Defendants’ supplemental reply is due by October 12, 2026.

Case Name: Reiten v. FCA US LLC, et al.

MOTION TO ENFORCE CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE § 871.26 AND REQUEST FOR SANCTIONS
[CCP § 871.26 subd. (b)]

Moving Party: Plaintiff, Alexander Reiten  
Responding Party: Defendant, FCA US LLC

RELIEF REQUESTED:
An order for monetary sanctions in the amount of $2,500.00 for Defendant’s failure to comply with CCP § 871.26 subdivision (b) and an order compelling Defendant to produce its warranty policy and procedure manuals. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint 
1) Breach of Express Warranty Obligations
2) Breach of Implied Warranty Obligations
3) Failure to Repair in a Good and Workmanlike Manner
4) Failure to Repair Within Thirty Days

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
This is a lemon-law action. Alexander Reiten (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on July 11, 2025, Plaintiff leased a 2025 Jeep Wagoneer for which FCA US LLC (“Defendant”) issued express warranties. Plaintiff alleges that defects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves within the express warranty period. The Complaint alleges that notwithstanding Plaintiff’s entitlement, Defendant has failed to either promptly replace the new motor vehicle or to promptly make restitution in accordance with the Song-Beverly Act. 

ANALYSIS:
“Within 60 days after the filing of the answer or other responsive pleading, all parties shall, without awaiting a discovery request, provide to all other parties an initial disclosure and documents pursuant to subdivisions (f), (g), and (h).” (CCP § 871.26 subd. (b).) The required initial documents include a defendant’s warranty policies and procedure manuals. (CCP § 871.26 subd. (h)(12).)

“Unless the party failing to comply with [section 871.26] shows good cause, [...] a court shall impose sanctions as follows: […] A one-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($1,500) sanction against the plaintiff's attorney or two-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($2,500) sanction against the defense attorney respectively, paid within 15 business days for failure to comply with the provisions relating to depositions as prescribed in subdivision (c).” (CCP, § 871.26, subd. (j)(2).) 

Defendant served its Answer to the Complaint on February 10, 2026. Accordingly, Defendant’s initial disclosures and documents were due by April 10, 2026. (CCP § 871.26 subd. (b).) Plaintiff asserts that on April 9, 2026, Defendant produced some documents and included a protective order for Plaintiff to sign concerning the remaining documents, including Defendant’s policy and procedure manuals. (Sanchez Decl., ¶¶ 4-5, Exs. A-B.) Plaintiff asserts that, despite executing the protective order, Defendant had failed to produce the additional documents as of the filing of the instant motion. (Sanchez Decl., ¶ 7.) 

In the opposition, Defendant asserts that the motion is unnecessary as it produced the remaining documents on May 13, 2026 pursuant to the protective order, and Plaintiff has not demonstrated any irreparable harm or prejudice caused by the delay. (Opp., pp. 2:13-4:22.) 

The stipulation and protective order was signed by Plaintiff on April 22, 2026, and signed by Defendant and filed with the Court on May 13, 2026. (5/13/2026 Stipulation and Protective Order.) Defendant asserts that the delay in producing the remaining documents was the result of Defendant’s counsel’s mistake and inadvertence due to counsel’s high volume of cases and discovery filings. (Opp., p. 2:2.) 

While the documents have since been provided, CCP section 871.26 requires sanctions when a party fails to provide initial disclosures within 60 days of the filing of the answer, irrespective of any subsequent production. (CCP § 871.26 subd. (b).) Thus, while the motion is MOOT to the extent Plaintiff requests an order compelling Defendant to produce its warranty policy and procedure manuals, the request for sanctions is GRANTED.  

RULING:
Plaintiff Alexander Reiten’s Motion to Enforce Code of Civil Procedure Section 871.26 is GRANTED. Defendant’s counsel is ordered to remit sanctions in the amount $2,500.00 to Plaintiff within 15 business days of this order. 

Counsel for moving party is ordered to prepare an order for sanctions and submit it on eCourt by 3:00 PM today in accordance with this order.

Date: September 11, 2026
Case No: 25NNCV09324 Trial Date:  Not yet set
Case Name: Pena v. CREH Pasadena LLC, et al.

MOTION TO STRIKE
[CCP § 436]

Moving Party: Defendant, CREH Pasadena, LLC
Responding Party: Plaintiffs, Francia Pena, Paola Contento, and Mayra Pena

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Strike the references to and requests for punitive damages. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint
1) Breach of Implied Warranty of Habitability
2) Tortious Breach of Implied Warranty of Habitability
3) Negligence
4) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress [dismissed on May 7, 2026] 
5) Private Nuisance
6) Violation of Business and Professions Code Section 17200 et seq.
7) Retaliation – Civil Code Section 1942.5

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
The Complaint alleges that from December 1, 2016 to June 21, 2025, Francia Pena, Paola Contento, and Mayra Pena (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) were occupants and tenants of real property located at 3335 Trevan Rd, Pasadena, CA 91107 (hereinafter, “Subject Property”) owned by CREH Pasadena, LLC (“Defendant”). Plaintiffs allege that throughout their tenancy they experienced widespread water leaks and chronic mold, roof leaks, defective plumbing, a defective water heater, defective electrical, rodent infestations, and various other physical defects. Plaintiffs allege that despite repeatedly informing Defendant of the aforementioned issues, Defendant failed to, and refused to, take any corrective actions. The Complaint alleges that on October 21, 2024, Defendant informed Plaintiffs that the lease would expire as of November 30, 2024, and Plaintiffs were to move out of the Subject Property. The City of Pasadena Code Enforcement subsequently inspected the Subject Property and cited Defendant with several code violations. As a result of the citations, Defendant relocated Plaintiffs to a hotel. Plaintiffs allege that were told that the relocation would last four to five days and that they would receive $74.00 per diem for food and incidental expenses per person. However, Defendants failed to timely complete the remediation work, causing Plaintiffs to remain in the hotel for nearly seven months. Plaintiffs further allege that Defendants never reimbursed Plaintiffs the $74.00 per person per day. 

ANALYSIS:
The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (a) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (b) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (CCP, § 436, subds. (a), (b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].)

“In an action for the breach of an obligation not arising from contract, where it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice, the plaintiff, in addition to the actual damages, may recover damages for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant.” (Civil Code, § 3294, subd. (a); College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 725.) Claims for punitive damages are disfavored in California. (Las Palmas Assocs. v. Las Palmas Center Assocs. (1991) 235 Cal.App.3d 1220, 1258).  

A motion to strike is properly granted when a complaint fails to allege facts to state a prima facie claim for punitive damages under the standards of the statute. (Turman v. Turning Point of Cent. California, Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) Alleging an intentional tort alone is not enough to support a claim for punitive damages, nor are conclusory allegations that merely parrot the language of the statute. (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166; Blegen v. Superior Court (1981) 125 Cal.App.3d 959, 963.) 

Defendant moves to strike the following portions of the Complaint: 
1) Page 11, Second Cause of Action for Tortious Breach of Warranty of Habitability, Paragraph 46, lines 11– 18;
2) Page 12, Third Cause of Action for Negligence, Paragraph 53, lines 17– 24;
3) Page 14, 15, Fifth Cause of Action for Private Nuisance, Paragraph 66, lines 23– 28; 1 – 3;
4) Page 16, Seventh Cause of Action for Tortious Breach of Warranty of Habitability, Paragraph 139, lines 23-27;
5) Prayer, Page 18, number 4, line 2;
6) Prayer, Page 18, number 7, line 15; and
7) Prayer, Page 19, number 17, line 5.

Defendant argues that Plaintiffs’ conclusory allegations are insufficient to support a claim for punitive damages. Defendant argues that Plaintiffs’ habitability allegations amount merely to claims that Defendant failed to address the habitability issues quicker, while Plaintiffs’ retaliation claims are based solely on the allegation that Defendant asked Plaintiffs to move out after the lease term ended. (Mot., p. 8:6-22.) Defendant argues that these allegations, even if true, do not amount to reckless, willful, oppressive, or malicious conduct so as to support an award of punitive damages. (Mot., pp. 8:23-9:8.) 

In the opposition, Plaintiffs, quoting the standard from Perkins v. Superior Court (1981) 117 Cal.App.3d 1, 6, argue that the Complaint “‘describe[s] a knowing and deliberate state of mind from which a conscious disregard of [Plaintiffs’] rights might be inferred.’” (Opp., p. 2:16-18.) 

California courts repeatedly have recognized that punitive damages may be recovered in tort actions arising from habitability violations. Plaintiffs reference Stoiber v. Honeychuck (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 903, in which the Court of Appeal found the trial court had abused its discretion in granting a motion for judgment on the pleadings with respect to a tenant’s claim for punitive damages against her landlords. (Id., at 920.) There, the Court of Appeal stated that a plaintiff may recover exemplary damages “when the landlord’s conduct justifies the award.”¿(Id. at 926.)

The tenant in Stoiber alleged numerous defective and dangerous conditions including, but not limited to, “leaking of sewage from the bathroom plumbing; defective and dangerous electrical wiring; structural weaknesses in the walls; deteriorated flooring; falling ceiling; leaking roof; dilapidated doors; broken windows; and other unsafe and dangerous conditions.” (Stoiber, supra, 101 Cal.App.3d at p. 912.) Also attached to the complaint in Stoiber was a copy of the health department’s notice to the landlord to vacate and demolish the premises which listed the following violations, among others: “heavy cockroach infestation, broken interior walls, broken deteriorated flooring on front porch, falling ceiling, deteriorated, overfused electrical wiring, lack of proper plumbing connection to sewage system in bathroom, sewage under bathroom floor, leaking roof, broken windows, and fire hazard.” (Ibid.) The tenant in Stoiber alleged that “she had repeatedly notified both Irene Honeychuck and Ron Perkins (an employee of Frank P. Smith Realty and Management) of defective conditions, and that no effort was made to repair the defects.” (Id. at p. 913.) This presentation was sufficient to allege that the landlord had actual and constructive knowledge of the defective conditions and failed to correct them. 

Here, the Complaint alleges that, beginning in 2018, Plaintiffs informed Defendant of mold inside the property, gopher infestations, a skunk living underneath the house, and electrical issues that caused appliances to fail, yet Defendant failed to address these conditions. (Compl., ¶¶ 11-12, 19-20.) Plaintiffs further allege that they informed Defendant that the freezer was not working, but Defendants refused to replace it, leaving Plaintiffs without a working freezer for over two years. Beginning in 2020, Plaintiffs allegedly notified Defendant of several water leaks through the roof. (Compl., ¶ 13.) In June 2021, a roofing company purportedly determined that the roof was beyond repair and needed to be replaced, but Defendants allegedly refused to replace it. (Compl., ¶ 14.) In March 2022, Plaintiffs assert that they again complained of roof leaks, yet Defendants did not send a roofing company to inspect the roof until October 2024. (Compl., ¶ 18.) The Complaint alleges that Defendants still failed to repair the roof, and the leaks continued throughout Plaintiffs’ tenancy. (Compl., ¶ 18.) Lastly, the Complaint alleges that, in November 2024, the City of Pasadena “inspected the Subject Property and cited Defendants with several code violations, including but not limited to water damage and mold.” (Compl., ¶ 22.)

The Court finds that, if proven true, the foregoing allegations would be sufficient to support an award of punitive damages. The Complaint adequately alleges that the Defendant had actual knowledge of the defective conditions yet failed to take reasonable steps to correct them. These allegations are materially similar to those in Stoiber, in which the Court of Appeal found comparable allegations were sufficient to support a claim for punitive damages against a plaintiff’s landlords. 

Thus, the motion to strike is DENIED.

RULING:
Defendant CREH Pasadena, LLC’s Motion to Strike is DENIED.

Case Name: Montgomery v. Blackburn, et al.

MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE A SAC
[CCP § 473 subd. (a)(1)]

Moving Party: Plaintiff, John Paul Montgomery  
Responding Party: Defendant, Joey Blackburn [Unopposed] 

RELIEF REQUESTED:
An order granting leave to file a Second Amended Complaint 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from First Amended Complaint 
1) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress
2) Civil Conspiracy
3) Cyberstalking

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
John Paul Montgomery (“Plaintiff”) alleges that beginning in July 2024, he had been the target of a coordinated harassment campaign orchestrated by Leon Apel (“Apel”), a business colleague of Plaintiff. The FAC alleges that on August 2, 2024, Plaintiff’s acquaintance from college and former roommate, Joey Blackburn (“Defendant”) sent Plaintiff a series of unusual text messages and documents containing Plaintiff’s personal information. Plaintiff alleges that Apel recruited Defendant to contact Plaintiff. 

ANALYSIS:
Procedural
Proof of electronic service shall include all of the following: (1) The electronic service address and the residence or business address of the person making the electronic service; (2) the date of electronic service; (3) the name and electronic service address of the person served; and (4) a statement that the document was served electronically. (CCP, § 1013b subd. (b)(4).) 

In the motion, Plaintiff states that “Plaintiff will serve these moving papers, together with the separately lodged [Proposed] Order, on Defendant Joey Blackburn through his attorney of record, James McKiernan, by electronic service to jmckiernan@mckiernanlaw.com and crowe@mckiernanlaw.com, through the Court’s electronic filing service provider, and will rely on the Proof of Electronic Service generated by that provider under Code of Civil Procedure sections 1010.6 and 1013b.” (Mot., p. 11:15-19.) 

Plaintiff’s assertion that he will serve the moving papers is not sufficient evidence that he did in fact serve the motion in the manner suggested. Plaintiff did not file proof of electronic service. If Plaintiff does not file proof of service prior to the hearing on this motion, the motion will be taken off calendar.  

Substantive
The court may, in its discretion and after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars. (CCP, § 473, subd. (a)(1); Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Association (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) 

As judicial policy favors resolution of all disputed matters between the parties, leave to amend is generally liberally granted. (See Kolani v. Gluska (1998) 64 Cal.App.4th 402, 412.) The court may deny the plaintiff’s leave to amend if there is prejudice to the opposing party, such as delay in trial, loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation. (Ibid.)¿California courts ordinarily will not consider the validity of a proposed amended pleading in ruling on a motion for leave to amend given that any grounds for a demurrer or a motion to strike will be premature at that time. (California Casualty General Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1985) 173 Cal.App.3d 274, 280-281 [citations omitted, overruled on other grounds.) 

A motion to amend a pleading before trial must (1) include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments; (2) state what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and (3) state what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located. (California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a).) A separate supporting declaration specifying (1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) the reason why the request for amendment was not made earlier must accompany the motion. (CRC, rule 3.1324 (b).)¿

(Montgomery Decl., ¶ 3.) The motion includes a copy of the proposed SAC and describes the additional allegations and their location within the pleading. (Mot., p. 6:9-21; Ex. 1.) 

Plaintiff has also submitted a separate declaration specifying the effect of the amendment (Montgomery Decl., ¶ 3), why the additional allegations are necessary and proper (Montgomery Decl., ¶ 4), when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered (Montgomery Decl., ¶ 5), and why the request for amendment was not made earlier (Montgomery Decl., ¶ 6.) Case Name: Fayad v. Sage Glendale II LLC, et al.

DEMURRER WITH MOTION TO STRIKE; MOTION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER; MOTION TO COMPEL DEPOSITION

Moving Party: Defendants, Sage Glendale II LLC, Agemark Management LLC, and Angela Smith [Demurrer]
Defendant, Agemark Management LLC [Motion for Protective Order]
Plaintiff, Emadia Fayad [Motion to Compel Deposition]
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Emadia Fayad [Demurrer and Motion for Protective Order]
Defendants, Sage Glendale II LLC, Agemark Management LLC, and Angela Smith [Motion to Compel Deposition]

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Sustain demurrer to the first cause of action for elder abuse alleged in the Complaint and the prayer for punitive and exemplary damage. 

Grant a protective order regarding the noticed depositions of corporate witnesses Martin Hug, Forrest Westin, and Michael Pittore.

Compel the deposition of Martin Hug. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint
1) Elder Abuse [Pursuant to the Elder Adult and Dependent Adult Civil Protection Act – Welfare and Institutions Code §§15600, et seq.]
2) Negligence 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
The Complaint alleges that on May 30, 2024, Emadia Fayad (“Plaintiff”) was admitted to Defendants’ residential care facility for the elderly suffering from dementia. Plaintiff alleges that during the time she was a resident at the facility, she suffered multiple severe witnessed and unwitnessed and avoidable falls. As a result of the multiple falls, Plaintiff suffered from significant injuries, including a fractured hip which required surgical repair. The Complaint alleges that upon Plaintiff’s admission to the facility, Defendants were aware that Plaintiff suffered from dementia, was at risk of falls, and required special care and assistance including 24-hour supervision and monitoring, assistance with ambulation and transferring, and the provision of safety and assistance devices to prevent accidents. The Complaint alleges that notwithstanding this knowledge, Defendants knowingly disregarded this risk and failed to adequately care for, supervise or monitor Plaintiff. 

DEMURRER:
A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; CCP, § 430.10, subd. (e).) 

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) “[E]ach evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.) 

In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-67.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) A demurrer, however, “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)  

First Cause of Action, Elder Abuse 
To state a cause of action for elder abuse and neglect, Plaintiff must allege “[p]hysical abuse, neglect, abandonment, isolation, abduction, or other treatment with resulting physical harm or pain or mental suffering.” (Welf. & Inst. Code, § 15610.07, subd. (a)(1).) Acts which are prohibited under the Elder Abuse Act provisions of the Welfare and Institute Code “do not include acts of simple professional negligence, but refer to forms of abuse or neglect performed with some state of culpability greater than mere negligence.” (Covenant Care, Inc. v. Superior Court (2004) 32 Cal.4th 771, 781.) 

To constitute abuse and neglect within the meaning of the Elder Abuse Act, thereby triggering the enhanced remedies available under the Act, Plaintiff “must demonstrate by clear and convincing evidence that defendant is guilty of something more than negligence; he or she must show reckless, oppressive, fraudulent, or malicious conduct,” with recklessness referring “to a subjective state of culpability greater than simple negligence, which has been described as a “deliberate disregard” of the “high degree of probability” that an injury will occur,” and “[o]ppression, fraud and malice [involving] intentional or conscious wrongdoing of a despicable or injurious nature.” (Sababin v. Superior Court (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 81, 88-89, quotation marks omitted.)

Defendants demur to the first cause of action for elder abuse on the ground that the Complaint fails to state sufficient facts as to how Defendants committed any act that would constitute elder abuse. (Demurrer, p. 5:14-19.) Defendants further argue that the Complaint fails to allege any specific conduct that would support an elder abuse cause of action against defendant Angela Smith (“Smith”).   

The Complaint alleges that Defendants knew that Plaintiff “suffered from Dementia, was at risk of falls, and therefore required special care and assistance including 24-hour supervision and monitoring, assistance and monitoring with ambulation and transferring, the provision of safety and assistance devices to prevent accidents, assistance and monitoring with [Plaintiff’s] other activities of daily living, and the implementation of interventions to prevent further falls.” (Compl., ¶ 20.) The Complaint alleges that Defendants “failed to adequately assess, generate and implement an adequate plan of care for [Plaintiff] and to implement adequate preventive measures for falling.” (Compl., ¶ 20.) The Complaint alleges that Defendants knew that if they failed to provide Plaintiff “with the aforementioned care, supervision, and monitoring, there was a high probability that [Plaintiff] would suffer injury.” (Compl., ¶ 21.) The Complaint alleges that Defendants were aware, upon Plaintiff’s admission to the facility, that she “required a higher level of care and care interventions to prevent injury” than the facility could provide. (Compl., ¶ 25.) The Complaint further alleges that Plaintiff suffered multiple witnessed and unwitnessed falls throughout her residency. (Compl., ¶ 26.) On April 26, 2026, the Complaint alleges that Plaintiff suffered fall resulting in her being transferred to Glendale Memorial Hospital where she was revealed to have suffered from a fractured hip. (Compl., ¶ 27.) 

As to Smith, the Complaint alleges that Smith, as the administrator of the facility, did not properly carry out her duties to administer and manage the facility or to “ensure the provision of services to the residents with appropriate regard for the residents' physical and mental well-being and needs.” (Compl., ¶¶ 44-47.) The Complaint alleges that Smith, and other named corporate officers and directors, ratified the conduct and were aware of the understaffing and the “sub-standard provision of care.” (Compl., ¶ 5.) The Complaint further alleges that Smith and the other officers and directors “were focused on unlawfully increasing the earnings in the operation of DEFENDANTS’ businesses as opposed to providing the legally mandated minimum care to be provided to elderly and/or infirm residents.” (Compl., ¶ 9.) 

Here, Plaintiff sufficiently has stated an elder abuse cause of action. Plaintiff has identified ways in which the facility defendants allegedly neglected Plaintiff’s care. (Compl., ¶¶ 20-27.) Plaintiff alleges that the facility knew Plaintiff required special care, and that, without said care, Plaintiff faced a high probability of injury. (Compl., ¶¶ 20-25.) Despite this knowledge, Plaintiff alleges that facility staff failed to adequately supervise and care for Plaintiff. (Compl., ¶¶ 20-27.) Plaintiff has also alleged that Smith, and other directors, managing agents and officers, ratified the misconduct, and knowingly underfunded and understaffed the facility in order to increase earnings. (Compl., ¶ 9.) Whether or not Defendants’ conduct amounts to recklessness is a factual inquiry. However, Plaintiff has stated facts that meet the pleading standard for elder abuse. Thus, Plaintiff has sufficiently stated a cause of action for elder abuse. 

The demurrer is OVERRULED. 

MOTION TO STRIKE
The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (a) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (b) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (CCP, § 436, subds. (a), (b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].)

Punitive damages may be recovered upon a proper showing of malice, fraud, or oppression. (Civil Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) “Malice” is defined as conduct intended to cause injury to a person or despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard for the rights or safety of others. (Turman v. Turning Point of Cent. Cal., Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) “Oppression” means despicable conduct subjecting a person to cruel and unjust hardship, in conscious disregard of the person’s rights. (Id.) “Fraud” is an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known by defendant, with intent to deprive a person of property, rights or otherwise cause injury. (Id.) Conclusory allegations, devoid of any factual assertions, are insufficient to support a conclusion that parties acted with oppression, fraud or malice. (Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1042.)

Defendants move to strike the requests for punitive damages on the ground that Plaintiff has failed to sufficiently allege a cause of action for elder abuse. As discussed above, the Court has found that the cause of action is adequately pled. Thus, the motion to strike is DENIED. 

MOTION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER
“The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other person from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense.” (CCP, § 2031.060 subd. (b).) The protective order may include that “all or some of the items or categories of items in the demand need not be produced or made available at all.” (CCP, § 2031.060 subd. (b)(1).)

With respect to protective orders generally, the burden of showing good cause is ordinarily on the party seeking the protective order. (Beverly Hills Nat. Bank & Trust Co. v. Superior Court (1961, 2nd Dist.) 195 Cal.App.2d 861, 866-867.) The granting of a protective order is within the discretion of the trial court. (Id.) The granting or denial of a protective order is reviewed for abuse of discretion. (See Meritplan Insurance Co. v. Superior Court (1981) 124 Cal.App.3d 237, 242.) When the record shows facts on which the trial court exercised its discretion, this exercise will not be disturbed on appeal. (Foster v. Gillette Co. (1979) 100 Cal.App.3d 569, 578.) 

Defendant seeks an order staying the depositions of Martin Hug, Forrest Westin, and Michael Pittore pending completion of the deposition of Defendants’ persons most qualified; requiring Plaintiff to identify with particularity any topics uniquely within each witness’s personal knowledge before any further individual depositions proceed; limiting any such depositions to nonduplicative matters within the witness’s personal knowledge; and narrowing the accompanying document requests to documents uniquely possessed by the individual witness and not otherwise available through entity discovery.

Defendants argue that Plaintiff seeks the same categories of information from Hug, Westin and Pittore that she has already sought through the depositions of Defendants’ two PMK witnesses. (Mot., p. 3:15-19.) Defendants argue that Hug, Pittore, and Westin do not have personal knowledge about Plaintiff, her residency at Sage Glendale II, or the facts concerning her case. (Seigler Decl., ¶ 14.)

In the opposition, Plaintiff assert that during the deposition of Sage Glendale’s deposition corporate representative, she testified that she communicates with Westin and Pittore about facility census, staffing, interactions with the Department of Social Services, and facility compliance with applicable rules, laws and regulations governing the operations of a residential care facility for the elderly in California. (Opp., pp. 3:13-4:22, Ex. 10.) Westin and Pittore are the current managing agents of the facility. (Opp., pp. 6:4-8:4.) 

The Court finds that Defendants have not shown good cause warranting a protective order. Plaintiff does not seek to depose Hug, Pittore, or Westin regarding any personal knowledge of Plaintiff or her care at the facility. Plaintiff seeks to depose Hug, Pittore, and Westin regarding facility staffing, budgets, census, regulatory compliance, financial performance, Department of Social Services interactions, and facility operations generally. (Seigler Decl., Exs. A, C, D.) Hug, Pittore, and Westin are appropriate parties to depose concerning these categories of information. 

Moreover, Defendants’ objections based on the Apex Doctrine are unavailing. The Court finds that Liberty Mutual Ins. Co, v. Superior Court of San Mateo County (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1282 does not apply to preclude Hug, Pittore, or Westin’s depositions. In Liberty Mutual, the plaintiff sought to depose Liberty Mutual's president and chief executive officer as part of her initial discovery demands, even though he had no connection to or information about the case. The Liberty Mutual court noted that allowing a party to depose a corporate officer at the “apex” of the corporate hierarchy before less intrusive discovery has been conducted raises a “tremendous potential for discovery abuse and harassment.” (Liberty Mutual, supra, 10 Cal.App.4th at p. 1287.) Thus, “it amounts to an abuse of discretion to withhold a protective order when a plaintiff seeks to depose a corporate president, or corporate officer at the apex of a corporate hierarchy, absent a reasonable indication of the officer's personal knowledge of the case and absent exhaustion of less intrusive discovery methods.” (Id.) 

Here, Plaintiff has presented evidence that Pittore and Westin, and thus presumably Hug when he previously held the same position, regularly communicate with Sage Glendale regarding the very topics on which Plaintiff seeks to depose them. (Opp., pp. 3:13-4:22, Ex. 10; Seigler Decl., Exs. A, C, D.) Thus, the Court does not find that the depositions of Hug, Pittore, or Westin are precluded by the apex doctrine such as was the case in Liberty Mutual. Here, Hug, Pittore, and Westin have knowledge of the topics Plaintiff has identified in the deposition notices.

Thus, the motion for protective order is DENIED.  

MOTION TO COMPEL DEPOSITION
“If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action or an officer, director, managing agent, or employee of a party, or a person designated by an organization that is a party under Section 2025.230, without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice, the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.” (CCP, § 2025.450 subd. (a).) 

Plaintiff moves to compel the deposition of Martin Hug, the Manager/Member of Agemark Management, LLC, which is the managing company for Sage Glendale Senior Living, the facility where Plaintiff was a resident. (Mot., p. 4:15-16.) Defendants oppose the motion on the same grounds as their motion for protective order, discussed above. Namely that Plaintiff has already deposed Defendants’ PMK’s on these same topics, and that the deposition violates the apex employee doctrine. Although Hug is undisputably a management level employee, Plaintiff seeks to depose him regarding his personal knowledge of corporate operations, not knowledge of Plaintiff or the care she received. (Liberty Mutual Ins. Co., supra, 10 Cal.App.4th at 1287-1288.) Plaintiff has presented evidence that Hug “typically would be included on any communications around regulatory or compliance.” (Reply, Ex. 1.) 

Thus, the Court finds that an order compelling the deposition of Martin Hug is warranted. The motion to compel deposition is GRANTED. 

Requests for Sanctions
“If a motion under subdivision (a) is granted, the court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) in favor of the party who noticed the deposition and against the deponent or the party with whom the deponent is affiliated, unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (CCP, § 2025.450 subd. The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though no opposition to the motion was filed, or opposition to the motion was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.” (Rules of Court, rule 3.1348, subd. MOTION TO QUASH SERVICE OF SUMMONS
[CCP §§ 415.10, 415.20]

Moving Party: Defendant, Jin Dai
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Robert Leung

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Quash service of summons and complaint as to Defendant Jin Dai. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Form Unlawful Detainer Complaint 
1) Unlawful Detainer 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
Plaintiff Robert Leung (“Plaintiff”) is the owner of real property located at 8632 E. Valley Blvd, in the City of Rosemead, California, 91770. Defendant Jin Dai, dba Yu-Cake, (“Defendant”) is the tenant in the premises pursuant to a commercial lease agreement. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant owes past due rent in the amount of $110,000.00. 

ANALYSIS:
“Service of process, under longstanding tradition in our system of justice, is fundamental to any procedural imposition on a named defendant.”(AO Alfa-Bank v. Yakovlev(2018)21 Cal.App.5th189, 202[internal citations omitted].) “To establish personal jurisdiction, compliance with statutory procedures for service of process is essential.” (Kremerman v. White (2021) 71 Cal.App.5th 358, 371.) Defendant’s knowledge of the action does not dispense with statutory requirements for service of summons. (Kappel v. Bartlett(1988) 200 Cal.App.3d 1457, 1466.) 

“A defendant, on or before the last day of his or her time to plead or within any further time that the court may for good cause allow” may move “to quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over him or her” that results from lack of proper service. (CCP, § 418.10, subd. (a)(1).)A defendant has 30 days after the service of the summons to file a responsive pleading. (CCP, § 412.20, subd. (a)(3).) 

Once a challenge to a court’s personaljurisdictionis filed, “the burden of proof is placed upon the plaintiff toestablishthe facts ofjurisdictionby a preponderance of the evidence. This may be done through presentation of declarations, with opposing declarations received in response.”(CenterPoint Energy, Inc. v. Superior Court(2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1101, 1118 (citingAquila, Inc. v. Superior Court(2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 556, 568).)“When a defendant challenges the court’s personaljurisdictionon the ground of improper service of process ‘the burden is on the plaintiff to prove the existence of jurisdiction by proving, inter alia, the facts requisite to an effective service.’”(Summers v. McClanahan(2006) 140Cal.App.4th 403, 413.) 

Defendant argues that the summons and complaint were not properly served through personal service or substituted service. 

“A summons may be served by personal delivery of a copy of the summons and of the complaint to the person to be served. Service of a summons in this manner is deemed complete at the time of such delivery.” (CCP, § 415.10, sub. (a)(1).) “If a copy of the summons and complaint cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to the person to be served […], a summons may be served by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the person’s dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge of their office, place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, at least 18 years of age, who shall be informed of the contents thereof, and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and of the complaint by first-class mail, postage prepaid to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left. Service of a summons in this manner is deemed complete on the 10th day after the mailing.” (CCP, § 415.20. subd. (b).)  

Defendant argues that the purported service of the summons and Complaint is not proper because Plaintiff did not first attempt personal service before serving Defendant via substitute service and also because the proof of service is materially deficient. 

The proof of service checks the box under item 5a for personal service, yet the date and time information is not filled out. (8/10/2026 POS.) The date and time is filled out under item 5b, for substituted service. (8/10/2026 POS.) Defendant asserts that Plaintiff’s wife purportedly delivered a copy of the summons and Complaint to an employee at the subject premises, but no effort had been made to first serve Defendant personally. (Dai Decl., ¶¶ 14-22.) In addition, the proof of service does not indicate that the documents were mailed to Defendant after being left at the premises nor does it appear that the employee was authorized to accept service on Defendant’s behalf. Finally, the proof of service is signed and dated on “July 31, 2024,” two years before this action was filed.  

In the opposition, Plaintiff reasserts the allegations made in the Complaint and asserts that Helen Leung can effect service because she is not a party to the action. However, the opposition does not address the substantive issues such as the failure to attempt personal service or the failure to comply with the statutory requirements for substituted service. Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiff failed to properly serve the summons and Complaint on Defendant.    

The motion is GRANTED. 

RULING:
Defendant Jin Dai’s Motion to Quash Service of the Summons and Complaint is GRANTED. The July 31 service of summons is QUASHED.
