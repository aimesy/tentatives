Case Number: 21PSCV00778    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: O

Tentative Ruling

 

MOTION TO SET ASIDE DEFAULT, PER CCP § 473.5 & CIV 1788.61 is DENIED.

 

Background

 

This is a breach of contract case wherein the plaintiff alleges there is an unpaid sum of $50,000 on the defendant’s account.

 

On September 24, 2021, Plaintiff Persolve Legal Group, LLP (the successor in interest to the original lender, Compass bank) filed suit against Defendant James Stewart for:

 

1.     Breach of Contract

2.     Money Lent

3.     Account Stated

 

On May 30, 2023, default was entered.

 

On July 18, 2023, default judgment was entered in the sum of $72,591.33 (comprised in part of $50,000 in damages plus $21,008.21 in interest at an annual rate of 8%, as alleged in the complaint).

 

On May 5, 2026, the instant motion was filed.

 

On September 15, 2026, an opposition was filed. (The court continued the hearing to allow for service of the motion upon newly named Plaintiff’s counsel.)

 

On September 18, 2026, a reply was filed.

 

Discussion

 

As set forth in the motion, Defendant only became aware of this proceeding in March 2026 when he was attempting to conduct a refinance of his current residential mortgage and he was notified that an Abstract of Judgment for this matter had been recorded with the Los Angeles County Recorder's Office. (Motion p. 3.) More specifically, Defendant argues “[d]uring this time period (2021 and 2022) Defendant did not reside at any of the listed addresses where service was allegedly attempted. Further, Defendant was not in communication with any of the occupants of the listed addresses.” (Motion p. 3:10-13, emphasis added.)[1]  He argues that “[a]t that time and continuing to the present, Defendant resided at the following address: 10955 Willow Lane, Adelanto, CA 92301.” (Motion p. 3.)[2] With that, Defendant seeks to set aside the default judgment based upon CCP section 473.5. (Motion p. 1:27-28.)[3]

 

 

But the opposition raises a persuasive point: if none of the five addresses was ever his why were all five connected to him through Plaintiffs public-records investigation — and why that investigation show him owning real property at three of them? Notably, the narrative somewhat changes in Reply. Defendant concedes that Plaintiff also attempted service by mail to P.O. Box 2086 Pomona, CA 91769. His utility bills, attached as Exhibit “A” to his own motion, are addressed “James Stewart, PO Box 2086, Pomona, CA 91769” — the very address Plaintiff used. In Reply “Defendant does not deny that this mailing address was used by him. However, Defendant denies receiving notice or service of any court documents at that address.” (Reply p. 4:3-6.) But that is a conclusive, blanket denial of notice.

 

The question/point is appropriate considering the governing law. The only question is not, as framed by Defendant, when Defendant “became aware of this proceeding and the default judgment entered against him” (Motion p. 3:10-13; see also Stewart Decl. P2.). As argued by Plaintiff in opposition and not meaningfully addressed in reply, CCP section 473.5 does not only require lack of actual notice[4] but that the “notice of motion to set aside a default or default judgment and for leave to defend the action …shall be accompanied by an affidavit showing under oath that the party's lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by the party's avoidance of service or inexcusable neglect.” (emphasis added; see also subd. (C) [also requiring that the party's lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by the party's avoidance of service or inexcusable neglect.”], emphasis added.) And Civil Code section 1788.61 also requires that the motion be brought within a reasonable time.

 

But when his own evidence establishes that Plaintiff directed notice to a mailing address he used and there is no evidence/declaration that he did not check said PO Box, then the court questions the credibility of Defendant’s statements about the lack of actual notice and lack of neglect.[5]

All in all, as noted in opposition, where the moving party's declaration fails to affirmatively establish that the lack of notice was not caused by his own avoidance or inexcusable neglect — or where it rests on conclusory statements, relief under section 473.5 is properly denied. (See Opp. p. 6, citing Anastos v. Kuo Chen Lee (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 1314, 1318-1319.)

 

The motion fails on another ground: a motion under section 473.5 requires a showing of a meritorious defense to the action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5(b).) As argued by Plaintiff, Defendant's proposed Answer (Exhibit “C”) consists of a general denial an twenty-two form affirmative defenses — comparative negligence, assumption of risk, unavoidable accident, act of God, etc. Defendant does not identify a single specific, fact-based defense the underlying debt: he does not deny opening the account, does not deny the balance, and does no assert payment. (Opp. p. 7.) In reply, Defendant blanketly avers the foregoing is enough because he “denies each, every and all of the allegations…” and that the “discovery process” will provide additional information.

 

Conclusion

 

Based thereon, the motion is denied.

Case Number: 23STCV05277    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: O

Tentative Ruling

 

(1)   Respondents ISABEL HSIEH; KAREN LEE; and TIFFANY LAW’s MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES is DENIED.

 

(2)   MOTION AND MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE CROSS-COMPLAINT is GRANTED.

 

Background

 

This action pertains to the HOA’s bylaws. Petitioner/Plaintiff CYNTHIA ESCUTIA-COBIAN in her petition states, inter alia, that two persons elected to the Board of Directors, Isabel Hsieh and Karen Lee, are not resident Unit Owners” as neither live in any condominium of in the Fall Creek community but rent the units to third parties, in violation of Article IV of the bylaws.

 

On June 21, 2023, Plaintiff filed an amended petition against FALL CREEK HOMEOWNERS ASSOCIATION, a California corporation (the “HOA”); ISABEL HSIEH, an individual; KAREN LEE, an individual; TIFFANY LAW for:

 

1.     WRIT OF MANDATE, DECLARATORY RELIEF AND INJUNCTION

2.     DECLARATORY AND INJUNCTIVE RELEIF FOR FAILURE TO PERFORM DUTIES

 

On July 13, 2023, the court issued, in pertinent part, the following minute regarding Plaintiff’s ex-parte application ‘For Temporary Restraining Order Pending Hearing On Preliminary Injunction And Order To Advance The Hearing On The Preliminary Injunction Presently Set For Hearing On October 9, 2023’: “The moving party has failed to make an affirmative factual showing of irreparable harm, immediate danger, or any statutory basis for granting relief Ex Parte pursuant to California Rules of Court 3.1202(c).”

 

On July 21, 2023, Respondents filed a verified answer to the first amended petition.

 

On July 26, 2023, Respondents filed a cross-complaint against Plaintiff for Violation/Breach of the Governing Documents; Injunctive Relief; and Damages. (Based upon a review of the cross-complaint, it is predicated upon the allegation that the HOA has determined it is necessary to re-pipe all of the buildings within the development, but Plaintiff has refused to allow the HOA access to Plaintiff’s unit for the re-pipe project.)

 

On July 31, 2023, Respondents filed their first amended verified answer.

 

On September 21, 2023, Plaintiff filed a substitution of attorney (replacing William B. Hanley with Feldsott, Lee & Nichter).

 

On November 1, 2023 (after supplemental briefing), the court denied Plaintiff’s motion for a preliminary injunction. Specifically, Plaintiff sought an injunction for an order that Respondents (a) comply with Article IV that only resident unit owners serve as members of the board of directors and (b) remove Hsieh and Lee as neither are ‘resident unit owners’ as required by the bylaws, but Defendants evidenced showed that respondents are resident unit owners. As for Plaintiff’s contention that the 2017 amendment is invalid, the one-year statute of limitations passed pursuant to Cal. Civ. Code, § 5145(a) and Cal. Civ. Code, § 5100(a) - amendment of the governing documents.

 

On September 26, 2024, the court signed the judgment dismissing Plaintiff’s first amended petition, allowing Defendants/Respondents to recover attorney fees, and enjoining Plaintiff from interfering with various projects/construction.

 

On December 3, 2024, the court granted the Motion to Set Aside/Vacate Judgment filed by Cynthia Escutia-Cobian on 10/29/2024.

 

On November 17, 2025, Defendants/respondents filed an MSJ/MSA.

 

On December 24, 2025, the court denied the MSJ/MSA for multiple defects.

 

On January 27, 2026, Petitioner filed a ‘request for dismissal’ of the petition without prejudice as to ‘individuals ISABEL HSIEH, KAREN LEE, & TIFFANY LAW.’

 

On March 23, 2026, the instant motion for attorney fees was filed.

 

On June 17, 2026, the court continued the hearing on the motion for attorney fees for supplemental briefing. The court did so as motion heavily relied upon three cases to support Respondents’ position that they are the prevailing party: Parrott v. Mooring (2004) 112 Cal.App.4th 873, Salehi v. Surfside III Condominium Owners’ Association (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1146, and Coltrain v. Shewalter (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 94. However, as noted in the motion itself, both Parrott and Salehi involved dismissals against the HOA whereas the 1/27/26 dismissal was not as to the HOA but against the individual board members (at which the 2nd COA was directed at). In response to this, the reply advance numerous cases that were not asserted in the motion. With that, the court allowed for supplemental briefing. The parties have submitted their respective briefs.

 

On August 20, 2026, Petitioner filed the instant motion for leave.

 

 

 

Discussion

 

Individual Respondents move this Court for an order determining that the Respondents are the prevailing party in this case under Civil Code section 5975(c), and awarding attorney fees in the amount of $84,488.25. In turn, the subsection of the statute provides that “In an action to enforce the governing documents, the prevailing party shall be awarded reasonable attorney’s fees and costs.” The issue then is whether Respondents, the individual board members, are the prevailing party under the statute. For reasons to be discussed below, they are not.  

 

The parties present numerous authority and arguments in support of their respective positions, but there is an argument raised in Petitioner’s supplemental opposition that obviates the need to reach all the arguments presented: Under the Davis-Stirling Act, the trial court makes a “pragmatic” inquiry into “which party had prevailed on a practical level.” (Opp. p. 6, citing Heather Farms Homeowners Assn. v. Robinson (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 1568, 1574 [“Winick, Donald, and Elster all share a common theme. In each case, the court declined to adopt a rigid interpretation of the term “prevailing party” and, instead, analyzed which party had prevailed on a practical level. Donald and Elster further clarify that the trial court must determine who is the prevailing party, and that the court's ruling should be affirmed on appeal absent an abuse of discretion.”];[1] Almanor Lakeside Villas Owners Assn. v. Carson (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 761, 773 [“The Davis–Stirling Act does not define “prevailing party” or provide a rubric for that determination. In the absence of statutory guidance, California courts have analyzed analogous fee provisions and concluded that the test for prevailing party is a pragmatic one, namely whether a party prevailed on a practical level by achieving its main litigation objectives.”.)

 

Here, the court determines that the individual directors are not the prevailing party because on a ‘practical level,’ they did not achieve the main litigation objection. For one, as heavily argued in opposition, “the movants’ camp has achieved no

substantive objective at all — its dispositive motion was denied — and both sides’ main litigation objectives remain in full contest against the Association.” (Opp. p. 6.) To this, the supplemental reply provides only one reference to “litigation objection” which is that the Respondents’ “litigation objective was to get the [First Amended Petition] dismissed and Petitioner dismissed it.” (Supp. Reply p. 8:1-3; see also Supp. Reply p. 8 [“Thus, the Dismissed Respondents are the prevailing parties, as none of the relief sought in the complaint was awarded to the Petitioner.”.) But when the litigation is still ongoing such that Petitioner may obtain damages/the requested relief, that reason is not accurate. What is more, the statute provides that a “prevailing party” is entitled to attorney fees, not a “dismissed” party. Subscribing to Respondents’ position would mean that every party who is dismissed from an action by the petitioner/plaintiff is entitled to attorney fees and there is no case law or statute supporting that interpretation.

 

Based thereon, the motion for attorney fees is denied.

 

Discussion Re: Cross Complaint[2]

 

Petitioner seeks to file a cross-complaint based upon events that happened after her October 5, 2023 answer to the Association’s Cross-Complaint: the vacatur of the $56,733.75 default judgment (December 3, 2024), the Board’s executive-session decision to keep that voided sum on Ms. Escutia-Cobian’s account; the January 15, 2025 disciplinary “hearing” of which she was never given the notice; the resulting $181,391.53 “special reimbursement assessment”; the City’s November 12, 2024 stop-work order; the April 2025 sewage backup; and the May 24, 2026 burst of the common hot-water main that flooded her home and nearly electrocuted her husband.

 

As the standard is that leave to file a compulsory cross-complaint “shall” be granted absent substantial evidence of bad faith, then the court grants the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 426.50.) In fact, courts at best have only a “modicum of discretion” in denying leave to file a compulsory cross-complaint. (Gherman v. Colburn (1977) 72 Cal.App.3d 544, 550; Sidney, v. Superior Court (1988) 198 Cal.App.3d 710, 718; c.f., Silver Organizations Ltd. v. Frank (1990) 217 Cal.App3d at pp. 98-99 [rejecting view the court may exercise discretion in the denial of a motion to file a compulsory cross-complaint; to the extent discretion is allowed to deny the motion, there must be substantial evidence to support the trial court's decision.)

 

In opposition, Respondents largely focus on the merits of the proposed amended petition, but motion for leave to file a pleading does not adjudicate the sufficiency of that pleading; the preferable practice is to permit

the pleading and allow the opposing party to test its legal sufficiency by demurrer or motion to strike. (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1048.) Thus, the court will not reach the SOL, litigation privilege, and other matters raised.

 

To the extent that Respondents object that the Motion seeks “to name a new plaintiff (i.e., cross-complainant)” (Opp. 3:24–26), Petitioner may do that: a cross-complaint may join “any person” against whom, or with whom, a related cause of action lies. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 428.10, subd. (b), 428.20.) 

 

To the extent that Respondents object on the grounds that the motion fails to comply with CRC Rule 3.1324, it is unclear how that would apply. Rule 3.1324 follows the premise into error. By its terms the rule governs “[a] motion to amend a pleading before trial.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a).) Its requirements that the proposed pleading be “serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments,” and that the moving papers identify “by page, paragraph, and line number” the allegations deleted from and added to “the previous pleading” presuppose an existing pleading being altered. Here, as also argued in reply, there is no previous cross-complaint by Petitioner to differentiate; nothing is deleted from anything; and every allegation of the proposed pleading is before the court in full, lodged as Exhibit 1.

 

All in all, there is no showing of prejudice (no trial date) or undue delay.

 

Based on the foregoing, this motion is granted.

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion for attorney fees is denied and the motion to file a compulsory CC is granted.

Case Number: 26PSCV01342    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: O

Tentative Ruling

 

DEFENDANT SOLAR MOSAIC LLC’S MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION AND STAY PROCEEDINGS is GRANTED; Plaintiffs have not met their burden of showing a (genuine) factual dispute that Helen did not sign the other two agreements namely as she attested that she received the agreements to her email. A proposed order has been filed.

 

As the court is staying all the proceedings, this renders the motion for a protective order moot; the court will also inquire as to whether the parties will stipulate to taking SOLAR SERVICING LLC’S motion to compel arbitration off calendar.

 

Background

 

This case arises from the installation of solar panels. Plaintiffs RAED FAWAZ and HELEN FAWAZ allege the following against Defendants ALL PREMIUM CONTRACTORS, INC., a California Corporation; SOLAR MOSAIC LLC; a Delaware Limited Liability Company; CONCORD SERVICING LLC, a Delaware Limited Liability Company, CONCORD SERVICING INTERNATIONAL LLC, an Arizona Limited Liability Company: In or around February 2023, Defendants, through their sales side, initiated contact with Plaintiffs by cold call in an effort to sell a residential solar system for the Subject Property; The solar sales company was All Premium Contractors, and the finance company or lender was Mosaic. The transaction was presented and consummated as a loan. The documents were signed on or about February 24, 2023, at Plaintiffs’ home; Plaintiffs are informed and believe, and based thereon allege, that after the initial $25,000 approval, Defendants and/or their agents used Helen’s personal information to

open a second loan for approximately $75,000 without Plaintiffs’ knowledge, consent, signature, or authorization (Plaintiffs never signed, approved, or were informed that a second loan was required); as for the system, although the system is presently capable of some operation, it has not functioned as represented. Plaintiffs have experienced system shutoffs, inconsistent system performance, failure to meet performance projections, and battery-related problems; after Mosaic originated and/or initially held the subject loan obligations, Mosaic assigned, transferred, sold, delegated, conveyed, and/or otherwise caused its rights to be acquired in whole or in part by Defendants Concord Servicing LLC and Concord Servicing International LLC.

 

 

On April 13, 2025, Plaintiffs filed suit asserting the following causes of action (COAs):

 

1. Fraud / Intentional Misrepresentation;

2. Negligent Misrepresentation;

3. Negligent Hiring, Retention, and Supervision;

4. Breach of Contract and

5. Unfair Business Practices in Violation of Business & Professions Code §

17200 et seq.

 

On June 16, 2026, Solar Mosaic, LLC filed the instant motion to compel arbitration.

 

On August 12, 2026, DEFENDANTS CONCORD SERVICING, LLC AND CONCORD SERVICING INTERNATIONAL, LLC filed a demurrer, which the court sustained with leave to amend.

 

On August 17, 2026, DEFENDANT SOLAR SERVICING LLC’S filed a motion to compel arbitration. The hearing is set for 10/7.

 

On September 9, 2026, the court denied Plaintiffs’ ex-parte to ‘TO ADVANCE THE HEARING ON DEFENDANT SOLAR MOSAIC LLC’S MOTION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER; ALTERNATIVE REQUEST TO CONTINUE THE MOTIONS TO COMPEL ARBITRATION.”

 

On September 11, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their opposition to the instant motion.

 

On September 17, 2026, the reply was filed.

 

Discussion

 

 

Defendant Solar Mosaic LLC (“Mosaic”) moves to compel Plaintiffs Raed Fawaz (“Raed”) and Helen Fawaz (“Helen”) (“Plaintiffs”)[1] to arbitrate their claims because Plaintiff Helen agreed to arbitration. More specifically, Mosaic explains that Helen entered into a home improvement agreement with All Premium Contractors, Inc. (“All Premium”) and to finance this project, Helen entered into two Home Improvement Loan Agreement and Promissory Notes (the “Loan Agreements”) with WebBank; the Loan Agreements include arbitration agreements.[2]  The arbitration agreement specifically extends to “service providers as well as their parent companies, subsidiaries, and affiliates…also include third parties, such as subcontractors, that you bring a Claim against at the same time you bring a Claim against us or any other Related Party” and the provision applies to “any claim, dispute or controversy between you and us (or any Related Party) that arises from or relates in any way to this Note.” Mosaic argues that even as a non-signatory it may invoke arbitration because it is a “related party,” which Plaintiffs do not appear to dispute. (See Opp. p. 11:23-25.)

 

For reasons to be described below, the motion is granted.

 

Preliminary Issue   

 

Before engaging in the merits, the court turns to an issue that was not meaningfully addressed by either party but that could have readily resolved much of Plaintiffs’ concern in opposition disputing the signing of the electronic documents: Helen acknowledges she did sign one of the ( (three) agreements which does contain an arbitration provision.[3] While Plaintiffs are disputing signing the additional $75,000 financing, Plaintiff Helen concedes signing a $25,000 initial loan. (See e.g. Opp. p. 1:15-18 [“Helen denies electronically signing either the $25,000 or the $75,000 loan agreement. [] Plaintiffs acknowledge a paper transaction concerning $25,000, but deny authorizing a second $75,000 loan or another person to execute the disputed electronic agreements.”], emphasis added; see also p. 2:22-23 [“The complaint alleges that Plaintiffs were solicited for residential solar improvements, had limited English proficiency, and agreed to an original $25,000 transaction.”], emphasis added; see also p. 6:19-20 [“[A] wet-signed document (the only such document that Plaintiffs contend exist)….”], emphasis added; see also Helen Decl., ¶3 [“In or around February 2023, a representative of Defendant All Premium Contractors, Inc. presented me with hard copy documents in person concerning approximately $25,000 in financing for a residential solar system at my home. The only loan documentation I knowingly signed was a hard copy document that I signed by hand concerning that original approximately $25,000 in financing.”], emphasis added; see also Helen ¶17 [“I was not told during the original transaction that I was entering into two separate loans totaling approximately $100,000. My understanding was that I had agreed to a single financing obligation of approximately $25,000. Accordingly, I did not personally place those signatures on the documents, and I did not authorize another person to place them there on my behalf.”], emphasis added.) While Plaintiffs tersely argue there is a “distinction between the original paper transaction and the disputed electronic documents” (Opp. p. 3:1-2), they do not identify any differences in the loan terms they admittedly agreed to and the electronic versions.[4]

 

While Mosaic does address the foregoing, they only do so in reply and still do not provide a copy of the $25,000 agreement. Consequently, the court will focus on the two other loans. To do that, the court will carefully employ the burden-shifting mechanism.

 

Legal Standard

 

Key to determining that the motion weighs in Mosaic’s favor is the burden shifting mechanism employed in arbitration motions. The party seeking arbitration has the burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence. (Ramirez v. Golden Queen Mining Co., LLC (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 821, 830.) Although the party seeking arbitration bears the ultimate burden of proof as to the existence of an arbitration agreement, the burden of producing evidence on the issue may shift pursuant to a three-step process recognized by California courts. (Ibid.)

 

The first step requires the party seeking arbitration to carry the initial burden of presenting prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy. (Ibid.) This step is met rather easily: the moving party merely has to attach a copy of the arbitration agreement purportedly bearing the opposing party’s signature. (Id. at p. 831.) It became well settled by the Condee court that the party seeking arbitration “need not following the normal procedures of document authentication and need only allege the existence of an agreement and support the allegation….” (Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 218.) When an electronic signature, as here, is involved, the burden does not become more demanding. (See Fabian v. Renovate America, Inc. (2019) 42 Cal.App.5th 1062, 1067, quoting Ruiz v. v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 844 [“The burden of authenticating an electronic signature is not great.”].) “The party seeking authentication may carry its burden in any manner, including by presenting evidence of the contents of the contract in question and the circumstances surrounding the contract's execution.” (Fabian, supra, 42 Cal.App.5th at p. 1068.)

 

Once that initial burden is met, the second step requires the party opposing arbitration to carry the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement. (Ramirez, supra, 102 Cal.App.5th at p. 831.) To do this, the plaintiffs/opposing party must “identify a factual dispute as to the agreement's existence” and must present admissible evidence to support the existence of that factual dispute. (Id. at p. 832.) “For example, if a party disputes the authenticity of his or her signatures, the party ‘must offer admissible evidence creating a factual dispute as to the authenticity of the[ ] signatures. The opponent need not prove that his or her purported signature is not authentic, but must submit sufficient evidence to create a factual dispute and shift the burden back to the arbitration proponent….” (Id. at pp. 832-833, italics original; see also Rosenthal v. Great W. Fin. Sec. Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413–14.) As some courts put it, it isn’t just a mere factual dispute, but a genuine factual dispute. (See Bolton v. Solar Mosaic, LLC 2025 WL 1548586 p. 4,  quoting Xinhua Holdings Ltd. v. Elec. Recyclers Int'l, Inc., No. 1:13-CV-1409 AWI SKO, 2013 WL 6844270, at *5 (E.D. Cal. Dec. 26, 2013) [“[T]he party opposing arbitration must show a genuine issue of material fact as to the validity of the agreement to arbitrate, and [t]he required showing mirrors the showing that is necessary to defeat summary judgment. As such, courts give the opposing party the benefit of all reasonable doubts and inferences that may arise.”], internal citations and quotations omitted.) 

 

If the opposing party meets the burden of producing sufficient evidence, the third step requires the party seeking arbitration to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the parties formed a valid contract to arbitrate their dispute. (Ramirez, supra, 102 Cal.App.5th at p. 833.) 

 

Step One: Initial Showing

 

Mosaic contends the agreements bearing Helen’s electronic initials and signature is authenticated by DocuSign. The court agrees. To reach that determination, the court will turn to Fabian v. Renovate Am., Inc. (2019) 42 Cal.App.5th 1062, a case relied upon by Plaintiffs.[5]

 

Facts of Fabian:

 

In Fabian, the plaintiff filed a complaint against the solar panel company alleging that they were improperly installed. (Fabian, supra, 42 Cal.App.5th at p. 1064.) As alleged, Fabian stated that “Renovate made an unsolicited telephone call to her home about financing the solar panels and signed her name on a financial agreement. All communications between Fabian and Renovate's representative occurred telephonically and she was never presented with any documents to sign” (i.e., the circumstances). (Ibid, emphasis added.) According to the contract Renovate provided in support of its motion, the contract stated that it was made and entered into between an entity and Fabian and her adult daughter, Diana S. (Id. at p. 1064.) The letters “DS” and the printed electronic initials “RF” appeared in a box at the end of the arbitration agreement and the words “DocuSigned by:” and the printed electronic signature “Rosa Fabian” appeared in a signature box at the end of the Contract; the date “2/28/2017,” a 15-digit alphanumeric character, and the words “Identity Verification Code: ID Verification Complete” also appeared in the signature box. (Id. at p. 1065.) Of note, the court continued the hearing to allow the parties to conduct discovery including depositions; the depositions revealed that Renovate's belief Fabian electronically signed the subject agreement was not based on first-hand knowledge. (Id. at p. 1066.) The trial court determined that Renovate did not meet its burden, and the appellate court affirmed. The relevant passage of the opinion is as follows:

 

Citing to Newton v. Am. Debt Servs (N.D. Cal. 2012) 854 F.Supp.2d 712 (Newton), Renovate argues that DocuSign renders Fabian's electronic initials and signature legally binding… Renovate's reliance on Newton is misplaced because, unlike here, the declarant in that case proved that the “docusigned” electronic signature was the plaintiff's by explaining the process used to verify the signature. [] There, the defendant submitted a declaration stating that it sent a contract to the plaintiff using DocuSign, and that the plaintiff signed the Client Signature portion of the contract. [] Once signed, the signature was assigned an identifying code, such as the one that appeared above the plaintiff's signature on the subject contract. [] Here, Renovate did not provide any evidence from or about DocuSign in its petition, reply, or supplemental declaration. Indeed, the word “DocuSign” does not appear in any of Renovate's moving papers. Renovate offered no evidence about the process used to verify Fabian's electronic signature via DocuSign, including who sent Fabian the Contract, how the Contract was sent to her, how Fabian's electronic signature was placed on the Contract, who received the signed the Contract, how the signed Contract was returned to Renovate, and how Fabian's identification was verified as the person who actually signed the Contract. We thus find Renovate's DocuSign authentication argument unsupported and unpersuasive. (Id. at pp. 1068, 1069, emphasis added.)[6]

 

But as noted in reply, the evidence here differs in a strikingly important manner: Plaintiff Helen received the loan agreements. This is found in the last paragraph of her declaration: “In sum, Exhibits A and C of the Treece Declaration are true and correct copies of the documents AS I RECEIVED THEM. My authentication of Exhibits A and C as documents I received is not intended as an admission that I executed, authorized, or agreed to either document electronically, or that the purported electronic signatures appearing on those documents are mine.” (all emphasis added.) Whereas in Fabian more about the process was needed because the plaintiff stated all communications were telephonic and she received no electronic arguments, here, that is not the case. Accordingly, the court finds Devon Treece’s declaration, the senior Vice President, Servicing & Collections for Solar Servicing LLC ("Solar Servicing"), servicer for the loans at issue in this dispute, to adequately authenticate the signatures.

 

The burden now shifts.

 

Step Two: Rebutting the Initial Showing

 

As stated above, Plaintiffs must present evidence to show a genuine dispute that they did not sign the loan agreements. They do not. Plaintiffs spend a large portion of the opposition dedicated to the need for discovery, specifically regarding “the complete welcome-call recordings, available transcripts, dates, calling and receiving numbers, participant-identification records, and related notes.” (Opp. p. 8:26-28.) But that isn’t necessary.

 

The court will rely upon West v. Solar Mosaic (2024) 105 Cal.App.5th 985, which Plaintiffs heavily rely upon in opposition. However, the facts of West are distinguishable. In West, a sales representative for a remodeling company, who claimed to work with a government program that helped senior citizens to fix up their homes, visited the plaintiffs to discuss solar installation. (Id. at p. 988-989.) Both of the plaintiffs were in their 90s and suffered from dementia and neither used email, computers, or mobile phones. (Id. p. 988.)  The representative quoted the work to cost $25,000, but the plaintiff noted how they could not afford that.[7] Because one of the plaintiffs did not understand what was going on, the representative sent the quote for the services and the loan documents to the daughter’s email address. (Id. at pp. 989-990.) The documents were signed electronically in the father’s name and completed within a matter of seconds, despite the documents being 33 pages, and, recall, both plaintiffs having dementia and despite phone recordings show unintelligible responses from the plaintiff. (Id. at p. 990.) Actually, the recording with the Mosaic representative revealed a female voice that could be heard in the background several times, likely the daughter telling her father how to answer. (Ibid.) Ultimately, the work began but then the daughter learned of the loan documents and tried to cancel it; Mosaic refused and petitioned the trial court to compel arbitration after the plaintiffs sued the renovation company and Mosaic. (Id. at pp. 991-992.)

 

The trial court denied their motion. On appeal, Mosaic argued that Plaintiffs failed to submit evidence sufficient to meet their burden of demonstrating a factual dispute as to the authenticity of the father’s electronic signatures. (Id. at p. 993.) The court disagreed heavily focusing on how the evidence strongly suggested that the father “lacked the technical facility” to access his daughter’s email, “to create a digital signature, electronically click through and execute the loan agreement in seven locations, and submit those signatures, all in the space of 23 seconds.” (Id. at p. 993.) Mosaic then argued that a declaration from the father was required, but that was rejected by the court. (Id.  at p. 994.) With that, the court affirmed the trial court’s ruling to deny the petition on the ground that “Mosaic had not met its ultimate burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement, specifically finding Mosaic had not proven [the father] was the person who completed the loan documents or that [the daughter] had the authority to bind [the father] to an arbitration agreement.” (Id. at p. 992.)

 

Here, no such evidence is present. In fact, as set forth in the complaint, “[t]he sales process did not occur through a single brief meeting. Instead, Defendants and/or their representatives subjected Plaintiffs to a prolonged sales effort lasting approximately five months, involving repeated visits and/or calls.” (¶22.) Accordingly, unlike West wherein the plaintiffs (and the daughter) did “not understand” what was going on (id. at p. 989, see also fn. 5) over a one-time conversation, here, Plaintiffs understood the transaction. And while Plaintiffs may argue that recordings were at the very least produced in West of the telephone call, that went to the issue of capacity, which is not at issue here. (See also Bolton v. Solar Mosaic LLC 2025 WL 1548586 pp. 4-5.)

 

With that, the court is not persuaded by Plaintiffs’ request for a “schedule” to complete certain discovery to help satisfy their burden. The evidence that is needed to create a factual dispute is within the knowledge of Plaintiffs.

 

Second, Plaintiffs focus on a time discrepancy. As explained in opposition, the first certificate identifies loan 664680 and envelope 2BDD9E2EBD3A4314B948FB432298DCAC. It reports sending on March 2, 2023 at 7:53:51 p.m., viewing at 7:54:02, and signing at 7:54:23. The corresponding package’s signature page bears February 24, 2023. The second certificate identifies loan 667885 and envelope 900C4C9E13C74F2CB2D315EAB2B48A8A, with sending on March 3 at 4:19:40 p.m., viewing at 4:19:52, and signing at 4:20:47. (Opp. pp. 5-6.) However, as persuasively argued by Mosaic in reply: “That discrepancy, at most, reflects that the template was generated before the DocuSign envelope was sent and Plaintiffs offer no evidence to the contrary.” (Reply p. 9.) As for the 21- and 55-second intervals between viewing and signing, that too does not suggest anything irregular as electronic signatures in DocuSign are automatically applied when clicked. (Reply p. 9.)

 

All in all, the packages were signed using her email address and Helen offers no evidence that any other person had access to her personal email account during either signing window, on March 2 or March 3, 2023. As argued by Mosaic, “[m]ere speculation that some unidentified person might have “controlled” her email account (Opp. at 4) does not satisfy Plaintiffs’ burden to raise a genuine dispute as to whether she accessed her personal email to sign the Loan Agreements.” (Reply pp. 10-11.)

 

Thus, the court determines that Plaintiffs did not meet their burden such that the burden does not shift back.

 

Non-Signatory

 

Lastly, Mosaic argues that Raed should be equitably estopped from avoiding the arbitration agreements because his claims in this litigation are entirely premised on the Loan Agreements between Helen and Mosaic. The court agrees, and courts have routinely enforced arbitration agreements against nonsignatories whose claims relate to an agreement containing an arbitration provision. (See Motion pp. 17-18; See also Reply. p. 11 citing Mormile v. Sinclair (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 1508, 1511 [compelling nonsignatory husband to arbitrate); Hawkins v. Sup. Ct. (1979) 89 Cal.App.3d 413, 416–419 (nonsignatory wife bound by

husband’s arbitration agreement).)

 

To the extent that Plaintiffs rely upon Warfield v. Summerville Senior Living, Inc. (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 443 to reject that proposition, that case is not instructive. Setting aside that the case involved a plaintiff using the residential care facility for elder abuse and the defendant attempting to compel arbitration based off the plaintiff’s spouse’s signing of an arbitration agreement and the spouse not being named as a plaintiff, the only discussion as to equitable estoppel there was brief. The court stated at page 450: “Rather, Yvonne has utilized the services of the residential care facility as provided under certain admissions documents. Those are separate agreements from the arbitration agreement—a different, optional agreement. Yvonne has not sought to make use of the arbitration agreement and she is not estopped to assert that it is unenforceable against her.” Here, by contrast, Plaintiffs’ own Opposition confirms as much by describing the transaction and its financing collectively and seeking relief — cancellation of the loans and restitution — that necessarily treats both Plaintiffs as bound by, or at least directly affected by, the same instruments.

 

Other Matters

 

As noted in opposition, Solar Servicing’s motion is based upon the same arguments. (Opp. p. 2 [“The Court should also admonish Mosaic, Solar Servicing LLC (“Solar Servicing”), and their common counsel for unnecessarily multiplying the presentation of the same arbitration dispute. Defendant Solar Servicing’s later motion repeats the same arguments and recycles the same Treece declaration and exhibits, and their common counsel states that the earlier declaration “equally supports” its motion.”].) Based upon this ruling, the court will inquire whether Plaintiffs still intend on proceeding with that motion. =

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion is granted; the case is stayed.

Case Number: 26PSCV01583    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: O

Tentative Ruling

 

DEMURRER OF DEFENDANT POMONA VALLEY HOSPITAL MEDICAL CENTER (erroneously served and sued as POMONA VALLEY HOSPITAL AND MEDICAL CENTER) TO PLAINTIFF’S FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT is SUSTAINED in part with 45 days leave to amend and OVERRULED in part; the demurrer is overruled as to the 1st COA for professional negligence because the SOL is an evidentiary issue not proper for a demurrer and sustained as to the 2nd and 3rd COAs for the elder abuse and EADACPA as they are insufficiently pled (i.e., no recklessness/custodial relationship).  

 

Background

 

This is an elder abuse case. Plaintiff Cecila Jackson alleges the following against Defendant Pomona Valley Hospital and Medical Center (“Pomona Valley”): From January 2023 to May 2023, Plaintiff was in a cardiac rehabilitation and physical therapy program at Pomona Valley. The program used treadmill therapy at increased speed and incline, which Plaintiff alleges caused her injury both during and after the program.

 

On May 5, 2026, Plaintiff (appearing in propia persona) filed suit.

 

On June 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed a first amended complaint (FAC) for:

 

1.     Professional Negligence

2.     Elder Abuse/Neglect

3.     Dependent Adult Civil Protection Act (EADACPA)

 

On June 17, 2026, the instant demurrer was filed.

 

On July 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed her opposition.

 

On August 6, 2026, a reply was filed.

 

Discussion

 

Pomona Valley (“Defendant”) brings forth this demurrer based on California Code of Civil Procedure sections 430.10(e) & (f)  upon the following grounds: (1) The FAC is barred in its entirety by the statute of limitations as set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 340.5 [the one-year statute of limitations] bars the FAC in its entirety; (2) In the alternative, the FAC fails to state a claim for Elder Abuse/Neglect; and (3) In the alternative, Plaintiff’s claim for Elder Abuse/Neglect is Barred by the statute of limitations as set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1.

 

For reasons to be explained below, the court overrules the demurrer on the first ground (SOL) but does sustain the demurrer as to elder abuse COAs.

 

The applicable statute of limitations, Code of Civil Procedure section 340.5, provides in pertinent part, “[i]n an action for injury or death against a health care provider based upon such person’s alleged professional negligence, the time for the commencement of action shall be three years after the date of injury or one year after the plaintiff discovers, or through the use of reasonable diligence should have discovered, the injury, whichever occurs first.” (Emphasis added.) Thus, the statute contains a one-year and a three-year provision, both of which must be met for an action to be timely. (Demurrer p. 6, citing Trantafello v. Medical Center of Tarzana (1986) 182 Cal. App.3d 315, 318.)

 

Defendant argues that because the FAC alleges that the “[d]uring and after the rehabilitation program, Plaintiff developed progressive neck pain, shoulder pain, upper back pain, spinal symptoms, weakness, reduced mobility, and functional limitations” ¶16, emphasis added) then that is when the SOL began to run because that is when she had notice of the wrong(doing). As correctly summarized in the demurrer, the limitations period begins when the plaintiff suspects, or should suspect, that she has been wronged. (See Jolly v. Eli Lilly & Co. (1988) 44 Cal.3d 1103, 1114; see also Sanchez v. South Hoover Hospital (1976) 18 Cal.3d 93.) One example this wrong is pain. (See Demurrer p. 6, citing Enfield v. Hunt (1984) 162 Cal.App.3d 302, 310.)

 

However, as argued in opposition, symptoms may alert a patient that something is wrong, but that does not always mean the patient reasonably suspected that a hospital-directed rehabilitation program was negligently administered. (See FAC ¶15 [“Plaintiff alleges that the rehabilitation program caused cumulative injury rather than one single isolated traumatic event.”], emphasis added].) And though not argued by Plaintiff, this is especially when the program involves exercise, which for many does involve pain just by the very nature of the activity (i.e., “working out”). 

 

To the extent that there is a dispute as to when the SOL of the wrong i.e., that the treadmill rehabilitation may have been negligently administered or that the rehabilitation protocols could have caused or contributed to Plaintiff’s injury, that but emphasizes that such an inquiry cannot be resolved on this demurrer. For one, all inferences must be made in light most favorable to a plaintiff and the untimeliness must clearly and affirmatively appear on the face of the complaint. (See e.g., Schmier v. City of Berkeley (2022) 76 Cal.App.5th 549, 554.) And this latter point is even illustrated by Defendant’s own citations which discussed the SOL at different procedural postures of the case. (See e.g. Norgart v. Upjohn Co. (1999) 21 Cal.4th 1999 [summary judgment]; Enfield, supra [summary judgment]; Sanchez, supra [summary judgment]; Trantafello, supra [summary judgment].)

 

Notwithstanding, the court agrees the elder abuse COA is insufficiently pled. In Covenant Care, Inc. v. Superior Court (2004) 32 Cal.4th 771, 784, the California Supreme Court stated that recovery against a health care provider under the Act was intended only for “egregious acts of misconduct distinct from professional negligence...” (emphasis added.) As stated by the court in Worsham v. O’Connor Hospital (2014) 226 Cal. App.4th 331, 336, “The Elder Abuse Act does not apply to simple or gross negligence by health care providers.” (emphasis added.) Thus, even gross negligence is insufficient to give rise to the cause of action. Rather, what is required by California Welfare & Institutions Code section 15657 is “recklessness, oppression, fraud or malice.”

 

Plaintiff’s opposition does not address the relevant law that governs these claims. While the court recognizes that Plaintiff is pro per such that the canons and commentary “provide a path to ensure a self-represented litigant can be fairly heard on the merits while the court maintains its impartiality and does not assume (or appear to assume) the role of advocate or partisan” (Holloway v. Quetel (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 1425, 1434), a pro per party is still held to the same standards of an attorney. (Burnete v. La Casa Dana Apartments (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 1262, 1267 [“Further, the in propria persona litigant is held to the same restrictive rules of procedure as an attorney. [] In other words, when a litigant accepts the risks of proceeding without counsel, he or she is stuck with the outcome, and has no greater opportunity to cast off an unfavorable judgment than he or she would if represented by counsel.”].) Plainly put, this means that where Defendant raises certain case law and supporting arguments, Plaintiff has an obligation to respond to them and also support her own arguments with case law. (Moulton Niguel Water Dist. v. Colombo (2003) 111 Cal.App.4th 1210, 1215 [“Contentions are waived when a party fails to support them with reasoned argument and citations to authority.”].)
 

Thus, as recklessness has not been pled, the court sustains the demurrer. Another grounds to sustain the demurrer as to these COAs is that Plaintiff does not address Defendant’s argument that the FAC fails to plead a custodial relationship. (See e.g., Winn v. Pioneer Medical Group, Inc. (2016) 63 Cal.4th 148,158 [Plaintiff must be able to plead, “the existence of a robust caretaking or custodial relationship—that is, a relationship where a certain party has assumed a significant measure of responsibility for attending to one or more of an elder's basic needs that an able-bodied and fully competent adult would ordinarily be capable of managing without assistance.”].) Though an amended complaint is unlikely to rectify this defect and Plaintiff has not met her burden in demonstrating what facts could be pled, in the interest of justice and fairness, the court gives leave to amend.

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, the demurrer is SUSTAINED with 45 days leave to amend as to the 2nd and 3rd COAs and OVERRULED as to the 1st COA.
