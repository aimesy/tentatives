Case Number: 24NNCV01263    Hearing Date: August 27, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING DEFENDANTS’ MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

I.         INTRODUCTION

This is a medical malpractice action in which Plaintiff Ty Vanderford (“Plaintiff”) alleges that Defendants Dwayne Logan, M.D. (“Dr. Logan”), Azul Vision, Inc. (“Azul Vision”), and Eyecare Specialists Medical Group, Inc. (“Eyecare Specialists”) (collectively, “Defendants”) were negligent in their care and treatment of Plaintiff in connection with a cataract in his right eye and resulting cataract surgery.

On May 18, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion for summary judgment. On August 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition, and on August 14, 2026, Defendants filed a reply.

Evidentiary objections

Defendants’ objections

·       Nos. 1-7 OVERRULED

Plaintiff’s objections

·       Nos. 1-2 OVERRULED

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) CCP Section 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67, citing FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal. App. 3d 367, 381-382.) 

As to each claim as framed by the complaint, the defendant moving for summary judgment must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate an essential element, or to establish a defense. (CCP § 437c(p)(2); Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.) 

Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto.  

To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

III.      ANALYSIS

Defendants move for summary judgment on the grounds that the care and treatment provided to Plaintiff complied with the applicable standard of care and that no act or omission by Defendants caused or contributed to Plaintiff’s alleged injuries. (Motion, p. 2.)

A prima facie case for medical malpractice consists of (1) a duty to conform to the relevant standard of care; (2) a breach of that duty, i.e., the negligent conduct; (3) a proximate causal connection between the negligent conduct and the injury; and (4) resulting damage. (Lattimore v. Dickey (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 959, 968.)¿ 

The standard of care against which the acts of health care providers are to be measured is a matter within the knowledge of experts. (Elcome v. Chin (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 310, 317.) Unless the conduct required by the circumstances is within the common knowledge of the layman, the standard of care in a malpractice action can only be proved by an expert’s testimony. (Id.) If the “common knowledge” exception does not apply to a medical malpractice action, expert evidence is conclusive and cannot be disregarded. (Id.) 

Whether the standard of care in the community has been breached presents the basic issue in a malpractice action and can only be proved by opinion testimony unless the medical question is within the common knowledge of laypersons. (See Jambazian v. Borden (1994) 25 Cal.App4th 836, 844.) “When a defendant moves for summary judgment and supports his motion with expert declarations that his conduct fell within the community standard of care, he is entitled to summary judgment unless the plaintiff comes forward with conflicting expert evidence.” (Munro v. Regents of University of California (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 977, 984-985.) 

In this case, the dispute between the parties centers on whether Defendants breached the applicable standard of care in connection with Plaintiff Ty Vanderford’s cataract surgery and preoperative evaluation, including the testing performed and the selection of the intraocular lens, and whether any alleged breach caused Plaintiff’s subsequent visual complaints. (Id. at p. 3.)

Defendants have submitted the declaration of Jonathan Macy, M.D., a California-licensed ophthalmologist. Dr. Macy is board certified by the American Board of Ophthalmology and has been continuously board certified since 1982. He currently practices at the Los Angeles Vision Institute and serves as a Clinical Professor at USC. Dr. Macy states that, based on his background, training, and experience, he is familiar with the standard of care applicable to ophthalmologists and is qualified to render opinions concerning the care and treatment provided to Plaintiff. (Macy Decl., ¶¶ 4-5.)

Dr. Macy reviewed the operative complaint, Plaintiff’s medical chart from Azul Vision and Chevy Chase Ambulatory Center, Plaintiff’s deposition transcript, and the parties’ responses to Special Interrogatories, Set One. He also relied on his education, training, experience, and expertise in forming his opinions. (Macy Decl., ¶ 6.) Based on that review, Dr. Macy opines that the care and treatment rendered to Plaintiff by Dr. Logan, as well as the care provided by other providers involved in Plaintiff’s evaluation and follow-up treatment at Azul Vision, Inc. and Eyecare Specialists Medical Group, Inc., was at all times within the applicable standard of care. (Macy Decl., ¶ 21.)

            Dr. Macy explains that Plaintiff presented with a visually significant posterior subcapsular cataract in the right eye and a history of bilateral LASIK surgery. He opines that cataract surgery was medically indicated, prior LASIK did not contraindicate cataract surgery, and use of an EDOF lens in a patient with prior LASIK was within the standard of care. (Macy Decl., ¶¶ 22, 24.)

As to preoperative testing, Dr. Macy opines that OCT imaging is not mandatory in the absence of clinical suspicion of macular pathology and that there were no documented clinical findings requiring an OCT before surgery. He explains that an ERM is not always clinically apparent on routine examination and may require OCT for subtle detection. Even assuming a preoperative OCT had identified an ERM, Dr. Macy opines that cataract extraction would still have been medically appropriate and a mild ERM would not have contraindicated use of an EDOF lens or required a different intraocular lens. (Macy Decl., ¶¶ 25-27.)

As to causation, Dr. Macy opines, to a reasonable degree of medical probability, that no act or omission by Defendants was a substantial factor in causing or contributing to Plaintiff’s alleged injuries. He states that cataract surgery does not cause an ERM and that there is no evidence Dr. Logan’s surgery caused or worsened Plaintiff’s ERM. (Macy Decl., ¶¶ 31-32.)

Finally, Dr. Macy opines that Plaintiff’s ongoing visual complaints are due to preexisting retinal pathology, including the ERM, together with the inherent refractive variability associated with cataract surgery in a post-LASIK eye, rather than any deviation from the standard of care. (Macy Decl., ¶ 35.)

Defendants satisfy their initial burden of presenting expert evidence that the care and treatment provided to Plaintiff complied with the applicable standard of care and that no act or omission by Defendants caused Plaintiff’s alleged injuries. Thus, the burden shifts to Plaintiff to present conflicting expert evidence sufficient to raise a triable issue of material fact as to breach or causation.

In opposition, Plaintiff submits the declaration of Winston D. Chamberlain, M.D., Ph.D., a board-certified ophthalmologist. Dr. Chamberlain received his B.S. in Biology from the California Institute of Technology and his Ph.D. in Immunology and M.D. from the University of Colorado Health Sciences Center. He completed an ophthalmology residency at the University of California, Irvine, followed by a fellowship in Cornea and Refractive Surgery at UC Irvine. (Chamberlain Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.)

Dr. Chamberlain states that, based upon his education, training, background, and experience, he is familiar with the standard of care for ophthalmologists and is qualified to provide expert opinions in this case. (Chamberlain Decl., ¶ 7.) In forming his opinions, Dr. Chamberlain reviewed the materials identified in paragraph 8 of his declaration, including Plaintiff’s medical records, reports, and deposition transcripts. (Chamberlain Decl., ¶ 8.) Based on his review of those materials and his education, training, and experience, Dr. Chamberlain opines that Plaintiff had various preexisting optical and anatomical abnormalities that needed to be addressed before surgery. He further opines that the care and treatment provided by Dr. Logan and the technicians at Eyecare Specialists and/or Azul Vision fell below the applicable standard of care and caused injury to Plaintiff. (Chamberlain Decl., ¶¶ 9-10.)

Dr. Chamberlain specifically opines that the failure to perform an OCT before surgery and proceeding without the data it would have provided was below the standard of care. He explains that Plaintiff’s preoperative findings warranted macular imaging before lens selection and surgery. Dr. Chamberlain also notes that Dr. Logan ordered an OCT of the macula on March 1, 2022, but the OCT was not performed before the April 26, 2022, surgery. (Chamberlain Decl., ¶¶ 11-14.)

Dr. Chamberlain also opines that use of the SRK/T formula to calculate Plaintiff’s IOL power was below the standard of care because it was not designed to account for changes caused by prior LASIK. According to Dr. Chamberlain, use of the ASCRS post-refractive surgery calculator would have resulted in a lens power of approximately 15.16 diopters rather than the +12.0-diopter lens implanted by Dr. Logan. He opines that the implanted lens was approximately three diopters too weak and was one of the substantial factors causing Plaintiff’s farsightedness and postoperative difficulty with reading. (Chamberlain Decl., ¶¶ 17-21.)

Finally, Dr. Chamberlain opines that each of the identified breaches, standing alone and/or collectively, was a substantial factor in causing Plaintiff harm. He explains that the failure to identify Plaintiff’s macular and corneal abnormalities and optical compromise, together with use of the incorrect IOL formula, resulted in implantation of an underpowered EDOF lens, causing a measurable “refractive surprise,” loss of depth of focus, farsightedness, difficulty reading close up, and uncorrectable blur even through spectacles. (Chamberlain Decl., ¶ 24.)

The Court finds that, based on Dr. Chamberlain’s expert testimony, Plaintiff has presented sufficient evidence to raise triable issues of material fact as to whether Defendants fell below the standard of care and whether their conduct caused Plaintiff’s injuries. The parties’ experts offer conflicting opinions on both issues, and those disputes cannot be resolved on summary judgment. Thus, Defendants’ motion is DENIED.

IV.       CONCLUSION AND ORDER

            The Court DENIES Defendants’ motion for summary judgment.

Defendants are to give notice.

Case Number: 24NNCV03705    Hearing Date: August 27, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR ISSUE AND EVIDENTIARY SANCTIONS AGAINST MASCHOLE PROPERTY MANAGEMENT, INC. FOR DISCOVERY ABUSES AND SPOILATION OF EVIDENCE

I.         INTRODUCTION

Plaintiffs Qing Ye Li, individually and as successor in interest to the Estate of Xingya Zhang, and Hui Hung Zhang (collectively, "Plaintiffs") filed this action on August 20, 2024, alleging causes of action for negligent security, negligence, battery, and elder abuse against Defendants Jade Court San Gabriel, LP ("Jade "), Starboard Seaview Manager, Inc. ("Starboard"), Jerry Uy ("Uy"), Mia Constance Williams, Jaumaul Rashaud Jackson, and Does 1 through 100. The action arises from the December 26, 2022, death of Xingya Zhang.

On July 29, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion for issue and evidentiary sanctions against Defendant Mashcole Property Management, Inc. (“Mashcole”) based on alleged discovery abuses and spoliation of evidence. Mashcole filed an opposition on August 14, 2026, and Plaintiffs filed a reply on August 20, 2026.

Evidentiary objections

·       Maschole’s objections to the Farrugia declaration are SUSTAINED.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Under CCP § 2023.030(d), the Court may impose terminating sanctions by “an order striking out the pleadings or parts of the pleadings of any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process” and “an order rendering a judgment by default against that party.” The Court must consider the alternatives available, which include intermediate sanctions available under CCP § 2023.030, namely increased monetary sanctions, issue sanctions, or evidence sanctions. “The court may impose an issue sanction ordering that designated facts shall be taken as established in the action in accordance with the claim of the party adversely affected by the misuse of the discovery process. The court may also impose an issue sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from supporting or opposing designated claims or defenses.” CCP § 2023.030(b). “The court may impose an evidence sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from introducing designated matters in evidence.” CCP § 2023.030(c).

“A party moving for discovery sanctions based on the spoliation of evidence must make an initial prima facie showing that the responding party in fact destroyed evidence that had a substantial probability of damaging the moving party's ability to establish an essential element of his claim or defense.” Williams v. Russ (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1227. Once such a showing is made, the burden shifts to the party seeking to avoid sanctions to disprove prejudice. Id. “Spoliation is the destruction or significant alteration of evidence, or the failure to preserve property for another's use as evidence in pending or reasonably foreseeable litigation. Destruction of evidence relevant to proof of an issue at trial can support an inference that the evidence would have been unfavorable to the party responsible for its destruction.” Reeves v. MV Transp., Inc. (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 666, 681.

III.      ANALYSIS

Plaintiffs move for issue and evidentiary sanctions against Mashcole pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 2023.010 and 2023.030 based on alleged discovery abuses and spoliation of evidence. Plaintiffs principally argue that Mashcole failed to preserve and produce relevant electronically stored information after its duty to preserve arose, despite its officers and counsel having actual knowledge of the litigation and despite Mashcole’s own document retention and litigation hold policies. (Motion, pp. 2-9; Reply, pp. 2-4.)

Specifically, Plaintiffs contend that communications concerning Mashcole’s knowledge of Jaumaul Jackson, including his alleged threats, prior police contacts, eviction and continued presence at the property in an RV, are missing. Plaintiffs assert Maschole has offered three inconsistent explanations concerning its retention practices – its verified discovery responses state that emails are automatically deleted after one year; its IT contractor, Zack Ghaly, testified that emails are retained for only two to three months and that he was never instructed to preserve ESI in this action; and Mashcole’s written Standards of Performance provide that incident and patrol reports are retained for the “life of the community” and that documents are stored “indefinitely in Yardi Voyager.” (Motion, pp. 5, 9.) Plaintiffs reiterate in reply that Mashcole failed to institute a litigation hold, that a key custodian’s email account was shut off after Mashcole knew of the lawsuit, and that Mashcole has provided “three irreconcilable retention stories.” (Reply, p. 2.)

In opposition, Mashcole argues that Plaintiffs have not established a basis for the requested issue or evidentiary sanctions. Mashcole first contends that the motion is procedurally defective because Plaintiffs failed to file a separate statement as required by California Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(a)(7) for motions seeking issue or evidentiary sanctions. (Opposition., p. 9.)

Further, Maschole argues that Plaintiffs have not established willful spoiltion or discovery abuse warranting nonmonetary sanctions. Maschole contends that Plaintiffs have not identified any discovery order that Maschole violated and, at most, have presented evidence of negligent loss rather than intentional or willful conduct. Maschole also argues that the safe-harbor provision of CCP section 2023.030 (f)(1) precludes sanctions for ESI lost through the routine, good faith operation of an electronic information system. (Id. at pp. 18-19,)

Mashcole also argues that Plaintiffs have not shown the allegedly destroyed evidence had a substantial probability of impairing their ability to establish an essential element of their claims, and that the requested sanctions would improperly place Plaintiffs in a better position than if the discovery had been produced. Finally, Maschole argues that any CACI No. 204 adverse-inference instruction should be addressed at trial. (Id. at pp. 12-18.)

The Court makes the following findings.

First, as to the procedural defect, the Court exercises its discretion to reach the merits despite Plaintiffs’ failure to file a separate statement. The issues and discovery conduct at issue are sufficiently identified in the papers.

Next, the Court is not persuaded with Mashcole’s proposition that issue or evidentiary sanctions necessarily require the violation of a prior discovery order. Plaintiffs allege spoliation of evidence that no longer exists, rather than merely a failure to comply with a discovery request. As Plaintiffs point out in reply, Code of Civil Procedure sections 2023.010 and 2023.030 authorize sanctions for misuse of the discovery process, including spoliation, without a prior order compelling production. (Reply, pp. 2-4.)

The Court finds Plaintiffs have raised legitimate concerns regarding Mashcole’s preservation of relevant evidence. Mashcole was aware of the litigation and had written procedures requiring the preservation of documents relating to litigation. Nevertheless, Mashcole’s IT contractor, Zack Ghaly, testified that no one instructed him to preserve electronically stored information in this matter and that Uy’s computer and email account were not preserved. Plaintiffs also identify materially inconsistent evidence regarding Mashcole’s retention practices, including a one-year retention period stated in verified discovery responses, a two-to-three-month period identified by Ghaly, and Mashcole’s written policy providing for longer retention of certain records. (Motion, pp. 5, 9.) Plaintiffs further present evidence that Uy’s email account was shut down in November 2024, after Mashcole and its counsel were aware of this litigation. (Reply, pp. 4-5.) On this record, the Court cannot conclude that the loss of the ESI falls within the safe harbor for the routine, good-faith operation of an electronic information system.

However, the Court finds that the issue and evidentiary sanctions requested by Plaintiffs go too far. Plaintiffs ask the Court to establish that Mashcole owed and breached a duty to protect Plaintiffs from the foreseeable conduct of Jackson and Williams. Alternatively, Plaintiffs seek to establish that Mashcole’s officers, directors, and/or managing agents had advance knowledge of Jackson’s and Williams’s dangerousness and prior threatening and criminal conduct, and to preclude Mashcole from presenting evidence to the contrary. (Motion, pp. 18-19.) Although the missing communications may have contained evidence relevant to these issues, the present record does not establish that the missing evidence would have conclusively established these facts. The requested sanctions would establish significant liability-related facts based on the absence of evidence whose actual contents are unknown. Imposing the requested sanctions would therefore place Plaintiffs in a materially better position than they would have occupied had the evidence simply been preserved and produced.

Finally, the Court declines to order Plaintiffs’ proposed modified CACI No. 204 instruction at this time. The proposed instruction goes beyond permitting an adverse inference by affirmatively stating what the missing communications would have established. Specifically, Plaintiffs ask the Court to instruct the jury that the missing communications “indicate the full extent” of Mashcole’s knowledge regarding Jackson’s criminality and violent conduct, even though the contents of those communications are unknown. The Court therefore reserves the issue of whether a standard or appropriately modified CACI No. 204 instruction is warranted based on the evidence presented at trial.

Thus, Plaintiffs’ motion for issue and evidentiary sanctions is DENIED without prejudice to Plaintiffs seeking an appropriate adverse-inference instruction at trial.

IV.       ORDER

            The Court DENIES Plaintiffs motion for issue and evidentiary sanctions without prejudice to Plaintiffs seeking an appropriate adverse-inference instruction at trial.

Plaintiffs are to give notice.

Case Number: 25NNCV07782    Hearing Date: August 27, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANT’S MOTION TO RECLASSIFY TO LIMITED JURISDICTION

I.         INTRODUCTION

On February 5, 2026, Plaintiff Ashford Thomas (“Plaintiff”) filed this premises liability action against Defendants Alpha Beta Company dba Food 4 Less (erroneously sued as Food 4 Less of California, Inc. dba Food 4 Less) (“Defendant”), and Does 1 to 10, inclusive asserting causes of action for: (1) Negligence and (2) Premises Liability. The Complaint alleges that Plaintiff was on a shopping aisle inside a Food 4 Less store located in Pasadena when he slipped and fell due to a puddle of unknown liquid in the aisle.  

On July 21, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to reclassify the action from an unlimited civil case to a limited civil case. The motion is unopposed.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

            Code of Civil Procedure section 403.040 allows a plaintiff to file a motion for reclassification of an action within the time allowed for that party to amend the initial pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 403.040, subd. (a).) If the motion is made after the time for the plaintiff to amend the pleading, the motion may only be granted if (1) the case is incorrectly classified; and (2) the plaintiff shows good cause for not seeking reclassification earlier. (Code Civ. Proc., § 403.040, subd. (b).)   

“[A]n action or special proceeding shall be treated as a limited civil case only if…(a) the amount in controversy does not exceed thirty-five thousand dollars ($35,000)…(b) the relief sought is a type that may be granted in a limited civil case…[and] the relief sought, whether in the complaint, a cross-complaint, or otherwise, is exclusively of a type described in one or more laws that classify an action or special proceeding as a limited civil case or that provide that an action or special proceeding is within the original jurisdiction of the superior court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 85.) 

“To reclassify an action because it does not meet the jurisdictional amount involves evaluation of the amount fairly in controversy, not an evaluation of the merits of the claim. The trial court must reasonably determine that the verdict will “necessarily” fall short of the $25,001 required for an unlimited civil case.” (Stern v. Superior Ct. (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 223, 233.) Essentially, “the trial court looks to the possibility of a jurisdictionally appropriate verdict not probability.” (Id.) The “‘amount in controversy’ means the amount of demand, or recovery sought, or the value of the property, or the amount of the lien, that is in controversy in the action, exclusive of attorneys’ fees, interest, and costs.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 85(a).) 

III.      ANALYSIS

Defendant moves to reclassify this action from unlimited to limited jurisdiction, arguing that Plaintiff verified discovery responses establish that the amount in controversy does not exceed $35,000. (Motion, pp. 3-5.)

Defendant asserts that Plaintiff has not sought or received any medical treatment for the injuries allegedly sustained in the December 9, 2024, incident. Defendant further points to Plaintiff’s verified discovery responses, in which Plaintiff claims approximately $300 to $400 in lost income from his work as an Uber driver and approximately $600 to $1,200 in lost income from freelance security guard work. Plaintiff also produced a single CVS receipt reflecting $22.91 in over-the-counter pain medication. (Motion, p. 3; Exh. C.) Plaintiff otherwise denies past medical expenses, future medical care, future wage loss, and property damage. (Motion, p. 5.)

Defendant also notes that although Plaintiff initially sought punitive damages, the Court granted Defendant’s motion to strike the punitive damages allegations on April 2, 2026. Plaintiff did not thereafter file an amended complaint. Thus, punitive damages no longer provide a basis for maintaining the action as an unlimited civil case. (Motion, pp. 3-5; Exh. B.)

Based on the record presented, the Court finds that the amount in controversy does not exceed $35,000. Plaintiff received no medical treatment and identifies only minimal economic damages, consisting of approximately $900 to $1,600 in lost income and $22.91 in over-the-counter medication. Plaintiff further denies past medical expenses, future medical care, future wage loss, and property damage.

Further, Plaintiff has not filed an opposition or presented evidence demonstrating that a verdict exceeding $35,000 is reasonably possible. Thus, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s motion to reclassify the action from unlimited to limited jurisdiction.

IV.       CONCLUSION

The Court GRANTS Defendant’s motion to reclassify action to limited jurisdiction.

            Defendant is to give notice.

    JUDGE OF THE SUPERIOR COURT          

                                                                             

Case Number: 25NNCV07791    Hearing Date: August 27, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING DEFENDANT GEMMA MONICA’S DEMURRER TO PLAINTIFF’S COMPLAINT

I.         INTRODUCTION

            Plaintiff Magdy Tawadrous (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants United States Fire Insurance Company (“U.S. Fire”), Blue Star Claims, LLC (“Blue Star”), and Gemma Monica (“Monica”) arising from the handling and denial of benefits under an occupational accident insurance policy. Plaintiff alleges causes of action for (1) breach of insurance contract, (2) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (3) violation of Insurance Code section 790.03, (4) violation of Business and Professions Code sections 17200/17500, (5) unjust enrichment, and (6) fraud.

On November 3, 2025, Plaintiff filed the Complaint. On June 3, 2026, Monica filed the instant demurrer to all six causes of action. Plaintiff filed an opposition on August 14, 2026, and Monica filed a reply on August 20, 2026.

Meet and confer

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a), the demurring party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading at least five days before the responsive pleading is due. This must occur in person, telephone or by video conference with the aim of resolving any objections raised in the demurrer. Additionally, the demurring party must file and serve a declaration detailing the meet and confer efforts. It should be noted, however, that the failure to meet and confer is not itself grounds for sustaining or overruling a demurrer or granting or denying a motion to strike. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a)(4), 435.5, subd. (a)(4).)

Defense counsel declares that beginning on May 15, 2026, she attempted to meet and confer with Plaintiff’s counsel by telephone and sent an email summarizing the grounds for the demurrer. Counsel further declares that Plaintiff’s counsel responded by email, but the parties were unable to resolve the issues. (Ingulsrud Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.) The declaration does not establish that the parties actually met and conferred in person, by telephone, or by video conference as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a).

Thus, the requirement is not satisfied, however, the Court elects to consider the demurrer on the merits.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.) 

CCP section 430.10(f) provides that a pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous and unintelligible. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(f).) “A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) “A demurrer for uncertainty will be sustained only where the complaint is so bad that defendant cannot reasonably respond—i.e., he or she cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him or her.” (Weil & Brown, Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group) § 7:85 (emphasis in original).) “The objection of uncertainty does not go to the failure to allege sufficient facts.” (Brea v. McGlashan (1934) 3 Cal.App.2d 454, 459.) “It goes to the doubt as to what the pleader means by the facts alleged.” (Id.) “Such a demurrer should not be sustained where the allegations of the complaint are sufficiently clear to apprise the defendant of the issues which he is to meet.” (People v. Lim (1941) 18 Cal.2d 872, 882.)  

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

III.      ANALYSIS

            Defendant Monica demurs to all six causes of action alleged against her. Monica argues she is not a proper party because she was not a party to the insurance contract and is alleged only to have acted within the course and scope of her employment as a claims adjuster. (Demurrer, p. 4.) Plaintiff alleges Monica was an “agent, claims adjuster, employee, and/or authorized representative” of Blue Star Claims, LLC and United States Fire Insurance Company, acting within the course and scope of that agency or employment. (Complaint, ¶ 4.) The Complaint otherwise refers generally to “Defendants” and does not separately identify conduct undertaken by Monica.

First and second causes of action: breach of insurance contract and breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing

“The standard elements of a claim for breach of contract are: ‘(1) the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) damage to plaintiff therefrom.’” (Wall Street Network, Ltd. v. New York Times Co. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1171, 1178.) 

The elements for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing are: (1) existence of a contract between plaintiff and defendant; (2) plaintiff performed his contractual obligations or was excused from performing them; (3) the conditions requiring defendant’s performance had occurred; (4) the defendant unfairly interfered with the plaintiff’s right to receive the benefits of the contract; and (5) the plaintiff was harmed by the defendant’s conduct. (Merced Irr. Dist. V. County of Mariposa (E.D. Cal. 2013) 941 F.Supp.2d 1237, 1280 [discussing California law].) 

Here, Monica argues that both causes of action fail because she is not a party to the insurance policy. Monica contends that Plaintiff alleges only that she acted as an agent and claims adjuster for Blue Star and U.S. Fire, which is insufficient to establish contractual privity. (Demurrer, pp. 6-7.) Plaintiff opposes arguing she may be held for tortious conduct she participated, notwithstanding her status as an agent or employee. (Opposition, pp. 6-7.)

The Court agrees with Monica. The Complaint does not allege that Monica was a party to the insurance policy. Rather, Monica is alleged only to have acted as an agent and claims adjuster for Blue Star and U.S. Fire. (Complaint, ¶ 4.) Thus, there is no contractual relationship between Plaintiff and Monica that could support either a breach of contract or breach of the implied covenant claim.

Plaintiff’s argument that an agent may be liable for her own tortious conduct does not change this result because these claims arise from duties imposed by the insurance contract.

Thus, the demurrer as to the first and second causes of action are SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

Third cause of action: California Insurance Code § 790.03

Monica demurs to the third cause of action, arguing that Insurance Code section 790.03 does not provide a private cause of action, citing Moradi-Shalal v. Fireman’s Fund Ins. Cos. (1988) 46 Cal.3d 287, 304. (Demurrer, p. 7.)

The Court agrees. In Moradi-Shalal, the California Supreme Court held that Insurance Code section 790.03 does not create a private right of action. (Id. at p. 304.) Thus, Plaintiff cannot maintain a standalone claim under section 790.03.

Thus, the demurrer as to this cause of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

Fourth cause of action: UCL violation

To set forth a claim for a violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200 (“UCL”), Plaintiff must establish Defendant was engaged in an “unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising” and certain specific acts. (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200.) A cause of action for unfair competition “is not an all-purpose substitute for a tort or contract action.” (Cortez v. Purolator Air Filtration Products Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 163, 173.) 

Section 17500 provides that it is unlawful for a person or corporation to disseminate false or misleading advertisements with the intent to dispose of real or personal property or perform services. (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17500.) To establish a false advertising cause of action under section 17500, Plaintiff must demonstrate the advertisement was likely to deceive or mislead consumers. (See Brockey v. Moore (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 86, 98-99.) 

Monica demurs to the fourth cause of action, arguing that the Complaint does not allege any unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent conduct by Monica individually and instead relies on the same claims-handling conduct alleged against Defendants collectively. (Demurrer, p. 8.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Monica may be individually liable under the UCL because she personally participated in the alleged unfair and fraudulent conduct. (Opposition, p. 8.)

The Court agrees with Monica. The Complaint does not identify any unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business practice undertaken by Monica individually. Rather, the allegations are asserted generally against “Defendants,” and Monica is alleged only to have acted as an agent and claims adjuster for the corporate defendants. Further, to the extent the UCL claim is based on the alleged fraudulent conduct, the Complaint does not plead any misrepresentation by Monica with the required specificity.

Thus, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to this cause of action without leave to amend. 

Fifth cause of action: Unjust enrichment

Monica demurs to the fifth cause of action, arguing that it fails to state sufficient facts and is uncertain. (Notice, p. 2.) In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Monica does not substantively address the unjust enrichment claim in her demurrer and therefore provides no basis for sustaining the demurrer to this cause of action. (Opposition, p. 9.)

The Court is not persuaded by Plaintiff’s argument. Monica expressly challenged the fifth cause of action in her Notice of Demurrer on the ground that it fails to state sufficient facts. (Notice, p. 2.) Moreover, “[u]njust enrichment is not a cause of action”; rather, it is simply “a restitution claim.” (Hill v. Roll International Corp. (2011) 195 Cal.App.4th 1295, 1307; see also Melchior v. New Line Productions, Inc. (2003) 106 Cal.App.4th 779, 793.)

Thus, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to this cause of action with 20 days’ leave to amend to allege facts sufficient to state a restitutionary claim against Monica.

Sixth cause of action: Fraud 

“The elements of fraud are (a) a misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) scienter or knowledge of its falsity; (c) intent to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Ctr. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) The facts constituting the alleged fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.) To properly allege fraud against a corporation, the plaintiffs must plead the names of the persons allegedly making the false representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written. (Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157.) 

Monica demurs to the sixth cause of action, arguing that Plaintiff fails to allege the specific misrepresentation made by Monica, including how, when, where, to whom, and by what means it was made. (Demurrer, p. 9.) In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the Complaint adequately alleges Monica personally participated in the fraudulent conduct.

The Court disagrees. The Complaint generally attributes the alleged misrepresentations to “Defendants” without identifying any representation made by Monica individually. It does not allege what Monica said or concealed, when or how the alleged misrepresentation occurred, or facts showing Monica’s knowledge of falsity and intent to induce Plaintiff’s reliance. Thus, the fraud claim is not pled with the required specificity.

The Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to this cause of action with 20 days leave to amend.

IV.       ORDER

The Court SUSTAINS Monica’s demurrer without leave to amend as to the first, second, third, and fourth causes of action. The Court SUSTAINS Monica’s demurrer with 20 days’ leave to amend as to the fifth and sixth causes of action.

Defendants are to give notice.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

I.         INTRODUCTION

Plaintiff Magdy Tawadrous (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants United States Fire Insurance Company (“U.S. Fire”), Blue Star Claims, LLC (“Blue Star”), and Gemma Monica (“Monica”) arising from the handling and denial of benefits under an occupational accident insurance policy. Plaintiff alleges causes of action for (1) breach of insurance contract, (2) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (3) violation of Insurance Code section 790.03, (4) violation of Business and Professions Code sections 17200/17500, (5) unjust enrichment, and (6) fraud.

On May 29, 2026, U.S. Fire and Blue Star (collectively, “Defendants”) filed the instant motion to compel arbitration and stay the action. Plaintiff filed an opposition on August 14, 2026, and U.S. Fire and Blue Star filed a reply on August 20, 2026.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Code Civ. Proc. §1281 provides: “A written agreement to submit to arbitration an existing controversy or a controversy thereafter existing is valid, enforceable, and irrevocable, save upon such grounds as exist for the revocation of any contract.”

Code Civ. Proc. §1281.2 provides that upon petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate and a party’s refusal to submit to arbitration, the court shall order the parties to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement exists, unless it determines that the right to arbitrate has been waived, that grounds exist for revocation, or that a party to the agreement is also party to a pending litigation arising out of the same facts and there exists a possibility of conflicting rulings on a common issue of fact or law.  In such a situation, the court may (1) refuse to enforce the arbitration agreement and order intervention or joinder of all parties in a single action, (2) order intervention or joinder as to all or only certain issues, (3) order arbitration among the parties who have agreed to arbitration and stay the action pending outcome of arbitration, or (4) stay arbitration pending outcome of the litigation.

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1330 provides, “A petition to compel arbitration or to stay proceedings pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 1281.2 and 1281.4 must state, in addition to other required allegations, the provisions of the written agreement and the paragraph that provides for arbitration. The provisions must be stated verbatim or a copy must be physically or electronically attached to the petition and incorporated by reference.”

The “involving” requirement is to be interpreted broadly. Allied-Bruce Terminix Companies v. Dobson (1995) 513 U.S. 265, 274. 

III.      ANALYSIS

The existence of written agreement to arbitrate is established.

Under both the Federal Arbitration Act and California law, arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a contract. (Winter v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943, 947.) In ruling on a motion to compel arbitration, the court must first determine whether the parties actually agreed to arbitrate the dispute, and general principles of California contract law help guide the court in making this determination. (Mendez v. Mid-Wilshire Health Care Center (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 534, 541.) “With respect to the moving party’s burden to provide evidence of the existence of an agreement to arbitrate, it is generally sufficient for that party to present a copy of the contract to the court.” (Baker v. Italian Maple Holdings, LLC (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 1152, 1160.) 

Here, Defendants submit the declaration of Erika Dempsey, Claims Program Manager for Crum & Forster, the parent company of Defendant United States Fire Insurance Company (“U.S. Fire”). Dempsey states that U.S. Fire issued a blanket occupational accident insurance policy to Lyft, Inc. (the “OA Policy”), which provides certain benefits to eligible transportation providers performing services through the Lyft platform. (Dempsey Decl., ¶¶ 1-3.) A copy of the applicable Certificate of Insurance is attached to Dempsey’s declaration as Exhibit A. (Id., ¶ 3, Exh. A.) 

The OA Policy contains the following arbitration provision:

“This Arbitration provision permanently bars the institution of any individual or class action lawsuit brought by the Covered Person or the Recipient of Payment. With this binding Arbitration provision, the Covered Person, for himself or herself or the Recipient of Payment is waiving the right to a trial by jury. “ (Exh. A; p. 20.) 

Dempsey further states that the OA Policy contains an arbitration provision requiring disputes arising from the contractual relationship between a Covered Person and the insurer to be resolved through binding arbitration. (Id., ¶ 4.)

Plaintiff does not dispute that the OA Policy contains the arbitration provision. Rather, Plaintiff argues that Defendants have not established that Plaintiff agreed to be bound by that provision, because Plaintiff did not sign the OA Policy, execute an arbitration agreement, or otherwise manifest assent to arbitration. (Opposition, pp. 3-5.) Defendants acknowledge that Plaintiff did not personally execute the OA Policy but contend that he is nevertheless bound by the arbitration provision under principles of equitable estoppel and third-party beneficiary law because he seeks benefits as a Covered Person under the Policy. (Reply, pp. 2-3.)

Thus, the Court finds that Defendants have met their initial burden of establishing the existence of a written arbitration provision contained in the OA Policy. Whether Plaintiff, as a nonsignatory to the OA Policy, is bound by that arbitration provision is addressed below.

Plaintiff is bound by the arbitration provision as a nonsignatory

Defendants argue that Plaintiff may be compelled to arbitrate notwithstanding that he did not sign the OA Policy. Defendants rely on Philadelphia Indemnity Ins. Co. v. SMG Holdings, Inc. (2019) 44 Cal.App.5th 834, in which the Court of Appeal held that an arbitration provision contained in an insurance policy was enforceable against an additional insured who was not a signatory to the policy and directed the trial court to order arbitration of the coverage dispute. Defendants contend the same principle applies here because Plaintiff seeks benefits as a Covered Person under the OA Policy. (Motion, p. 5.)

Defendants further argue that Plaintiff is bound under the doctrine of equitable estoppel. A nonsignatory may be compelled to arbitrate where his claims are “‘intimately founded in and intertwined’ with the underlying contract obligations.” (Boucher v. Alliance Title Co., Inc. (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 262, 271.) Defendants contend that because Plaintiff seeks benefits allegedly owed under the OA Policy, his claims necessarily depend upon the Policy and he is therefore estopped from avoiding its arbitration provision. (Motion, pp. 5-6.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that he cannot be compelled to arbitrate because he was not a party to or signatory of the OA Policy and did not assent to its arbitration provision. (Opposition, p. 5.) Plaintiff further argues that Defendants have not established an exception applicable to nonsignatories because they have not shown that the arbitration provision was intended for his benefit or that he knowingly exploited the OA Policy. (Opposition, pp. 5-7.) Plaintiff also contends that equitable estoppel does not apply because his claims arise from Defendants’ alleged misconduct in handling and paying benefits. (Opposition, p. 7.)

The Court finds Defendants’ arguments persuasive. Although Plaintiff did not execute the OA Policy, that fact does not preclude enforcement of the arbitration provision against him. A nonsignatory may be compelled to arbitrate where his claims are “‘intimately founded in and intertwined’ with the underlying contract obligations.” (Boucher, supra, 127 Cal.App.4th at p. 271.)

Here, Plaintiff seeks benefits allegedly owed to him as a Covered Person under the OA Policy. Plaintiff submitted a claim for benefits under the Policy and now alleges that Defendants failed to pay benefits owed under it. He asserts, among other claims, breach of contract and breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing arising from the handling and payment of those benefits. (Opposition, p. 2..) Thus, Plaintiff’s claims are founded upon and intertwined with the OA Policy containing the arbitration provision.

Moreover, Philadelphia Indemnity Ins. Co. v. SMG Holdings, Inc. (2019) 44 Cal.App.5th 834 is instructive. There, the Court of Appeal enforced an insurance policy’s arbitration provision against a nonsignatory additional insured seeking coverage under the policy. (Id. at pp. 841-842.) Similarly, Plaintiff here affirmatively seeks benefits under the OA Policy and cannot rely upon the Policy as the source of those benefits while avoiding its arbitration provision.

Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiff is bound by the arbitration provision under principles of equitable estoppel. The action is STAYED pending results of the arbitration.

Stay as Defendant Gemma Monica

Although Monica is not a party to the arbitration agreement, the Court may stay the action as to her where the claims involve the same controversy being arbitrated. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4; Marcus v. Superior Court (1977) 75 Cal.App.3d 204, 209.)

Here, Plaintiff’s claims against Monica arise from the same handling and denial of benefits under the OA Policy. Thus, the Court stays the action as to Monica pending completion of the arbitration.

IV.       CONCLUSION AND ORDER

            Defendants’ petition to compel arbitration is GRANTED.

            The action is STAYED pending results of the arbitration.

            Defendants are to give notice.
