Case Number: 24STCV21165    Hearing Date: July 30, 2026    Dept: 730

TENTATIVE

 

The request for default judgment submitted on February 24, 2026, is DENIED for the following reasons:

 

The total judgment to be entered on the CIV-100 form and the JUD-100 form differ. In Section 2 of the CIV-100 form, Petitioner Farzad Nourollah (“Nourollah”) requests judgment totaling $258,366.09; the Court notes this amount is lesser than the sum of the other categories, totaling $264,886.61, outlined in the same section. The JUD-100 form requests judgment of $264,866.61. Although it is likely the CIV-100 form total is a mistake, Nourollah must submit uniform numbers across form to allow the Court to confirm the requested judgment.

 

Nourollah also failed to provide sufficient interest calculation, as required by California Rules of Court rule 3.18000, subdivision (a)(3). Although Nourollah provided all of the figures associated with the requested interest, Nourollah did not show any of the calculation involved in reaching those figures. Any new default judgment packet should show, at the least, how Nourollah calculated the daily interest figure.

 

The OSC re entry of default judgment is continued to the court’s next available date in approximately 60 days. No later than 15 days before the new hearing date, Nourollah is to submit a new default judgment package correcting these defects. Failure to do so may result in the imposition of sanctions, including monetary sanctions and/or dismissal.

ORDER
DEEMING MOTION TO COMPEL COMPLIANCE
WITH SECTION 871.26 MOOT.

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Luis Rivera (“Rivera”)
entered into a warranty contract with Defendant Nissan North America, Inc. (“Nissan”)
regarding a 2020 Nissan Frontier (“subject vehicle”). The subject vehicle allegedly
has defects and nonconformities making the car effectively worthless. Nissan allegedly
failed to replace the vehicle or reimburse Rivera. Rivera sued Nissan for five
causes of action, including violation of the Song-Beverly act.

On
April 8, 2026, Rivera filed the instant motion to compel compliance with Code
of Civil Procedure section 871.26, subdivisions (b) and (h).[1]
Nissan filed an opposition.

 

II.        REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Nissan requests that the Court take judicial
notice of certain documents. The Court grants the requests, taking judicial
notice of all requested documents.

 

III.      LEGAL STANDARD

Effective January 1, 2025, in any action
seeking restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle under § 871.20, the
parties must follow these rules: within 120 days after an answer or other
responsive pleading is filed, each party shall, without awaiting a discovery
request, provide 1) a copy or access to an owner’s manual for the subject
vehicle; 2) any warranties issued in conjunction with the sale of the motor
vehicle; 3) sample brochures published for the motor vehicle; 4) the motor
vehicle’s original invoice to the selling dealer, 5) sales or lease agreements;
6) motor vehicle information reports; 7) entire warranty transaction history
for the motor vehicle; 8)a listing of required field actions applicable to the
motor vehicle; 9) published technical service bulletins (“TSBs”) for the same
make, model, and year reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to
the motor vehicle; 10) published information service bulletins (“ISBs”) for the
same make, model, and year reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining
to the motor vehicle; 11) records relating to communications between the
manufacturer or dealership and the owner or lessee of the motor vehicle,
including those related to repair orders or claims involving the motor vehicle;
12) warranty policies and procedure manuals; 13) service manuals reasonably
related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle; 14) if a
pre-suit restitution or replacement request is made, all call recordings of
pre-suit communications with the consumer available at the time of service of
the complaint; 15) if a pre-suit restitution or replacement request is made,
the manufacturer's written statement of policies and procedures used to
evaluate customer requests for restitution or replacement pursuant to “Lemon
Law” claims; 16) if a pre-suit restitution or replacement request is made, any
nonprivileged, prelitigation evaluation; 17) any warranty extensions or
modifications issued by the manufacturer on the motor vehicle. (§ 871.26, subdivision
(h)(1)–(17).) 

The California Legislature
enacted section 871.20, et seq., which added requirements for certain lemon law
actions. (Code Civ. Proc. § 871.20, subd. (a) [“Notwithstanding any other law,
this chapter applies to an action, brought against a manufacturer who has
elected under Section 871.29 to proceed under this chapter, seeking restitution
or replacement of a motor vehicle pursuant to subdivision (b) or (d) of Section
1793.2, Section 1793.22, or Section 1794 of the Civil Code, or for civil
penalties pursuant to subdivision (c) of Section 1794 of the Civil Code, where
the request for restitution or replacement is based on noncompliance with the
applicable express warranty”]; subd. (b) [“This chapter does not apply to
service contract claims under Section 1794 of the Civil Code or any action
seeking remedies that are not restitution or replacement of a motor
vehicle”].) 

           

IV.       DISCUSSION

A.        Compel Mandatory Disclosures

Rivera
requests the Court compel Nissan to produce all documents required under
section 871.26, subdivision (h), as Nissan failed to provide any verified
written responses or documents required under statute. Pursuant to section
871.26, subdivision (b) and (h), certain documentation must be provided within
60 days of filing a responsive pleading in a lemon law action such as this case.

Nissan
filed an answer on August 8, 2025. Therefore, the required documents should
have been produced by October 7, 2025. No responses were provided by that time.
On December 22, 2025, Rivera’s counsel e-mailed Nissan to request documents for
463 separate cases in which Nissan’s initial disclosures were past due. (Mot.,
Shahian Decl. ¶ 6, Ex. 1.) Nissan requested parties agree for Nissan to produce
disclosure for 50 cases per month. (Ibid.) It is unclear if parties
agreed to this, and, if so, when Rivera’s disclosures would be due. As of the
filing of this motion on April 3, 2026, Nissan has still failed to provide any
of the required documents. (Mot., Shahian Decl., ¶ 5.)

On
April 24, 2026, Nissan served initial disclosures and document production as
required by section 871.26, subdivision (h). (Opp., Sweeney Decl. ¶¶ 10-12, Ex.
A-B.) As disclosures have been produced, this motion is MOOT.

 

B.        Sanctions

Rivera requests the Court impose sanctions of $2,500.00 against
Nissan’s counsel, pursuant to section 871.26, subdivision (j)(2). Section
871.26, subdivision (j)(2), provides unless the party failing to comply shows
good cause, the court shall impose $2,500.00 in sanctions upon defendant's
counsel for failure to comply with deposition requirements.

Nissan claims it had good cause to fail to abide by the
statutory requirements as parties mutually agreed to pause disclosure
obligations while they engaged in active settlement negotiations. However, Nissan
provides no evidence to demonstrate parties agreed to suspend obligations while
pursuing settlement. In sole support of this assertion, Nissan cites to
“Exhibit 1 to Plaintiff’s Declaration pp. 3-5.” (Opp., Sweeney Decl. ¶ 6.)
There is no submitted declaration attributed to the plaintiff, Rivera. The
Court assumes Nissan refers to Rivera’s only submitted declaration, the
Declaration of Payam Shahian. Shahian’s declaration does not discuss settlement
in any capacity. The attached Exhibit 1 is an e-mail chain between Rivera’s
counsel and Nissan’s counsel, requesting timely overdue disclosures. (Mot.,
Shahian Decl. ¶ 6, Ex. 1.) Nissan’s counsel, in return, requested to serve
disclosure for 50 cases per month. (Ibid.) There is no discussion of
settlement or even discussion of Nissan’s counsel’s proposal. There is also no
evidence counsel otherwise replied to Nissan’s request. Additionally, the Court
notes, these e-mails are dated to December 2025, two months after initial
disclosures were due. (Ibid.) Any agreement made at that point would not
account for the already overdue responses.

Nissan also cites to multiple other court orders in other
lemon law cases by Rivera’s counsel against Nissan, in which those judges
denied both the motion and the request for sanctions. (Opp., Sweeney Decl. ¶¶
14-22, Ex. C-J.) The Court notes some of the applicable minute orders for these
cases do not provide information as to why sanctions were denied, while other
note the motions were taken off calendar. While multiple of these cases opted
not to impose sanctions due to informal resolution efforts, Nissan has provided
no evidence in this case to support any indication resolution efforts were
ongoing in this case or included an agreement to suspend discovery. Therefore,
the Court does not find there is evidence Nissan acted in good faith.

Accordingly, the Court imposes sanctions.

 

V.        CONCLUSION

Plaintiff Jose Rivera Rivera’s
motion to compel compliance with
section 871.26 is MOOT.

Plaintiff Luis Rivera’s request for sanctions
is GRANTED. Nissan's counsel is ordered to pay Rivera $2,500.00 in sanctions
within 15 business days.

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice.

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

Department 730

 

FAITH NATHANIEL BECK,

Plaintiff,

vs.

SAUGUS UNION SCHOOL DISTRICT, et al.,

Defendants.

Case No.:

26STCV02217

Hearing Date:

July 30, 2026

ORDER DEEMING DEFENDANTS' DEMURRER WITH MOTION TO STRIKE MOOT

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Faith Nathaniel Beck (“Beck”) reported believed misconduct by Defendant Charlotte Spainhower (“Spainhower”) to appropriate personnel. Following this report, Spainhower and agents of Defendant Saugus Union School District (“SUSD”) allegedly engaged in a retaliatory campaign against Beck. In addition, Defendants allegedly harassed Beck via misgendering and ‘deadnaming’ Beck. Beck sued Defendants for sixteen causes of action, including whistleblower retaliation, defamation, and gender-based harassment and discrimination.

On March 25, 2026, Defendants filed the instant demurrer with motion to strike.

On June 10, 2026, Beck filed a proposed amended complaint.

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10[1] states: “The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) The person who filed the pleading does not have the legal capacity to sue; (c) There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) There is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) The pleading is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, uncertain includes ambiguous and unintelligible; and (g) In an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct.”

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.  (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.”  (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.)  As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations.  (Ibid.)  The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.”  (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

Pursuant to section 472, a party may amend its pleading once without leave of court. If the amendment is filed after a demurrer or motion to strike is filed, it must be filed and served no later than the date for filing an opposition to the demurrer or motion to strike. (Ibid.)

III.      DISCUSSION

Pursuant to section 472, a party may amend its pleading once, without leave of court, following the filing of a demurrer or motion to strike, so long as it is filed and served by the date for filing an opposition to those pleadings. The date for filing an opposition to demurrer is nine court days prior to the hearing on the pleadings. (§ 1005, subd. (b).)

Beck’s initial complaint was filed on January 22, 2026. On March 13, 2026, Defendants filed a special motion to strike (or “anti-SLAPP motion”). Following the filing of the motion, Beck filed a proposed FAC on April 8, 2026. As clarified by the Court, the April FAC was never operative. (See May 1, 2026, minute order.) A plaintiff may not freely amend a complaint when an anti-SLAPP motion is pending; Beck needed to seek leave of court to file an amended complaint, at that time. As Beck did not seek leave, the April FAC never amended the initial complaint.

Following the Court’s ruling on the anti-SLAPP motion, granting it in part, Defendants filed the instant demurrer with motion to strike to the original complaint. Upon filing of a demurrer, Beck was entitled to file one amended complaint pursuant to the timeline outlined in section 472. As this demurrer was always set to be heard on July 30, 2026, the amended complaint needed to be filed by July 17, 2026, to be deemed operative without leave of court. Beck filed the “Second Amended Complaint” (“June FAC”) on June 10, 2026, far before the deadline.

Although labeled the “Second Amended Complaint,” the June FAC is effectively the first amended complaint, as Beck’s April FAC should have been rejected upon filing. The mere fact a party has filed a rejected amendment does not preclude them from otherwise utilizing their one-time statutory right to amend under section 472. As Beck’s June FAC was filed and served prior to the opposition due date, it is the new operative complaint. Defendants’ demurrer with motion to strike to the original complaint is MOOT.

As there was potential dispute as to the operative complaint, the Court orders Defendants’ time to file a demurrer is to run from the hearing on this matter.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

Defendants' demurrer to the initial complaint is MOOT.

Beck is to give notice.

ORDER DENYING MOTION TO SEAL

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Faith Nathaniel Beck (“Beck”) reported believed misconduct by Defendant Charlotte Spainhower (“Spainhower”) to appropriate personnel. Following this report, Spainhower and agents of Defendant Saugus Union School District (“SUSD”) allegedly engaged in a retaliatory campaign against Beck. In addition, Defendants allegedly harassed Beck via misgendering and ‘deadnaming’ Beck. Beck sued Defendants for sixteen causes of action, including whistleblower retaliation, defamation, and gender-based harassment and discrimination.

On June 17, 2026, Beck filed the instant motion to seal. SUSD filed an opposition. Beck filed a reply brief.

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Under California Rules of Court rule 2.550, subdivision (d)[1] “[t]he court may order that a record be filed under seal only if it expressly finds facts that establish: (1) There exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) The overriding interest supports sealing the record; (3) A substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and (5) No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest.”

 

III.      DISCUSSION

Beck requests the Court seal the “unredacted versions of the following exhibits: Exhibit A – Clinical Summary; Exhibit B – Health Conditions Summary; and Exhibit C – Medication List.” (Mot., p. 2 ¶¶ 4-8.)

It is unclear from the text of the moving papers as to what papers these exhibits are related. According to Defendants’ opposition, these papers are not connected to any motion, application or other recognized court filing. (Opp., p. 2 ¶¶ 1-10.) Beck’s reply further confirms these is no separate motion to which these exhibits are related but are only offered in connection with the instant motion to seal.

Pursuant to rule 2.550, subdivision (a)(3), rules regarding sealed records do not apply to discovery motions, records filed or lodged in connection with discovery motions or proceedings. As no explanation is provided in what capacity, outside of the motion to seal, these exhibits are relevant, the Court cannot determine these exhibits are not offered in connection with discovery motions or proceedings.

Even when evaluated on the merits, the Court does not find a basis to allow these documents to be filed under seal absent context. A record may only be filed under seal if the Court finds all required elements are established. (Rule 2.550, subd. (d).) A moving party must demonstrate: (1) There exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) The overriding interest supports sealing the record; (3) A substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and (5) No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest. (Ibid.)

Although Beck’s medical privacy may constitute an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access, satisfying the first element, no other element is satisfied. The overriding interest does not support sealing the records, as the records have been offered by Beck, devoid of context. Beck has chosen to put the documents at issue without offering said documents in support of any actual motion other than the motion to seal. There is no substantial probability Beck’s right to medical privacy will be prejudiced if the records are not sealed because there is no indication these records will be publicly available outside of this motion. The proposed sealing is not narrowly tailored, because Beck requests these documents be redacted without any context. Less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest of protecting Beck’s medical privacy by simply not entering the records into the Court record, in any capacity, absent other relevant motion work.

The Court denies the motion, evaluating both procedural issues and merit.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

Plaintiff Faith Nathaniel Beck’s motion to seal is DENIED.
