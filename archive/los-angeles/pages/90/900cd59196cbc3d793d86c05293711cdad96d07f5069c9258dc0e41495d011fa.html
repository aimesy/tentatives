Case Number: 23STCV18147    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: 732

 


Fermina Chavez v. General Motors, LLC, et al.

 

Tuesday, August 4, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 23STCV18147

 

OPPOSED

 

___________________________________________________________________

 

Plaintiff Fermina Chavez’s Motion for Attorney Fees
___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is a lemon law action. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff Fermina Chavez (Plaintiff) in June 2023 purchased a 2023 Cadillac Lyriq with express warranties. (Complaint ¶ 7.) The vehicle contained or developed various defects. (Complaint ¶ 8.)  

 

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on August 1, 2023, alleging three causes of action:

 

1.      Breach of Express Warranty

2.      Breach of Implied Warranty

3.      Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (b)

 

This court on March 25, 2024, this court granted in part Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses to requests for production and interrogatories from Defendant.

 

Plaintiff filed a notice of settlement on October 15, 2025.

 

Plaintiff filed the present motion for attorney fees on May 4,  2026.

 

Defendant filed an opposition on May 21, 2026.

 

Plaintiff filed a reply on May 28, 2026.

 

Analysis:

 

I.                   MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES

Parties to litigation must generally bear their own attorney’s fees, unless they otherwise agree. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1021.) However, the Song-Beverly Act provides for the award of attorneys’ fees to prevailing plaintiffs as follows:

If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney's fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.

(Civ. Code § 1794, subd. (d).)

“It is well established that the determination of what constitutes reasonable attorney fees is committed to the discretion of the trial court, whose decision cannot be reversed in the absence of an abuse of discretion.”  (Melnyk v. Robledo (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 618, 623.)  In exercising its discretion, the court should consider a number of factors, including the nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, the skill required in handling the matter, the attention given, the success or failure, and the resulting judgment.  (See id.) 

In determining the proper amount of fees to award, courts use the lodestar method.  The lodestar figure is calculated by multiplying the total number of reasonable hours expended by the reasonable hourly rate.  “Fundamental to its determination . . . [is] a careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney . . . in the presentation of the case.”  (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 48 (Serrano III).)  A reasonable hourly rate must reflect the skill and experience of the attorney.  (Id. at p. 49.)  “Prevailing parties are compensated for hours reasonably spent on fee-related issues.  A fee request that appears unreasonably inflated is a special circumstance permitting the trial court to reduce the award or deny one altogether.”  (Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 635 (Serrano IV).)  The Court in Serrano IV also stated that fees associated with preparing the motion to recover attorneys’ fees are recoverable.  (See id. at p. 624.)

Plaintiff Fermina Chavez (“Plaintiff”) seeks $45,020.34 in attorney fees and costs, representing a lodestar fee award of $35,685.00, plus $3,267.64 in costs and expenses, plus a 1.2x multiplier. (Motion at pp. 9–10.) The requested fee lodestar is based on 73.8 hours of attorney work at rates ranging from $375 per hour to $575 per hour, with a mean hourly rate of $483.54. (Mizrahi Decl. Exh. B.)

As an initial matter, no lodestar multiplier is appropriate in this case. “Once the court has fixed the lodestar, it may increase or decrease that amount by applying a positive or negative ‘multiplier’ to take into account a variety of other factors, including the quality of the representation, the novelty and complexity of the issues, the results obtained, and the contingent risk presented.” (Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank., N.A. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 819, 833.) The court’s file and the billings submitted in support of the motion demonstrate that this lemon law case, which did not proceed to trial, was not particularly novel or complex.

Defendant General Motors, LLC (“Defendant”) opposes the motion by reference to several charges in the billing records that it identifies as excessive and unreasonable. (Opposition at pp. 4–7.) Much of these objections are directed to the template-based nature of much of this lemon law litigation. But the fees charged for a number of matters, including drafting the complaint, preparing and responding to discovery, and preparing and defending the present motion for fees, already appear to account for the templates used, and require no reduction. Certain other objections, such as that Plaintiff ought not to be able to claim fees for client-onboarding or post-settlement arrangement of the surrender of the subject vehicle, are not supported by authority. And Defendant’s contention that Plaintiff billed 5.6 hours to respond to discovery only with boilerplate objections is not borne out by the responses that Defendant presents to the court. (Opposition at pp. 5–6; Gruszecki Decl.  Exh. G.)

Certain charges do appear to warrant reduction, however. Plaintiff claims 11.1 hours and $4,162.50 in connection with two template motions to compel further which were granted in part on March 25, 2024. (Mizrahi Decl. Exh. B.) Reducing this charge to a more reasonable eight ours yields a reduction in the lodestar of $1,162.50.

Additionally, Plaintiff seeks a substantial amount of time associated with motions to compel compliance with deposition subpoenas directed to third-parties. While this time is reasonably compensable, substantial time — 3.7 hours and $1,387.50 in block-billed entries  — are billed for “research” into the subpoena process. (Mizrahi Decl. Exh. B, 4/16/2025, 4/18/2025.) Given the routine nature of this case and the discovery at issue, this amount too is properly reduced from the lodestar.

Accordingly, the lodestar is reduced by $2,550.00, from $35,685.00 to $33,135.00.

Defendant also objects to various cost items. Many of these items, such as filing fees and jury fees, are facially compensable under Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5. However, Plaintiff’s request for $1,077.33 in costs for courtesy copies is not reasonably included. While such costs may be compensable where reasonably necessary to the litigation, the costs here are neither dated nor labeled according to what matter they correspond, and do not appear to be “reasonably necessary for the conduct of the litigation.” (Gorman v. Tassajara Development Corp. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 44, 77.) Thus the cost award is properly reduced by $1,077.93.

The motion for attorney fees is thus GRANTED for fees in the amount of $33,135.00 and for costs in the amount of $2,189.41.

Case Number: 24STCV17175    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: 732

 


Nicolas Moore v. Volkswagen Group of America, Inc., et al.

 

Tuesday, August 4, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 24STCV17175

 

OPPOSED

 

___________________________________________________________________

 

Plaintiff Nicolas Moore’s Motion for Attorney Fees
___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is a lemon law action. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff Nicolas Moore (“Plaintiff”) in June 2023 leased a 2023 Audi e-tron with express warranties. (Complaint ¶¶ 2, 5, 8.) The vehicle contained or developed various defects, which the manufacturer has not been able to remedy. (Complaint ¶ 10.)  

 

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on July 11, 2024, alleging three causes of action:

 

1.      Civil Code § 1793.1, subd. (d)

2.      Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (b)

3.      Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability

 

Defendant Volkswagen Group of America, Inc. filed an answer on August 12, 2024.

 

A notice of settlement was filed on February 25, 2026.

 

Plaintiff filed the present motion for attorney fees on May 4,  2026.

 

Defendant filed an opposition on May 21, 2026.

 

Plaintiff filed a reply on May 28, 2026.

 

Analysis:

 

I.                   MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES

Parties to litigation must generally bear their own attorney’s fees, unless they otherwise agree. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1021.) However, the Song-Beverly Act provides for the award of attorneys’ fees to prevailing plaintiffs as follows:

If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney's fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.

(Civ. Code § 1794, subd. (d).)

“It is well established that the determination of what constitutes reasonable attorney fees is committed to the discretion of the trial court, whose decision cannot be reversed in the absence of an abuse of discretion.”  (Melnyk v. Robledo (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 618, 623.)  In exercising its discretion, the court should consider a number of factors, including the nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, the skill required in handling the matter, the attention given, the success or failure, and the resulting judgment.  (See id.) 

In determining the proper amount of fees to award, courts use the lodestar method.  The lodestar figure is calculated by multiplying the total number of reasonable hours expended by the reasonable hourly rate.  “Fundamental to its determination . . . [is] a careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney . . . in the presentation of the case.”  (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 48 (Serrano III).)  A reasonable hourly rate must reflect the skill and experience of the attorney.  (Id. at p. 49.)  “Prevailing parties are compensated for hours reasonably spent on fee-related issues.  A fee request that appears unreasonably inflated is a special circumstance permitting the trial court to reduce the award or deny one altogether.”  (Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 635 (Serrano IV).)  The Court in Serrano IV also stated that fees associated with preparing the motion to recover attorneys’ fees are recoverable.  (See id. at p. 624.)

Plaintiff Nicolas Moore (“Plaintiff”) seeks attorney fees following the settlement of his Song Beverly lemon law claims in the amount of $28,930.00 in attorney fees and $902.61 in costs. (Motion at p. 14.) This amount reflects a lodestar of 54 hours of attorney and paralegal work by six timekeepers (three attorneys and three paralegals/law clerks) by  ranging in hourly rates from $285 per hour to $695 per hour. (Sannipoli Decl. ¶ 50, Exh. A.) This yields a mean hourly rate of about $535.74 across the lodestar.

Defendant in opposition argues that the hourly rates charged by Plaintiff’s counsel in this matter are excessive. (Opposition at pp. 5–8.) Defendant also objects to various charges submitted by Plaintiff’s counsel as unreasonable, excessive, or ill-explained. (Opposition at pp. 8–11.) Defendant finally argues that Plaintiff seeks costs not compensable under Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5. (Opposition at p. 11.)

Defendant’s argument as to hourly rates is persuasive. The “reasonable hourly rate [used to calculate the lodestar] is the product of a multiplicity of factors ... the level of skill necessary, time limitations, the amount to be obtained in the litigation, the attorney's reputation, and the undesirability of the case.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1139.) Plaintiff’s attorneys here charge rates of $695 and $665/$685 (depending on the year)  for partners and $595/$585 (depending on the year) for one other senior attorney. (Sannipoli Decl. ¶ 50.) But based on the relative simplicity of this case, the skill necessary to prosecute it, and the survey of fees charged by other attorneys of similar experience presented by Plaintiff’s counsel (Sannipoli Decl. ¶ 53), the court concludes that the reasonable hourly rates charged in this matter ought to be $550 for partners, and $425 for other attorneys. Applying this figure yields the following immediate reduction to the lodestar:

·         Brian J. Bickel: $145

·         Jordan K. Sannipoli: $2,496.50

·         Joshua J. Youssefi: $2,257.00

(Sannipoli Decl. Exh. B.) Thus applying reasonable hourly rates to the request yields a reduction of $4,898.50.

Defendant’s other objections to the fee lodestar are hit-and-miss. Defendant objects to a single block-billed “flat rate” charging entry describing a variety of work performed during the onboarding of Plaintiff as a client, amounting to 7.3 hours of attorney work. (Opposition at pp. 8–9; Sannipoli Decl. Exh. A.) Yet the billing entry describes the work performed in reasonable detail, all of it is compensable under Civil Code § 1794, subd. (d), and Plaintiff offers a persuasive justification for this form of billing in reply, noting that different billing practices are used for client intake prior to formal retention as counsel. (Reply at pp. 4–5.) “Trial courts retain discretion to penalize block billing when the practice prevents them from discerning which tasks are compensable and which are not.” (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1010.) The use of the practice here does not prevent the court from undertaking this task. Nor does Defendant offer a persuasive explanation as to why the eleven hours sought in connection with settlement negotiations ought not to be compensated. (Opposition at pp. 9–10.)

However, the amount sought in connection with the present fee motion ought to be reduced. Review of the billings presented by Plaintiff indicates that he seeks reimbursement for 16.3 hours of attorney work associated with the fee motion or the auditing of billing records associated therewith, plus 9.1 hours of related paralegal or law clerk work. (Sannipoli Decl. Exh. A.) In light of Plaintiff’s admitted use of templates for the present motion (Reply at p. 7), and in light of the relatively straightforward path of this litigation — with no exchange of written discovery, depositions, or motion practice prior to settlement — the amount of attorney work associated with the present motion is reasonably reduced to eight hours, and the paralegal time to four hours, yielding a reduction of $4,675.50.

Accordingly, the requested fee lodestar is reduced from $28,930.00 to $19,356.00.

Defendant’s objections to cost items, meanwhile, are not persuasive. Although Defendant distinguishes costs for service of process — compensable as of right under Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5 — from costs for service of documents generally (Opposition at p. 11), these latter costs are not prohibited by section 1033.5, and are in any event compensable as “expenses” under Civil Code § 1794, subd. (d).

Thus the motion for attorney fees is GRANTED for fees in the amount of $19,356.00 and costs in the amount of $902.61.

Case Number: 24STCV26192    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: 732


Raul R. Constantino v. General Motors, LLC, et al.

 

Tuesday, August 4, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 24STCV26192

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Defendant General Motors, LLC’s Motion for Summary Judgment.

 ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is a lemon law action. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff Raul Constantino (“Plaintiff”) purchased a 2020 Chevrolet Bolt on February 24, 2020, with an express written warranty. (Complaint ¶¶ 7–8.) The vehicle was delivered with or developed defects during the warranty period that the manufacturer has been unable to repair within a reasonable number of attempts. (Complaint ¶¶ 8–9.)

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on October 9, 2024, alleging five causes of action:

1.    Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (d)

2.    Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (b)

3.    Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (a)(3)

4.    Breach of Express Written Warranty

5.    Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability

On April 7, 2026, this court granted in part Plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses to requests for production of documents.

On April 16, 2026, this court denied Defendant’s motion to dismiss

Defendant filed the present motion for summary judgment on May 13, 2026.

Plaintiff filed an opposition on July 24, 2026.

Defendant filed a reply on July 24, 2026.

Analysis

 

I.                    OBJECTIONS

 

Plaintiff submits several objections to the evidence in support of Defendant General Motors, LLC’s motion for summary judgment. Objection No. 2 to the declaration of Ryan Bowton is SUSTAINED, as Bowton’s testimony concerning whether Plaintiff opted out of the class settlement is offered without any foundation in personal knowledge. The remaining objections are OVERRULED, as the facts stated merely introduce judicially noticeable court records, repeat the allegations of Plaintiff’s own Complaint, or else are directed to the characterization of facts in Defendant’s separate statement, and thus are not a proper subject of evidentiary objection.

 

II.                 MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

A party may move for summary judgment “if it is contended that the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (a).) “[I]f all the evidence submitted, and all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law,” the moving party will be entitled to summary judgment.  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) A motion for summary adjudication may be made by itself or as an alternative to a motion for summary judgment and shall proceed in all procedural respects as a motion for summary judgment.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (f)(2).)

 

The moving party bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact, and if he does so, the burden shifts to the opposing party to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850; accord Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)

 

Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto.  (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at 850.)  The plaintiff may not rely upon the mere allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists but, instead, shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto.  (Ibid.)  To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence.  (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

 

Defendant General Motors, LLC (“Defendant”) moves for summary judgment on the claims of Plaintiff Raul R. Constantino (“Plaintiff”) because they are barred by the class action settlement and release finally approved on December 22, 2025, in the case In re Chevrolet Bolt EV Battery Litigation, No. 2:20-cv-13256-TGB-CI (E.D. Mich.) (“The Class Action”). (Motion at pp. 5–6.)

 

““[A] judgment pursuant to a class settlement can bar [subsequent] claims based on the allegations underlying the claims in the settled class action. This is true even though the precluded claim was not presented, and could not have been presented, in the class action itself.” (Villacres v. ABM Industries Inc. (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 562, 586.) “Under the doctrine of res judicata, a valid, final judgment on the merits is a bar to a subsequent action by parties or their privies on the same cause of action.” (Id. at p. 575.) “The doctrine is applicable “if (1) the decision in the prior proceeding is final and on the merits; (2) the present proceeding is on the same cause of action as the prior proceeding; and (3) the parties in the present proceeding or parties in privity with them were parties to the prior proceeding.” (Id. at p. 577.)

 

Plaintiff in opposition argues that Defendant cannot assert the affirmative defense of res judicata based on the release because it failed to plead the defense in its answer, and further that Defendant’s separate statement fails to designate each cause of action to which the facts pertain. (Opposition at pp. 7–9.)

 

Both arguments fail. “[C]ourts generally have allowed an affirmative defense to be asserted for the first time in a motion for summary judgment “absent a showing of prejudice.” (Atkins v. St. Cecilia Catholic School (2023) 90 Cal.App.5th 1328, 1341.) Plaintiff articulates no prejudice resulting from the lack of any applicable defense pleaded in Defendant’s answer. And as Defendant notes, it did not possess the defense until the order finally approving the class action settlement and the judgment to be entered thereon in December 2025, after which it brought a motion to dismiss, followed by the present motion for summary judgment. Nor does Defendant’s separate statement need to delineate which causes of action are at issue, as Defendant’s arguments apply equally to each cause of action pleaded by Plaintiff.  Thus Plaintiff’s procedural challenges to the present motion do not warrant denial.

 

This is what the evidence before the court now shows. A final approval of settlement and judgment was entered in the Class Action on December 22, 2025. (Bowton Decl. Exh. C.) Per that order, the action involved defects in the battery system of Chevy Bolt vehicles nationwide. (Bowton Decl. Exh. C.) The court in its order divided the claims at issue in the class action into three categories: “(1) claims of fraudulent concealment, fraudulent misrepresentation, and violation of state consumer protection statutes; (2) federal and state warranty law claims; and (3) unjust enrichment claims. Plaintiffs seek to represent a putative nationwide class as to some claims and fourteen state subclasses.” (Bowton Decl. Exh. C at p. 3.) 

 

The class certified for settlement was defined as follows:

 

Any persons in the United States who purchased or leased, other than for resale, a 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2021, or 2022 Model Year Chevrolet Bolt built and shipped to a dealer on or before August 19, 2021 and who have not received a buyback of their vehicle from General Motors or a General Motors authorized dealer.

 

(Bowton Decl. Exh. C at p. 6.)

 

The settlement included a release stating as follows:

 

Upon the Effective Date, Plaintiffs and each Settlement Class Member, on behalf of themselves and any other legal entity or natural persons who may claim by, through, or under them, shall fully, finally and forever release, relieve, and discharge the Released Parties from and against any and all claims, demands, actions, suits, causes of action, allegations, rights, obligations, costs, losses, interests, debts, penalties, costs, liabilities, injunctive or declaratory relief, attorneys’ fees, and damages of any sort, known and unknown, suspected or unsuspected, fixed or contingent, now existing or hereafter, arising in whole or in part from or in connection with acts or omissions of any of the Defendants and their attorneys that were brought or could have been brought in this Action whether in law or in equity, in tort or contract, or arising under any nature or regulation.

 

(Bowton Decl. Exh. B, § 8.1.)  

 

Per the final approval order, preliminary settlement approval was granted on September 26, 2024. (Bowton Decl. Exh. C at p. 6.) The deadline for mailing in opt-out notices was February 24, 2025. (Bowton Decl. Exh. C at p. 8.) A fairness hearing was held on May 19, 2025, whereupon it was learned that around 5,000 vehicle owners had not been included in the notice list, and additional time was granted to permit their receipt of notice and an opportunity to opt out or object. (Id. at pp. 9–10.) In the meantime, the court heard motions from those who felt they had been omitted in error from the settlement administrator’s “exclusion report,” the list of those who had submitted valid opt-out requests. (Ibid.) A supplemental fairness hearing was held on September 29, 2025. (Id. at p. 10.) The settlement was finally approved on December 22, 2025. 

 

Based on the above, Defendant has shown the following undisputed material facts. First, Defendant has shown that Plaintiff was a member of the class at issue in the Class Action by virtue of his purchase and ownership of the subject vehicle, as alleged in the Complaint. Second, Defendant has shown that the class action settlement and release approved and ordered by the court encompasses the claims that Plaintiff alleges here. These claims include causes of action for violation of California warranty statutes, sounding in defects in the subject vehicle’s battery, causing it to overheat and potentially catch fire. (Complaint ¶ 8.) This comports with the claims at issue in the Class Action. (Bowton Decl. Exh. C at p. 2.) The release encompassed all claims “arising in whole or in part from or in connection with acts or omissions of any of the Defendants and their attorneys that were brought or could have been brought in this Action.” (Bowton Decl. Exh. B, § 8.1.) This encompasses Plaintiff’s claims here.

 

But as with its prior motion, Defendant does not demonstrate that Plaintiff was among those bound by the settlement through failure to opt out. This is the second motion in which Defendant raises the class action settlement as a bar to Plaintiff’s claims. In denying Defendant’s prior motion to dismiss, the court noted that Defendant relied on an “exclusion report” submitted with a declaration of the settlement administrator purporting to identify all persons who had opted out of the class, but that the final order that Defendant presented for judicial notice had relied on a different exclusion report, submitted after the first on November 10, 2025, which Defendant had not presented. (See 4/16/2026 Order; Bowton Decl. Exh. C at p. 43.)

 

Strangely, Defendant does not rectify the defect in the present motion. Instead of presenting the operative exclusion report, which was incorporated into the federal court’s order granting final approval of settlement, it presents no report at all, and submits the declaration of its own counsel, who states, “Based on GM’s records and information from the settlement administrator, Plaintiff did not submit a timely opt- out request.” (Bowton Decl. ¶ 5.) Plaintiff rightly objects to this assertion as lacking foundation in personal knowledge, and as being evidently based on hearsay.

 

Defendant in opposition argues that Plaintiff fails to present evidence that he opted out, and in fact presents the declaration of his counsel to the effect that he did not receive notice of the settlement, which ought to require a conclusion that he did not opt out of it. (Reply at pp. 4–5.) This argument is somewhat diminished by the fact that Plaintiff’s counsel has no foundation in personal knowledge for making any assertion concerning the notice received by his client. (Banayan Decl. ¶ 7.) It is further diminished when Defendant goes on to argue that the assertion of Plaintiff’s counsel is wrong, and that Plaintiff had actual knowledge of the settlement on March 6, 2026, in time to raise a belated request to be excluded from the settlement. (Reply at p. 6.) To the extent that Defendant argues that the court ought to infer the non-existence of any valid opt-out from Plaintiff’s failure to assert one, it is substantially vitiated by the counter-inference that if the operative exclusion report in fact omitted Plaintiff, then Defendant would have presented the report, particularly in light of the court’s prior order on the subject.  At present, the court lacks any evidentiary basis to embrace Defendant’s contention that the no opt out request was sent. And as Defendant is the one asserting the res judicata bar, the burden is on them to show that the bar applies: “A defendant who advances an affirmative defense to the plaintiff's claims bears the burden of proof on the defense.” (Hinerfeld-Ward, Inc. v. Lipian (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 86, 93.)

 

The motion is therefore DENIED.

Case Number: BC478744    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: 732


Ray W. Exley v. Barry Harlan, et al.

 

Tuesday, August 4, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: BC478744

 

UNOPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Cross-Complainant Lewit, Hackman, Shapiro, Marshall, & Harlan’s Motion to Substitute Personal Representative for Deceased Cross-Defendant, Allowing Action to be Continued against Personal Representative, and Allowing the Filing of a supplemental Cross-Complaint ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is a legal malpractice action. The Second Amended Complaint (SAC) alleges as follows. Plaintiff Ray W. Exley (Plaintiff) employed Defendant Evan E (Schenkel) to represent him in a marital dissolution action. (SAC ¶ 8.) Schenkel unreasonably overbilled Plaintiff and was negligent in his representation, failing to allege appropriate claims or conduct adequate discovery. (SAC ¶¶ 10–25.)

Plaintiff also employed Defendants Barry Harlan (Harlan) and the firm Lewitt, Hackman, Shapiro, Marshall, & Harlan (LHSMH) to represent him in the dissolution action and a concurrent civil action against Plaintiff’s ex-wife. (SAC ¶ 26.) Harlan and LHSMH negligently represented Plaintiff in the civil and dissolution action by failing to allege appropriate claims and failing to conduct adequate discovery. (SAC ¶¶ 27–28.)

Schenkel’s First Amended Cross-Complaint (FAXC) alleges that Plaintiff has failed to pay amounts owed for services rendered. (FAXC at p. 6.) Analysis:

 

I.                   MOTION FOR SUBSTITUTION OF DEFENDANT

 

 “[A] cause of action for or against a person is not lost by reason of the person's death, but survives subject to the applicable limitations period.” (Code Civ. Proc. 377.20, subd. (a).)

 

“On motion, the court shall allow a pending action or proceeding against the decedent that does not abate to be continued against the decedent's personal representative or, to the extent provided by statute, against the decedent's successor in interest, except that the court may not permit an action or proceeding to be continued against the personal representative unless proof of compliance with Part 4 (commencing with Section 9000) of Division 7 of the Probate Code governing creditor claims is first made.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 377.41.)

 

(a) An action or proceeding pending against the decedent at the time of death may not be continued against the decedent's personal representative unless all of the following conditions are satisfied:

 

(1) A claim is first filed as provided in this part.

 

(2) The claim is rejected in whole or in part.

 

(3) Within three months after the notice of rejection is given, the plaintiff applies to the court in which the action or proceeding is pending for an order to substitute the personal representative in the action or proceeding. This paragraph applies only if the notice of rejection contains a statement that the plaintiff has three months within which to apply for an order for substitution.

 

(b) No recovery shall be allowed in the action against property in the decedent's estate unless proof is made of compliance with this section.

 

(Prob. Code § 9370.)

 

Defendant and Cross-Complainant Lewit Hackman Shapiro Marshal & Harlan (“LHSMH”) seeks leave to substitute Keenan Brown, administrator of the estate of Decedent Plaintiff and Cross-Defendant Robert Exley, as cross-defendant, and for leave to file a supplemental cross-complaint under Code of Civil Procedure § 464 describing the grounds upon which suit may lie against Brown. Per the declaration of Stephen T. Holzer, LHSMH filed its creditor’s claim with Decedent’s estate on March 11, 2022, and received a notice of rejection on April 22, 2026, following Brown’s appointment on April 24, 2025. (Holzer Decl. ¶¶ 3–7.)

 

LHSMH has demonstrated compliance with Probate Code § 9370 as reuird by Code of Civil Procedure § 377.41. No opposition to the present motion has been filed.

 

Accordingly, the motion to substitute Keenan Brown for Decedent as Cross-Defendant, and to file a supplemental cross-complaint, is GRANTED.
