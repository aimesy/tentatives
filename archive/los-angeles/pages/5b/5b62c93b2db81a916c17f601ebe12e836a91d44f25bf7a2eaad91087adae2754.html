     CASE NO.: 25VECV03269 

 

ORDER GRANTING IN PART DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO TAX COSTS

 

I.               BACKGROUND 

Plaintiff David Christopher Snow (“Plaintiff”) brings this lemon law action against Defendant Polestar Automotive USA, Inc. (“Polestar”), the vehicle manufacturer, and Galpin 2: Electric B, LLC, d/b/a Polestar Los Angeles (“Galpin”) (jointly, “Defendants”), the authorized repair facility.

Defendants move to tax costs.

///

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On June 12, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Complaint alleging (1) Violation Of Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act - Breach Of Express Warranty; (2) Violation Of Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act - Breach Of Implied Warranty; and (3) Negligent Repair

On July 16, 2025, Defendants separately filed their Answers.

On June 16, 2026, Plaintiff dismissed Galpin.

On July 6, 2026, the Court entered judgment against Polestar consistent with Plaintiff’s acceptance of Polestar’s Offer to Compromise pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 998.[1]

On July 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Memorandum of Costs.

On August 6, 2026, Defendants filed the instant Motion to Tax Costs.[2]

On August 19, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

On August 25, 2026, Polestar filed a Reply.

III.         LEGAL STANDARD

The Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act authorizes recovery of costs “reasonably incurred by the buyer.” (Civ. Code § 1794(d).) “If the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary.” (Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal. App. 4th 761, 774.) “On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue, and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.” (Id.) 

“A prevailing party who claims costs must serve and file a memorandum of costs within 15 days after the date of service of the notice of entry of judgment or dismissal by the clerk under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5 or the date of service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, or within 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is first. The memorandum of costs must be verified by a statement of the party, attorney, or agent that to the best of his or her knowledge the items of cost are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case.” (Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).)

“If items on a memorandum of costs appear to be proper charges on their face, those items are prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses, and services are proper and necessarily incurred. [citations.] The burden then shifts to the objecting party to show them to be unnecessary or unreasonable.” (Doe v. Los Angeles County Dept. of Children & Family Services (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 675, 693.) “In ruling upon a motion to tax costs, the trial court's first determination is whether the statute expressly allows the particular item and whether it appears proper on its face. If so, the burden is on the objecting party to show the costs to be unnecessary or unreasonable.” (Foothill De Anza Cmty. College Dist. v. Emerich (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 11, 2930.) “Whether a cost item was reasonably necessary to the litigation presents a question of fact for the trial court.” (Ladas v. Cal. State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 774.) 

“[T]he mere filing of a motion to tax costs may be a ‘proper objection’ to an item, the necessity of which appears doubtful, or which does not appear to be proper on its face. [citation.] However, ‘[i]f the items appear to be proper charges, the verified memorandum is prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses and services therein listed were necessarily incurred by the defendant [citations], and the burden of showing that an item is not properly chargeable or is unreasonable is upon the [objecting party].’” (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131.)  

IV.          ANALYSIS

1.               Anticipated Fees from Item 1, Item 11, Item 15

Polestar argues Plaintiff cannot recover anticipated fees on its Memorandum of Costs, as only expenses actually incurred are recoverable. Polestar identifies the following costs: $60.00 for an “[Anticipated] Motion for Attorney Fees,” $650.00 for “[Anticipated] Deft Mtn to Tax Costs,” $650.00 for “[Anticipated] Pltf Mtn for Atty Fees,” $200.00 for “[Anticipated] Pltf Mtn for Atty Fees (Costs & Expenses),” and $100.00 for “[Anticipated] Deft Mtn to Strike/Tax Costs (Costs & Expenses). (See Mot. at 3:12-18,4:26-5:5.)

The Court will grant Polestar’s Motion to Tax costs of $1,660 comprising the anticipated costs. As Plaintiff acknowledges, he is only entitled to reasonably incurred costs and expenses pursuant to Civil Code § 1794(d). (See Opp. at 4:20.) Plaintiff cannot recover anticipated costs that had not yet been incurred and paid at the time the Memorandum of Costs was filed. Any future costs associated with this Motion or Motion for Attorneys Fees may be sought in the event the fee motion is filed.

The Court GRANTS Polestar’s Motion to Tax costs of $1,660 for anticipated costs.

2.               Item 14: Fees for electronic filing or service

“Fees for the electronic filing or service of documents through an electronic filing service provider if a court requires or orders electronic filing or service of documents” are expressly recoverable costs. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1033.5(a)(14).”

Polestar challenges costs of $668.20 in fees for electronic filing or service of documents through an electronic filing service provider on grounds they cannot determine whether the $668.20 reflects recoverable court-imposed filing fees or nonrecoverable electronic filing service-provider charges, such as convenience, processing, vendor, or service fees. (See Mot. at 5:10-13.)

Plaintiff argues Polestar does not properly object to this cost. (See Opp. at 9:14-18.) Plaintiffs nonetheless provide an itemization of the $668.20 cost and receipts. (See id., at 9:19-11:18; Declaration of Elizabeth Meehan filed August 19, 2026, ¶ 14, Ex. 5.)

Polestar contends Plaintiff seeks $74.75 in vendor charges imposed by One Legal and $385.00 courtesy-copy delivery charges which are not recoverable under § 1033.5(a)(14). (See Reply at 7:25-28, 8:26-27.) Polestar also argues the cost of delivering courtesy copies is a recoverable litigation cost as opposed to an ordinary overhead expense of practicing law.

The Court DENIES Polestar’s Motion to Tax these costs on grounds they are not explicitly recoverable under § 1033.5(a)(14). “[I]n enacting Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) the Legislature intended the phrase ‘costs and expenses’ to cover items not included in “‘the detailed statutory definition of ‘costs’” set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5.” (Warren v. Kia Motors Am., Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 42, 43 [“it is indisputable that Warren ‘reasonably incurred’ the $5,882 cost of the trial transcripts ‘in connection with the ... prosecution of [the] action.’”].) Polestar has not shown the costs they challenge were not reasonably incurred in connection with prosecuting this action.

Thus, the Court DENIES Polestar’s Motion to Tax costs from item 14.

V.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS in part Polestar’s Motion to Tax Costs in the amount of $1,660.00. Plaintiff to recover costs in the amount of $1,663.20.

          IT IS SO ORDERED.

ORDER GRANTING IN PART DEFENDANT JAB LOGISTICS LLC’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS, SET ONE

 

I.               BACKGROUND 

Plaintiff Martin Ortega Delgado (“Plaintiff”) filed this motor vehicle tort action against Defendants Jab Logistics LLC (“Jab”), ABC Transport LLC (“ABC”), Christopher E Figueroa Salguero (“Salguero”), and Carlos Rodriguez (“Rodrigeuz”). Plaintiff alleges Salguero negligently operated his vehicle and collided into Plaintiff’s vehicle. Plaintiff alleges Rodriguez was the registered owner of the trailer negligently operated by Salguero. Plaintiff alleges Jab and ABC were the registered owners of the tractor negligently operated by Salguero. Plaintiff alleges Salguero was working in the course and scope of his employment with Jab at the time of the subject collision.

Jab moves to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to its Requests for Production of Documents, Set One (“RFPs”).

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On January 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Complaint alleging (1) Motor Vehicle tort and (2) Negligence.

On April 3, 2026, Jab and Salguero filed an Answer.

On April 6, 2026, ABC filed an Answer and Cross-Complaint.

On August 4, 2026, Jab filed the instant Motion.

On August 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed an untimely Opposition. The Court will exercise its discretion to consider it.

On August 25, 2026, Jab filed a Reply.

III.         LEGAL STANDARD

A.             Request for Production of Documents  

In responding to a request for production of documents, a party must provide one of the following: (1) a legally adequate statement of compliance; (2) a statement of inability to comply; or (3) an objection to the particular demand. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.210(a).) [1] If any objections are made, they must: (1) “[i]dentify with particularity any document … falling within any category of item in the demand to which an objection is being made”; and (2) “[s]et forth clearly the extent of, and the specific ground for, the objection.”¿(Id. at § 2031.240(b).) A statement that the responding party is unable to comply with a particular demand “shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand”; “shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of the responding party”;¿ and “shall set forth the name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item.” (Id. at § 2031.230.)¿ 

A party who believes a responding party has served (1) an incomplete statement of compliance, (2) an inadequate, incomplete, or evasive statement of compliance, or (3) an unmeritorious objection can move for an order compelling further responses but must “set forth specific acts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (See id. at §§ 2031.310(a)-(b)(1).)  

IV.          MEET AND CONFER

Before bringing a motion to compel further responses to any discovery request, the moving party must make efforts to meet and confer in good faith and submit a declaration showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion. (See Code Civ. Proc. §§ 2016.040, 2031.310(b)(2), 2030.300(b), 2033.290(b).) Failure to make such attempt constitutes “misuse of discovery process”. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2023.010(i) [amended eff. 1/1/26].)  

“A reasonable and good-faith attempt at informal resolution…requires that counsel attempt to talk the matter over, compare their views, consult, and deliberate.” (Townsend v. Superior Court (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 1431, 1439.) In evaluating whether a meet and confer was reasonable and in good faith, “[a]n evaluation of whether, from the perspective of a reasonable person in the position of the discovering party, additional effort appeared likely to bear fruit, should also be considered.” (Clement v. Alegre (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1277, 1293-94.)  

Jab’s counsel attests she sent Plaintiff’s counsel a meet and confer letter and that Plaintiff’s counsel did not answer their scheduled meet and confer phone call twice. (See Declaration of Kayla K. Smith filed August 4, 2026 (“Smith Decl.”), ¶¶ 5, 7-9, Exs. C-D.) Thus, the Court finds Jab satisfied their meet and confer obligation and reminds Plaintiff’s counsel that failure to meet and confer is a sanctionable abuse of discovery pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure §§ 2023.010 and 2023.020.

V.             ANALYSIS

A.             No. 20

“Any and all documents concerning any insurance or disability claims you have made concerning the incident which is the subject of your Complaint in this action.” (Sep. Stmt. No. 20.)

Plaintiff asserts the following objections: insurance privacy under Valley Bank of Nevada v. Sup. Ct. (1975) 15 Cal.3d 652, 656, relevance, not reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence since it is not limited to payments for medical services, and collateral source rule. (See id.)

Jab argues insurance and disability claims for the subject injuries are relevant. Additionally, because Plaintiff has stated he may pursue a loss-of-future earnings claim, any disability payments for a diminished ability to work are relevant to damages. (See id.)

Plaintiff argues this Motion is based on Jab’s incorrect belief Plaintiff has reserved a wage loss claim while refusing to produce the documents that would allow Jab to test it. Plaintiff argues his verified, operative response to Form Interrogatory No. 8.1 states: “Plaintiff is not making a claim for loss wages.” (See Opp. at 3: 9-13; Declaration of Anita Keshishyan filed August 24, 2026 (“Keshishyan Decl.”), ¶ 3, Ex. 1.) Plaintiff also argues he has identified his automobile insurance carrier with the policy number and limits and his health insurance policy number. (See Opp., at 4:1-7.) Plaintiff reasserts that No. 20 is not limited to payments for medical treatment at issue and instead reaches every document including collateral source payments and private insurance information. (See id., at 10:13-20.)

Jab argues the documents may reflect prior injury and thus bear on causation, credibility of Plaintiff’s claimed injuries and extent of damages. (See Reply at 6:3-14.)

The Court finds further response to the disability claims portion of the request is not warranted. The Court notes the Complaint alleges Plaintiff has suffered wage loss. (See Compl., at 3, No. 11.) However, Plaintiff has sworn in his verified discovery response that he is not making a claim for lost wages. Thus, the Court finds Jab’s basis for compelling a further response as to Plaintiff’s disability claims/payments is not justified.

However, the Court finds further response with respect to the insurance claims portion of the request is warranted. Insurance claims relevant to the subject injury are discoverable. Whether Plaintiff has already disclosed his insurance policy number in other discovery responses has no bearing on this request, as he must still provide a code-complaint response to this request pursuant to § 2031.210(a)(1)(2). The Court therefore overrules Plaintiff’s objections. The collateral source rule does not bar discovery outright, however it is (1) a substantive rule of damages, under which a personal injury plaintiff can still recover full damages even though they already have received compensation from such collateral sources, and (2) a rule of evidence which precludes introduction of evidence of plaintiff being compensated by a collateral source unless there is a persuasive showing that such evidence is of substantial probative value for purposes other than reducing damages. (See Arambula v. Wells (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 1006, 1015.) Additionally, Plaintiff’s insurance objection pursuant to Griffith v. State Farm Mutual Auto Ins. Co. (1990) 230 Cal.App.3d 59, 65-71, is not applicable here. That case concerned claimants seeking to have an insurer disclose a third party’s policy limits, which is not the case here.

Thus, the Court GRANTS IN PART Jab’s Motion as to No. 20. 

B.             No. 21

“Any and all documents that evidence your earnings from January 1, 2019, through the present.” (Sep. Stmt. No. 20.)

Plaintiff’s response objects on grounds that he is “not presently asserting a loss of earnings claim at this time. Accordingly, this request violates Plaintiff’s right to privacy protected pursuant to the Cal. Const., Article I, §1.” (Id.) Plaintiff also asserts a tax privilege. (See id.)

Jab argues Plaintiff responded to Form Interrogatory No. 8.8 that he may have sustained a loss of income or earning capacity from the incident. Jab argues he cannot reserve a wage-loss claim while withholding the documents and thus his earnings from January 1, 2019, to the present are relevant. (Id.)

Plaintiff argues his response to No. 8.8, which asks whether Plaintiff “will lose income in the future as a result of the INCIDENT”, was “Unknown at this time.” (See Keshishyan Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 1.) Plaintiff argues he has affirmatively disclaimed any wage loss claim in the preceding interrogatory responses and this response means he cannot presently attribute any future income loss to the incident. (See Opp. at 7:14-19.) Plaintiff reasserts the request is irrelevant, overbroad, and subject to privacy and tax privileges. (See id., at 8:10-9:10.)

The Court finds further response to No. 21 is warranted. Although Plaintiff has sworn under oath he is not pursuing a claim for lost wages in response to Form Interrogatory 8.1, that only applies to lost wages up to the date of his verified response to that interrogatory. His response to Form Interrogatory 8.8 left open the possibility of pursuing lost wages after the date of his response. Thus, any responsive documents covering the period of time since that response is discoverable. Accordingly, this request is overbroad as framed and Plaintiff’s further response shall only cover the period of time between his verified response to Form Interrogatory No. 8.8 and the date of this Order.

The Court GRANTS IN PART Jab’s Motion as to No. 21.

C.             No. 24

“Any and all documents which support your contention that you have incurred property damage as a result of the incident which is the subject of your Complaint on file herein.” (Sep. Stmt. No. 24.)

Plaintiff asserted objections and “All non-privileged documents responsive to this request which are currently in Responding Party’s possession, custody or control will be produced. Attached hereto are copies of photographs related to the Subject Incident.” (Id.)

The Court finds no further response is warranted to No. 24 as Plaintiff’s substantive response is code compliant. (See Code Civ. Proc. §§ 2031.210(a)(1), 2031.220.)

The Court DENIES the Motion as to No. 24.

D.            No. 29

“Any and all documents that identify or describe your job duties for any and all jobs you had at the time of and since the incident alleged in the Complaint on file herein.” (Sep. Stmt. No. 29.)

Plaintiff responded “Objection. Plaintiff is not presently asserting a loss of earnings claim at this time. Accordingly, this request violates Plaintiff’s right to privacy protected pursuant to the Cal. Const., Article I, § 1.” (Id.)

Jab argues Plaintiff’s job duties bear on his claimed injuries, both because they show whether and how those injuries limit his activities and because they may reveal a mitigating factor for damages Plaintiff attributes solely to this collision. Jab also argues his stated potential loss-of-earning-capacity claim makes the information further relevant to damages. (See id.)

Plaintiff argues further response to No. 29 is not warranted for the same reasons set forth under No. 21. Additionally, Plaintiff argues Jab does not show good cause on the basis that his job duties may reveal another potential cause of his injuries. Moreover, Plaintiff has disclosed his employment in his verified interrogatory responses. (See Opp. at 9:11-18.)

Jab argues Plaintiff’s response to Form Interrogatory 2.6 is only that he is a baker for Vons. Jab argues it is entitled to job descriptions or employer records to explore its theory plaintiff’s occupational activities may constitute a contributing cause of claimed injures. (See Reply at 5:3-13.)

The Court finds further response is warranted to No. 29. The Court agrees Jab can discover information relevant to causation – and effect – of Plaintiff’s claimed injuries. The Court acknowledges the request encompasses personnel records, which are within the scope of the protection provided by the state and federal Constitutions.” (San Diego Trolley, Inc. v. Superior Court (2001) 87 Cal.App.4th 1083, 1097, overruled on other grounds; see also Board of Trustees v. Superior Court (1981) 119 Cal.App.3d 516, 530.) Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541, and Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1, 35, describe the applicable test when limiting discovery based on privacy interests. The three-pronged Hill test, applied to discovery disputes via Williams, proceeds as follows: 

The party asserting a privacy right must establish [1] a legally protected privacy interest, [2] an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and [3] a threatened intrusion that is serious. ([Hill,] at pp. 35–37 ... .) The party seeking information may raise in response whatever legitimate and important countervailing interests disclosure serves, while the party seeking protection may identify feasible alternatives that serve the same interests or protective measures that would diminish the loss of privacy. A court must then balance these competing considerations. (Id. at pp. 37–40 ... .)  

 

(Williams, 3 Cal.5th at p. 552.)  

Plaintiff has not established its privacy right under the Hill test. Moreover, because the request encompasses personnel records showing only Plaintiff’s job duties, the Court believes it unlikely Plaintiff could show a threatened, serious intrusion that outweighs Jab’s right to discover information that may support its theory. Additionally, the Court overrules Plaintiff’s tax privilege objection, as it is not implicated by this request, and his objection based on non-assertion of a loss of earnings claim. Nevertheless, in an abundance of caution regarding Plaintiff’s privacy interest in his personnel records, the Court orders the parties to submit a Proposed Stipulated Protective Order for the Court’s approval so that any documents produced in response to this request may be produced pursuant to it.

Thus, the Court GRANTS Jab’s Motion as to No. 29.

VI.          DISCOVERY SANCTIONS

The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further response to a demand, interrogatories, or requests for admission, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. (See Code Civ. Proc. §§ 2031.310(h), 2030.300(d), 2033.290(c).) 

Jab requests discovery sanctions in the amount of $3,660.00. This total comprises 9.5 hours to meet and confer and prepare the Motion, 2.5 hours to prepare the Reply and attend the hearing, and a $60.00 filing fee. (See Smith Decl., ¶ 11.) Counsel’s hourly rate is $300.00. (See id.)

Plaintiff requests discovery sanctions of $2,100.00.

The Court declines to award sanctions as both parties acted with substantial justification in making and opposing the Motion.

VII.       CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS IN PART Jab’s Motion to Compel Further Responses as to RFP Nos. 20 as to insurance claims, 21, and 29, which shall be responded to pursuant to a Stipulated Protective Order. The Court DENIES the Motion as to No. 24. Plaintiff is ordered to provide further code complaint responses on or before September 18, 2026. The Court denies the parties’ requests for discovery sanctions.

          IT IS SO ORDERED.
