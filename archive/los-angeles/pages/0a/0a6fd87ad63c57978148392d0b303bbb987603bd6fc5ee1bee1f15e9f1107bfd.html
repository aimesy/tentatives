Case Number: 25CHCV00915    Hearing Date: September 14, 2026    Dept: F47

Dept. F47

Date: 9/14/26                                                      TRIAL DATE: 8/16/27

Case #25CHCV00915

 

MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES

(Requests for Production of Documents, Set 1)

 

Motion filed on 3/2/26.

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff Kyunga Kim

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendant Kia America

NOTICE: ok

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: An order striking Defendant Kia America’s objections and compelling further responses to Plaintiff Kyunga Kim’s Requests for Production of Documents, Set 1, Nos. 10, 16-19, 21-22, 27, 40-43 and 56.

 

RULING: The motion is granted as set forth below. 

 

SUMMARY OF FACTS & PROCEDURAL HISTORY

 

This action arises out of Plaintiff Kyunga Kim’s (Plaintiff) purchase of a 2022 Kia Carnival (the Vehicle) on or about 1/29/22.  The Vehicle was manufactured and/or distributed by Defendant Kia America (Defendant).

 

Plaintiff contends that the Vehicle suffers from defects, including the Engine Defect, and that Defendant has been unable to repair the Vehicle within a reasonable number of attempts.  Plaintiff also alleges that Defendant knew the Vehicle suffered from prevalent defects, but still refused to repurchase the Vehicle in violation of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act. 

 

On 3/14/25, Plaintiff filed this action against Defendant alleging causes of action for: (1) Violation of Civil Code 1793.2(d); Violation of Civil Code 1793.2(b); (3) Violation of Civil Code 1793.2(a)(3); (4) Breach of Express Written Warranty – Civil Code 1791.2(a) and Civil Code 1794 and (5) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability – Civil Code 1791.1 and 1794.  On 4/14/25, Defendant answered the complaint.  On 7/11/25, a Stipulated Protective Order was entered in the action. 

 

On 11/7/25, Plaintiff served Defendant with Requests for Production of Documents, Set 1, by mail along with a meet and confer letter regarding electronically stored information (ESI), search terms, and a litigation hold .  (Beck Decl., Ex.11, 13).  On 12/15/25, Defendant served responses and on 2/20/26 produced some documents.  (Id., Ex.12).  On 12/15/25, Plaintiff began the meet and  confer process regarding Defendant’s responses which Plaintiff found to be deficient during which the parties agreed to extend the deadline to file a motion to compel further responses until 3/2/26.  (Id., Ex.14-17).  The parties were unable to informally resolve the discovery dispute.  (Beck Decl. ¶33).

 

On 3/2/26, Plaintiff filed and served the instant motion seeking an order striking Defendant’s objections and compelling further responses to Plaintiff’s Requests for Production of Documents, Set 1, Nos. 10, 16-19, 21-22, 27, 40-43 and 56.  Defendant has opposed the motion and Plaintiff has filed a reply to the opposition. 

 

ANALYSIS

 

First, the Court notes that the notice of motion indicates that Requests 10, 16-19, 21-22, 27, 40-43 and 56 are at issue which are the same Requests set forth in the Separate Statement.  (See Notice of Motion, p.ii:7).  However, the Memorandum of Points and Authorities also includes references to Requests 7, 8, 34 and 37.  (See Motion, p.1:14, p.13:8).  Additionally, the proposed order also includes Request 7, but does not include Request 40.  The ruling on the motion only addresses the Requests set forth in the notice of motion and accompanying separate statement.   

 

A party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the action or to the determination of any motion made in the action, if the matter itself is admissible or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.  See CCP 2017.010.  Doubts as to relevance are generally resolved in favor of allowing discovery.  Colonial Life & Accident Insurance Co. (1982) 31 C3d 785, 790.

 

CCP 2031.310(a) provides:

 

“On receipt of a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, the demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to the demand if the demanding party deems that any of the following apply:

(1) A statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete.

(2) A representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive.

(3) An objection in the response is without merit or too general.”

 

To prevail on a claim brought under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (the Act), a plaintiff has the burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, several elements, including nonconformity of a vehicle that substantially impaired its use, value or safety; presentation of the vehicle to the manufacturer or authorized representative for repair; and failure to repair the defect after a reasonable number of attempts.  Ibrahim (1989) 214 CA3d 878, 886-887; Oregel (2001) 90 CA4th 1094, 1101; Lundy (2001) 87 CA4th 472, 478. 

 

Additionally, under the Act, a buyer may recover damages and other relief when there has been a breach of the implied warranty of merchantability if the vehicle was sold with a known defect.  Civil Code 1794(a); Mexia (2009) 174 CA4th 1297, 1304-1305.  Further, a buyer may be entitled to a civil penalty upon a showing that the manufacturer willfully failed to abide by any of its obligations under the Act.  Civil Code 1794(c).

 

The subject requests seek documents relevant to Plaintiff’s claims against Defendant under the Act including issues related to Defendant’s good faith compliance with the Act.  As such, Plaintiff has provided sufficient facts to establish the requisite good cause for production of the subject documents.  See CCP 2031.310(b)(1).

 

The subject requests seek documents relating to: (1) Defendant’s internal investigation and analysis of the defects affecting the Vehicle and Defendant’s refusal to repurchase the Vehicle despite Defendant’s prior knowledge of such defects (Requests 16-19, 21-22, 56) and (2) Defendant’s warranty and vehicle repurchase policies, procedures and practices (Requests 10, 27, 40-43).

 

Contrary to Defendant’s assertion in the opposition that it “has either agreed to produce documents or stated that it was unable to comply based on a diligent search and reasonable inquiry,” Defendant’s responses to the subject requests show otherwise.  (See Opposition, p.2:10-12).  Defendant has responded to all of the requests at issue, except for Request 22, solely with objections.  In response to Request 22, Defendant responded with objections and then stated that without waving the objections, it “will produce technical service bulletins, recalls, and campaigns, if any, related to the issues alleged by Plaintiff to exist with the subject vehicle.”

 

Information regarding vehicles other than Plaintiff’s vehicle is relevant to the subject matter of this action as it could assist Plaintiff in proving Defendant’s willful violation of the Act.  Documents responsive to such requests may reasonably lead to the discovery of information as to the nature and duration of the defects, Defendant’s knowledge of the defects, and Defendant’s inability to repair the defects.  While cases relied on by Plaintiff (i.e., Donlen (2013) 217 CA4th 138 and Doppes (2009) 174 CA4th 967) may not involve the exact circumstance before this Court, they are sufficient to show that the requested information could itself be admissible or lead to the discovery of admissible evidence in this case.  Moreover, in a recent case involving Defendant, the Court of Appeal found that the production of such documents is warranted.  See Higginson (2026) 118 CA5th 316, 325-326.   

 

Defendant’s objections lack merit and/or have not been properly supported.  Defendant has the  burden of justifying its objections to the requests at issue.  Fairmont Insurance Co. (2000) 22 C4th 245, 255; Coy (1962) 58 C2d 210, 220-221; Williams (2017) 3 C5th 531, 541-542.  Defendant has failed to meet this burden.  As set forth above, the documents sought are relevant and/or could lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. Therefore, the requests are not irrelevant or overbroad.  The requests are also not vague and/or ambiguous considering the definitions provided by Plaintiff.  Defendant has also failed to establish that responding to any of the subject requests would be overly burdensome or oppressive.  To the extent that documents are being withheld on the basis of attorney-client privilege and/or work product doctrine, Defendant is required to provide a privilege log.  See CCP 2031.240(c).  Defendant has failed to establish that the documents sought contain confidential and/or propriety information and that dissemination of the information would result in injury.  See Nativi (2014) 223 CA4th 261, 318.  To the extent that any of the requests actually seek confidential, proprietary and/or trade secret information, the Court finds that the protective order entered in this case should adequately protect Defendant’s interests. 

 

Defendant’s argument that Assembly Bill 1755 somehow limits Plaintiff’s scope of discovery is without merit.  Any limits imposed by the bill are lifted after mediation.  See CCP 871.26.  Here, the parties mediated on 11/5/25 and the instant discovery requests were served on 11/7/25.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The motion is granted.  With regard to Requests 10, 16-19, 21-22, 27, 40-43 and 56, Defendant’s objections are stricken and Defendant is ordered to serve further written responses and to produce documents in accordance therewith within the next 30 days.

 


Case Number: 26CHCV01011    Hearing Date: September 14, 2026    Dept: F47

Dept. F47

Date: 9/14/26

Case #26CHCV01011

 

MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

Motion filed on 5/1/26.

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant FCA US, LLC

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff Esmeralda Sepulveda and Jose Gonzalez Luna

NOTICE: ok

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: An order compelling arbitration of this controversy and staying this action. 

 

RULING: The motion is granted.

 

SUMMARY OF FACTS & PROCEDURAL HISTORY

 

This action arises out of Plaintiff Jose Gonzalez Luna’s (Luna) lease of a 2024 Jeep Wrangler (the Vehicle) on or about 1/13/24.  (See Complaint ¶16; Dhillon Decl., Ex.A).  Also on 1/13/24, Defendant FCA US, LLC (Defendant) gave Luna a written warranty regarding the Vehicle.  (Complaint ¶16).  Plaintiffs Jose Gonzalez Luna and Esmeralda Sepulveda* (collectively, Plaintiffs) contend defects and nonconformities to warranty in the Vehicle manifested themselves within the warranty period.  (Id. ¶17).  Additionally, Plaintiffs contend that they delivered the Vehicle to an authorized Defendant repair facility for repair and Defendant was unable to conform the Vehicle to the applicable express warranty after a reasonable number of repair attempts and that Defendant failed to promptly replace the Vehicle or make restitution in accordance with the Song-Beverly Act.  (Id. ¶¶18-22).

 

On 3/11/26, Plaintiffs filed this action against Defendant for: (1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Express Warranty and (2) Violation of Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Implied Warranty.  On 4/23/26, Defendant requested that Plaintiffs submit their claims to arbitration pursuant to the Agreement to Arbitrate signed by Luna.  (Dhillon Decl., Ex.B, C).  Plaintiffs did not respond.  (Dhillon Decl. ¶5).  *Plaintiff Esmeralda Sepulveda’s standing to bring the claims in the complaint is unclear as Luna was the sole lessee under the lease agreement.  (Dhillon Decl., Ex.A).

 

On 4/27/26, Defendant answered the complaint. 

 

On 5/1/26, Defendant filed and served the instant motion seeking an order compelling arbitration of this controversy and staying this action.  Plaintiffs have opposed the motion and Defendant has filed a reply to the opposition. 

 

ANALYSIS

 

Plaintiffs’ evidentiary objections, numbers 1-7, are overruled.

 

California and federal law favor enforcement of valid arbitration agreements.  See Sanchez (2015) 61 C4th 899; Armendariz (2000) 24 C4th 83; AT&T Mobility, LLC (2011) 563 U.S. 333, 338; AT&T Technologies, Inc. (1986) 475 U.S. 643, 649-650; CCP 1281, CCP 1281.2; 9 U.S.C. §2.

 

Here, during the leasing of the Vehicle, Luna was presented with and signed a separate Agreement to Arbitrate which informed Luna that any party, including Defendant or Luna, could elect to have any claims related to the Vehicle decided by arbitration.  (Dhillon Decl., Ex.B).   Plaintiffs’ claims relate to the Vehicle; therefore, they are subject to the arbitration agreement.  Despite Defendant’s request that Plaintiffs arbitrate the disputes in this action pursuant to the arbitration agreement, Plaintiffs have not agreed to do so.

 

The party opposing arbitration has the burden of establishing that the arbitration agreement is unenforceable, or to establish some defense to the enforcement of the agreement.  Crippen (2004) 124 CA4th 1159, 1165.  Plaintiffs have failed to meet their burden.

 

Plaintiffs’ argument that Defendant has failed to provide competent evidence that an arbitration agreement that covers Plaintiffs’ claims exists is without merit.  With regard to motions/petitions to compel arbitration, normal authentication procedures are not required.  See Condee (2001) 88 CA4th 215, 218-219.  Once Defendant alleged that agreement to arbitrate existed, the burden shifted to Plaintiffs to prove otherwise.  Id.

 

Defendant submitted the standalone “Agreement to Arbitrate” which Luna signed.  (Dhillon Decl., Ex.B).  The Agreement to Arbitrate provides that both parties agreed that they would resolve any dispute through a neutral, binding arbitration process, not by a court action.  Id.   The  Dhillon and Portillo declarations properly authenticate the Agreement to Arbitrate.  A document may be authenticated by a witness who is familiar with the record-keeping system used to generate it.  See Evidence Code 1414; People v. Goldsmith (2014) 59 C4th 258, 266–268.   Xinia Portillo identifies her role as custodian of records, the source of the documents (Defendant’s business records), reviews the documents, and explains how they were obtained in the regular course of business.   Gonzalez (2026) 831 F.Supp.3d 871, 877-878.  Plaintiffs have not submitted any evidence indicating that Luna did not receive the Agreement to Arbitrate or that he did not sign it. 

 

Any objection based on hearsay fails as the Agreement to Arbitrate falls within the business-records exception and is independently admissible as an operative contractual document.  The  document is offered for the fact that the agreement exists, not for the truth of its contents. As noted above, Plaintiffs offer no contrary evidence and Luna does not deny signing the Agreement to Arbitrate. 

 

Contrary to Plaintiffs’ assertion the Agreement to Arbitrate is not unconscionable. 

In order for an agreement to be unenforceable based on grounds of unconscionability, the agreement must be both procedurally and substantively unconscionable.  Sanchez (2015) 61 C4th 899, 910.  Procedural unconscionability focuses on oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power and substantive unconscionability focuses on overly harsh or one-sided results.  Id.; Roman (2009) 172 CA4th 1462, 1479; Pinnacle Museum Tower Association (2012) 55 C4th 223; Armendariz (2000) 24 C4th 83, 114, 117; Baltazar (2016) 62 C4th 1237, 1248.

 

Here, there was no surprise as the Agreement to Arbitrate was a clearly designated, separate agreement that was signed by lessee, Luna.  (Dhillon Decl., Ex.A, B).  Additionally, the arbitration agreement contained an opt-out provision that allowed Luna 30-days to opt-out of the agreement after signing.  (Dhillon Decl., Ex.B).  As such, it is not an unconscionable contract of adhesion. 

 

The Agreement to Arbitrate is not substantively unconscionable.  The terms of the agreement  state that both parties agree to arbitrate their claims, Defendant will bear the costs of the arbitration, elects the American Arbitration Association under its Consumer Arbitration Rules as the arbitration forum, and indicates that the arbitration would take place in the County of Luna’s residence.  (Dhillon Decl., Ex.B). 

 

Plaintiffs’ argument that the Agreement to Arbitrate is void due to lack of consideration also fails.  Plaintiff received the express written warranty issued by Defendant for the Vehicle and now seeks to enforce that warranty.  Plaintiffs argue that Defendant made “affirmations, promises, and descriptions of the goods sufficient to create an express warranty through advertisement of the warranties to the general public.”  However, the same express warranties Plaintiff cites contain the arbitration provision which they now seek to avoid.  Plaintiffs cannot pick and choose which terms of the warranty they seek to enforce.  Plaintiffs also incorrectly argue that Defendant had already provided the express warranty before Luna leased the Vehicle.  Contrary to Plaintiffs’ assertion, there is no evidence that Defendant provided the express written warranty before the lease of the vehicle.  Additionally, mutual promises to arbitrate also constitute adequate consideration for an arbitration agreement.  See Garner (2020) 52 CA5th 619, 625; Baker (2008) 159 CA4th 884, 895.  Therefore, even if Defendant had not offered a warranty contract as consideration for Luna’s Agreement to Arbitrate, the subject agreement would be supported by consideration based on the parties’ mutual obligation to arbitrate their disputes.  Further, under California law, there is a presumption that written agreements are supported by adequate consideration.  Civil Code 1614-1615.  Plaintiffs have presented no evidence to rebut this presumption. 

 

Finally, Plaintiffs’ argument that Luna did not enter into a direct Agreement to Arbitrate with Defendant lacks merit.  Defendant has provided evidence of a stand alone Agreement to Arbitrate which provides that Defendant and Luna agree to arbitrate their disputes concerning the Vehicle.  (Dhillon Decl., Ex.B).  If Plaintiffs contend that Agreement to Arbitrate is not enforceable because Defendant did not sign it, the same would be true for the warranty itself which is the basis for Plaintiffs’ claims.    

 

CONCLUSION

 

The motion is granted.  This action is stayed pending the conclusion of the arbitration.  CCP 1281.4.
