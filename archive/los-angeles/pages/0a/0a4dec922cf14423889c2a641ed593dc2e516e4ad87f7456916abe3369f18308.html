Patel Burica & Associates v. Jason Lin, et al.,

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff's Supplemental/Second Amended Motion for Terminating, Issue, Evidentiary and Monetary Sanctions is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART.

The request for terminating sanctions is DENIED.

The request for issue sanctions is GRANTED in part. The Court hereby orders issue sanctions that (1) JKL's annual gross income for each year for which no financial data has been produced is deemed to be the average of the years for which some financial data exists; AND (2) Defendants are precluded from offering evidence or argument that they did not possess or use PBA's software, programs, and macros to generate their design product.

Monetary sanctions are GRANTED in the amount of $68,615.50 against Defendants Jason Lin and JKL Structural Engineering, Inc., jointly and severally, payable to Plaintiff's counsel within 30 days of notice of this order.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

The Complaint in this action was filed on May 15, 2020. The First Amended Complaint was filed on February 16, 2021.

On September 8, 2025, Plaintiff Patel Burica & Associates filed a Motion for Terminating Sanctions.

On February 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Amended Motion for Terminating Sanctions.

On March 19, 2026, the Court heard the Amended Motion and ordered Defendants to produce all previously ordered documents by April 17, 2026, as kept in the ordinary course of business; ordered Plaintiff to report on compliance by April 24, 2026; and set an Informal Discovery Conference for April 30, 2026.

On April 30, 2026, the Court held an Informal Discovery Conference and ordered Defendants to produce, by May 15, 2026, all documents previously ordered through the end of 2025, including QuickBooks data in native format, bank statements, proposals, invoices, and all software and macros previously or currently in Defendants' possession. The Court continued the Motion to August 3, 2026 and continued trial to November 2, 2026.

On June 2, 2026, the Court held a further Informal Discovery Conference, again ordered production by June 19, 2026, ordered Defendants to serve verified supplemental responses explaining the loss or destruction of any unproduced material, and set a compliance hearing for June 26, 2026.

On June 25, 2026, Defendants served amended supplemental responses.

On June 26, 2026, the Court held a further Informal Discovery Conference and ordered supplemental briefing on the Motion.

On July 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Supplemental/Second Amended Motion for Terminating, Issue, Evidentiary and Monetary Sanctions, with the Supplemental Declaration of Noel E. Macaulay and a Compendium of previously filed material.

On July 21, 2026, Defendants filed a Supplemental Opposition and the Declaration of Jason Lin.

Motion for Sanctions

Plaintiff Patel Burica & Associates, Inc. moves the Court for an Order imposing terminating, issue or evidentiary sanctions against Defendants Jason Lin and JKL Structural Engineering, Inc., along with monetary sanctions in the amount of $68,615.50 against Defendants and/or their counsel, on the grounds that Defendants failed to comply with this Court's Orders of March 19, 2026, April 30, 2026 and June 2, 2026, and the prior order of the predecessor court, requiring production of the software, programs and macros, financial records, client contracts, and project files at issue, and further destroyed evidence they were on notice to preserve.

Legal Standard

If a party fails to obey a court order compelling it to provide a discovery response, “the court may make those orders that are just, including the imposition of an issue sanction, an evidence sanction, or a terminating sanction . . . In lieu of or in addition to this sanction, the court may impose a monetary sanction . . . .” (CCP §§ 2030.290(c), 2030.300(e), 2031.300(c), 2031.320(c).)

“The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct. The court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney who advised that assertion, or on both. If a monetary sanction is authorized by any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (CCP § 2023.030(a).)

“The court may impose an issue sanction ordering that designated facts shall be taken as established in the action in accordance with the claim of the party adversely affected by the misuse of the discovery process. The court may also impose an issue sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from supporting or opposing designated claims or defenses.” CCP § 2023.030(b).

“The court may impose an evidence sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from introducing designated matters in evidence.” CCP § 2023.030(c).

“Dismissal is a proper sanction to punish the failure to comply with a rule or an order only if the court's authority cannot be vindicated through the imposition of a less severe alternative.” (Rail Services of America v. State Comp. Ins. Fund (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 323, 331. See also Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsystem (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 262, 279-80 (“A decision to order terminating sanctions should not be made lightly. But where a violation is willful, preceded by a history of abuse, and the evidence shows that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with the discovery rules, the trial court is justified in imposing the ultimate sanction.”); R.S. Creative, Inc. v. Creative Cotton, Ltd. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 486, 496 (“The power to impose discovery sanctions is a broad discretion subject to reversal only for arbitrary, capricious, or whimsical action.

It is a commonly stated axiom that discovery sanctions “should be appropriate to the dereliction and should not exceed that which is required to protect the interests of the party entitled to but denied discovery.”¿¿Deyo v. Kilbourne¿(1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 793. Discovery sanctions are not to be imposed for punishment, but instead are used to encourage fair disclosure of discovery to prevent unfairness resulting for the lack of information.¿Electronic Funds Solutions, LLC v. Murphy¿(2005) 134 Cal.App.4th 1161, 1183;¿McGinty v. Sup. Ct.¿(1994) 26 Cal.App.4th 204, 214;¿Midwife v. Bernal¿(1988) 203 Cal.App.3d 57, 64, superseded on other grounds as stated in¿Kohan v. Cohan¿(1991) 229 Cal.App.3d 967, 971.

Before issuing a sanction, a trial court “should consider both the conduct being sanctioned and its effect on the party seeking discovery and, in choosing a sanction, should attempt to tailor the sanction to the harm caused by the withheld discovery.” Doppes v. Bentley Motors, Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 967, 992; Caryl Richards, Inc. v. Superior Court (1961) 188 Cal.App.2d 300, 304. A violation of one discovery order is sufficient for the imposition of sanctions pursuant to CCP § 2023.030. See¿Collison & Kaplan v. Hartunian¿(1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 1611, 1620-1621.

Discussion

Following four separate IDCs on the issues, on June 26, 2026, this Court observed that the process had “gone as far as it could with what Jason Lin was willing to produce” and ordered the parties to supply supplemental, amended briefings, on the instant Motion for Sanctions.

Plaintiff contends that terminating, issue, and monetary sanctions are warranted for Defendants’ continued failure to produce documents despite this Court’s orders. Namely, Plaintiff's position is that, despite the Court's months of intervention, Defendants still has not produced the software, macros, or programs (admitted in 2021 verified responses to exist), has provided no genuine explanation for their disappearance, has produced QuickBooks data that omitted the pre-2019 years and Excel financials Lin admitted destroying, has produced empty early-period project folders, and has never produced a single contract.

In opposition, Defendants contend that the PBA files were on a computer discarded in 2016, a 2019 hard-drive crash corrupted files that then failed to migrate through successive cloud accounts (leaving the empty folders), old drives were destroyed to prevent identity theft rather than to spoliate, and paper proposals were lost in home moves. Defendants represented that Lin had produced over 45 gigabytes of native files since March 2026 and served amended supplemental responses on June 25, 2026 clarifying that the original PBA materials were no longer in his possession.

Terminating Sanctions

The record before the Court amply supports the conclusion that Defendants’ conduct across the life of this case has been dilatory and, at points, obstructive. The multi-year history of unfulfilled promises to produce and the continued failure, despite four IDCs, to produce court-ordered software, macros, contracts, and complete financial records are serious and reflect a pattern of abuse.

Nevertheless, the Court declines to impose the ultimate sanction at this juncture. Numerous cases hold that severe sanctions (i.e., terminating sanctions or evidentiary sanctions) for failure to comply with a court order are allowed only where the failure was willful. (See R.S. Creative, Inc. v. Creative Cotton, Ltd. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 486, 495; Vallbona v. Springer (1996) 43 Cal.App.4th 1525, 1545; Biles v. Exxon Mobil Corp. (2004) 124 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1327).

“Discovery sanctions must be tailored in order to remedy the offending party's discovery abuse, should not give the aggrieved party more than what it is entitled to, and should not be used to punish the offending party.” (Karlsson v. Ford Motor Co. (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1202, 1217.) “Dismissal is a proper sanction to punish the failure to comply with a rule or an order only if the court's authority cannot be vindicated through the imposition of a less severe alternative.” (Rail Services of America v. State Comp. Ins. Fund (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 323, 331. See also Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsystem (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 262, 279-80 (“A decision to order terminating sanctions should not be made lightly. But where a violation is willful, preceded by a history of abuse, and the evidence shows that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with the discovery rules, the trial court is justified in imposing the ultimate sanction.”).)

Terminating sanctions are a last resort, and the incremental structure of the Discovery Act counsels that lesser, tailored remedies be tried before a party is deprived of a trial on the merits. Since new counsel associated in and the Court began actively supervising production in March 2026, Defendants have made substantial, albeit still incomplete, productions. That movement, though it does not excuse the earlier conduct or the remaining gaps, distinguishes this record from one of willful, total defiance.

Under the circumstances, the Court does not find that Defendants’ failure to respond was willful such that extreme sanctions such as terminating sanctions are warranted at this time. “The discovery statutes evince an incremental approach to discovery sanctions, starting with monetary sanctions and ending with the ultimate sanction of termination.” (Doppes v. Bentley Motors, Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 967, 992.)

Issue Sanctions

Plaintiff alternatively requests four issue sanctions: (1) deeming all JKL design work product from 2015–2021 to have been generated by, derived from, and based upon material taken from PBA; (2) deeming JKL’s income for the years without financial data to be the average of the years for which some data exists; (3) deeming that, from 2015 through 2023 (or the present), Defendants used PBA’s software, programs, and macros to generate their design product; and (4) deeming the value of that software equal to PBA’s cost of creating it.

Issue sanctions may be imposed “ordering that designated facts shall be taken as established in the action in accordance with the claim of the party adversely affected by the misuse of the discovery process. The court may also impose an issue sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from supporting or opposing designated claims or defenses.”¿(Code of Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (b).)¿

The sanction must be tailored to the harm caused by the withheld discovery. It may restore the party seeking discovery to the position it would have occupied had the discovery been produced; it may not place that party in a better one, and it may not resolve a genuinely disputed merits question as a proxy for punishment. (Victor Valley, supra, 91 Cal.App.5th at pp. 1141–1148; Padron, supra, 16 Cal.App.5th at p. 1259.)

Here, the Court is inclined to find that issue sanctions are warranted. Defendant's conduct is not a matter of isolated omission or inadvertent gaps in a large production. The materials at issue were demanded in 2020, made the subject of verified agreements to produce in 2021, ordered produced by the predecessor court in 2023, and ordered produced three more times by this Court in 2026 on March 19, April 30, and June 2. Across all of these hearings, Defendant has still not fully complied with the requested production. On this record, the misuse of the discovery process is willful, and a remedy beyond fees is warranted. The Court addresses each of Plaintiff’s requested issue sanctions in turn:

Issue Sanction Request Nos. 1, 4

The Court does not find these sanctions to be reasonably tailored to the harm caused by the withheld discovery. Request No. 1 would conclusively establish the central liability question across a six-year span, every project, and every customer, without regard to any nexus to the materials actually withheld. That is a de facto terminating sanction on liability. (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 793; Padron, supra, 16 Cal.App.5th at p. 1259.) Request No. 4 would similarly fix the measure of damages as a matter of law, displacing expert testimony and cross-examination on a disputed valuation question.

Sanction Requests Nos. 1 and 4 are therefore DENIED as framed.

Issue Sanction Request No. 2

Unlike Requests Nos. 1 and 4, the income-averaging sanction of Request No. 2 does not adjudicate liability, does not adopt Plaintiff's valuation theory, and does not import any figure of Plaintiff's choosing. Defendants' objection that averaging overstates early-year revenue, given JKL's growth,

is not available to the party who failed to produce the records. The risk of uncertainty falls on the spoliator, not on the party deprived of proof.

Accordingly, JKL's annual gross income for each year without produced financial data is deemed to be the average of the years for which some data exists.

Sanction Request No. 2 is GRANTED.

Issue Sanction Request No. 3

As to Request No. 3, the Court declines Plaintiff's "through the present" framing given Defendants' acquisition of commercial software, but is inclined to impose a narrower sanction.

The Court will impose an order precluding Defendants from offering evidence or argument that they did not possess or use PBA’s software, programs, and macros to generate their design product.

Sanction Request No. 3 is GRANTED, in part.

Monetary Sanctions

Monetary sanctions are GRANTED in the full amount requested, $68,615.50, against Defendants Jason Lin and JKL Structural Engineering, Inc., jointly and severally, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 2023.010, 2023.030(a), and 2031.320. Defendants failed to comply with orders compelling production, and monetary sanctions are mandatory in that circumstance absent substantial justification or unjust circumstances. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2023.030, subd. (a), 2031.320, subd. (b).)

The Court finds the lodestar to be reasonable. Counsel sets forth hourly rates of $350 for counsel and $85 for paralegal time (Supplemental Macaulay Declaration ¶¶ 31-32), and the 190 hours incurred on sorting the April 2024 production. The $6,000 attributable to reply papers and the August 3, 2026 hearing has now been incurred and is likewise reasonable. Defendants have not shown substantial justification, and the sustained pattern of non-compliance forecloses any finding that the imposition of sanctions would be unjust. The Court does not impose sanctions against Defendants' present counsel, whose association has coincided with materially improved compliance.

The Court reminds Defendants that if a lesser sanction fails to curb misuse, a greater sanction is warranted; continuing misuses of the discovery process warrant incrementally harsher sanctions until the sanction is reached that will curb the abuse. (Doppes v. Bentley Motors, Inc. (2009) 174 Cal. App. 4th 967, 992.)

Monetary sanctions are GRANTED in the amount of $68,615.50.

Conclusion

Plaintiff's Supplemental/Second Amended Motion for Terminating, Issue, Evidentiary and Monetary Sanctions is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART as follows:

1. The request for terminating sanctions is DENIED.

2. The request for issue sanctions is GRANTED in part. The Court hereby orders issue sanctions that (1) JKL's annual gross income for each year for which no financial data has been produced is deemed to be the average of the years for which some financial data exists; AND (2) Defendants are precluded from offering evidence or argument that they did not possess or use PBA's software, programs, and macros to generate their design product.

3. Monetary sanctions are GRANTED in the amount of $68,615.50 against Defendants Jason Lin and JKL Structural Engineering, Inc., jointly and severally (exclusive of his counsel), payable to Plaintiff's counsel within 30 days of notice of this order.

Hurtado et al., v. Pomona Valley Hospital et al., 

TENTATIVE RULING

The motion is GRANTED. The May 11, 2026 supplemental designation is stricken, and the opinion testimony of Drs. Macho and Lubow is excluded at trial.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On October 1, 2021, Plaintiff John Louis Hurtado, by and through his successor-in-interest, filed this action against Defendant Pomona Valley Hospital Medical Center and others.

On February 27, 2026, Defendant served a Demand for Exchange of Expert Witness Information in advance of a May 11, 2026 trial date.

On March 23, 2026, the parties served their respective expert witness designations.

On May 11, 2026, Defendant served a Supplemental Designation of Expert Witnesses.

On May 13, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant Motion to Strike Defendant's Tardy Supplemental Expert Witness Designation.

No opposition has been filed.

Legal Standards

CCP § 2034.300 requires the Court to, on objection of any party who has made a complete and timely compliance with Section 2034.260, “exclude from evidence the expert opinion of any witness that is offered by any party who has unreasonably failed to do any of the following: (a) List that witness as an expert under Section 2034.260. (b) Submit an expert witness declaration. (c) Product reports and writing of expert witnesses under Section 2034.270. (d) Make that expert available for a deposition under Article 3 (commencing with Section 2034.410).”

Motion to Strike Expert Designation

As an initial matter, Plaintiffs may invoke section 2034.300, as they served a complete designation and declaration on the March 23, 2026 exchange date. (Ex. D.) On the record presented, Plaintiffs' compliance is complete and timely, and they may invoke section 2034.300.

Plaintiffs first challenge Defendant’s supplemental designation as untimely. The Court agrees that it is. Section 2034.280(a) requires any supplemental list to be served within 20 days after the exchange. Here, the May 11, 2026 supplemental designation was not authorized by section 2034.280, as the 20-day window closed on or about April 12, 2026, and Defendant served its designation 49 days after the exchange.

Further, the designation also exceeds the substantive scope of a supplemental designation. A supplemental list may reach only subjects covered by an adverse party's expert on which the supplementing party "has not previously retained an expert to testify." (§ 2034.280(a).) Here, Defendant had already retained experts on the very subjects Drs. Macho and Lubow are offered to address. Dr. Josephson was designated on standard of care, causation, damages, wound-related care, and the reasonableness of medical costs. (Motion, Exh. E, p. 7). Nurse Higelin was likewise designated on the wound-care standard of care. Section 2034.280 does not permit a party to add further experts on subjects it already covered in its initial exchange.

Because the supplemental designation was neither timely nor within the statute's authorized scope, it did not operate to "list" Drs. Macho and Lubow as experts under section 2034.260. (§ 2034.300(a).)

Conclusion

The motion is GRANTED. The May 11, 2026 supplemental designation is stricken, and the opinion testimony of Drs. Macho and Lubow is excluded at trial.

Logan v. King et al.,

TENTATIVE RULING

The Motion to Seal is GRANTED.

The Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED.

The Motion for Summary Adjudication is GRANTED as to the seventh and eighth causes of action, and DENIED as to the first, second, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, ninth, tenth, and eleventh causes of action and as to Plaintiff's request for punitive damages.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On December 9, 2021, Plaintiff Elisabeth Logan filed the operative Complaint in this action.

On December 29, 2025, Defendants Zachary King and King Studio, LLC filed a Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication of Plaintiff's Complaint.

On December 30, 2025, Defendants filed a Motion to Seal Documents Attached to the Declaration of Nannina Angioni.

On March 13, 2026, Plaintiff filed her Opposition.

On March 20, 2026, Defendants filed a Reply.

Motion to Seal

Defendants move under California Rules of Court, rules 2.550 and 2.551 for an order sealing Exhibits D, E, G, I, J, K, L, M, O, P, and Q to the Declaration of Nannina L. Angioni filed in support of the summary judgment motion.

“Unless confidentiality is required by law, court records are presumed to be open.” (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 2.550(c).) However, a party may move to seal records pursuant to California Rules of Court, rules 2.550-2.551. “A party requesting that a record be filed under seal must file a motion or an application for an order sealing the record. The motion or application must be accompanied by a memorandum and a declaration containing facts sufficient to justify the sealing.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551(b)(1).) The parties requesting a record be sealed must lodge it with the court. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551(b)(4).) “The court must not permit a record to be filed under seal based solely on the agreement or stipulation of the parties.” (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 2.551(a).)

To grant an order pursuant to California Rules of Court, rules 2.550 and 2.551, the Court must expressly find facts that establish: (1) There exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) The overriding interest supports sealing the record; (3) A substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and (5) No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d).)

Having reviewed the Exhibits, the Court finds that each falls within one or more of the following three categories, and that an overriding interest exists as to each.

Exhibits I, J, K, M, and Q consist of invoice amounts paid to Plaintiff (Exh. I), Plaintiff’s annual pay by year (Exh. J), payment statements and pay issued to Plaintiff in 2020 (Exh. K), email communications and attached invoices regarding 2020 invoicing and payments (Exh. M), and excerpts of Plaintiff’s wage statements (Exh. Q). Exhibit D is King Studio’s 2017 annual performance review of Plaintiff — a personnel record in which Plaintiff retains a recognized privacy interest. Exhibits E, L, and P are internal and inter-party communications reflecting King Studio’s operational decision-making, brand and agency relationships, and its positions in negotiating Plaintiff’s engagement terms.

The privacy interests of non-party employees and contractors in their own compensation and personnel information are substantial and are entitled to protection independent of any interest asserted by Defendants. (Board of Trustees v. Superior Court (1981) 119 Cal.App.3d 516, 526 [privacy interest in confidential personnel files]; El Dorado Savings & Loan Assn. v. Superior Court (1987) 190 Cal.App.3d 342, 345 [privacy interest of a nonparty employee in his personnel file].) The Court finds an overriding privacy interest as to this category, and that the interest supports sealing.

Exhibit G is the 2019 Independent Contractor Agreement between King Studio and Logan Media Group, LLC. Exhibit O comprises Plaintiff’s proposed terms and conditions under which she sought to provide services to King Studio for 2020. Both documents set out King Studio’s commission structures, percentage rates for brand-deal, usage-renewal, talent-fee, speaking, and budget-packaging work, the allocation of crew and production costs, and the studio’s pre-negotiated rate practices. Disclosure would place King Studio at a competitive disadvantage in future negotiations with brands, agencies, and talent, and whose confidentiality is expressly protected by the agreements themselves. The Court finds an overriding commercial-confidentiality interest as to this category, and that the interest supports sealing.

There is a substantial probability that the interests will be prejudiced if the Exhibits are not sealed. The material is not otherwise available to the public, each page bears a confidentiality legend, Defendants have not previously placed the material in the public record, and Plaintiff has herself lodged the identical 2019 Independent Contractor Agreement and 2017 annual review conditionally under seal in connection with her opposition. The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored, extending only to some of the exhibits to the Angioni Declaration and leaving the memorandum of points and authorities, the separate statement, the deposition excerpts, and the balance of the evidentiary record in the public file. Finally, no less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interests. Redaction would not serve here because the protected content is not confined to discrete passages. The compensation figures and pay comparisons are coextensive with the invoices, payment statements, and wage statements themselves; the commission percentages and deal terms constitute virtually the entirety of Exhibits G and O; Exhibit D is confidential in its entirety; and the business-strategy content of Exhibits E, L, and P is interwoven throughout rather than confined to discrete passages.

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED. Exhibits D, E, G, I, J, K, L, M, O, P, and Q to the Declaration of Nannina L. Angioni are ordered sealed.

MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

Defendants Zachary King and King Studio, LLC move for summary judgment, or in the alternative summary adjudication, as to each of Plaintiff’s first through eleventh causes of action, and Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages.

As a threshold matter, the Court notes that the Motion frames twelve issues addressing the first through eleventh causes of action and punitive damages. The Complaint pleads a twelfth cause of action for PAGA penalties. The Motion does not address this cause of action.

Summary judgment is therefore DENIED, and the Court proceeds issue by issue below.

Evidentiary Objections

Defendants submit evidentiary objections to the Declarations of Morgan Malinics, Elisabeth Logan, Shelby Jacobsen Robinson, and Vina Nguyen, submitted in support of Plaintiff’s Opposition to the Motion for Summary Judgment.

The objections are OVERRULED.

Legal Standard

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial.¿(Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843). In analyzing such motions, courts must apply a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent's claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.”¿(Hinesley¿v.¿Oakshade¿Town Center¿(2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294).¿ Thus, summary judgment or summary adjudication is granted when, after the Court’s consideration of the evidence set forth in the papers and all reasonable inferences accordingly, no triable issues of fact exist and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.¿(CCP § 437c(c);¿Villa v.¿McFarren¿(1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 733, 741).¿

As to each claim as framed by the complaint, the party¿moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate an essential element.¿(Scalf¿v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc.¿(2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520). Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.”¿(Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc.¿(2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389). A motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication must be denied where the moving party's evidence does not prove all material facts, even in the absence of any opposition or where the opposition is weak.¿(See¿Leyva v. Superior Court¿(1985) 164 Cal.App.3d 462, 468-469;¿Salesguevara¿v. Wyeth Labs., Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 379, 384, 387.)¿¿¿

Once the¿moving¿party has met the burden, the burden shifts to the opposing party¿to show via specific facts that a triable issue of material facts exists as to a cause of action or a defense thereto.¿(CCP § 437c(p)(2)). When a party cannot establish an essential element or defense, a court must grant a motion for summary adjudication.¿(CCP § 437c(p)(1)-(2)).¿

First Cause of Action: Harassment (Issue No. 1)

The elements of a harassment claim under FEHA are: (1) plaintiff belongs to a protected group; (2) plaintiff was subject to harassment; (3) the harassment complained of was based on plaintiff's membership in the protected group; (4) the harassment was sufficiently severe or pervasive so as to alter the conditions of employment and create an abusive working environment; and (5) respondeat superior. (Jones v. Department of Corrections & Rehabilitation (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 1367, 1377; Lewis v. City of Benicia (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1519, 1524; CACI No. 2521A.)

Defendants move for summary adjudication of the first cause of action on the grounds that that the claim is time-barred because all allegedly harassing conduct predates the three-year limitations period; and, that Plaintiff cannot prove a hostile work environment because the conduct was neither severe nor pervasive.

Timeliness

A verified complaint with the Civil Rights Department must be filed within three years of the alleged unlawful practice. (Gov. Code § 12960(e)(5).)

In support, Defendants provide evidence that Plaintiff filed her administrative complaint on or about May 20, 2021. (Angioni Decl., Exh. A [Complaint, Exh. A].) The limitations period therefore reaches back to May 20, 2018. Defendants further provide evidence that the conduct Plaintiff attributes to Mr. Gauthier decreased in April 2016 when she obtained a private office and “ceased entirely” in early 2018. (Angioni Decl., Exh. R [Logan Depo. 155:19-156:2, 159:18-160:25]; Exh. S [King Depo. 171:20-22].) Defendants also provide evidence that Mr. King's clothing changes before September 2016 and that the alleged boxer shorts incident occurred in 2017. (Angioni Decl., Exh. R [Logan Depo. 245:16-246:15, 247:4-25].)

Defendant contends that their evidence shows that Mr. Gauthier’s conduct “ceased entirely” in 2018, however, the Logan Deposition provides only that the conduct “was better” because Mr. Gauthier “was no longer in the office every day” in mid-2018. (Angioni Decl., Exh. R [Logan Depo. 155:19-156:2, 159:18-160:25].) Defendants’ argument that “Mr. Gauthier stopped all [harassing] conduct” by March 2018 is not supported by the proffered evidence.Testimony that conduct "was better" because a coworker was in the office less often is not testimony that the conduct ended, and it does not establish that it ended before May 20, 2018. Accordingly, the Court is not inclined to find that Defendants have met their burden as to Mr. Gauthier’s conduct.

As to Mr. King's conduct, the Court finds that Defendants have met their initial burden. Both incidents predate May 20, 2018 on the face of the evidence Defendants cite.

Summary adjudication may be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action. (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(f)(1).) Because Defendants' timeliness showing does not reach the conduct attributed to Mr. Gauthier, that ground cannot dispose of the first cause of action irrespective of how the continuing violation question is resolved as to Mr. King's two incidents.

Defendants have not met their burden and the Motion cannot be granted on that ground.

Severe or Pervasive

In support, Defendants provide evidence that Mr. Gauthier at most occasionally poked or tickled Plaintiff, once invited her to a premiere which she declined without incident before accepting his tickets and attending with her boyfriend, and made generalized comments that were not sexual and not directed exclusively at her. (Angioni Decl., Exh. R [Logan Depo. 155:19-156:2, 159:18-160:25, 162:15-23, 162:24-164:13].) Defendants provide evidence that Mr. King's clothing changes occurred on a full set in front of the crew, that he was never fully naked, and that Plaintiff did not view the conduct as harassment. (Angioni Decl., Exh. R [Logan Depo. 245:16-246:15, 247:4-10].) Defendants further provide evidence that Plaintiff never reported harassment and instead praised the workplace. (Angioni Decl., Exh. R [Logan Depo. 35:7-14, 38:3-19, 137:22-140:4, 150:2-153:21]; Exh. C [email of Aug. 19, 2016]; Exh. E [emails expressing appreciation].)

Defendants have met their initial burden. Defendants provide Plaintiff's own testimony characterizing the physical contact as intermittent poking and tickling that decreased over time, the clothing changes as occurring openly on set and not perceived by her as harassment, and the underwear incident as a single occurrence. The burden shifts to Plaintiff.

In opposition, Plaintiff provides her testimony that Mr. Gauthier poked and tickled her at least weekly for years, commented regularly on her appearance, asked whether she had an eating disorder, scrolled through dating profiles and commented on women's bodies in her office, and pressured her to end her relationship so they could become a producing "power couple." (Garcia Decl., Exh. 1 [Logan Depo. 141:8-18, 144:6-147:9]; Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶ 3].) She provides testimony that Mr. King changed clothes in front of her four or five times in a manner that made her uncomfortable and left his underwear in her office. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 1 [Logan Depo. 245:16-246:3, 247:11-15].) She provides declarations from two former employees stating that Mr. Gauthier directed unwanted attention at another woman, that he was "creepy," and that he told Plaintiff to "go clean." (Garcia Decl., Exh. 11 [Robinson Decl. ¶¶ 9-10]; Exh. 12 [Nguyen Decl. ¶ 16].) She provides evidence that King Studio did not implement anti-harassment training until 2021 or 2022. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 2 [Riggs Depo. 99:23-100:2, 102:4-11].)

Defendants reply that this testimony contradicts other portions of the same deposition. Both sides cite the same transcript. That is a conflict in the evidence and a question of weight and credibility, which the Court may not resolve on summary judgment.

The Legislature has directed that a single incident of harassing conduct is sufficient to create a triable issue regarding the existence of a hostile work environment if it unreasonably interfered with the plaintiff's work performance or created an intimidating, hostile, or offensive working environment. (Gov. Code § 12923(b).) A discriminatory remark may be relevant circumstantial evidence even if not made in the context of an employment decision. (Gov. Code § 12923(c); Reid v. Google, Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 512, 539-541.) The Legislature has further declared that harassment cases are rarely appropriate for disposition on summary judgment. (Gov. Code § 12923(e).)

Plaintiff has met her burden. Plaintiff provides evidence that the conduct was constant and pervasive over a period of years, included comments on her body and questions about whether she had an eating disorder, and was corroborated by other women who describe similar treatment – evidence from which a jury could find the totality of the circumstances altered the conditions of her work.

The Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the first cause of action.

Second Cause of Action: Gender Discrimination (Issue No. 2)

To establish a prima facie case, Plaintiff must show she was a member of a protected class, was performing competently, suffered an adverse employment action, and that some other circumstance suggests discriminatory motive. (Guz v. Bechtel National, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 355.)

Defendants move for summary adjudication of the second cause of action on the grounds that Plaintiff suffered no adverse employment action and that she has no evidence of a discriminatory motive.

Adverse Employment Action

In support, Defendants provide evidence that Plaintiff was not terminated, that her company REPT held a one-year contract that expired by its terms on December 31, 2019, and that the parties simply failed to agree on new terms after Plaintiff demanded either exclusivity or a 20% commission. (Angioni Decl., Exh. G [2019 Independent Contractor Agreement]; Exh. H [2019 Freelance Rates]; Exh. O [Plaintiff's proposed 2020 terms]; Exh. P [King correspondence re 2020 terms].) Defendants provide evidence that Plaintiff's compensation increased year after year. (Angioni Decl., Exh. T [Riggs Depo. 46:8-47:1, 48:7-49:1, 62:13-63:9]; Exh. J [payroll records].) They provide evidence that the 2017 travel decisions were driven by brands supplying a single hotel room, that King Studio changed the policy once Plaintiff raised the issue, that she attended meetings when her role required it, and that duties shifted to employees after she became a contractor. (Angioni Decl., Exh. R [Logan Depo. 42:11-45:8]; Exh. S [King Depo. 41:2-42:22; 34:9-35:4, 94:14-96:25; 24:22-26:11, 204:6-206:3, 228:14-229:16]; Exh. T [Riggs Depo. 90:21-91:18, 94:4-15; 126:5-8].)

Before turning to Plaintiff's showing, the Court addresses Defendants' legal premise. Defendants rely on Tollefson v. Roman Catholic Bishop (1990) 219 Cal.App.3d 843, 854, and Labor Code section 2920, subdivision (a), for the proposition that a nonrenewal cannot be an adverse action. Those authorities govern the common-law tort of wrongful discharge in violation of public policy, addressed in connection with the sixth cause of action below, and not a statutory claim under FEHA. Tollefson is a contract and tort case; it holds that a decision not to enter into a new contract or renew an expired one will not support a claim for wrongful discharge, because the relationship ends by operation of the parties' own agreement rather than by an act of the employer. Neither Tollefson nor Daly v. Exxon Corp. (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 39 places a discriminatory or retaliatory nonrenewal beyond the reach of a statutory claim. Addressing a retaliation claim under Labor Code section 6310, subdivision (b), Daly drew precisely this distinction, affirming dismissal of the common-law count while recognizing that "[w]here an employer discriminates against the employee and retaliates by not renewing the employment contract, the employee may sue for statutory damages." (Id. at pp. 45-46.) The same reasoning applies to a statutory claim under FEHA, which reaches not only ultimate employment actions but "the entire spectrum of employment actions that are reasonably likely to adversely and materially affect an employee's job performance or opportunity for advancement." (Yanowitz v. L'Oreal USA, Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1053-1054.) Alleged adverse acts are considered collectively, not in isolation. (Id. at pp. 1055-1056.)

Defendants have met their initial burden. The burden shifts to Plaintiff.

In opposition, Plaintiff provides evidence that after her April 2018 pay complaint King Studio presented her with an ultimatum: accept a demotion losing her Head of Production and Producer titles and handling only sales and bookings, or transition to independent contractor status. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶ 12].) She provides the December 14, 2018 email stating that "Ron from HR" required a resignation letter and an LLC, and that "this needs to be a priority for us to continue working together." (Garcia Decl., Exh. 9.) She provides evidence that she opposed submitting a resignation letter and had no intention of resigning. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶¶ 14-15].) She provides testimony that she was excluded from creative meetings and from film and television opportunities she had been promised, and from business travel. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶¶ 9-10]; Exh. 1 [Logan Depo. 169:5-13, 171:8-172:19].) She provides testimony that Mr. King ended the relationship by video call on May 14, 2020. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶ 19].)

Plaintiff has met her burden on this element.

Discriminatory Motive

In support, Defendants provide evidence that Plaintiff never reported that she was harassed or discriminated against because of her gender, that she praised Mr. King's leadership and thanked Mr. Riggs for fostering a supportive workplace, and that the two concerns she did raise were met with immediate action. (Angioni Decl., Exh. R [Logan Depo. 35:7-14, 38:3-19, 137:22-140:4, 150:2-153:21, 153:22-154:18]; Exh. C; Exh. E.)

Defendants have met their initial burden. The burden shifts to Plaintiff.

In opposition, Plaintiff provides testimony that after interviewing a female candidate for a VFX supervisor position, Mr. King told her he did not want a woman overseeing an all-male post team because he did not want to reduce the men's morale, and that he wanted a man for the role. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 1 [Logan Depo. 172:10-173:20].) She provides evidence that King Studio hired Luke Snedecor, a male. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 1 [Logan Depo. 174:14-18].) She provides Mr. Riggs's testimony that the rationale for King Studio's travel practice was to protect Mr. King's image by avoiding situations that could be perceived as romantic involvement with someone other than his wife. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 2 [Riggs Depo. 95:15-20].) She provides Mr. King's testimony that he and Mr. Sommerville "totally agreed" that Ms. Malinics had been subjected to gender discrimination. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 3 [King Depo. 41:21-42:19].) She provides declarations from three former employees corroborating unequal treatment of women. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 11 [Robinson Decl. ¶¶ 7-12]; Exh. 12 [Nguyen Decl. ¶¶ 18-21]; Exh. 13 [Malinics Decl. ¶¶ 10-18].)

The plaintiff's prima facie burden on this element is not onerous; she need only offer evidence supporting a rational inference that gender was a substantial motivating factor. (Guz v. Bechtel National, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 355; Sandell v. Taylor-Listug, Inc. (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 297, 310.)

Plaintiff has met her burden on this element.

Legitimate Business Reason and Pretext

Defendants further provide evidence of a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason. They provide evidence that they were reassessing relationships with outside agencies due to operational inefficiencies and inconsistent messaging, that the decision to bring brand deals in-house applied to all agencies, and that Plaintiff's proposed 20% commission was not financially viable. (Angioni Decl., Exh. P [King correspondence re 2020 terms]; Exh. O.)

Defendants have met that burden. The burden shifts back to Plaintiff to produce substantial responsive evidence of pretext.

In opposition, Plaintiff provides Mr. Riggs's testimony that King Studio hired a male, Noah Pacifici, to work with brands. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 2 [Riggs Depo. 15:3-13].) She provides his testimony that King Studio continued using third-party agencies, including Creative Artists Agency, Captiv8, Influential, and Spacestation. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 2 [Riggs Depo. 15:15-16:3].) She provides his testimony that King Studio continued to use Mr. Gauthier for producing work as recently as 2022. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 2 [Riggs Depo. 16:10-13].) This evidence comes from Defendants' own officer and directly contradicts the stated reason. Inconsistencies and implausibilities in an employer's proffered reasons may support an inference of pretext. (Hersant v. Department of Social Services (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 997, 1005.)

Plaintiff has met her burden.

The Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the second cause of action.

Third Cause of Action: Wage Discrimination (Issue No. 3)

An employer shall not pay an employee at wage rates less than the rates paid to employees of another sex for substantially similar work, when viewed as a composite of skill, effort, and responsibility, and performed under similar working conditions. (Lab. Code § 1197.5(a).) If a differential is shown, the employer must demonstrate that it is based on a listed factor, that the factor is applied reasonably, and that the factors account for the entire wage differential. (Lab. Code § 1197.5(a)(1)-(3).)

Defendants move for summary adjudication of the third cause of action on the ground that Plaintiff cannot establish a prima facie case because she was paid more than any similarly situated male.

In support, Defendants provide evidence that Mr. Gauthier was the only male producer, that both received a base salary plus a 2% commission in 2016 rising to 2.5% in 2017, and that Plaintiff earned $119,369.69 in 2017 and $256,874.50 in 2018 while Mr. Gauthier earned $84,697.87 and $66,805.37. (Angioni Decl., Exh. S [King Depo. 58:25-61:10]; Exh. T [Riggs Depo. 46:8-47:1]; Exh. J [payroll comparison].) Defendants provide evidence that Plaintiff was the second highest paid person at the company. (Angioni Decl., Exh. J.) They provide evidence that Sommerville and Gerlicher were retained as independent contractor writers and sound producers who developed story concepts, character arcs, trick designs, and sound design, and who received commission without any base salary. (Angioni Decl., Exh. S [King Depo. 26:5-32:24, 28:22-30:9, 35:17-36:21, 53:4-54:5].)

Defendants rely on Hall v. County of Los Angeles (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 318, 323-325. Hall was decided under the former version of section 1197.5, which required "equal work" in the same establishment. The Legislature replaced that standard effective January 1, 2016. The comparison now turns on actual job content rather than job title.

Defendants have met their initial burden. Defendants provide payroll evidence that Plaintiff out-earned Mr. Gauthier in each relevant year and was the second-highest paid person at the company, and evidence that Sommerville and Gerlicher held different roles with different duties and received no salary. The burden shifts to Plaintiff.

In opposition, Plaintiff provides testimony that Mr. Sommerville and Gerlicher received a 5% commission on brand deals while she received 2%, then 2.5%, then 3%. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 1 [Logan Depo. 48:2-49:1].) Defendants' own evidence confirms the 5% rate. (Angioni Decl., Exh. S [King Depo. 28:22-30:9].) She provides evidence that all three worked on the same brand deal projects and were compensated by project-based commission. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 1 [Logan Depo. 48:2-49:1].) She provides a declaration from Ms. Malinics stating she experienced gender-based pay disparity at King Studio. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 13 [Malinics Decl. ¶¶ 17-19].)

Plaintiff also argues that Mr. Riggs's testimony provides that, asked whether Mr. Sommerville's role was more valuable than hers, he answered, "I don't think it necessarily was more valuable, in terms of pay, at the end of the day." (Garcia Decl., Exh. 2 [Riggs Depo. 59:13-19].) However, the Court could not locate this evidence in the record. Page 59 of the Riggs Deposition has not been provided in Plaintiff’s evidence.

Defendants reply that the statute requires comparison of total compensation rather than isolated commission rates. The statute speaks of "wage rates." Whether individuals who worked on the same branded content projects performed substantially similar work when viewed as a composite of skill, effort, and responsibility is a question of fact. Comparators need not be identical, only similar in all relevant respects. (Wawrzenski v. United Airlines, Inc. (2024) 106 Cal.App.5th 663, 689-690; Gupta v. Trustees of California State University (2019) 40 Cal.App.5th 510, 520.) The statutory defenses under section 1197.5(a)(1)-(3) are not briefed.

Plaintiff has met her burden. Plaintiff provides evidence that she and the identified comparators worked the same brand deal projects for project-based commissions at materially different rates.

The Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the third cause of action.

Fourth Cause of Action: Retaliation (Issue No. 4)

Plaintiff must show she engaged in protected activity, was subjected to an adverse employment action, and that a causal link exists between the two. (Yanowitz, supra, 36 Cal.4th at p. 1042.) The burden then shifts to Defendants to articulate a legitimate, nonretaliatory reason, and back to Plaintiff to show pretext. (Morgan v. Regents of Univ. of Cal. (2000) 88 Cal.App.4th 52, 68; Loggins v. Kaiser Permanente Internat. (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 1102, 1109.)

Defendants move for summary adjudication of the fourth cause of action on the grounds that Plaintiff suffered no adverse employment action and that King Studio had a legitimate, non-retaliatory reason for declining her proposed terms.

In support, Defendants provide the same evidence offered as to the second cause of action. (Angioni Decl., Exh. G; Exh. H; Exh. O; Exh. P; Exh. J; Exh. T [Riggs Depo. 46:8-47:1, 48:7-49:1, 62:13-63:9].)

Defendants have met their initial burden. Defendants provide evidence that Plaintiff's compensation and title increased after each complaint, that the 2019 contract expired according to its written terms, and that King Studio declined to renew for stated business reasons unrelated to any protected activity. The burden shifts to Plaintiff.

In opposition, Plaintiff provides testimony that she told Mr. Riggs in April 2018 that she felt treated differently because she was a woman and that her male colleagues were paid more for comparable work. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 1 [Logan Depo. 38:20-40:7, 56:22-57:4]; Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶ 11].) She provides testimony that she raised pay disparity concerns to Mr. Riggs on at least three occasions. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 1 [Logan Depo. 56:11-57:4].) An employee need not use legal terminology to engage in protected activity; opposing conduct the employee reasonably believes to be discriminatory suffices. (Yanowitz, supra, 36 Cal.4th at p. 1043.) That Mr. Riggs testified he could not recall Plaintiff raising gender in connection with her pay complaints creates a credibility conflict for the trier of fact, not a basis for adjudication. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 2 [Riggs Depo. 79:19-21].)

Plaintiff provides the same adverse action evidence discussed above. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 9; Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶¶ 9-12, 14-15, 19].) On causation, she provides evidence that the December 14, 2018 email and the January 2019 reclassification followed within months of the April 2018 complaint. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 9; Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶¶ 11-15].) A causal link may be established by evidence that the employer knew of the protected activity and the adverse action followed within a relatively short time. (Wawrzenski, supra, 106 Cal.App.5th at p. 702.)

On pretext, she provides the Riggs testimony discussed above. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 2 [Riggs Depo. 15:3-16:13].)

Plaintiff has met her burden. Plaintiff provides evidence that she told Mr. Riggs in April 2018 she was being treated differently because she was a woman, that she raised pay disparity concerns to him on at least three occasions, that she was presented with the reclassification ultimatum within months of the April 2018 complaint, and that Defendants' officer testified to conduct – the retention of outside agencies, the continued engagement of Mr. Gauthier, and the hiring of a male to work with brands – from which a trier of fact could find that the stated reason for ending the relationship was not the actual reason.

The Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the fourth cause of action.

Fifth Cause of Action: Failure to Prevent Harassment, Discrimination, and Retaliation (Issue No. 5)

To establish this claim, a plaintiff must show she was subjected to discrimination, harassment, or retaliation, that the employer failed to take all reasonable steps to prevent it, and that the failure caused injury. (Trujillo v. North County Transit Dist. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 280, 289.)

Defendants move for summary adjudication of the fifth cause of action on the sole ground that it is derivative and fails with the underlying claims.

In support, Defendants provide no evidence directed at the second or third elements. Their showing rests entirely on the premise that the first, second, and fourth causes of action fail. Because triable issues remain as to each of those claims, that premise is not established.

Defendants have not met their initial burden. The burden does not shift to Plaintiff.

The Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the fifth cause of action.

Sixth Cause of Action: Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy (Issue No. 6)

Plaintiff must establish an employer-employee relationship, that the employer terminated her employment, that the termination was substantially motivated by a violation of public policy, and resulting harm. (Yau v. Allen (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 144, 154; Alamo v. Practice Management Information Corp. (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 466, 479.)

Defendants move for summary adjudication of the sixth cause of action on three grounds: that Plaintiff was not an employee in 2019 and 2020, that she was never terminated because the contract expired by its terms, and that no protected characteristic motivated the decision.

Employment Relationship

In support, Defendants provide evidence that Plaintiff formed Logan Media Group, LLC and then REPT, that her company rather than Plaintiff personally contracted with King Studio, that she no longer received a salary, and that she set her own schedule, chose her work location in Colorado, selected crew, and was free to work for others. (Angioni Decl., Exh. F [email of Jan. 4, 2019]; Exh. G; Exh. H; Exh. R [Logan Depo. 262:24-263:3].)

That showing carries Defendants' initial burden, and the burden shifts to Plaintiff.

For the reasons set forth in the classification discussion below, a triable issue exists as to whether Plaintiff was King Studio's employee during 2019 and 2020.

The first ground does not dispose of the claim.

Termination

Unlike the FEHA claims addressed above, this common-law tort requires an actual termination of the employment relationship by the employer; a nonrenewal that leaves the plaintiff with a statutory remedy will not support it. Employment for a specified term is terminated by expiration of its appointed term. (Lab. Code § 2920(a).) A decision not to renew a fixed-term contract that expires by its own terms will not support this tort. (Tollefson, supra, 219 Cal.App.3d at p. 854; Daly, supra, 55 Cal.App.4th at pp. 45-46.) This ground therefore depends on establishing that the parties' relationship ended by expiration of the 2019 agreement.

In support, Defendants provide evidence that the 2019 agreement contained a fixed end date of December 31, 2019 and that the parties thereafter failed to reach new terms. (Angioni Decl., Exh. G; Exh. H; Exh. P.)

Defendants have not met their initial burden on this element. Their own evidence establishes that the relationship did not end on December 31, 2019. Defendants provide evidence that the parties negotiated a further arrangement in 2020 under which King Studio would pay REPT 15% on pipeline deals, and that King Studio paid approximately $270,000 in 2020. (Angioni Decl., Exh. P; Exh. J; Exh. K [2020 payment statements].) The relationship did not end on December 31, 2019. On Defendants' own account the 2020 engagement was a distinct arrangement, and Defendants offer no evidence that it carried a fixed term that expired of its own force.

The burden therefore does not shift as to this element. Even were it otherwise, Plaintiff provides testimony that on February 6, 2020 Mr. King assured her that her position was safe and that she would remain the priority producer on brand deals if the terms stayed the same as 2019, that she emailed him that evening agreeing to proceed on that basis, and that Mr. King ended the relationship on May 14, 2020 after she proposed a modified agreement. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶¶ 18-19].) Whether the relationship ended by expiration of a fixed term or by an affirmative act of termination in May 2020 is disputed.

The second ground does not dispose of the claim.

Public Policy Motivation

In support, Defendants provide the same legitimate business reason evidence offered as to the second cause of action: that they were reassessing relationships with outside agencies, that the decision to bring brand deals in-house applied to all agencies, and that Plaintiff's proposed 20% commission was not financially viable. (Angioni Decl., Exh. P; Exh. O.) That showing carries Defendants' initial burden, and the burden shifts to Plaintiff.

Plaintiff meets it with the pretext evidence discussed as to the second cause of action. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 2 [Riggs Depo. 15:3-16:13].) Whether the stated reason was the actual reason is disputed.

Because none of the grounds Defendants advance disposes of the sixth cause of action, the Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the sixth cause of action.

Independent Contractor Classification (Issues Nos. 6 and 8 through 11)

Defendants' sixth and eighth through eleventh issues rest substantially on the premise that Plaintiff was an independent contractor in 2019 and 2020 and therefore outside the Labor Code provisions she invokes.

In support, Defendants provide evidence of the contractual label, Plaintiff's formation of an LLC, and her control over her own schedule and location. (Angioni Decl., Exh. F; Exh. G; Exh. H; Exh. R [Logan Depo. 262:24-263:3].) Defendants assert Plaintiff was an independent contractor from 2019 forward but apply no classification standard in their moving papers, invoking Borello and the Labor Code section 2780 exemption for the first time on reply.

A label is not controlling. Under both S.G. Borello & Sons, Inc. v. Department of Industrial Relations (1989) 48 Cal.3d 341, 350, and Dynamex Operations West, Inc. v. Superior Court (2018) 4 Cal.5th 903, 916-917, the substance of the relationship governs.

Even assuming the burden shifted, in opposition, Plaintiff provides Mr. King's admission that her job responsibilities in 2018, when she was undisputedly an employee, and in 2019, when she was allegedly a contractor, were "pretty much the same." (Garcia Decl., Exh. 3 [King Depo. 193:6-9].) She provides the Freelance Rate Sheet requiring Mr. King's approval on all key roles including Director, Art Director, VFX Supervisor, Editor, and Director of Photography, and providing that studio pre-negotiated crew rates applied unless approved otherwise by Mr. King or Mr. Riggs. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 7; Exh. 3 [King Depo. 212:9-13].) She provides Mr. King's testimony that the default was to use a salaried employee before hiring a third party. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 3 [King Depo. 222:9-22].) She provides Mr. Riggs's testimony that it was possible he told Mr. King that workers were misclassified. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 2 [Riggs Depo. 147:5-9].) She provides the December 14, 2018 email conditioning continued work on a resignation letter and formation of an LLC. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 9.) She provides testimony that Mr. Riggs told her King Studio would not pay a higher commission to a W-2 employee because Mr. King did not want to pay more taxes, so she had to be paid through an LLC. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶ 13].)

Defendants argue for the first time in Reply that Plaintiff falls within the exemption at Labor Code section 2780(a)(1)(J), which they contend mandates the Borello test. Points raised for the first time in a reply will ordinarily not be considered, because doing so deprives the opposing party of an opportunity to respond. (American Drug Stores, Inc. v. Stroh (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1446, 1453; Jay v. Mahaffey (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 1522, 1537-1538.) The argument also does not withstand scrutiny. Section 2780(a)(1) applies to enumerated occupations "in connection with creating, marketing, promoting, or distributing sound recordings or musical compositions," and subdivision (a)(2)(A) expressly excludes film and television unit production crews working on audiovisual works. The section was added by Stats. 2020, ch. 38, § 2 (AB 2257), effective September 4, 2020, after the parties' relationship ended.

The Court need not decide which test governs. Triable issues remain.

Seventh Cause of Action: Breach of Implied Contract (Issue No. 7)

Employment having no specified term is presumed terminable at will. (Lab. Code § 2922.) That presumption may be overcome by evidence of an implied-in-fact agreement limiting the employer's termination rights, assessed under the totality of the circumstances and considering the employer's personnel policies or practices, the employee's longevity of service, actions or communications reflecting assurances of continued employment, and industry practice. (Foley v. Interactive Data Corp. (1988) 47 Cal.3d 654, 680-681; Guz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at pp. 336-337.) Longevity, raises, and promotions do not alone establish such a contract. (Guz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at pp. 341-342.) An express agreement and an implied agreement cannot coexist where each embraces the identical subject but requires a different result. (Tollefson, supra, 219 Cal.App.3d at pp. 854-855.)

The Complaint alleges King Studio promised by words and conduct that Plaintiff would be a Producer on its television or film projects and would not be discharged if she continued to perform well. (Complaint ¶ 127.)

Defendants move for summary adjudication of the seventh cause of action on the grounds that the parties' relationship was governed by an express fixed-term agreement terminable upon notice, and that no implied promise of permanent employment exists.

In support, Defendants provide evidence that the relationship was governed by an express written agreement between Logan Media Group, LLC and King Studio, that the agreement fixed an end date of December 31, 2019, that either party could terminate on ten days' notice, and that the agreement contained no for-cause limitation and no promise of continued engagement. (Angioni Decl., Exh. G; Exh. H.) They provide evidence that Plaintiff individually was not a party to that agreement. (Angioni Decl., Exh. G.) They provide evidence that Plaintiff's own freelance rate sheet reflected the December 31, 2019 end date. (Angioni Decl., Exh. H.)

Defendants have met their initial burden. The burden shifts to Plaintiff.

Plaintiff's Opposition is silent as to this cause of action. She offers no argument and no citation to evidence directed at it, and identifies no assurance, policy, practice, or course of conduct from which an implied promise of continued engagement could be found as to any period. Where the moving party has carried its burden, the opposing party must set forth specific facts showing a triable issue of material fact; a failure to respond leaves no such issue. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subds. (b)(3), (p)(2).)

Plaintiff has not met her burden. Plaintiff's Opposition does not address this cause of action, offers no responsive evidence, and thereby fails to demonstrate a triable issue.

The Motion for Summary Adjudication is GRANTED as to the seventh cause of action.

Eighth Cause of Action: Failure to Pay Minimum Wage (Issue No. 8)

Labor Code sections 1194, 1194.2, and 1197 require that an employee receive at least the applicable minimum wage for each hour worked.

Defendants move for summary adjudication of the eighth cause of action on the grounds that Plaintiff was an independent contractor from 2019 forward, and that in any event she was paid well above minimum wage at all times.

In support, Defendants provide evidence that Plaintiff received a salary plus commission throughout her employment, that her commission rate rose from 2% to 2.5% to 3% to 5%, and that she earned $119,369.69 in 2017 and $256,874.50 in 2018. (Angioni Decl., Exh. T [Riggs Depo. 46:8-47:1, 48:7-49:1, 62:13-63:9]; Exh. D [2017 Employee Annual Review]; Exh. J [payroll records].) They provide evidence that King Studio paid approximately $245,000 in 2019 and approximately $270,000 in 2020. (Angioni Decl., Exh. I [2019 invoices]; Exh. K [2020 payment statements].)

Defendants have met their initial burden. The burden shifts to Plaintiff.

In opposition, Plaintiff does not dispute the amounts she was paid. She argues only that if she was an employee, and if her compensation was commission-only without a base salary, then a triable issue exists as to whether there were pay periods in which her effective hourly rate fell below minimum wage. She cites no evidence of hours worked, identifies no pay period, and offers no evidence that her compensation in any period fell below the applicable rate. Her argument is conditional and speculative. Speculation is not evidence and does not create a triable issue. (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at pp. 850, 864.)

The classification question is not material to this issue. Even if Plaintiff was an employee throughout, she has produced no evidence that she was paid less than minimum wage for any hour worked.

Plaintiff has not met her burden.

The Motion for Summary Adjudication is GRANTED as to the eighth cause of action.

Ninth Cause of Action: Failure to Reimburse Business Expenses (Issue No. 9)

An employer must indemnify an employee for all necessary expenditures incurred in direct consequence of the discharge of her duties. (Lab. Code § 2802.) The section applies to employees, not independent contractors. (Arnold v. Mutual of Omaha Ins. Co. (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 580, 586-590.) Any agreement by an employee to waive the benefits of that article is null and void. (Lab. Code § 2804.)

Defendants move for summary adjudication of the ninth cause of action on three grounds: that Plaintiff was a contractor during the relevant period, that the parties' agreements allocated the claimed expenses to her, and that she identifies no unreimbursed expense from her employment period.

Contractor Status and Contractual Allocation

In support of the first ground, Defendants provide the classification evidence identified above. (Angioni Decl., Exh. F; Exh. G; Exh. H; Exh. R [Logan Depo. 262:24-263:3].) That showing carries Defendants' initial burden, and the burden shifts to Plaintiff. Plaintiff meets it for the reasons set forth in the classification discussion above. A triable issue exists as to whether she was King Studio's employee during 2019 and 2020.

In support of the second ground, Defendants provide the 2019 Freelance Rates schedule stating that any fees payable to an agent or manager's assistant are the responsibility of the Freelancer, and the Independent Contractor Agreement providing that Plaintiff's company would not be reimbursed for expenses incurred in performing services. (Angioni Decl., Exh. H; Exh. G.) Plaintiff admits both facts as undisputed.

Defendants have not met their initial burden on this ground. The allocation is enforceable only if Plaintiff was not an employee. If she was, Labor Code section 2804 renders it void. Because the classification question is triable, these grounds do not dispose of the claim.

Identification of Expenses

In support, Defendants provide Plaintiff's interrogatory responses and Mr. King's testimony. (Angioni Decl., Exh. U [Response to Special Interrogatory No. 69]; Exh. S [King Depo. 216:3 et seq.].) They provide evidence that during her employment Plaintiff submitted receipts and was reimbursed, and that as a contractor she had a company credit card and routinely submitted reimbursable expenses. (Angioni Decl., Exh. B [email of Feb. 15, 2016]; Exh. N [email of Apr. 17, 2020].)

Defendants have not met their initial burden on this ground. Their own cited evidence identifies a specific claimed expense. Plaintiff's interrogatory responses identify Ms. Katie Schwineck's compensation, and Mr. King testified that Ms. Schwineck represented she was employed by REPT. (Angioni Decl., Exh. U; Exh. S [King Depo. 216:3 et seq.].) The responses are not factually devoid. Whether that expenditure was a necessary expense incurred in direct consequence of Plaintiff's duties, if she was an employee, is a question of fact.

The burden does not shift, and the third ground does not dispose of the claim.

Because none of the grounds Defendants advance disposes of the ninth cause of action, the Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the ninth cause of action.

Tenth Cause of Action: Failure to Provide Accurate, Itemized Wage Statements (Issue No. 10)

Labor Code section 226 applies to employees.

Defendants move for summary adjudication of the tenth cause of action on the sole ground that Plaintiff stopped receiving wage statements in January 2019 because she was no longer an employee and was not entitled to them. Plaintiff claims a violation "since at least January 2, 2019." (Complaint ¶ 146.)

In support, Defendants provide excerpts of the wage statements issued to Plaintiff during her employment, and the classification evidence identified above. (Angioni Decl., Exh. Q [wage statement excerpts]; Exh. G; Exh. H.)

Defendants have met their initial burden. The burden shifts to Plaintiff.

In opposition, Plaintiff provides the classification evidence set forth above. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 3 [King Depo. 193:6-9, 212:9-13, 222:9-22]; Exh. 7; Exh. 9; Exh. 2 [Riggs Depo. 147:5-9]; Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶ 13].) This claim rises and falls with that question. Defendants concede Plaintiff received no wage statements after January 2019. Because a triable issue exists as to whether Plaintiff was an employee during that period, a triable issue exists as to her entitlement to wage statements.

Plaintiff has met her burden.

The Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the tenth cause of action.

Eleventh Cause of Action: Waiting Time Penalties (Issue No. 11)

Labor Code sections 201 through 203 require prompt payment of all wages owing at the conclusion of employment. Wages include amounts calculated on a commission basis. (Lab. Code § 200(a).)

Defendants move for summary adjudication of the eleventh cause of action on three grounds: that it is derivative of the eighth cause of action, that Plaintiff was a contractor, and that her list of unpaid deals is demonstrably inaccurate.

Derivative Ground

Defendants have not met their initial burden on this ground. The claim is not derivative of the minimum wage claim. Plaintiff's theory rests on unpaid commissions, which are wages under Labor Code section 200(a).

Contractor Status

In support, Defendants provide the classification evidence identified above. (Angioni Decl., Exh. F; Exh. G; Exh. H; Exh. R [Logan Depo. 262:24-263:3].) Defendants have met their initial burden. The burden shifts, and Plaintiff has met it for the reasons set forth in the classification discussion.

Unpaid Deals

In support, Defendants provide evidence that King Studio paid Plaintiff for many of the deals she claims to have negotiated in 2020, that at least one deal was cancelled by the contracting party and refunded, and that at least one deal was declined by King Studio. (Angioni Decl., Exh. K [2020 payment statements]; Exh. M [2020 invoicing correspondence]; Exh. L [Riggs letter of Oct. 21, 2020].)

Defendants have not met their initial burden on this ground. Plaintiff's interrogatory responses assert she negotiated 31 brand deals in 2020 and is entitled to compensation for each. (Angioni Decl., Exh. U [Responses to Special Interrogatories Nos. 55 and 69].) Defendants' evidence does not account for all 31. The Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the eleventh cause of action.

Punitive Damages (Issue No. 12)

Punitive damages require clear and convincing evidence of oppression, fraud, or malice. (Civ. Code § 3294(a).) As to a corporate employer, the conduct must be committed, authorized, or ratified by an officer, director, or managing agent. (Civ. Code § 3294(b).) On summary adjudication, the Court considers whether a reasonable trier of fact could find by clear and convincing evidence that the standard is met. Whether conduct warrants punitive damages is ordinarily a question for the trier of fact. (American Airlines, Inc. v. Sheppard, Mullin, Richter & Hampton (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 1017, 1049.)

Defendants move for summary adjudication of Plaintiff's request for punitive damages on the ground that she has no clear and convincing evidence of oppression, fraud, or malice.

In support, Defendants point to the absence of evidence and rely on the same record offered as to the underlying claims, namely that Plaintiff was promoted and repeatedly given raises, that her concerns were met with action, and that the relationship ended over a failed contract negotiation. (Angioni Decl., Exh. T [Riggs Depo. 46:8-47:1, 48:7-49:1, 62:13-63:9]; Exh. R [Logan Depo. 153:22-154:18]; Exh. J; Exh. O; Exh. P.)

Defendants have met their initial burden. The burden shifts to Plaintiff.

In opposition, Plaintiff provides testimony that Mr. King made repeated gender-based statements about her availability once she married and had children, and that he did not want a woman overseeing an all-male team. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 1 [Logan Depo. 172:10-173:20, 187:10-25]; Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶¶ 17].) She provides the December 14, 2018 email conditioning continued work on a resignation letter she opposed and on forming an LLC. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 9; Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶¶ 14-15].) She provides testimony that Mr. Riggs told her the arrangement was structured so Mr. King would not pay more taxes. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶ 13].) She provides Mr. Riggs's testimony that it was possible he told Mr. King workers were misclassified. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 2 [Riggs Depo. 147:5-9].) She provides testimony that on February 6, 2020 Mr. King assured her that her position was safe and urged her to trust his integrity. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 10 [Logan Decl. ¶ 18].) She provides Mr. Riggs's testimony contradicting the stated reason for ending the relationship. (Garcia Decl., Exh. 2 [Riggs Depo. 15:3-16:13].)

Discrimination followed by concealment through false explanations may support punitive damages. (Cloud v. Casey (1999) 76 Cal.App.4th 895, 911-912.)

On this record, a reasonable trier of fact could find by clear and convincing evidence that Defendants acted with malice or oppression.

Plaintiff has met her burden. Plaintiff provides evidence that an officer of King Studio stated he would not place a woman over the male post team and then hired a man, and that Plaintiff was required to submit a resignation letter she believed to be false – evidence from which a trier of fact could find oppression or malice by clear and convincing proof.

The Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the request for punitive damages.

The Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED.

Raya v. General Motors

TENTATIVE RULING

The demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND. The Motion to Strike punitive damages from the prayer for relief is GRANTED.

Defendant to file an Answer in 30 days.

ANALYSIS

INTRODUCTION

This is a lemon law case. On or about August 1, 2023, Plaintiff Miguel Gonzalez Raya (“Plaintiff”) entered into a warranty contract with Defendant General Motors, LLC (“Defendant”) regarding a 2023 Chevrolet Suburban (“Vehicle”). (FAC, ¶ 6.) Plaintiff alleges that defects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves which substantially impair the use, value, or safety of the Vehicle, and the value of the Vehicle is worthless and/or de minimis. (Id. at ¶¶ 11-13.)

On December 26, 2024, Plaintiff filed a Complaint. On October 21, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging (1) Violation of Subdivision (D) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (2) Violation of Subdivision (B) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (3) Violation of Subdivision (A)(3) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (4) Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability; and (5) Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment.

On November 17, 2025, Defendant filed this Demurrer with Motion to Strike.

On July 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Non-Opposition to Defendant’s Demurrer.

DEMURRER

LEGAL STANDARD

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

“The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds... (e) the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (f) The pleading is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, “uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible." (Code Civ. Proc. [AW13.1]§ 430.10.) “[D]emurrers for uncertainty are disfavored, and are granted only if the pleading is so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (A.J. Fistes Corp. v. GDL Best Contractors, Inc. (2019) 38 Cal. App. 5th[AW14.1] 677, 695.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id. [AW15.1]; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

DISCUSSION

Defendant demurs to the fifth cause of action for Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment on the grounds that it fails to plead fraud with the requisite specificity and fails to allege a transactional relationship giving rise to a duty to disclose.

Defendant argues that the cause of action fails to allege (i) the identity of the individuals at GM who purportedly concealed material facts or made untrue representations about his Suburban (ii) their authority to speak and act on behalf of GM, (iii) GM’s knowledge about alleged defects in Plaintiff’s Suburban at the time of purchase, (iv) any interactions with GM before or during the purchase of his Suburban, or (v) GM’s intent to induce reliance by Plaintiff to purchase the specific Suburban at issue. Additionally, Plaintiff failed to allege that Plaintiff purchased the Vehicle directly from Defendant or otherwise entered into a transaction with Defendant, thus, Plaintiff has not alleged facts demonstrating that Defendant was under a duty to disclose.

Fraud based on concealment requires that “(1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.” (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 310-311 (Bigler-Engler).) “Even under the strict rules of common law pleading, one of the canons was that less particularity is required when the facts lie more in the knowledge of the opposite party.” (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn., Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1256, 1384.)

An essential element of intentional concealment includes the duty to disclose, which must be based upon a transaction, or a special relationship, between plaintiff and defendant. (Id. at p. 314.) “There are ‘four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.’” (Id. at p. 311.) “[O]ther than the first instance, in which there must be a fiduciary relationship between the parties, ‘the other three circumstances in which nondisclosure may be actionable presuppose[ ] the existence of some other relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise. . . . “[W]here material facts are known to one party and not to the other, failure to disclose them is not actionable fraud unless there is some relationship between the parties which gives rise to a duty to disclose such known facts.” [Citation.]’ [Citation.]” (Hoffman v. 162 North Wolfe LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 1178, 1187 (Hoffman).) “Such a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.” (Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at p. 312.)

The FAC alleges that Defendant knew or should have known about the Engine Defect through its exclusive knowledge of non-public, internal data about the Engine Defect. (FAC, ¶ 52.) The FAC also alleges that Defendants actively concealed the Engine Defect and failed to disclose the defect to Plaintiff at the time of purchase. (Ibid.) The FAC also alleges that Defendant had superior and exclusive knowledge of the Engine Defect and knew or should have known that the Engine Defect was not known or reasonably discoverable by Plaintiff before the purchase of the Vehicle. (Id. at ¶ 57.) The FAC also alleges that had Plaintiff known about the defects at the time of sale, Plaintiff would not have purchased the Vehicle. (Id. at ¶ 55.) The FAC further alleges that Defendant never disclosed the Engine Defect to Plaintiff prior to the purchase of the Vehicle or at any point during Plaintiff’s ownership. (Id. at ¶ 63.) These allegations are specific enough to allege the information that was concealed and the danger posed. (See Jones v. ConocoPhillips Co. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1187, 1199-1200.)

However, Plaintiff does not allege facts showing the existence of a relationship between the parties that gives rise to a duty to disclose facts. Nowhere in the FAC does Plaintiff allege that Plaintiff directly bought the Vehicle from Defendant. Instead, Plaintiff alleges to have purchased the Vehicle at “Delano Chevrolet Buick GMC in Delano, CA (GM’s authorized dealer).” (FAC, ¶ 6.) Plaintiff also has failed to allege that the authorized dealership was an agent of Defendant. Nor are conclusory statements that the dealership is Defendant’s agent without more sufficient to establish a direct transactional relationship. (See Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 844 [finding the allegations sufficient where plaintiffs alleged they bought the car from a dealership and facts that the dealership was an agent of defendant]; see also Epperson v. General Motors, LLC (S.D. Cal. 2023) 706 F.Supp.3d 1031, 1045 [finding the allegations insufficient where the complaint merely concluded that stated that the vehicle was bought from an authorized dealership and agent of defendant].) Thus, Plaintiff has failed to allege sufficient facts to show Defendant was under a duty to disclose the fact to Plaintiff.

Accordingly, the demurrer is sustained. As Plaintiff did not oppose the demurrer, or offer facts that would cure the defects, the demurrer is sustained without leave to amend.

MOTION TO STRIKE

LEGAL STANDARD

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436(a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id., § 436(b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Id., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Id., § 437.)

DISCUSSION

Defendant moves to strike the demand for punitive damages in the Prayer for Relief. Defendant argues that punitive damages are not recoverable for Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act claims and that the FAC fails to state facts sufficient to support punitive damages.

The Court agrees with Defendant that punitive damages are not recoverable for Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act claims. (See Covert v. FCA USA, LLC (2022) 73 Cal.App.5th 821, 828.) Instead, the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act allows for civil penalties. (Civ. Code, § 1794.) Accordingly, punitive damages may not properly be sought for the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act claims. However, while the Prayer for Relief requests punitive damages generally, the specific allegations for the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act claims do not show that Plaintiff is seeking punitive damages for those claims. Accordingly, the motion to strike punitive damages as to the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty claims is GRANTED. Requests for punitive damages are stricken.

As to the remaining cause of action for Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment, the Court has sustained the demurrer to the cause of action, thus the motion GRANTS the motion to strike as to the fraudulent concealment cause of action. 

CONCLUSION

The demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND. The Motion to Strike punitive damages from the prayer for relief is GRANTED.

Jose Melendez v. General Motors, LLC

TENTATIVE RULING

The Court OVERRULES Defendant’s Demurrer to the First Amended Complaint. The Motion to Strike punitive damages is DENIED.

Defendant's Answer is due in 30 days.

On its own motion, the Court continues the Case Management Conference on August 28, 2026, to October 5, 2026 at 8:30 a.m. given Defendant's Answer is due after the CMC.

ANALYSIS

INTRODUCTION

This is a lemon law case. On July 14, 2022, Plaintiffs Jose Melendez and Isabel Melendez (“Plaintiffs”) purchased a 2022 Cadillac Escalade ESV (“Vehicle”) from Cadillac of Beverly Hills. (FAC, ¶ 5.) Plaintiffs allege that Defendant General Motors, LLC (“Defendant”) has been aware of the Defective Engine since at least early 2007 and has continued to actively conceal it, exposing Plaintiffs to safety risks and diminishing the value of the Vehicle. (Id. at ¶¶ 18-19.)

On January 30, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Complaint. On March 3, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging (1) Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; (2) Breach of Express Warranty under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; (3) Fraudulent Concealment; and (4) Violation of Civil Code Section 1750 et seq.

On April 10, 2026, Defendant filed this Demurrer to Plaintiffs’ First Amended Complaint and Motion to Strike Punitive Damages.

On June 18, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an opposition to the Demurrer and the Motion to Strike.

On July 27, 2026, Defendant filed a reply in support of the Demurrer and Motion to Strike.

DEMURRER

LEGAL STANDARD

In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

“The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds... (e) the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (f) The pleading is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, “uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible." (Code Civ. Proc. [AW13.1]§ 430.10.) “[D]emurrers for uncertainty are disfavored, and are granted only if the pleading is so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (A.J. Fistes Corp. v. GDL Best Contractors, Inc. (2019) 38 Cal. App. 5th[AW14.1] 677, 695.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id. [AW15.1]; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

DISCUSSION

Defendant demurs to the third cause of action for fraudulent concealment on the grounds that it is barred by the statute of limitations, fails to state facts sufficient to establish the action, and fails to allege a transactional relationship giving rise to a duty to disclose. Defendant also demurs to the fourth cause of action for violation of the Consumer Legal Remedies Act (“CLRA”) because it is barred by the statute of limitations, it fails to set forth sufficient facts, and it is barred by Plaintiffs’ failure to provide proper notice.

A. Statute of Limitations

Defendant argues the three-year statute of limitations for fraud bars the cause of action for fraudulent concealment and violation of the CLRA. Defendant contends that the Plaintiff bought the Vehicle on July 14, 2022, and should have filed their claim no later than July 14, 2025. Plaintiffs did not file their Complaint until March 2026.

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that, had they known about the fraudulent concealment violation on the date the Vehicle was purchased (July 14, 2022), Plaintiffs would not have purchased the Vehicle. Plaintiffs also argue that they do not allege that they were aware of the problems with the Defective Engine at the time of purchase; rather, Defendant has made this fact up. Plaintiffs allege that they could not have discovered the defective nature of the engine until shortly before the litigation was commenced. Plaintiffs allege the first time they discovered the defect was on February 1, 2023. The Complaint was filed on January 30, 2026, not in March 2026.

To sustain a demurrer on the statute of limitations, the running of the statute must appear clearly and affirmatively on the face of the complaint. (Geneva Towers Ltd. Partnership v. City of San Francisco (2003) 29 Cal.4th 769, 781.) “‘[I]t is not enough that the complaint might be time-barred. [Citation.]’” (Ibid.) “The general rule is that the statute of limitations begins to run when the wrongful act is done, even though the plaintiff is ignorant of his or her cause of action or of the identity of the wrongdoer.” (Snow v. A. H. Robins Co. (1985) 165 Cal.App.3d 120, 127.) The running of the statute of limitations may be postponed on certain causes of action until the time of discovery of the facts. (Ibid.) “With respect to actions based on fraud, the statute of limitations is tolled whenever plaintiff is able to show the defendant fraudulently concealed facts which would have led him to discover his potential cause of action.” (Id. at pp. 127-128.)

The statute of limitations for an action for relief on the ground of fraud or mistake is three years and the cause of action in that case is not deemed to have accrued until the discovery, by the aggrieved party, of the facts constituting the fraud or mistake. (Code Civ. Proc., § 338, subd. (d).) Similarly, the statute of limitations for violation of the CRLA is also three years. (Civ. Code, § 1783.) The statute of limitations on a CLRA claim does not being to run until the car buyer knows or appreciate his injury. (See Medina v. St. George Auto Sales, Inc. (2024) 103 Cal.App.5th 1194, 1210.)

Plaintiffs filed the Complaint on January 30, 2026, not in March 2026. Plaintiffs allege they purchased the Vehicle on July 14, 2022. (FAC, ¶ 5.) Plaintiffs allege that they would not have purchased the Vehicle or would have paid substantially less had they known about the Defective Engine prior to or at the time of the purchase on July 14, 2022. (Id. at ¶ 24.) Plaintiffs allege that the Engine Defect was reported on February 1, 2023. (Id. at ¶ 42.)

Plaintiffs have sufficiently alleged that they did not discover the fact that Defendant had fraudulently concealed the defect or appreciate their injury on the date of purchase because, had Plaintiffs known of the fraudulent concealment of the defect, Plaintiffs would not have purchased the Vehicle. Thus, the statute of limitations began to run on February 1, 2023, not July 14, 2022. Three years from February 1, 2023 would be February 1, 2026. The Complaint was filed January 30, 2026. Thus, the claims are not time-barred based on the Complaint.

The demurrer to the third and fourth causes of action based on the statute of limitations is overruled.

B. Fraudulent Concealment

Defendant contends that the third cause of action fails to state sufficient facts and fails to allege a transactional relationship giving rise to a duty to disclose. Defendant argues that Plaintiffs failed to plead specific facts that Defendant allegedly failed to disclose, allegations that Defendant knew of those facts when Plaintiff purchased the Vehicle, or what materials Plaintiffs reviewed and relied upon in purchasing the Vehicle. Also, the Complaint does not allege that Plaintiffs purchased the Vehicle directly from Defendant, nor have Plaintiffs alleged any specific facts showing that the dealer from which they purchased their Vehicle was Defendant’s agent.

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that they have adequately pled each element of fraudulent concealment. Also, Plaintiffs have alleged a transactional relationship because they alleged that Defendant backed the Vehicle with warranties, that Defendant advertises and markets its’ vehicles to the public through press releases, marketing brochures, and websites, and that Defendant’s authorized dealerships are Defendant’s agents for the purposes of selling and marketing Defendant’s vehicles to consumers.

Fraud based on concealment requires that “(1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.” (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 310-311 (Bigler-Engler).) “Even under the strict rules of common law pleading, one of the canons was that less particularity is required when the facts lie more in the knowledge of the opposite party.” (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn., Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1256, 1384.)

An essential element of intentional concealment includes the duty to disclose, which must be based upon a transaction, or a special relationship, between plaintiff and defendant. (Id. at p. 314.) “There are ‘four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.’” (Id. at p. 311.) “[O]ther than the first instance, in which there must be a fiduciary relationship between the parties, ‘the other three circumstances in which nondisclosure may be actionable presuppose[ ] the existence of some other relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise. . . . “[W]here material facts are known to one party and not to the other, failure to disclose them is not actionable fraud unless there is some relationship between the parties which gives rise to a duty to disclose such known facts.” [Citation.]’ [Citation.]” (Hoffman v. 162 North Wolfe LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 1178, 1187 (Hoffman).) “Such a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.” (Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at p. 312.)

The FAC alleges that Defendant has been aware of the Defective Engine since at least early 2007 and refused to disclose the Defective Engine’s existence. (FAC, ¶¶ 18-19.) Plaintiffs allege that Defendant intentionally concealed information from Plaintiffs and that “at no time were Plaintiffs told or made aware, either publicly or privately, that the Vehicle was equipped with the Defective Engine.” (Id. at ¶ 39.) Plaintiffs allege that Defendant had exclusive and superior knowledge of the Defective Engine. (Id. at ¶ 20). Plaintiffs allege they would not have purchased the Vehicle had they been made aware of the defects. (Id. at ¶ 40.) Plaintiffs alleged that they were damaged by Defendant’s conduct. (Id. at ¶¶ 25-27.) These allegations are specific enough to allege the information that was concealed and the danger posed. (See Jones v. ConocoPhillips Co. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1187, 1199-1200.)

As to the transactional relationship, the FAC alleges that Plaintiffs purchased the Vehicle from Cadillac of Beverly Hills, an authorized dealership of Defendant. (FAC, ¶¶ 5-6.) The FAC alleges that sales of new motor vehicles to consumers take place exclusively through Defendant’s authorized dealerships. (Id. at ¶ 6.) The FAC also alleges that, in order to do business with Defendant, Plaintiffs were required to purchase the Vehicle from one of Defendant’s authorized dealerships. (Ibid.) Furthermore, Defendant provides all Cadillac customers, including Plaintiffs, with a warranty. (Id. at ¶ 9.) Plaintiffs allege that the dealers are agents of Defendant. (Id. at ¶ 11.) Thus, because Plaintiffs have alleged that Plaintiffs purchased the Vehicle from an authorized dealer and agent of Defendant with a warranty provided by Defendant, Plaintiffs have adequately alleged a transactional relationship from which a duty to disclose arises. (See Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 844 [finding the allegations sufficient where plaintiffs alleged they bought the car from a dealership and facts that the dealership was an agent of defendant].)

Accordingly, the demurrer to the third cause of action is OVERRULED.

C. Violation of the CLRA

Defendant contends the fourth cause of action fails to state sufficient facts and is barred by Plaintiffs’ failure to provide proper notice. Defendant contends that no letter or notice was sent to Defendant that qualifies under section 1782(a). Defendant argues that Plaintiffs admit this fact in the Complaint. Nor is Plaintiffs’ action seeking injunctive relief. Also, the cause of action fails because Plaintiff did not identify what Defendant misrepresented. Defendant is not legally responsible to Plaintiffs for representations that the dealership sales personnel may have made.

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that CLRA notice does not need to be sent before commencement of a lawsuit. Plaintiffs also argue that they seek injunctive relief and will amend the claim to state a claim for damages. Plaintiffs allege that the FAC states that notice was given in writing and sent on January 30, 2026. Also, no heightened pleading requirement is necessary for CLRA claims, even if they are based in fraud. Moreover, Plaintiffs allege detailed representations made by Defendant that form the basis of the alleged violations of the CLRA.

i. Notice Requirements

Civil Code section 1782(a) requires: “Thirty days or more prior to the commencement of an action for damages pursuant to this title, the consumer shall do the following:

(1) Notify the person alleged to have employed or committed methods, acts, or practices declared unlawful by Section 1770 of the particular alleged violations of Section 1770.

(2) Demand that the person correct, repair, replace, or otherwise rectify the goods or services alleged to be in violation of Section 1770.

The notice shall be in writing and shall be sent by certified or registered mail, return receipt requested, to the place where the transaction occurred or to the person's principal place of business within California.”

(Civ. Code, § 1782, subd. (a).) “An action for injunctive relief brought under the specific provisions of Section 1770 may be commenced without compliance with subdivision (a). Not less than 30 days after the commencement of an action for injunctive relief, and after compliance with subdivision (a), the consumer may amend his or her complaint without leave of court to include a request for damages. The appropriate provisions of subdivision (b) or (c) shall be applicable if the complaint for injunctive relief is amended to request damages.” (Civ. Code, § 1782, subd. (d).)

The FAC states: “Plaintiffs complied with the notice provisions of Civil Code section 1782 by notifying Defendants on January 30, 2026, of the particular alleged violations of Civil Code section 1770 and demanding that it correct, repair, replace, or otherwise rectify the goods or services alleged to be in violation of Civil Code section 1770. The notice was given in writing and was sent by certified mail, return receipt requested, to Defendants. Defendants have failed, within thirty (30) days of receipt, to respond to Plaintiffs' demand and/or provide Plaintiffs with an appropriate correction, repair, replacement, relief, cure or any other remedy.” (FAC, ¶ 167.) Plaintiffs seek injunctive relief and damages. (Id. at ¶¶ 171-174.) Thus, Plaintiffs have alleged facts sufficient to demonstrate that they have complied with the notice requirements.

Accordingly, the demurrer to the fourth cause of action is overruled on these grounds.

ii. Sufficiency of Pleadings

An omission is actionable under the CLRA if the omitted fact is (1) “contrary to a [material] representation actually made by the defendant” or (2) is “a fact the defendant was obliged to disclose.” (Gutierrez v. Carmax Auto Superstores California (2018) 19 Cal.App.5th 1234, 1258.) Failure to disclose a fact constitutes a deceptive practice actionable under the CRLA when “the defendant is the plaintiff's fiduciary, when the defendant has exclusive knowledge of material facts not known or reasonably accessible to the plaintiff, and when the defendant actively conceals a material fact. In addition, the duty to disclose exists ‘when the defendant makes partial representations that are misleading because some other material fact has not been disclosed.’ In the context of the CLRA, a fact is ‘material’ if a reasonable consumer would deem it important in determining how to act in the transaction at issue.” (Ibid.) In other words, a defendant has a duty to disclose when the fact is known to the defendant and the failure to disclose it is “ ‘misleading in light of other facts ... that [the defendant] did disclose.’ ” (Ibid.) Causes of action under the CLRA must be stated with reasonable particularity, which is a more lenient pleading standard than is applied to common law fraud claims. (Id. at p. 1261.)

Here, the FAC alleges that Defendant sold Plaintiffs the Vehicle knowing that the Defective Engine did not and could not conform to the standard, quality, and/or grade of the vehicles it was advertising. (FAC, ¶ 157.) The FAC alleges Defendant knew by 2007 that the Engine Defect could not be fixed. (Id. at ¶ 159.) The FAC alleges that Defendant entered a warranty with Plaintiffs ensuring proper operation and performance of the Vehicle and its components. (Id. at ¶ 162.) The FAC alleges the Defendant represented to Plaintiffs and other consumers that the Defective Engine had “more power”, increasing fuel economy while not reducing performance and durability. (Id. at ¶ 152.) A salesperson for an authorized dealership and agent of Defendant represented quality, durability, fuel efficiency, and impressive performance of the Vehicle. (Id. at ¶ 38.) Plaintiffs also reviewed Defendant’s advertising and marketing materials prior to purchase and none of them disclosed the ongoing Defective Engine. (Id. at ¶ 104.) Plaintiffs list marketing statements in the FAC. (See id. at ¶¶ 91-95.) The FAC alleges that Defendant knew that the Vehicle, equipped with the Defective Engine, did not have the characteristics, uses, and benefits Defendant represented. (Id. at ¶ 153.) Plaintiffs allege that the Defective Engine was a safety-related concern that obligated Defendant to disclose it to all consumers. (Id. at ¶ 104). Plaintiffs have alleged facts which demonstrate that the defect implicated safety concerns important to the reasonable consumer, that Defendant marketed the Vehicle in good condition, that Defendant was aware of the Engine Defect, and that Defendant and its agents misrepresented or concealed facts about the defect. Thus, the Court finds that these allegations are sufficient to state a duty to disclose exists because Defendant and its agents have misrepresented the safety and quality of the Vehicle. (See Gutierrez, supra, 19 Cal.App.5th at pp. 1261-1262.)

Accordingly, the demurrer to the fourth cause of action is OVERRULED on these grounds.

MOTION TO STRIKE

LEGAL STANDARD

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436(a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id., § 436(b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Id., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Id., § 437.)

DISCUSSION

Defendant moves to strike the demand for punitive damages in the Prayer for Relief.

Defendant argues that punitive damages are not recoverable for Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act claims and that the FAC fails to state facts sufficient to support punitive damages. Defendant argues that Plaintiffs have failed to allege facts to justify the demand for punitive damages. Defendant incorporates arguments from the Demurrer to argue that neither the cause of action for fraudulent concealment nor the cause of action for violation of the CLRA state sufficient causes of action and, therefore, cannot support punitive damages.

In opposition, Plaintiffs contend they do not seek punitive damages for the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act claims. Plaintiffs argue that they have specifically alleged that Plaintiffs relied on statements made during the purchase process by Defendant’s agents, that Defendant and its agents omitted disclosing any information about the Defective Engine, and that Defendant’s authorized technician represented to Plaintiffs that the Vehicle was operating as intended and safe to drive. Plaintiffs argue the defects were a safety hazard and that Defendant intentionally defrauded Plaintiffs to enrich itself at their expense.

As an initial matter, the Court agrees that punitive damages are not recoverable for Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act claims. (See Covert v. FCA USA, LLC (2022) 73 Cal.App.5th 821, 828.) Instead, the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act allows for civil penalties. (Civ. Code, § 1794.) Accordingly, punitive damages may not properly be sought for the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act claims. However, while the Prayer for Relief requests punitive damages generally, the specific allegations for the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act claims do not show that Plaintiff is seeking punitive damages for those claims. Accordingly, the motion to strike punitive damages as to the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty claims is moot.

As to the remaining causes of action for fraudulent concealment and violation of the CLRA, the Court has overruled the Demurrer. Thus, the Court must determine whether the FAC state facts sufficient to support punitive damages.

Civil Code section 3294 authorizes the recovery of punitive damages in non-contract cases where “the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice . . . .” (Civ. Code § 3294(a).) “‘Malice’ means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Id., § 3294(c)(1).) A plaintiff must establish the defendant was aware of the probable dangerous consequences of his conduct and that he willfully and deliberately failed to avoid those consequences to support an award of punitive damages based on conscious disregard of the safety of others. (Penner v. Falk (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 858, 867.) “‘Oppression’ means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.” (Civ. Code § 3294(c)(2).)

“‘Fraud’ means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.” (Id., § 3294(c)(3).)

“In order to survive a motion to strike an allegation of punitive damages, the ultimate facts showing an entitlement to such relief must be pled by a plaintiff.” (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.) Punitive damages thus require more than the mere commission of a tort. (See Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890, 894-95.) Specific facts must be pleaded in support of punitive damages. (See Hillard v. A.H. Robins Co. (1983) 148 Cal.App.3d 374, 391-92.) Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim.” (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166 (citations omitted).) Conclusory characterizations of defendant’s conduct as willful, intentional or fraudulent is a patently insufficient statement of the necessary factual grounds for punitive damages. (Brousseau v. Jarrett (1977) 73 Cal.App.3d 864, 872.)

The Court finds that Plaintiffs have not merely pled conclusory statements and have sufficiently pled facts to support a finding of punitive damages. As pointed to in the Demurrer, the FAC alleges that Defendant sold Plaintiffs the Vehicle knowing that the Defective Engine did not and could not conform to the standard, quality, and/or grade of the vehicles it was advertising. (FAC, ¶ 157.) The FAC alleges Defendant knew by 2007 that the Engine Defect could not be fixed. (Id. at ¶ 159.) The FAC alleges that Defendant entered a warranty with Plaintiffs ensuring proper operation and performance of the Vehicle and its components. (Id. at ¶ 162.) The FAC alleges the Defendant represented to Plaintiffs and other consumers that the Defective Engine had “more power”, increasing fuel economy while not reducing performance and durability. (Id. at ¶ 152.) A salesperson for an authorized dealership and agent of Defendant represented quality, durability, fuel efficiency, and impressive performance of the Vehicle. (Id. at ¶ 38.) Plaintiffs also reviewed Defendant’s advertising and marketing materials prior to purchase and none of them disclosed the ongoing Defective Engine. (Id. at ¶ 104.) Plaintiffs list marketing statements in the FAC. (See id. at ¶¶ 91-95.) The FAC alleges that Defendant knew that the Vehicle, equipped with the Defective Engine, did not have the characteristics, uses, and benefits Defendant represented. (Id. at ¶ 153.) Plaintiffs allege that the Defective Engine was a safety-related concern that obligated Defendant to disclose it to all consumers. (Id. at ¶ 104). The Court finds that these allegations sufficiently allege facts for punitive damages.

Accordingly, the Motion to Strike punitive damages is DENIED.

CONCLUSION

The Court OVERRULES Defendant’s Demurrer to the First Amended Complaint. The Motion to Strike punitive damages is DENIED.
