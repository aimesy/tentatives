CASE:                          Nolasco v. Lee Kum Kee (U.S.A.) Inc., et al.

 

CASE NO.:                   21STCV12160

 

           

 

MOTION FOR APPROVAL OF SETTLEMENT

 

 

MOVING PARTY/PARTIES: Plaintiff Juan Nolasco, individually and on behalf of other aggrieved employees

 

RESPONDING PARTY/PARTIES: None

 

PROOF OF SERVICE:

·        Correct Address: Yes

·        Served electronically on March 16, 2026 for September 3, 2026 hearing date

 

CASE HISTORY:

·        March 30, 2021: Complaint filed.

·        August 30, 2022: Motion to compel Plaintiff to arbitrate his individual claim granted.

·        September 11, 2023: Motion to compel arbitration of Plaintiff’s representative claims denied.

·        August 28, 2025: Arbitrator issued award in favor of Plaintiff on Plaintiff’s individual claim.

·        January 16, 2026: Parties executed settlement of remaining claims after mediation.

 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

            Plaintiff Juan Nolasco (“Plaintiff”), individually and on behalf of other similarly situated employees, sued Defendants Lee Kum Kee (U.S.A.), Inc. and Lee Kum Kee (USA) Foods, Inc. (“Defendants”) for wage and hour violations.  Plaintiff seeks court approval of the settlement of Plaintiff’s representative claims under the Labor Code Private Attorneys General Act of 2004, or PAGA. Defendants do not oppose the motion.

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

Plaintiff’s motion for approval of settlement is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE

Moving party to give notice. 

 

DISCUSSION:

 

Motion for approval of settlement

“PAGA settlements are subject to trial court review and approval, ensuring that any negotiated resolution is fair to those affected.”  (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 549.)  Per Labor Code section 2699, subdivision (a), “civil penalties recovered by aggrieved employees shall be distributed as follows: 75 percent to the Labor and Workforce Development Agency . . . and 25 percent to the aggrieved employees.” (Lab. Code, § 2699, subd. (i).)

The parties agreed to settle the PAGA claims for $1,250,000 total. Of this amount, the parties propose that Plaintiff’s counsel will receive one-third of the gross settlement amount, or $416,625, in fees and $26,817.04 in costs, and the administrator will receive up to $3,500 in costs for settlement administration.  The parties also propose that Plaintiff receive $10,000.  This would leave $793,016.29 of the settlement for PAGA penalties. Of this amount, the Labor and Workforce Development Agency would receive $594,762.22, and $198,254.07 would go to individual penalty payments for aggrieved employees.  The Court has concerns regarding the proposed settlement.

The Court compelled Plaintiff to arbitrate his individual claim against Defendants.  The arbitrator issued an award in Plaintiff’s favor.  It is therefore unclear to the Court why Plaintiff should receive an additional $10,000 in settlement funds.  Secondly, Plaintiff’s counsel advances evidence that they billed attorneys’ fees in the total amount of $217,742.50.  This lodestar figure is based on billing at hourly rates as high as $1,275 per hour, which amply compensate Plaintiff’s counsel for their work on this matter.  Accordingly, the Court is not inclined to permit Plaintiff’s counsel to recover more than this lodestar figure from the settlement.  Other than these issues, the Court considers this settlement fair and reasonable.  If the parties stipulate to reduce the attorneys’ fees from the settlement to $217,742.50 and to eliminate the $10,000 payment to Plaintiff, the Court will approve the settlement.  If not, the Court must continue the matter for further briefing. 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion for approval of settlement is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE. 

Moving party to give notice. 

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 22STCV10372    Hearing Date: September 3, 2026    Dept: 415

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Rolf M. Treu, Department 415

 

 

HEARING DATE:        September 3, 2026                               TRIAL DATE:  Vacated

                                                          

CASE:                          Schmitz v. Beach City Capital, LLC, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                   22STCV10372

 

           

 

MOTION FOR CHARGING ORDER

MOTION FOR ASSIGNMENT OR TURNOVER

 

 

MOVING PARTY/PARTIES: Plaintiff and Judgment Creditor Florian Schmitz

 

RESPONDING PARTY/PARTIES: None

 

PROOF OF SERVICE:

·        Correct Address: Yes

·        Served electronically on February 5, 2026 for an original hearing date of June 4, 2026, continued to this date by this Court’s order of June 8, 2026

 

CASE HISTORY:

·        March 25, 2022: Complaint filed.

·        September 15, 2023: Stipulated judgment entered.

 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

            Plaintiff and Judgment Creditor Florian Schmitz (“Plaintiff”) moves for a charging order against the interests of Defendants and Judgment Debtors Jason Muller (“Muller”), Beach City Capital, LLC, and/or Beach City Capital Management, LLC (collectively, “Defendants”) in Local Collaborative Hermosa, LLC; Local Collaborative Laguna, LLC, and Local Collaborative, LLC (collectively, “Local Collaborative”).  Plaintiff also moves for an assignment of any payments from Local Collaborative to Defendants.  The Court previously considered this matter and, in its order of June 8, 2026, continued the matter to this date.

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

Plaintiff’s motion for charging order and Plaintiff’s motion for turnover order are DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE

Moving party to give notice. 

 

DISCUSSION:

 

Motion for charging order

 

Assets in a limited liability company are not subject to a monetary judgment against a member personally. A judgment creditor must obtain a court order charging the assets in a limited liability company with the amount of the judgment against the member.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 699.720(a)(2), 708.310.)  The judgment creditor may make a motion for a charging order in the court that entered the judgment.  (See Corps. Code, §§ 15907.03, subd. (a), 16504, subd. (a), 17705.03, subd. (a); Code Civ. Proc., §§ 708.310, 708.320.)

The judgment creditor’s personal service of the motion on the limited liability company creates a lien on the judgment debtor’s interest in the limited liability company. (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.320. subd. (a).)  Unless the Court refuses to issue the charging order, the lien continues until the judgment becomes unenforceable.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 697.030, 697.040, 708.320, subd. (b).)

As the Court noted in its order of June 8, 2026, Plaintiff served this motion on Muller by email.  While Muller is the registered agent for service of process for Local Collaborative, Plaintiff nonetheless must personally serve Muller as the agent for service of process for Local Collaborative.  Accordingly, the Court continued the matter to this date for Plaintiff to properly serve Local Collaborative.  Plaintiff nonetheless has not filed proof of personal service on Muller.  As such, the Court must DENY he matter.

Motion for turnover order

A judgment creditor may obtain an assignment of all or part of a right to payment due, or to become due.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.510, subd. (a).)  The Court must make a noticed motion for an assignment order, and must serve the notice on the judgment debtor personally or by mail.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.510, subds. (a), (b).) 

Plaintiff served this motion on Muller by email.  While Muller is the registered agent for service of process for Local Collaborative, Plaintiff nonetheless must personally serve Muller as the agent for service of process for Local Collaborative, or serve Muller by mail.  Accordingly, the Court must continue the matter for Plaintiff to properly serve Local Collaborative. 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion for charging order and motion for turnover order are DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.    

Moving party to give notice. 

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV11101    Hearing Date: September 3, 2026    Dept: 415

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Rolf M. Treu, Department 415

 

 

HEARING DATE:        September 3, 2026                               TRIAL DATE:  Not set.

                                                          

CASE:                          S David Sedaghat v. Jerry Roles, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                   24STCV27659

 

           

 

DEMURRER TO COMPLAINT

MOTION TO STRIKE PORTIONS OF COMPLAINT

 

 

MOVING PARTY/PARTIES: Defendant Tarzana Post Acute, LLC

 

RESPONDING PARTY/PARTIES: None

 

PROOF OF SERVICE:

·        Correct Address: Yes

·        Served electronically on March 25, 2026 for September 3, 2026 hearing date

 

CASE HISTORY:

·        April 16, 2025: Complaint filed.

 

 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

           

            Plaintiff S David Sedaghat (“Plaintiff”), as a self-represented litigant, initiated this action based on the alleged wrongful death of his father while his father was a resident of a long-term care facility.  Defendant Tarzana Health & Rehabilitation (“Defendant”) demurs to the complaint.  Defendant also moves to strike portions of the complaint.  Plaintiff has not opposed the motions.

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Defendant’s demurrer to the complaint is SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to the first, second, third, fourth, eighth, tenth, and eleventh causes of action in the complaint.  Defendant’s demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend as to the sixth, seventh, and ninth causes of action in the complaint.

To the extent it is not moot, Defendant’s motion to strike punitive damages allegations from Plaintiff’s complaint is GRANTED with leave to amend.

Moving party to give notice. 

 

DISCUSSION:

 

Demurrer to Complaint

 

“It is black letter law that a demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.”  (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.)  In ruling on a demurrer, the court must “liberally construe[]” the allegations of the complaint.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)  “This rule of liberal construction means that the reviewing court draws inferences favorable to the plaintiff, not the defendant.”  (Perez v. Golden Empire Transit Dist. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1228, 1238.)

Standing to sue

            To the extent that Plaintiff seeks to maintain this action as the successor-in-interest of Plaintiff’s deceased father, Plaintiff must file an affidavit or declaration that complies with the requirements of Code of Civil Procedure section 377.32.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 377.32; see In re A.C. (2000) 80 Cal.App.4th 994, 1002-1003.)  Plaintiff has not done so.  Accordingly, Plaintiff fails to allege he has standing to assert claims as the successor-in-interest of his deceased father. 

Statute of limitations

            Defendant purports to demur based on the statute of limitations.  However, as Defendant concedes, “Plaintiff’s Complaint contains no dates . . . .”  (Memorandum of Points and Authorities in Support of Demurrer, p. 9.)  Accordingly, the Court cannot reach the issue of whether the statute of limitations bars Plaintiff’s claims on demurrer.  

First cause of action: wrongful death

To state a cause of action for wrongful death, Plaintiff need only allege that Defendant’s wrongful acts caused the death of Plaintiff’s father.  (Norgart v. Upjohn Co. (1999) 21 Cal.4th 383, 404.) Plaintiff’s allegations suffice to support the cause of action for wrongful death.  (Complaint, ¶ 22.)  However, as set forth above, Plaintiff fails to allege he has standing to sue as his father’s successor-in-interest.  Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the first cause of action with leave to amend.

Second cause of action: elder abuse

            Plaintiff’s second cause of action is for elder abuse.  The Elder Abuse and Dependent Adult Civil Protection Act defines an elder as “any person residing in this state, 65 years of age or older.” (Welf. & Inst. Code, § 15610.27.) The statute prohibits “[a]buse of an elder or a dependent adult[,]” which is defined as “physical abuse, neglect, financial abuse, abandonment, isolation, abduction, or other treatment with resulting physical harm or pain or mental suffering[.]” (Welf. & Inst. Code, § 15610.07.)   Because it is a statutory claim, Plaintiff must allege the cause of action for elder abuse with particularity.  (Covenant Care, Inc. v. Superior Court (2004) 32 Cal.4th 771, 790.)  Plaintiff alleges Defendant’s actions caused his father’s “being abused, emotionally and physically , suffering pain , infection, unjustified repeated falls,[] malnutrition an[d] dehydration , harm from [] harmful[] medication and lack of proper and urgent medical care . . . .”  (Complaint, ¶ 24.)  Plaintiff fails to allege specific facts to support the claim against Defendant.  Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the second cause of action with leave to amend.

Third cause of action: Unruh Civil Rights Act

Plaintiff’s third cause of action is under the Unruh Civil Rights Act.  To state a cause of action under the Unruh Civil Rights Act, Plaintiff must allege that Defendant is a business establishment that intentionally denied Plaintiff full and equal accommodations, advantages, facilities, privileges, or services based on Plaintiff’s sex, race, color, religion, ancestry, national origin, disability, medical condition, marital status, or sexual orientation. (Civ. Code, § 51, subd. (b).)  Plaintiff does not allege facts in support of the third cause of action to show how or on what basis Defendant purportedly discriminated against Plaintiff’s late father.  (See Complaint, ¶ 26.)  Furthermore, as set forth above, Plaintiff fails to allege he has standing to sue as his father’s successor-in-interest.  Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the third cause of action with leave to amend.

Fourth cause of action: negligence and negligent supervision

            In support of the fourth cause of action for negligence, Plaintiff alleges, “Defendant[] [was] negligent in providing adequate support, care , rehabilitation and monitoring.  Further defendant[] had a duty to have a supervisory supervision of the staff and failed to implement proper and adequate environment of support, care and supervision leading to decedent suffering multiple episodes of infection , falls, broken bones, dehydrating, malnutrition , becoming substantially underweight , unable to walk and ill.”  (Complaint, p. 6.)  There is no requirement that Plaintiff plead negligence with particularity.  As such, Plaintiff’s allegations suffice.  However, as set forth above, Plaintiff fails to allege he has standing to sue as the successor-in-interest of his father.  Accordingly, Plaintiff must amend.  The Court sustains the demurrer to the fourth cause of action with leave to amend.

Fifth cause of action: failure to warn

Defendant demurs to the fifth cause of action.  However, Plaintiff does not assert the fifth cause of action against Defendant.  Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action is moot.

Sixth cause of action: reckless endangerment

Plaintiff’s sixth cause of action is for reckless endangerment.  This is not a recognized cause of action.  As such, the Court sustains the demurrer to the sixth cause of action without leave to amend.

Seventh cause of action: unjust enrichment

Plaintiff’s seventh cause of action is for unjust enrichment.  Unjust enrichment is not a cause of action, but rather a remedy.  (Melchior v. New Line Productions, Inc. (2003) 106 Cal. App. 4th 779, 794.)  The Court sustains the demurrer to the seventh cause of action without leave to amend.

Eighth cause of action: fraudulent concealment

            Plaintiff’s eighth cause of action is for fraud.  Plaintiff must allege fraud with particularity.  “This means: (1) general pleading of the legal conclusion of fraud is insufficient; and (2) every element of the cause of action for fraud must be alleged in full, factually and specifically, and the policy of liberal construction of pleading will not usually be invoked to sustain a pleading that is defective in any material respect.”  (Wilhelm v. Pray, Price, Williams & Russell (1986) 186 Cal.App.3d 1324, 1331.)  There are four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.”  (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336, internal quotations and citations omitted.)  Here, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant “conspired to conceal the tacit conspiracy to harm patient and deprive proper care and rehabilitation and instructed and threatened other employees to go along with the conspiracy.”  (Complaint, p. 7.)  Plaintiff does not allege specific facts to support these conclusory allegations.  Plaintiff has not alleged his eighth cause of action with particularity.  The Court sustains the demurrer to the eighth cause of action with leave to amend.

Ninth cause of action: conspiracy to breach duty of care

Plaintiff’s ninth cause of action is for conspiracy to breach duty of care.  This is not a recognized cause of action.  (Favila v. Katten Muchin Rosenman LLP (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 189, 206; see also Patrick v. Alacer (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 995, 1016, fn.11.)  As such, the Court sustains the demurrer to the ninth cause of action without leave to amend.

Tenth cause of action: intentional and negligent infliction of mental and emotional distress

Per California Rules of Court, rule 2.112, each cause of action in Plaintiff’s complaint must be “separately stated . . . .”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.112.)  Plaintiff cannot lump together causes of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress and negligent infliction of emotional distress as one cause of action.  Further, Plaintiff’s complaint does not include any allegations in support of this cause of action.  Plaintiff must amend.  The Court sustains the demurrer to the tenth cause of action with leave to amend.

Eleventh cause of action: Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act

Plaintiff’s eleventh cause of action is under the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act, or RICO.  To state a claim under RICO, Plaintiff must allege the conduct of an enterprise that caused injury to Plaintiff’s business or property through a pattern of racketeering activity that affects interstate commerce.  (McMartin v. Children's Institute International (1989) 212 Cal.App.3d 1393, 1406.)  Plaintiff alleges, “conduct of [D]efendant w[as] organized, to recklessly provide substandard acre with reckless disregard of patient health and safety.”  (Complaint, p. 8.)  Plaintiff alleges no facts to support this conclusion.  Furthermore, Plaintiff does not allege that Plaintiff’s business or property sustained damages.  The Court sustains the demurrer to the eleventh cause of action with leave to amend.

Motion to strike portions of complaint

Sustaining Defendant’s demurrer renders the motion to moot.  However, to the extent it is not moot, the Court grants the motion to strike the punitive damages allegations with leave to amend.

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a motion to strike the whole pleading or any part thereof.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1322, subd. (b).)  On a motion to strike, the Court may: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the Court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782.)

In ruling on a motion to strike punitive damages, “judges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth.” (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)  To state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a plaintiff must allege the elements set forth in the punitive damages statute, Civil Code section 3294.  (Coll. Hosp., Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 721.)  Per Civil Code section 3294, a plaintiff must allege that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud or malice.  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).)  “Malice is defined in the statute as conduct intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.”  (Coll. Hosp., Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 725.)  “The mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages. Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim.”  (Grieves v. Superior Ct. (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166, internal citations and footnotes omitted.)

“[T]he imposition of punitive damages upon a corporation is based upon its own fault. It is not imposed vicariously by virtue of the fault of others.” (City Products Corp. v. Globe Indemnity Co. (1979) 88 Cal.App.3d 31, 36.)  “Corporations are legal entities which do not have minds capable of recklessness, wickedness, or intent to injure or deceive. An award of punitive damages against a corporation therefore must rest on the malice of the corporation’s employees. But the law does not impute every employee’s malice to the corporation. Instead, the punitive damages statute requires proof of malice among corporate leaders: the officers, directors, or managing agents.” (Cruz v. Home Base (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 160, 167, internal quotations and citation omitted.)

Here, Plaintiff fails to allege specific facts to show that Defendant’s officers, directors, or managing agents acted with malice.  As such, Plaintiff’s allegations do not support a claim for punitive damages.  For the same reason, Plaintiff’s allegations do not support exemplary damages.

Defendant moves to strike Plaintiff’s allegations that the various Defendants acted as agents of each other.  The Supreme Court of California has criticized allegations like this as “egregious examples of generic boilerplate . . . .” (Moore v. Regents of University of California (1990) 51 Cal.3d 120, 134, fn. 12.)  However, “such allegations may be necessary, especially at the outset of a lawsuit, before discovery.”  (Barsegian v. Kessler & Kessler (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 446, 451, internal quotations and citations omitted.)  For pleading purposes, Plaintiff’s agency allegations suffice.

Defendant also moves to strike allegations it contends are irrelevant or inflammatory.  A motion to strike cannot be used as a “‘line item veto’ for the civil defendant . . . .” (PH II, Inc. v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1680, 1683.) Defendant is not entitled to remove any allegation it contends is superfluous.  The motion to strike these allegations is without merit.

Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer to the complaint is SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to the first, second, third, fourth, eighth, tenth, and eleventh causes of action in the complaint.  Defendant’s demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend as to the sixth, seventh, and ninth causes of action in the complaint.

To the extent it is not moot, Defendant’s motion to strike punitive damages allegations from the complaint is granted with leave to amend. 

Plaintiff is to file an amended complaint within 10 days.

Moving party to give notice. 

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.
