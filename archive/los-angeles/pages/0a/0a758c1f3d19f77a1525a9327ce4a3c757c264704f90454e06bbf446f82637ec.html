VAISA, SR., ET AL. v. DOLPHIN PLACE PARTNERS, LLC, ET AL.

CASE NO.: 23NWCV01134 

HEARING: 09/08/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#7

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

I.             Petitioner John Angelo Vaisa Sr.’s Petition for Approval of Compromise of Claim for Minor Jacob Vaisa is CONTINUED to September 24, 2026 at 9:30 AM in Dept. P.

 

II.            Petitioner is instructed to file the omitted attachment prior to the continued hearing date.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Petitioner John Angelo Vaisa Sr. (Petitioner) petitions for approval of compromise of claim or action or disposition of proceeds for minor Jacob Vaisa (Claimant).

 

Background

 

On April 12, 2023, Plaintiffs John Angelo Vaisa Sr.; Erika Vaisa; Jesus Oswaldo Martinez And Stephanie Martinez, minors, by and through their Guardian Ad Litem, Erika Vaisa; and Jaylene Angela Vaisa and Jonathan Joseph Vaisa, minors, by and through their Guardian Ad Litem, John Angelo Vaisa Sr. (collectively, Plaintiffs) filed this habitability action against Defendants Dolphin Place Partners, LLC; Dashmesh K Chopra; Ivon Monteon; Leticia Lopez; and Does 1 through 30 (collectively, Defendants). On June 2, 2023, Plaintiffs filed the operative first amended complaint (FAC). The FAC alleges that Plaintiffs, as tenants of 8434 Tweedy Lane #102, Downey, State of California, 90240 (Subject Premises) notified Defendants, the owners and managers of the Subject Premises, of mold in their unit, to which Defendants failed to take sufficient action. (FAC, ¶¶ 9-16.) The FAC asserts four causes of action: (1) breach of the implied warranty of habitability; (2) negligent training and supervision; (3) violation of Civil Code § 1942.4; and (4) violation of the Unfair Competition Law [Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200].

 

Legal Standard

 

A Petition for approval of compromise of claim or action or disposition of proceeds of judgment for minor or person with a disability must conform with Code of Civil Procedure section 372 and Probate Code sections 2504 and 3600 et seq. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950.) Such a petition “must be verified by the petitioner and must contain a full disclosure of all information that has any bearing upon the reasonableness of the compromise.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950.) Moving papers should include the Petition of Guardian Ad Litem for Compromise of Disputed Claim of Minor or Person with Disability (Petition) on Form MC-350 and an Order Approving Compromise of Minor’s Claim (Order) Form MC-351.

 

Discussion

 

Petitioner, as Guardian ad Litem and father of Claimant, age three, petitions for approval or compromise of claim or disposition of proceeds for Claimant. Petitioner provides a Petition on Form MC-350, Proposed Order on Form MC-351, and Proposed Order to Deposit Funds in Blocked Account on Form-355. This Petition is unopposed.

 

Allocation

 

Defendants will pay $25,000 to Claimant as part of a global settlement to all Plaintiffs in the amount of $410,000. (Pet., ¶ 10; Attach.,10c.) As reflected in Attachment 10c, the agreement was agreed to verbally in a mediation. Petitioner contends that Defendants have not provided a long-form agreement. (Pet., ¶ 10c.)

 

Petitioner is a Plaintiff in this action and will receive $142,500. (Pet., ¶ 11b(5).) Claimant’s mother will receive $142,400, and the three other minor claimants will each receive $25,000. (Pet., ¶ 11b(5).) Petitioner indicates that the reason for the allocation is so the parents can use their net recovery towards purchasing a family residence. (Attach., 11b(6).)

 

Claimant’s net recovery will be $20,000. (Pet., ¶ 15.)

 

          Medical Expenses

 

The Petition does not claim any medical expenses. (Pet., ¶ 12a.) Petitioner represents that there are no statutory or contractual liens for payment of Claimant’s medical expenses. (Pet., ¶ 12b(5).)  

 

          Attorney’s Fees & Costs

 

The Petition seeks approval of $5,000 in attorney’s fees. (Pet., ¶ 13a; Attach., 13a.) This represents a 20% contingency fee that Plaintiffs’ counsel charges for minors. (Golde Decl., ¶ 8.) Plaintiffs’ counsel provides the retainer agreement, signed by Petitioner, in which Petitioner agreed to a 25% contingency fee. Thus, Plaintiffs’ counsel voluntarily reduced the fee to 20% as to Claimant. (Golde Decl., ¶ 8; Attach., 17a.) The Petition does not seek costs. (Pet., ¶ 13b.)

 

Based on the declaration of counsel and information provided in this Petition, the Court finds this amount reasonable. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.955.)

 

          Disposition of Settlement Funds

 

Petitioner requests that the proceeds be deposited in insured accounts in one or more financial institutions in this state, subject to withdrawal only on authorization of the Court. (Pet., ¶ 18b(2).)

 

Here, Petitioner does not file an Attachment 18b(2) to name the specific financial institution. The Court acknowledges that Petitioner submits Form MC-355 which includes a financial institution, but this information is omitted from the Petition itself. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950 [Petition “must contain a full disclosure of all information that has any bearing upon the reasonableness of the…disposition.”].)

 

Accordingly, this Petition is CONTINUED to September 24, 2026 at 9:30 AM in Dept. P to allow Petitioner to file the omitted attachment. Petitioner is instructed to file the omitted attachment prior to the continued hearing date.


Case Number: 23NWCV02532    Hearing Date: September 8, 2026    Dept: P

E.G. v. ABC UNIFIED SCHOOL DISTRICT, ET AL.

CASE NO.: 23NWCV02532

HEARING: 09/08/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#8

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

I.             Defendant ABC Unified School District’s demurrer to the third and fourth causes of action in the third amended complaint is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

 

II.            Defendant ABC Unified School District’s motion to strike is GRANTED.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant ABC Unified School District (ABCUSD) demurs to the third and fourth causes of action alleged against it in the third amended complaint (TAC).

 

Background

 

On August 7, 2023, Plaintiff E.G., a minor by her guardian ad litem Eric Gravitt, (Plaintiff) filed this action against Defendants ABCUSD; Dr. Crechena Wise, as an individual and in her official capacity as Director Secondary Curriculum & Professional Learning for ABCUSD; Melissa Christensen, as an individual and in her official capacity as principal of Kennedy Stem Academy; Melinda Ortiz, as an individual and her Director of School Services, Child Welfare and Attendance, and Does 1 to 50, inclusive (collectively, Defendants). Following the Court’s ruling on the demurrer sustaining in part and overruling in part the complaint, Plaintiff filed a first amended complaint (FAC). On October 14, 2025, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion for leave to file the second amended complaint (SAC). On February 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative TAC.

 

The TAC alleges the following: In January of 2022, when Plaintiff was a fifth grader attending a public school, she was informed she must get the COVID-19 vaccine or wear a mask to go to class. (TAC, ¶ 12.) Plaintiff’s father refused to take Plaintiff home and objected to the requirements on religious grounds. (TAC, ¶ 13.) The TAC alleges male students who objected to the requirements on religious grounds were allowed to return to class. (TAC, ¶ 14.) On January 25, 2022, Plaintiff was barred from entering the classroom by the school principal, Christensen, and three district administrators, Ortiz, Scott Smith, and Wise. (TAC, ¶¶ 15-16.) The administrators spoke to Plaintiff’s father, who recorded the conversation. (TAC, ¶ 17.) Ortiz directed Wise to call the sheriff’s department and file a police report claiming Plaintiff’s father was hostile. (TAC, ¶ 20.) During this time, Christensen and Ortiz walked Plaintiff to an isolation room and the sheriff took Plaintiff’s phone. (TAC, ¶¶ 22-23.) Plaintiff was then kept in a “closet-sized room” where Plaintiff had difficulty breathing. (TAC, ¶ 26.) The next day, Plaintiff and her father returned to school where they were blocked from entering the campus by police. (TAC, ¶ 31.) On February 21, 2022, Plaintiff reported that wearing a mask caused her to be dizzy and unable to breathe. (TAC, ¶ 35.) Bill Hundley (Mr. Hundley), the Child and Welfare and Attendance Unit Coordinator for ABCUSD, gave Plaintiff the option of sitting outside the classroom, which Plaintiff denied, or being directed to an isolation room until her parent picked her up. (TAC, ¶ 36.) Plaintiff was then prohibited from going to school for fifteen days. (TAC, ¶ 37.)

 

The TAC asserts five causes of action: (1) false imprisonment, (2) negligence, (3) discrimination, (4) harassment, and (5) defamation.

 

Legal Standard

 

Demurrer Standard

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) In assessing a demurrer, the court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of law.” (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.” (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at p. 747.) A complaint will be upheld against a demurrer if it pleads facts sufficient to place the defendant on notice of the issues sufficient to enable the defendant to prepare a defense. (Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 549-50.) 

 

“Generally it is an abuse of discretion to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment.” (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) A “[p]laintiff must show in what manner he can amend his complaint and how that amendment will change the legal effect of his pleading.” (Ibid.) 

 

Motion to Strike Standard 

 

Motions to strike are used to reach defects or objections to pleadings that are not challengeable by demurrer (i.e., words, phrases, prayer for damages, etc.). (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 435-437.) A motion to strike lies only where the pleading has irrelevant, false, or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleadings or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.) 

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that the demurring party meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference at least five days before the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) The demurring party shall file and serve a declaration stating either: (a) “The means by which the demurring party met and conferred…and that the parties did not reach an agreement” or (b) “[t]hat the party who filed the pleading subject to demurrer failed to respond to the meet and confer request of the demurring party or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).)

 

Defense counsel provides a declaration attesting to its attempts to meet and confer with Plaintiff’s counsel including (1) calling Plaintiff’s counsel and receiving an extension, (2) transmitting letters to her email address, (3) sending a meet and confer letter via Priority Mail Express. (Strumreiter Decl., ¶¶ 2-4.) Defense counsel explains that Plaintiff’s counsel let him a voicemail that certain letters were lost by her mail center. (Strumreiter Decl., ¶ 5.) Based on these attempts, the Court will proceed to rule on the demurrer and motion to strike. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(4).)

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

ABCUSD’s Notice of Demurrer refers to a Request for Judicial Notice filed December 18, 2025.

 

Plaintiff opposes this request for judicial notice on the grounds that ABCUSD failed to serve the request for judicial notice. Plaintiff puts forth a declaration by an individual who manages relevant mail who states that the envelope containing the demurrer did not contain the request for judicial notice. (Landau Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.) Plaintiff argues that ABCUSD’s demurrer is frivolous “because ABC failed to introduce and/or attempted to introduce the alleged government claim that ABC claims is at issue here so that Plaintiff can assess the authenticity and accuracy of the version of the government claim that ABC based this demurrer on. In doing so, ABC has deprived this Court from being able to make decision based on substantial evidence.” (Opp., 6:10-17.)

 

In reply, ABCUSD contends that it was filed and served on December 17, 2025, and thus was not filed concurrently with the instant demurrer.

 

Here, the Court finds that ABDUSD’s request for judicial notice is technically compliant. Under California Rules of Court, rule 3.1306(c), “[a] party requesting judicial notice of material under Evidence Code sections 452 or 453 must provide the court and each party with a copy of the material. If the material is part of a file in the court in which the matter is being heard, the party must: (1) Specify in writing the part of the court file sought to be judicially noticed; and (2) Either make arrangements with the clerk to have the file in the courtroom at the time of the hearing or confirm with the clerk that the file is electronically accessible to the court.” Plaintiff’s counsel does not dispute whether it was served or filed on December 17, 2025. Moreover, government claims are subject to judicial notice. (See Gong v. City of Rosemead (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 363, 377 [“If a plaintiff alleges compliance with the claims presentation requirement, but the public records do not reflect compliance, the governmental entity can request the court to take judicial notice under Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (c) that the entity's records do not show compliance.”].)

 

However, to the extent that Plaintiff disputes the accuracy of the government claim, the Court denies judicial notice. The Court notes that Plaintiff both refers to the importance of the government claim for adjudication on the merits of this demurrer and also disputes the accuracy of requested documents. However, the Court finds that judicial notice of the government claim is not essential to the ruling on this demurrer because both the statute of limitations argument and sufficiency of the allegations argument provide independent bases for the determination of this demurrer.

 

Discussion

 

ABCUSD demurs to the TAC on the grounds that the third and fourth causes of action fail to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action and are uncertain.

 

          Statute of Limitations

 

ABCUSD first demurs to the third and fourth causes of action on the grounds that they are barred by the statute of limitations.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that ABCUSD waived the statute of limitations argument because the Court “already decided the issue multiple times before finding that the added allegations arose from the same general set of facts” and also, because ABCUSD did not raise it in opposition to Plaintiff’s motion for leave to amend. (Opp., 5:25-28.)

 

In reply, ABCUSD argues that the Court has not addressed these arguments. The Court agrees. The only demurrer the Court heard on the merits in this action was the demurrer to the complaint, on October 9, 2024, where the statute of limitations was not raised. (Order, 10/9/24.) To the extent Plaintiff is referring to the demurrer to the SAC, the Court never heard that demurrer because prior to the hearing, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion for leave to amend. (See Order, 2/10/26; Notice of Withdrawal of Demurrers to SAC.) Moreover, as argued by ABCUSD, Plaintiff does not cite authority showing that a statute of limitations argument is waived if it is not raised in opposition to a motion for leave to amend. Therefore, the Court does not find that ABCUSD waived the statute of limitations argument and proceeds to address it for the first time here.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 338, subdivision (a) establishes a three-year statute of limitations for “[a]n action upon a liability created by statute.” “Unless an amended complaint relates back to a timely filed original complaint, it will be barred by the statute of limitations.” (Scholes v. Lambirth Trucking Co. (2017) 10 Cal.App.5th 590, 597, citation omitted.) “Under the relation-back doctrine, in order to avoid the statute of limitations, the amended complaint must: rest on the same general set of facts as the general complaint, refer to the same accident and same injuries as the original complaint, and refer to the same instrumentality as the original complaint.” (Id., at p. 597-98, citing Norgart v. Upjohn Co. (1999) 21 Cal.4th 383, 408-09.) “The relation-back doctrine requires us to compare the factual allegations in the original and amended complaints.” (Id., at p. 598.)

 

The TAC brings the third cause of action for discrimination pursuant to Education Code section 220, subdivision (a) and Government Code section 11135, subdivision (a). (TAC, ¶¶ 63-65 [“E.G. was deprived for her statutory right to fully participate in the educational process, free from discrimination and harassment.].) The fourth cause of action similarly arises out of violations of Education Code section 220, subdivisions (a), (b), and (c). Therefore, as argued by ABCUSD, these causes of action are governed by the statute of limitations under Code of Civil Procedure section 338, subdivision (a).

 

ABCUSD recounts the relevant procedural history regarding the prior complaints. The original complaint asserted claims for (1) false imprisonment, (2) negligence, (3) IIED, (4) violation of the Bane Act, (5) violation of the right to free speech, and (6) human experimentation. The second amended complaint (SAC), filed on October 14, 2025, marked the first time that the discrimination and harassment causes of action were added. While ABCUSD filed a demurrer and motion to strike the SAC, the Court found ABCUSD’s demurrer moot due to the granting of Plaintiff’s motion for leave to amend. (Order, 2/10/26.)

 

ABCUSD argues that the third and fourth causes of action are time-barred as they arise out of events that took place in February and March of 2022, over three years before October 14, 2025, when the claims were first brought in the SAC. (TAC, ¶ 38.) ABCUSD further argues that the third and fourth causes of action do not arise out of the same facts as those alleged in the FAC and therefore do not relate back.

 

ABCUSD refers to the original complaint to show that the gravamen of the complaint challenged its “anti-COVID” measures. The original complaint alleges that ABCUSD’s measures, including mandating masks and not permitting Plaintiff to remain on campus without a mask or vaccination, were negligent and violated the Bane Act and right to free speech. (See Complaint.) The TAC alleges that Plaintiff was deprived of her right to participate in the educational process as follows:

 

(1) targeting E.G. for discrimination for objecting to taking the COVID-19 vaccine on religious grounds; (2) allowing males to fully participate in the educational process without being required to get the COVID-19 vaccine, wearing a mask, not getting burns on their laps from trying to study, not being subjected to isolation; (3) allowing non-Christians to fully participate in the educational process without being required to get the COVID-19 vaccine, wearing a mask, not getting burns on their laps from trying to study, not being subjected to isolation; (4) physically assaulting E.G. for asserting her faith, while males and non-Christians were not harmed; and harming E.G.'s reputation by making false statements to third parties falsely accusing E.G. of committing the crime of recording private conversations.

 

(TAC, ¶ 63.) The harassment claim includes the foregoing, and adds:

 

(5) falsely accusing E.G. of committing the crime of recording private conversations; (6) reporting E.G. to the police; (7) physically assaulting E.G. by jerking her hand while involuntarily taking her phone away; (8) causing E.G. to suffer burns to her lap, (9) causing E.G. to have fainting spells when she complained about the school-required-mask was preventing her from breathing and causing her pain.

 

(TAC, ¶ 67.)

 

“An amended complaint relates back to an earlier complaint if it is based on the same general set of facts, even if the plaintiff alleges a different legal theory or new cause of action.” (Pointe San Diego Residential Community, L.P. v. Procopio, Cory, Hargreaves, & Savitch, LLP (2011) 195 Cal.App.4th 265, 277, citations omitted.) “In determining whether the amended complaint alleges facts that are sufficiently similar to those alleged in the original complaint, the critical inquiry is whether the defendant had adequate notice of the claim based on the original pleading.” (Ibid.)

 

Here, the Court agrees that there is no mention of religious-based or gender-based reasons for discriminating against Plaintiff in the complaint. The original complaint alleged “[v]accinated students were sent back to class, without delay. Comparatively, parents of unvaccinated students were contacted and ordered to retrieve their children in a manner that amounted to unequal treatment of those who chose not to enter themselves into the COVID medical trials across the board. This policy is discriminatory and repugnant on its face.” (Complaint, ¶ 24.) Additionally, “E.G. later told her parents she was scared because she thought she was somehow ‘in trouble’ for being unvaccinated…E.G. still felt that she was being punished for her organic status.” (Complaint, ¶ 29.)

 

In contrast, the TAC alleges “when male students and their parents objected to the COVID-19 vaccine or the males wearing the masks on religious grounds, they were allowed to come on campus and to go to their classroom for instruction/class without being forced to take the COVID-19 vaccine and/or wear a mask. This differential treatment caused E.G. to experience extreme confusion and anxiety. The differential treatment made it clear to E.G. that she was being targeted because of her faith and gender.” (TAC, ¶ 14.) Further, “[s]he felt helpless for being targeted because she is a female and a Christian, while other males and non-Christian were allowed to go to school without taking the COVID-19 vaccine or having to wear a mask, but not her.” (TAC, ¶ 30.)

 

The Court finds that the discrimination and harassment claims are new theories arising out of new, distinct facts. There is nothing in the complaint to allege or imply that Plaintiff was injured not only due to the vaccine policies themselves, but a disparate application of the policies based on Plaintiff’s religion or gender. As the TAC, and the SAC, in which these claims were first raised, were filed over three years from the subject time period in January through March of 2022, these claims are time-barred under the three-year statute of limitations.

 

The demurrer can therefore be sustained on this basis.

 

Government Claims Act Requirements

 

ABCUSD additionally argues that the facts underlying the third and fourth causes of action were not reflected in the claim.

 

The Court does not rule on this argument based on the denial of ABCUSD’s request for judicial notice.

 

          Sufficiency of Allegations: Discrimination and Harassment

 

ABCUSD additionally demurs to the third and fourth causes of action on the grounds that the TAC fails to state facts sufficient to constitute the causes of action.

 

Education Code section 220 provides:

 

No person shall be subjected to discrimination on the basis of disability, gender, gender identity, gender expression, nationality, race or ethnicity, religion, sexual orientation, or any other characteristic that is contained in the definition of hate crimes set forth in Section 422.55 of the Penal Code, including immigration status, in any program or activity conducted by an educational institution that receives, or benefits from, state financial assistance, or enrolls pupils who receive state student financial aid.

 

“An interpretation of section 220 requires consideration of various other provisions in the Education Code, including section 200, which sets forth the state's antidiscrimination policy; section 201, which contains legislative declarations in support of that policy; and sections 262.3 and 262.4, which provide for enforcement of the antidiscrimination law.” (Donovan v. Poway Unified School District (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 567, 590.) Pursuant to Education Code section 262.4, Education Code section 220 may be enforced through a civil action. The Legislature intended Chapter 2 of the Education Code to “be interpreted as consistent with Article 9.5 (commencing with Section 11135) of Chapter 1 of Part 1 of Division 3 of Title 2 of the Government Code, Title VI of the federal Civil Rights Act of 1964 (42 U.S.C. Sec. 1981, et seq.), Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972 (20 U.S.C. Sec. 1681, et seq.), Section 504 of the federal Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (29 U.S.C. Sec. 794(a)), the federal Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (20 U.S.C. Sec. 1400 et seq.), the federal Equal Educational Opportunities Act (20 U.S.C. Sec. 1701, et seq.), the Unruh Civil Rights Act (Secs. 51 to 53, incl., Civ. C.), and the Fair Employment and Housing Act (Pt. 2.8 (commencing with Sec. 12900), Div. 3, Gov. C.).” (Ed. Code, § 201, subd, (g).)

 

A cause of action for a claim arising out of Education Code section 220 requires a plaintiff to show: “(1) he or she suffered ‘severe, pervasive and offensive’ harassment, that effectively deprived plaintiff of the right of equal access to educational benefits and opportunities; (2) the school district had ‘actual knowledge’ of that harassment; and (3) the school district acted with ‘deliberate indifference’ in the face of such knowledge.” (Donovan, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at p. 579, 581 [“We conclude the Legislature intended Title IX's elements to govern an action under section 220.”].) Education Code section 220 protects an individual based on its stated characteristics. (Ed. Code, § 220.)

 

ABCUSD contends that the TAC’s allegations about religious and sex-based discrimination are conclusory. ABCUSD argues that the TAC contains allegations that Plaintiff believed she was being targeted on protected grounds rather than alleging that she was discriminated against on protected grounds. For example, the TAC alleges that when ABCUSD administrators approached her “she thought she was being targeted because of her Christian faith and for being a female.” (TAC, ¶ 16.) This was made “clear” to Plaintiff due to other male students being allowed on campus despite objecting to the vaccine or mask requirements on “religious grounds.” (TAC, ¶ 13.) Here, the Court agrees that any alleged discrimination or harassment against Plaintiff arises out of vaccination status and/or the objection to wearing a mask.

 

The allegations in the complaint allege that ABCUSD first initiated the anti-COVID policy against Plaintiff by calling her parents to pick her up on the basis of her vaccine status: “In January 2022, school officials required E.G. to get the COVID-19 vaccine to go to class. Even though she did not have any symptoms of having COVID-19, school officials insisted that E.G. get the COVID-19 vaccine or she would not be allowed to go back to class. E.G. and her parents objected to E.G. getting the COVID-19 vaccine on religious grounds/their Christian faith.” (TAC, ¶ 12.) Any action taken by the school against Plaintiff is alleged to be in furtherance of anti-COVID policies. The TAC alleges that ABCUSD ignored Plaintiff’s religious objection rather than discriminated on the basis of it: “E.G. and her father again objected on religious grounds. And again, Ortiz and the Principal ignored their religious objection.” (TAC, ¶ 32.) Further, the TAC alleges that on February 21, 2022, Plaintiff initially reported to her teacher “that the mask was making her dizzy, see black dots, she was unable to breath with it over her nose and mouth, and she that it was making her faint,” after which the teacher called the principal. (TAC, ¶ 35.) Once the principal arrived, “Principal told E.G. she must leave the classroom if she was not going to put on a mask. E.G. stated she was not going to put a mask because she could not breath and on religious grounds.” (TAC, ¶ 36.) The principal then provided certain options to accommodate Plaintiff, including sitting outside, which Plaintiff objected to due to the weather, or then going to the “isolation room.” (TAC, ¶ 36.) While a religious objection is mentioned, neither the teacher (who is not alleged to initially be aware of a religious objection) nor the principal are alleged to enforce the anti-COVID policy based on Plaintiff’s religion. Thus, the Court agrees that the TAC does not sufficiently allege that ABCUSD prevented Plaintiff from attending school based on her religion or gender, outside of her vaccination status. Moreover, as argued by ABCUSD, these allegations do not rise to severe or pervasive levels.

 

Further, ABCUSD contends that the allegations about discrimination due to vaccination status are not covered under Education Code section 220. The Court agrees. Vaccination status is not covered under the statute. (Ed. Code, § 220.)

 

Additionally, ABCUSD argues that the TAC’s reference to Government Code section 11135 does not warrant a damages remedy for Plaintiff. The Court agrees. Government Code section 11139 provides that Section 11135 “may be enforced by a civil action for equitable relief, which shall be independent of any other rights and remedies.” The Donovon court noted that the Legislature “expressly provide[d] for a private right of action, but expressly limited enforcement to a ‘civil action for equitable relief.’” (Donovan, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at p. 594.) As such, Government Code section 11135 does not support a basis for the relief Plaintiff seeks here.

 

As to the harassment claim, ABCUSD also raises that Plaintiff cites to statutory provisions that do not exist, namely, Education Code section 220, subdivisions (a), (b), (c). The Court agrees that these do not exist but does not find the claim fatally uncertain on this basis.

 

Accordingly, ABCUSD’s demurrer to the third and fourth causes of action are SUSTAINED without leave to amend based as they are barred under the applicable statute of limitations and because the TAC fails to allege facts sufficient to constitute the causes of action.

 

Motion to Strike

 

ABCUSD moves to strike the following portions of the TAC: (1) the prayer for punitive damages (TAC, Prayer, ¶ 4); (2) the prayer for civil penalties pursuant to Civil Code sections 51 and 52 (TAC, Prayer, ¶ 5); and (3) he prayer for attorney’s fees (TAC, Prayer, ¶ 6).

 

First, ABCUSD argues that punitive damages claims against a public entity are improper based on ABCUSD’s immunity. “[A] public entity is not liable for damages awarded under Section 3294 of the Civil Code or other damages imposed primarily for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant.” (Gov. Code, § 818.) As the TAC seeks punitive damages pursuant to Civil Code section 3294, it is improper.

 

Next, ABCUSD moves to strike the prayer for civil penalties pursuant to Civil Code sections 51 and 52 on the grounds that Plaintiff does not seek damages under the Unruh Civil Rights Act. The Court agrees. On the same grounds, ABCUSD moves to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for attorney’s fees under Civil Code sections 51 and 52 and “all applicable provisions of law.” (TAC, Prayer, ¶ 6.) As argued by ABCUSD, none of Plaintiff’s claims carry an award of attorney’s fees.

 

Accordingly, ABCUSD’s motion to strike is GRANTED.


Case Number: 23NWCV03581    Hearing Date: September 8, 2026    Dept: P

MOORE v. L’ABRI MANAGEMENT, INC., ET AL.

CASE NO.: 23NWCV03581

HEARING:  09/08/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#9

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendants L’Abri Management, Inc., Ronald A, Kolar, and Edgl Has’s motion for terminating sanctions is GRANTED. This action is dismissed with prejudice.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendants L’Abri Management, Inc., Ronald A, Kolar, and Edgl Has (Defendants) move for an order issuing terminating sanctions in its favor and against Plaintiff Sedric Moore (Plaintiff).

 

Background

 

On November 6, 2023, Plaintiff filed this action against Defendants L’Abri Management, Inc. (L’Abri Management); Ronald A, Kolar, individually, and as CEO of Defendant; Edgl Has, Vivianna Escamilla, and Does 1 to 10. This action arises out of alleged instances of harassment, discrimination, and hostile living conditions faced by Plaintiff during Plaintiff’s tenancy at 2525 Carson Street, Unit No. 6, Lakewood, CA 90712, County of Los Angeles (Subject Property). (Complaint, ¶¶ 23-34.) The complaint asserts the following causes of action: (1) Breach of Warranty of Quiet Enjoyment, (2) Negligence, (3) Constructive Eviction, (4) Harassment, and (5) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress.

 

On February 18, 2025, the Court granted L’Abri Management’s motions to compel responses to form interrogatories, set one, special interrogatories, set one, and requests for production of documents, set one (Order, 2/18/25).

 

On October 9, 2025, the Court granted L’Abri Management’s motion to compel further responses to form interrogatories, set one, special interrogatories, set one, and requests for production of documents, set one (10/9/2025 Order).

 

This is a continuation. On February 26, 2026, this motion first came on for hearing. At the hearing, “[t]he Court confirm[ed] that Plaintiff’s counsel, Gary Saunders, has been ineligible to practice law since 11/30/2025.” (Order, 2/26/26.) Plaintiff appeared to request a continuance to obtain new counsel, and the Court continued the hearing to May 28, 2026. (Order, 2/26/26.)

 

On May 28, 2026, this motion came on for hearing again. The Court continued this motion to August 13, 2026 at defense counsel’s request based on an issue with notice. (Order, 5/28/26.)

 

On August 13, 2026, this motion was called for hearing again. Plaintiff appeared and represented that he found an attorney to represent him and orally moved for a continuance so that he may oppose this motion. The Court granted Plaintiff’s oral motion for a continuance and continued the hearing to today’s date.

 

As of this date, there are no new filings by Plaintiff.

 

Legal Standard

 

If a party fails to comply with a court order compelling discovery responses or attendance at a deposition, the court may impose monetary, issue, evidence, or terminating sanctions. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2023.010, subd. (g); 2030.290, subd. (c); 2023.030; R.S. Creative, Inc. v. Creative Cotton, Ltd. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 486, 495.) Ultimate discovery sanctions are justified where there is a willful discovery order violation, a history of abuse, and evidence showing that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with discovery rules. (Van Sickle v. Gilbert (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1495, 1516.) “[A] penalty as severe as dismissal or default is not authorized where noncompliance with discovery is caused by an inability to comply rather than willfulness or bad faith.” (Brown v. Superior Ct. (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 701, 707.)

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030, subdivision (d), “[t]he court may impose a terminating sanction by one of the following orders:

 

(1) An order striking out the pleadings or parts of the pleadings of any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process.

(2) An order staying further proceedings by that party until an order for discovery is obeyed.

(3) An order dismissing the action, or any part of the action, of that party.

(4) An order rendering a judgment by default against that party.”

 

“The trial court may order a terminating sanction for discovery abuse ‘after considering the totality of the circumstances: [the] conduct of the party to determine if the actions were willful; the detriment to the propounding party; and the number of formal and informal attempts to obtain the discovery.’” (Los Defensores, Inc. v. Gomez (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 377, 390, quoting Lang v. Hochman (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1225, 1246.)

 

Discussion

 

Defendants move for terminating sanctions against Plaintiff. Specifically, Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s failure to comply with the 10/9/2025 Order and other violations of the Civil Discovery Act warrant an order of dismissal with prejudice in favor of Defendants.

 

This motion is unopposed. The Court notes that this motion has been continued twice. On February 26, 2026, when this motion first came on for hearing, Plaintiff appeared and confirmed that Plaintiff’s counsel, Gary Saunders, has been ineligible to practice law since November 30, 2025. (Order, 2/26/26.) The Court continued the motion to permit Plaintiff to retain new counsel. (Order, 2/26/26.) On May 28, 2026, when this motion came on for a second time, the Court continued the hearing again based on defense counsel’s representation that timely notice of the prior continuance was only provided to Plaintiff’s counsel and not plaintiff. (Order, 5/28/26.) The notice included a representation that Plaintiff was seeking new counsel and was to comply with all outstanding orders and discovery at least 30 days prior to the hearing. (Order, 5/28/26.) The Court then continued the hearing to today’s date and instructed the parties “to apprise the Court of any relevant updates via declaration or briefing prior to the hearing.” (Order, 5/28/26.)

 

There have been no new filings except a Notice of Non-Opposition filed by Defendants indicating that they have not received any opposition from Plaintiff as of August 4, 2026. The Court proceeds to rule on this motion.

 

Defendants recount the following: on February 8, 2024, Defendants served Plaintiff with initial written discovery. (Hoffman Decl., ¶ 3.) On February 18, 2025, the Court granted Defendant’s motion to compel discovery responses to the subject discovery requests. (Order, 2/18/25.) On April 7, 2025, Plaintiff served untimely responses with objections to the subject requests, form interrogatories, set one, special interrogatories, set one, and requests for production, set one. (Hoffman Decl., ¶ 7.) The majority of the responses said “[d]iscovery is continuing” and the objections were in violation of the 2/28/25 Order. (Hoffman Decl., ¶ 7.) Defendants moved for further responses. (Hoffman Decl., ¶ 11.) Defendants did not receive any responses to their meet and confer efforts and Plaintiff only opposed the motions the day before, blaming the failure to respond earlier on lack of communication and administrative staff turnover. (Hoffman Decl., ¶¶ 7-11.) This is the same justification Plaintiff provided for their initial motions to compel. (Hoffman Decl., ¶ 11.)

 

During the October 9, 2025 hearing on the motions to compel further responses, “Wayne Quint appeared for counsel for record on behalf of Plaintiff. Mr. Quint informed the Court that counsel of record, Gary Saunders, was determined to be no longer eligible to practice law by the California State Bar. Mr. Quint stated that he would be working on the firm’s cases.” (Hoffman Decl., ¶ 12.) The Court granted L’Abri Management’s motions to compel further responses and ordered Plaintiff and his counsel to provide further responses pay sanctions in the amount of $2,620.00 within 30 days of the 10/9/25 Order. (Hoffman Decl., ¶ 13.)

 

After Mr. Quint introduced himself to defense counsel via email, Mr. Quint did not respond to meet and confer efforts. (Hoffman Decl., ¶¶ 14-16.) Defendants emailed Mr. Quint, Mr. Saunders, firm staff, and their firm’s general litigation email address on December 8, 2025, stating that the further responses and monetary sanctions are overdue and providing an extension. (Hoffman Decl., ¶ 16.) Defense counsel “received a message from Microsoft Outlook stating that the email could not be sent to Mr. Quint’s email address, as it was inactive, or to Ms. Barreto’s email address, as it did not exist.” (Hoffman Decl., ¶ 17.) As of the date of this motion, Defendants have not received any communications from Plaintiff.

 

The Court finds that Defendants show Plaintiff engaged in repeated, willful violations of Court orders and the discovery process. The subject discovery has been pending for nearly two years, since April 30, 2024. Plaintiff responded with objections in violation of the 2/18/25 Order and failed to provide further responses in violation of the 10/9/25 Order. Willfulness is apparent through Plaintiff’s counsel’s failure to meet and confer and failure to comply with Court Orders, despite representing to the Court and opposing counsel that Plaintiff will comply. Despite claiming that Plaintiff’s firm experienced staffing issues in the late oppositions to prior discovery motions, Plaintiff failed to request any extensions from Defendants nor appraise the Court or Defendants of any difficulties.

 

Outside of Plaintiff’s counsel, Plaintiff has again failed to oppose this motion. It has been over six months since Plaintiff appeared in this Court, represented that he sought to retain new counsel, and was instructed to comply with the pending discovery orders. Plaintiff has since appeared before this Court and requested another continuance on the grounds that he has found counsel to represent him. Despite these assertions, Plaintiff has not supplied a written opposition or any filing regarding new representation. Moreover, the issuance of monetary sanctions has not influenced Plaintiff’s participation in discovery, as Plaintiff has also not paid any monetary sanctions.

 

“If a plaintiff wishes to avail himself of the California courts, he owes a duty to comply with its rules, such as the rules of discovery. To hold otherwise, would permit a litigant to disappear and not be obliged to comply with our discovery statutes.” (Cornwall v. Santa Monica Dairy Co. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 250, 253.)

 

Defendants’ motion for terminating sanctions is GRANTED. This action is dismissed with prejudice.

 

 


Case Number: 25NWCV00315    Hearing Date: September 8, 2026    Dept: P

BARRERA v. JAGUAR LAND ROVER NORTH AMERICA, LLC

CASE NO.: 25NWCV00315 

HEARING: 09/08/2026 @ 10:30 AM

 

#11

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Plaintiff Oliver R. Barrera’s motion to compel further responses to request for production, set two, from Defendant Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC is MOOT.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Plaintiff Oliver R. Barrera (Plaintiff) moves to compel further responses from Defendant Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC (Defendant) to requests for production of documents (RPDs), set two.

 

Background

 

On January 27, 2025, Plaintiff filed this lemon law action against Defendant and Does 1 through 10. The complaint alleges that Plaintiff purchased a certified pre-owned 2019 Land Rover Range Rover Sport which was delivered to Plaintiff with defects and nonconformities to warranty. (Complaint, ¶¶ 8-10.) The complaint asserts three causes of action: (1) violation of Song-Beverly Act – breach of express warranty; (2) violation of Song-Beverly Act – breach of implied warranty; (3) violation of Song Beverly Act, section 1793.2.

 

Legal Standard

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.260, subdivision (a), a party must respond to requests for production of documents within 30 days of service. Failure to do so waives all objections including privilege or on the protection of work product. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).) Parties may move for a further response to requests for production of documents where a statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete, a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive, or where an objection is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).) 

 

Meet and Confer

 

A motion to compel further discovery shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(2).) A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.)

 

Plaintiff’s counsel states that it requested defense counsel’s availability for a telephonic meet and confer meeting but defense counsel did not respond. (Enav Decl., ¶¶ 14-16.)

 

In opposition, Defendant contends that this was insufficient because “[a]t no point during this timeframe did Plaintiff's counsel provide a substantive letter outlining the legal or factual basis for challenging Defendant's objections. At no point did Plaintiff's counsel propose narrowing the scope of the overbroad nationwide requests. At no point did Plaintiff's counsel pick up the telephone to initiate a dialogue.” (Opp., 4:24-27.)

 

While the Court agrees that meeting and conferring requires more than sending an email, here, the facts show that Defendant did not respond to Plaintiff’s email requesting his availability. Neither party participated in the meet and confer process in good faith.

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiff moves to compel Defendant to provide further responses to RPDs, set two, nos. 1 and 3.

 

In opposition, Defendant argues that this motion is moot because it served supplemental responses to RPDs, set two, nos. 1 and 3. Defendant served the responses on August 25, 2026. (Calabia Decl., ¶ 7.)

 

In reply, Plaintiff contends that the motion is not moot because it does not provide responsive documents.

 

Based on the supplemental production to all the RPDs at issue, the arguments raised in the instant motion are moot.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses to RPDs, set two, is MOOT.

 

 


Case Number: 26NWCV01086    Hearing Date: September 8, 2026    Dept: P

RAMIREZ, ET AL. v. AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR CO., INC.

CASE NO.: 26NWCV01086

HEARING:  09/08/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#10

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.’s petition to compel arbitration is GRANTED. This action is STAYED pending the outcome of arbitration.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (Defendant) petitions to compel Plaintiffs Carlos A. Lucas Ramirez and Sixto C. Garcia Gomez (Plaintiffs) to submit their claims to binding arbitration.

 

Background

 

On March 25, 2026, Plaintiffs filed this lemon law action against Defendant and Does 1 through 10. The complaint alleges that Plaintiffs experienced defects and nonconformities to warranty with their leased 2026 Honda CR-V. (Complaint, ¶¶ 8-10.) The complaint asserts three causes of action: (1) violation of Song-Beverly Act – breach of express warranty; (2) violation of Song-Beverly Act – breach of implied warranty; (3) violation of Song Beverly Act, section 1793.2.

 

Legal Standard

 

Under both the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA) and California law, arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a contract. (Winter v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943, 947.) The party moving to compel arbitration must establish the existence of a written arbitration agreement between the parties. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.) This is usually done by presenting a copy of the signed, written agreement to the court. “A petition to compel arbitration or to stay proceedings pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 1281.2 and 1281.4 must state…the provisions of the written agreement and the paragraph that provides for arbitration. The provisions must be stated verbatim or a copy must be physically or electronically attached to the petition and incorporated by reference.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330.) The moving party must also establish the other party’s refusal to arbitrate the controversy. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.) “Once such a document is presented to the court, the burden shifts to the party opposing the motion to compel, who may present any challenges to the enforcement of the agreement and evidence in support of those challenges.” (Baker v. Italian Maple Holdings, LLC (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 1152, 1160.)

 

In ruling on a motion to compel arbitration, the court must first determine whether the parties actually agreed to arbitrate the dispute, and general principles of California contract law help guide the court in making this determination. (Mendez v. Mid-Wilshire Health Care Center (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 534, 541.) A petition to compel arbitration is a suit in equity to compel specific performance of a contract. (Frog Creek Partners, LLC. v. Vance Brown, Inc. (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 515, 532.)

 

Under the FAA, general contract defenses, such as fraud, duress, or unconscionability, may provide grounds for invalidating an arbitration agreement if they are enforced evenhandedly and do not interfere with fundamental attributes of arbitration. (9 U.S.C.A. § 1 et seq.) To invalidate an arbitration provision, a plaintiff must show that it is both procedurally and substantively unconscionable. (Marin Storage & Trucking, Inc. v. Benco Contracting & Eng'g, Inc. (2001) 89 Cal. App. 4th 1042, 1053; Chemours Co. v. DowDuPont Inc. (2020) No. CV 2019-0351- SG, 2020 WL 1527783, at *12.) An agreement to arbitrate is unenforceable only if both the procedural and substantive elements are satisfied. (Stirlen v. Supercuts, Inc. (1997) 51 Cal.App.4th 1519, 1533.) However, Armendariz held, “[T]he more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.” (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychare Services, Inc. 24 Cal.4th 83, 114).” “A court should consider substantive unconscionability only after procedural unconscionability has been established.” (Ramirez v. Charter Communications, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 478, 494.)

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

Plaintiffs make two evidentiary objections to the declaration of Charles G. Bakaly. Plaintiffs’ evidentiary objections are OVERRULED. The Court further explains its ruling on the evidentiary objections below.

 

Discussion

 

Defendant petitions to compel Plaintiffs to submit their claims to binding arbitration.

 

Defendant first notes that it made a demand for arbitration to the AAA on April 23, 2026 and that the arbitration is pending. (Bakaly Decl., ¶¶ 5-8.) Defendant shows that the arbitrator continued the AAA Preliminary Hearing pending the outcome of this Petition. (Bakaly Decl., ¶¶ 11-12.)

 

Defendant moves to compel arbitration pursuant to an arbitration agreement (Arbitration Agreement) contained in its standard limited warranty (Warranty Contract). The limited warranty contains the following Arbitration Agreement:

 

Agreement to Arbitrate

Please carefully read this provision, which applies to any dispute between you and American Honda Motor Co., Inc. and its parent entities, subsidiaries, affiliates, agents, authorized service and repair facilities, employees, predecessors in interest, successors, and assigns (together “Honda,” “we,” or “us”). You and we agree that any dispute arising out of or relating to any aspect of the relationship between you and Honda will not be decided by a judge or jury but instead by a single arbitration administered by the American Arbitration Association (AAA) under its Consumer Arbitration Rules in effect on the date of delivery of your vehicle to you. This Agreement to Arbitrate includes all claims, whether based in contract, tort, statute, fraud, misrepresentation, or any other legal theory; claims arising out of your warranty; claims arising before or after this Agreement, such as claims related to statements about our products; claims about the performance, design of our products, or manufacturing of our products; and claims that are currently the subject of purported class action litigation in which you are not a member of a certified class.

 

[…]

You may opt out of arbitration within 30 days after the date of delivery of your vehicle to you by sending a letter to: Honda’s Office of the General Counsel, 1919 Torrance Blvd., Mail Stop CHI-5, Torrance, CA 90501, stating your name, Vehicle Identification Number, and intent to opt out of the arbitration provision. If you do not opt out, then this agreement to arbitrate is binding.

 

(Bakaly Decl., Exh. A.) Defendant shows that when Plaintiffs leased the Subject Vehicle from Ocean Honda of Whittier, they signed a “LEASE AGREEMENT (WITH ARBITRATION PROVISION)” (Lease Agreement). (Bakaly Decl., Exh. B.) The Lease Agreement provides “16. Warranties. The Vehicle is subject to the manufacturer's standard warranty, unless this box is checked.” (Bakaly Decl., Exh. B.) The box was not checked. (Bakaly Decl., Exh. B.) Defendant contends that Plaintiffs’ expressly and impliedly agreed to binding arbitration and otherwise are equitably estopped from denying the validity of this arbitration agreement. Defendant shows that Plaintiffs are relying on the Warranty Contract in the complaint. The complaint alleges “[t]hese causes of action arise out of warranty and repair obligations of [Defendant] in connection with a vehicle Plaintiffs leased and for which [Defendant] issued an express warranty. The warranty was not issued by the leasing dealership.” (Complaint, ¶ 4.) Additionally, “[i]n connection with the sale, Plaintiff received various warranties.” (Complaint, ¶ 9.)

 

Plaintiffs argue that Defendant’s reliance on the Warranty Contract is procedurally defective because it is not authenticated. Plaintiffs argue that the Warranty Contract is not properly authenticated because it was produced by Defendant’s counsel. Here, Plaintiff’s evidentiary objection based on Defendant’s counsel is overruled because the Court’s first step is only to determine “that a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy exists.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.) “A plain reading of [§ 1281.2] indicates that as a preliminary matter the court is only required to make a finding of the agreement's existence, not an evidentiary determination of its validity.” (Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 219.)

 

Plaintiffs additionally raise Gamboa v. Northeast Comm. Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165-66 to argue the burden shifts back to Defendant to prove existence of an arbitration agreement with admissible evidence. Plaintiffs provide a declaration stating that “[t]o the best of my memory, I was never personally provided with a copy of any warranty manual when I leased the Subject Vehicle. Additionally, I was never given notice by either Ocean Honda of Whittier or Defendant that a subsequent enforceable Arbitration Provision existed inside the Warranty manual. Nor was I ever given notice by either Ocean Honda of Whittier or Defendant that I was required to opt out of this post-lease. (Ramirez Decl., ¶ 5; Gomez Decl., ¶ 5.) Plaintiff attests that he never signed it because it does not require consent or provide a signature line. (Ramirez Decl., ¶ 6.)

 

Plaintiffs further argue that there is no mutual assent. Plaintiffs refer to their declarations in which they state Ocean Honda of Whittier never showed them a copy of the Warranty Contract or informed them that their claims would be subject to a binding arbitration agreement. (Ramirez Decl., ¶ 4; Gomez Decl., ¶ 4.) Plaintiffs argue that the complaint’s general allegation of warranty coverage does not admit that they received this particular booklet, saw its arbitration provision, or agreed to be bound, because receipt of warranty protection is not assent to a separate agreement to arbitrate. (Knutson v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc. (9th Cir. 2014) 771 F.3d 559, 565; Norcia v. Samsung Telecommunications America, LLC (9th Cir. 2017) 845 F.3d 1279, 1284-1286.)

 

In reply, Defendant refers to Kostandian v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 872, 884, in which a plaintiff similarly argued that there was no mutual assent because he did not receive a warranty contract until after the sale and a Court of Appeal noted that “no evidence was presented supporting this assertion.” Likewise, here, Plaintiffs do not present evidence disputing the existence of this Warranty Contract, only that Ocean Honda of Whittier never showed them a copy during pre-lease negotiations. The Kostandian court also rejected arguments relying on Knutson and Norcia. Besides being nonbinding on this Court, Norcia is inapposite as it does not involve a warranty dispute and Knutson is distinguishable because the plaintiff produced evidence that it did not receive the respective arbitration agreement until a month after the service began. (See Kostandian, supra, 120 Cal.App.5th at p. 884.) Hernandez v. LG Electronics U.S.A., Inc. (C.D. Cal., Mar. 28, 2024, No. 222CV08813SVWJEM) 2024 WL 1601260, while involving a warranty claim, is non-binding, as it is an unpublished federal opinion. (See Gong v. City of Rosemead (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 363, 375 [“While unpublished federal district court opinions are citable, they do not constitute binding authority.”].)

 

Lastly, the Court agrees that Plaintiffs’ reliance on Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2025) 17 Cal.5th 1122, 1126 does not defeat Defendant’s equitable estoppel argument. Ford Motor Warranty Cases concerned nonsignatory manufacturers seeking to enforce arbitration provisions in separate dealer sales contracts, whereas Defendant seeks to enforce the Arbitration Agreement in its own Warranty Contract. (Id., at p. 1133.) The doctrine of equitable estoppel applies if (1) the plaintiff relies upon the contract’s terms in asserting claims against the non-signatory defendant, or those claims are “intimately founded in or intertwined with” the contract itself, or (2) if the plaintiff alleges “substantially interdependent and concerted misconduct” by the defendant, where such allegations of misconduct are “founded in or intimately connected with” the obligations of the contract. (Goldman v. KPMG LLP (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 209, 221; Metalclad Corp. v. Ventana Environmental Organizational Partnership (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1705, 1713-1714 (citations omitted) [the doctrine of equitable estoppel is meant to “prevent a party from playing fast and loose with its commitment to arbitrate, honoring it when advantageous and circumventing it to gain undue advantage”].) 

 

Here, Defendant establishes the existence of the Arbitration Agreement within the Warranty Contract by attaching a copy and reciting its terms. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.) Plaintiffs do not provide evidence disputing the existence of this Arbitration Agreement. Moreover, Plaintiffs are estopped from avoiding arbitration where their complaint relies on the existence of the same Warranty Contract.  Plaintiffs’ complaint cites to a New Vehicle Limited Warranty, a powertrain warranty, and a battery warranty, all provided for in the Warranty Contract, in pages subsequent to the pages in which the Arbitration Agreement is contained. (Complaint, ¶ 9; Bakaly Decl., Exh. A.)

 

Accordingly, the Court finds that Defendant establishes the existence of a valid, enforceable arbitration agreement that covers Plaintiffs’ claims and is governed by the FAA.

 

Petition

 

Defendant additionally refers the Court to its Petition to Compel Arbitration and Stay Action that it filed on April 27, 2026.

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1290: “Any person named as a respondent in a petition may file a response thereto. The allegations of a petition are deemed to be admitted by a respondent duly served therewith unless a response is duly served and filed.” A response is due within 10 days after service of the Petition. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1290.6.) Plaintiffs did not file any responses.

 

The Petition asserts that Plaintiffs entered into the Warranty Contract and that the Warranty Contract is governed by the FAA as Defendant is engaged in interstate commerce. (Pet., ¶¶ 1-3.) Plaintiffs are bound to the factual allegations of the Petition based on their failure to timely respond. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1290.) Thus, the admitted allegations in the Petition constitute another basis as to which the Court finds an enforceable arbitration agreement.

 

Unconscionability

 

Plaintiffs next argue that the arbitration provision in the Warranty Contract is unconscionable.

 

Procedural Unconscionability

 

“Procedural unconscionability concerns the manner in which the contract was negotiated and the circumstances of the parties at that time. It focuses on factors of oppression and surprise. The oppression component arises from an inequality of bargaining power of the parties to the contract and an absence of real negotiation or a meaningful choice on the part of the weaker party.” (Pinnacle Museum Tower Assn. v. Pinnacle Market Dev. (US), LLC (2012) 55 Cal.4th 223, 247.)

 

Plaintiffs argue that the Arbitration Agreement is a contract of adhesion because it was not disclosed to Plaintiffs and the opt-out option is buried in the Warranty Contract. (See Dougherty v. Roseville Heritage Partners (2020) 47 Cal.App.5th 93, 103.)

 

Defendant counters by arguing that the opt-out provision shows that this agreement is not a contract of adhesion. Defendant points to Plaintiffs’ citation to Dougherty to emphasize its argument—the arbitration provision in Dougherty was buried 70 pages into a document with no-opt out provision and no notice. Here, the Arbitration Agreement was on page 6 with an opt-out provision. (Bakaly Decl., Exh. A.)

 

The Court finds that there is a low degree of procedural unconscionability where the arbitration provision was located on page 6 of the Warranty Contract and contained an opt-put provision. Adhesion contracts are subject to scrutiny because they are ‘not the result of freedom or equality of bargaining.’ However, they remain valid and enforceable unless the resisting party can also show that one or more of the contract's terms is substantively unconscionable or otherwise invalid.” (Ramirez v. Charter Communications, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 478, 492-93.) The Court therefore will assess whether there is a high degree of substantive unconscionability. (Id. at p. 494.)

 

Substantive Unconscionability

 

“Substantive unconscionability pertains to the fairness of an agreement’s actual terms and to assessments of whether they are overly harsh or one-sided. A contract term is not substantively unconscionable when it merely gives one side a greater benefit; rather, the term must be so one-sided as to shock the conscience.” (Carmona v. Lincoln Millennium Car Wash, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 74, 85.) “[T]he paramount consideration in assessing [substantive] unconscionability is mutuality.” (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiffs argue that the arbitration agreement is substantively unconscionable because Plaintiffs will receive limited due process and discovery. Plaintiffs do not point to any terms that are unconscionable or any authority stating that arbitration is not permitted under the Song-Beverly Act. The Court does not find an arbitration provision unconscionable by virtue of compelling an arbitration. A waiver of jury rights and limited discovery are facets of arbitration and do not support a finding of substantive unconscionability within this arbitration provision.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED.

 

Motion to Stay Proceedings

 

California Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.4 states that the court shall stay the action or proceeding if the court has ordered arbitration. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1281.4.) Accordingly, the Court will STAY proceedings pending arbitration.
