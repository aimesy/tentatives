DEPARTMENT 529 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 23STCV15597    Hearing Date: July 9, 2026    Dept: 529

S.K., A MINOR, BY AND THROUGH HER GUARDIAN AD LITEM, T.K., V. CITY OF LOS ANGELES, ET AL.

 

MOTION FOR LEAVE TO AMEND THE CITY’S ANSWER TO PLAINTIFF’S COMPLAINT AND BIFURCATE TRIAL AND

 

Date of Hearing:        July 9, 2026                                        Trial Date:       January 25, 2027

Department:              529                                                      Case No.:        23STCV15597

 

BACKGROUND

 

On July 5, 2023, Plaintiff S.K., a minor, by and through her Guardian ad Litem, T.K., an individual filed a complaint against Defendants City of Los Angeles, City of La Mirada, and Doe 1 for (1) Negligence, (2) Negligent Hiring, Supervision, Training and/or Retention, (3) Common Carrier Liability – Civil Code §2100 et seq., (4) Dangerous Condition of Public Property, (5) Negligence, (6) Negligent Hiring, Supervision, Training and/or Retention, (7) Common Carrier Liability – Civil Code §2100 et seq., (8) Dangerous Condition of Public Property, (9) Sexual Battery, (10) Battery, (11) Assault, (12) False Imprisonment, and (13) Negligence.

 

City of Los Angeles filed a Cross-Complaint against City of La Mirada on September 29, 2023 for (1) Indemnification, (2) Apportionment of Fault, and (3) Declaratory Relief. City of La Mirada filed a Cross-Complaint against City of Los Angeles for (1) Indemnification and (2) Apportionment of Fault.

 

On August 5, 2025, Plaintiff dismissed the third and fourth causes of action against Defendant City of Los Angeles.  

 

[TENTATIVE] RULING

 

I.                    Defendant City of La Mirada’s Motion for Leave to Amend the City’s Answer to Plaintiff’s Complaint and Bifurcate Trial is GRANTED IN PART.

II.                  Defendant City of La Mirada’s Motion to Compel Cross-Defendant City of Los Aneles’ Further Responses to Special Interrogatories, Set One is GRANTED.

 

DISCUSSION

 

I.                    DEFENDANT CITY OF LA MIRADA’S MOTION FOR LEAVE TO AMEND THE CITY’S ANSWER TO PLAINTIFF’S COMPLAINT AND BIFURCATE TRIAL

 

Defendant City of La Mirada moves this court for an order granting leave to file an amended answer to Plaintiff S.K., a minor, by and through her Guardian ad Litem, complaint to include a design immunity affirmative defense. The City of La Mirada also moves to bifurcate trial on the affirmative defense of design immunity.

 

Objections

 

In opposition, Plaintiff submits objections to the declaration of Michelle R. Prescott. The court OVERRULES objections 1 – 5.

 

Discussion

 

“A court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading.” (CCP¿§473(a)(1).) The court’s discretion will usually be exercised liberally to¿permit¿amendments of the pleadings. (Nestle v. City of Santa Monica¿(1972) 6 Cal.3d 920, 939.)¿A complainant may obtain leave from the trial court to amend their pleading beyond the number of amendments allowed under Code of Civil Procedure section 472 (a) by filing a noticed motion. (CRC, Rule 3.1324.)¿¿ 

¿ 

The motion must: (1) include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments; (2) state what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are¿located; and (3) state what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located. (CRC, Rule 3.1324(a).)¿The motion must also be accompanied by a declaration stating: (1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) why the request was not made earlier.¿(CRC, Rule 3.1324 (b).)¿¿ 

 

Code of Civil Procedure § 598 provides in pertinent part that, “[t]he court may, when the convenience of witnesses, the ends of justice, or the economy and efficiency of handling the litigation would be promoted thereby, on motion of a party, after notice and hearing, make an order . . . that the trial of any issue or any part thereof shall precede the trial of any other issue or any part thereof.” Similarly, CCP § 1048(b) provides in pertinent part that, “[t]he court, in furtherance of convenience or to avoid prejudice, or when separate trials will be conducive to expedition and economy, may order a separate trial of any cause of action, including a cause of action asserted in a cross-complaint, or of any separate issue or of any number of causes of action or issues.”¿¿ 

 

The court’s discretionary authority to sever claims and try them separately may also be employed to avoid undue prejudice to a party. (Stencel Aero Engineering Corp., v. Superior Court (1976) 56 Cal.App.3d 988.) Courts have inherent power to regulate the order of trial, and therefore can entertain a motion to bifurcate at any time—even during the trial itself. (McLellan v. McLellan (1972) 23 Cal.App.3d 343, 353.)¿¿ 

 

City of La Mirada argues when Defendant  filed its answer to the complaint on September 20, 2023, the City of La Mirada inadvertently failed to explicitly assert the affirmative defense of Design Immunity in its answer. Upon substitution of counsel, Defendant sought a stipulation from Plaintiff’s counsel and counsel for Defendant the City of Los Angeles for the filing of an Amended Answer. Plaintiff refused to stipulate to the amendment or bifurcation and City of Los Angeles only agreed to stipulate to the amended answer. City of La Mirada contends leave is appropriate as the motion is timely, Plaintiffs will not be prejudiced by the proposed amendment, and design immunity is a viable affirmative defense. City of La Mirada also seeks bifurcation of the affirmative defense as applicability of design immunity is an issue of law to be decided by the court, rather than by the jury or judge sitting as a trier of fact.

 

The City of Los Angeles filed a notice of non-opposition to the motion. Plaintiffs, in opposition, argue Defendants have failed to state when it was discovered the facts necessitating amendment or articulate a credible reason for its unreasonable delay in bringing this motion as well as Plaintiffs will suffer prejudice if amendment is allowed. Plaintiffs maintain courts are much more critical and less inclined to grant leave when a defendant seeks to amend its answer after an unexplained delay, where there is a lack of diligence, or prejudice to the other party. (Permalab-Metalab Equipment Corp., v. Maryland Cas. Co. (1975) 25 Cal.App.3d 465, 472.)Plaintiffs further argue even if amendment is allowed, bifurcation should be denied because the motion is premature, design immunity defense will not dispose of the entire case, it will not promote the ends of justice, and there is no prejudice to Defendant in having the matter tried as a whole.

 

First, the court finds the motion sufficiently complies with Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324. Defendant provides counsel discovered the need to amend when they were going over the answer to prepare for trial. (Prescott Decl. ¶¶11, 12.) The declaration also provides the amendment is necessary and proper because “the proposed amendments are necessary and proper because the evidence discovered in this case demonstrates that the City of La Mirada was responsible for the plan, design and/or construction of the waterpark known as SPLASH! In addition, the plan and/or design was approved in advance by the legislative body of the City of La Mirada.” (Prescott Decl. ¶10.) The court also finds Plaintiffs will not be prejudiced by the amendment. Trial is not set until January 25, 2027. Plaintiff only speculates “protracted discovery” will be needed. Even if the amendment required further discovery, it would not deny Plaintiffs a full opportunity to present evidence at trial. Moreover, while it is true that courts are critical of proposed amendments to answers where there has been a long unexplained delay or a lack of diligence, as noted above, the court finds Defendant has sufficiently provided for the delay.

 

Regarding bifurcation, the court agrees bifurcation at this time is premature and would not dispose of the entire case. As noted by Plaintiffs, design immunity bars only design caused claims. (See Mozzetti v. City of Brisbane (1977) 67 Cal.App.3d 565.) Accordingly, it does not immunize a public entity from liability caused by negligence independent of the design. Moreover, Defendant still has not shown bifurcation will serve judicial economy. 

 

Accordingly, Defendant City of La Mirada’s Motion for Leave to Amend the City’s Answer to Plaintiff’s Complaint and Bifurcate Trial is GRANTED IN PART.

 

II.                  MOTION TO COMPEL CROSS-DEFENDANT CITY OF LOS ANGELES’ FURTHER RESPONSES TO SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES, SET ONE

 

Defendant City of La Mirada moves this court for an order compelling disclosure of the address and contact information of A.J., the alleged perpetrator in this case. City of La Mirada makes this motion on the grounds (1) the requested information is directly relevant to the claims and defenses in this action; (2) the information is reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence; (3) the witness is a minor who participated in a summer camp program operated by Defendant City of Los Angeles; and (4) good cause exists to permit the disclosure subject to appropriate protective conditions.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2017.010 provides any party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or to the determination of any motion made in that action. “’The disclosure of the names and addresses of potential witnesses is a routine and essential part of pretrial discovery.’ [Citation.]” (Puerto v. Superior Court (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1249–1250.)

 

City of La Mirada argues Plaintiffs claims against the City of La Mirada are directly from the alleged conduct of A.J. The issues in this case include what occurred between the two minors; the duration and context of their interaction; the presence or absence of supervision; and whether the incident was foreseeable. As a result, A.J.’s testimony is therefore directly relevant and essential to the City’s defense. The City of La Mirada seeks only A.J. 's current address and contact information for the purposes of noticing and conducting a deposition and proposes the following safeguards: (1) Disclosure limited to counsel of record; (2) "Confidential, Attorneys' Eyes Only" designation; (3) Use solely for the purposes of this litigation; (4) No dissemination beyond counsel and retained experts; (5) Return or destruction at the conclusion of litigation; (6) Deposition limited to a single session; and (7) Attendance of a parent, guardian, or guardian ad litem.

 

City of Los Angeles filed a limited opposition on the grounds absent a court order or permission from A.J.’s parent to disclose this information, the City of Los Angeles is legally obligated to assert privacy rights on behalf of the minor camper.

 

The court GRANTS the motion to compel further. The court finds the privacy concerns can be sufficiently addressed under the proposed safeguards. In order for the City to meaningfully defend itself, it must have access to the only direct witness.

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant City of La Mirada’s Motion to Compel Cross-Defendant City of Los Aneles’ Further Responses to Special Interrogatories, Set One is GRANTED.   

Case Number: 25STCV04975    Hearing Date: July 9, 2026    Dept: 529

JERRY CAMPISI, ET AL. VS GLOBAL INTEGRITY REALTY CORPORATION

 

DEMURRER TO FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT

 

Date of Hearing:        July 9, 2026                                        Trial Date:       September 14, 2026  

Department:              529                                                     Case No.:        25STCV04975

 

Moving Party:            Defendant Global Integrity Realty Corporation

Responding Party:     Plaintiffs Jerry Campisi, Chritopher Hart, Whitney Starr, Brian Valdez, Valerie Martinez and Omar Maya

Meet and Confer:      Yes. Taylor Decl.

 

BACKGROUND

 

On February 21, 2025, Plaintiffs Jerry Campisi, Chritopher Hart, Whitney Starr, Brian Valdez, Valerie Martinez and Omar Maya filed a complaint against Defendants Global Integrity Realty Corporation for Violations of the Investigative Consumer Reporting Agencies Act (“ICRAA”) – Civ. Code §1786 et seq.; Failure to Provide Receipt for Tenant Screening – Civ. Code §1950.6; and Declaratory Relief.

 

On March 11, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a First Amended Complaint.

 

[TENTATIVE] RULING:

 

Defendant Global Integrity Realty Corporation’s Demurrer to the First Amended Complaint is OVERRULED, in part. Defendant Global Integrity Realty Corporation’s Motion to Strike is DENIED.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Defendant Global Integrity Realty Corporation demurs to the First Amended Complaint by Plaintiffs Jerry Campisi, Chritopher Hart, Whitney Starr, Brian Valdez, Valerie Martinez and Omar Maya on the grounds that the First Amended Complaint fails to state claims for causes of action two through seven for Negligence, Violations of Unfair Competition Law, Violations of the Consumer Legal Remedies Act, Invasion of Privacy, False Advertisement, and Fraud. Defendant also seeks to strike Plaintiff’s allegations for punitive damages.

 

Second Cause of Action

 

Defendant demurs to the second cause of action for negligence on the grounds Plaintiffs cannot satisfy the injury requirement.

 

To plead a cause of action for negligence, one must allege (1) a legal duty owed to plaintiffs to use due care; (2) breach of duty; (3) causation; and (4) damage to plaintiff. (County of Santa Clara v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2006) 137 Cal. App. 4th 292, 318.) Evidence Code section 669, holds that “a presumption of negligence arises from the violation of a statute which was enacted to protect a class of persons of which the plaintiff is a member against the type of harm which the plaintiff suffered as a result of the violation of the statute.” (Urhausen v. Longs Drugstores Cal., Inc. (2007) 155 Cal.App.4th 254, 267.)

 

Defendant argues it is difficult to link Defendant’s breach to Plaintiffs’ damages or to even determine whether Plaintiff suffered damages constituting an actual, concrete injury. Outside of conclusory allegations of “actual damages,” “emotional distress,” and invasion of privacy rights”, Defendant contends Plaintiff’s allegations that Defendant violated ICCRA by not providing Plaintiffs’ the opportunity to check a box to request copies of the Reports is a legal injury that cannot satisfy the well-settled injury requirement for negligence. Rather, Plaintiffs need to allege facts that establish Defendant’s violation either resulted in Plaintiff’s identity being stolen or Defendant took an adverse action based on the report, for example.  

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue the allegations are sufficient. Rather, Defendant’s demurrer refuses to acknowledge (1) Plaintiffs have any privacy rights in the contents of reports that are regulated to protect privacy rights, (2) GIRC spent Plaintiffs’ money on reports GIRC had no right to, and (3) Plaintiffs had a right to those reports.

 

The court finds Plaintiffs have sufficiently alleged a negligence claim for the purposes of a demurrer. Plaintiff’s damages are based on Defendant using a portion of each application fee to pay for investigative consumer reports about Plaintiffs, obtaining private information about Plaintiffs through these reports, and not providing a means by which the Plaintiffs could indicate that he or she wished to receive a copy of any report. Whether those damages can be proven are not appropriate for demurrer. On demurrer, the court must accept the allegation that Plaintiffs paid money and were deprived of a statutory entitlement, and must construe it liberally. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)  The complaint, here, alleges a cognizable injury.  Defendant raises other new arguments in reply which the court does not consider here.

 

Based on the foregoing, the demurrer to the second cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

Third Cause of Action

 

Defendant demurs to the third cause of action for violation of the UCL on the grounds Plaintiffs cannot satisfy the injury requirement.

 

To successfully plead a UCL claim for unfair business practices, a plaintiff must allege facts justifying relief in the form of protecting the public from unfair business practices or deceptive advertising. (Day v. AT&T Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 325, 331-332.) A plaintiff must plead and prove that the defendant engaged in a business practice that was either unlawful (i.e., is forbidden by law) or unfair (i.e., harm to victim outweighs any benefit) or fraudulent (i.e., is likely to deceive members of the public).  (Albillo v. Intermodal Container Services, Inc. (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 190, 206.) Pursuant to Business and Professions Code section 17204, a private party must have “suffered injury in fact and has lost money or property as a result of the unfair competition” to pursue a claim.

 

Defendant first argues there is no violation or injury under Section 1950.6(b) because the alleged $30 used to process the application fee may be used for out of pocket costs to cover any background information obtained under the Section. Moreover, the deprivation of the fee cannot constitute a “loss of money” under the UCL for the purposes of establishing a legal injury as the approximately $30 application fee is a legally permissible fee payment. Defendant also notes Plaintiffs were rented the units they sought, and therefore cannot sustain a claim for the purposes of the UCL. (See Yeh v. Barrington Pacific LLC (2026) 117 Cal.App.5th 1303 holding “[p]laintiffs identify no lost opportunity, adverse action, or financial detriment caused by the timing or absence of the report. Indeed, their applications were approved and they obtained the very housing they sought.”)

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue Plaintiffs have sufficiently brought an unfair business practices cause of action based upon Defendant’s violations of the ICRAA as well as Civil Code section 1950.6. Here, Defendant had a statutory obligation to only charge Plaintiffs for Defendant’s actual, out-of-pocket expenses, and to provide Plaintiffs with copies of consumer credit reports, or, if no reports were obtained, to return the amount Plaintiffs paid Defendant for reports. As alleged, Defendant collected application screening fees, while failing to provide Plaintiffs with copies of reports prepared about them thus depriving Plaintiffs of both money and property.

 

The court finds Plaintiffs have alleged sufficient facts to support a claim for violation of the UCL based upon Defendant’s violation of Civil Code section 1950.6. While they obtained housing, they suffered economic injury by paying for services they did not fully receive. (See Civ. Code §1950.6(f).) The court does note Yeh has held there was no UCL claim in the ICRAA where the plaintiffs received what they paid for (application processing) despite the statutory violation. However, where a portion of the complaint stated a cause of action, a general demurrer to the whole complaint was improperly sustained. (Jones v. Iverson (1900) 131 Cal. 101, 104.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the demurrer to the third cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

Fourth Cause of Action

 

Defendant demurs to the fourth cause of action for violation of the CLRA on the grounds the statute’s notice requirements were not met and ICRAA preempts its application.

 

“The CLRA makes unlawful, in Civil Code section 1770, subdivision (a) … various ‘unfair methods of competition and unfair or deceptive acts or practices undertaken by any person in a transaction intended to result or which results in the sale or lease of goods or services to any consumer.’ The CLRA proscribes 27 specific acts or practices.”  (Rubenstein v. The Gap, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 870, 880-881.) 

 

Defendant argues the CLRA only applies to the sale of goods and services, not to the rental of real property. Additionally, the CLRA requires a 30-day notice pre-suit to allow the defendant an opportunity to cure the issue. Here, there are no allegations of notice in the FAC.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue authority that provides the CLRA does not apply to this matter because the ICRAA preempts it. Moreover, an action for injunctive relief brought under the CRLA may be brought without need to comply with the 30-day notice required when damages are sought.

 

The court agrees the CLRA claim does not apply to real property. “Goods” are unequivocally chattels under the CLRA and, therefore, real property is not a “good' under the CLRA. ‘Services' means work, labor, and services for other than a commercial or business use, including services furnished in connection with the sale or repair of goods.” Civil Code § 1761(b). The rental of real property does not fall within these definitions.

 

Based on the foregoing, the demurrer to the fourth cause of action is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Fifth Cause of Action

 

Defendant demurs to the fifth cause of action for invasion of privacy fails because there are insufficient facts to establish a claim.

 

An invasion of privacy claim requires a protected privacy interest, a reasonable expectation of privacy, and a serious invasion. (Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1, 37.)

 

Defendant argues the allegations in the FAC fail to state that the Plaintiffs had a reasonable expectation that Defendants would not obtain information about them. There are no allegations that Plaintiffs did not consent to Defendant obtaining information that was relevant to determining whether Plaintiffs were appropriate tenants. Rather, Plaintiffs consented to such checks by signing the authorization and completing the rental application.  Moreover, the alleged belief that Defendants would not “would not solicit, provide or receive information concerning their character . . . unless Defendants complied with all the governing laws in the state of California[]” is irrelevant to the central issue of whether Defendant intruded on Plaintiffs’ expectation of privacy. Viewing the allegations altogether, Defendant contends the fifth cause of action is merely duplicative of the ICRAA cause of action.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues privacy rights are neither abandoned by coerced consent, nor by bringing ICRAA actions. Plaintiffs have properly pled their invasion of privacy cause of action and demurrer cannot be sustained. (See Connor v. First Student, Inc. (2018) 5 Cal.5th 1026, 1032-1033; see also Civ. Code, § 1786.52 [confirming ICRAA litigant’s right to bring invasion of privacy action].) With due respect for prospective tenants’ privacy, the ICRAA makes clear that a landlord has no right to procure investigative consumer reports in housing applications unless the conditions precedent identified in Civil Code section 1786.16 have been fulfilled. (Civ. Code, § 1786.16(a); see also Civ. Code, § 1786(b).) Here, Plaintiffs have properly alleged that Defendant invaded their privacy interests by procuring investigative consumer reports about them, without fulfilling the requisite preconditions.

 

The court finds the allegations sufficient for the purposes of a demurrer. Civil Code section 1786.52 explicitly preserves invasion of privacy claims, stating that “Nothing in this chapter shall in any way affect the right of any consumer to maintain an action against an investigative consumer reporting agency, a user of an investigative consumer report, or an informant for invasion of privacy or defamation.” When a landlord fails to meet statutory preconditions of Civil Code section 1786.16, they violate both the ICRAA's procedural safeguards and potentially invade the tenant's privacy rights.

 

Based on the foregoing, the demurrer to the fifth cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

Sixth Cause of Action

 

Defendant demurs to the sixth cause of action for false advertising on the grounds there are insufficient facts to establish a claim.

 

A cause of action for false advertising requires the following elements: (1) the statements in the advertising materials made or disseminated by Defendant are untrue or misleading and (2) Defendant knew, or by the exercise of reasonable care should have known, the statements were untrue or misleading. (See Business & Professions Code §17500.1; see also People v. Lynam (1967) 253 Cal.App.2d 959, 965-966.)

 

Defendant argues the only alleged misrepresentation in the FAC is a generic statement that “In connection with Defendant’s rental application and tenant-screening process, Defendant made statements and representations to prospective applicants, including Plaintiffs, in application materials and related communications, concerning the tenant-screening process and the availability of required disclosures and the opportunity for applicants to request and obtain a copy of investigative consumer reports procured in connection with the application.” (FAC ¶72.) Defendant contends there are no facts alleging that these statements were made as part of an “extensive and long term advertising campaign.” Even with a properly plead misrepresentation, the allegations in the FAC are too thin to establish reliance.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues Plaintiffs have sufficiently alleged that they lost the value of the amount spent on the application fee, while also being deprived of their property interest in the reports themselves. Since Plaintiffs have also alleged these losses were induced by Defendant’s false advertisements, Plaintiffs’ False Advertising cause of action has been sufficiently pled.

 

The court finds the allegations sufficient for the purposes of a demurrer. Reading the complaint as a whole, the complaint sufficiently alleges Defendant made statements and representations concerning the tenant-screening process, the availability of required disclosures and the opportunity to request investigative consumer reports; they were false because Defendant did not comply with those statements and Plaintiff was harmed as a result.

 

Based on the foregoing, the demurrer to the sixth cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

Seventh Cause of Action

 

Defendant demurs to the seventh cause of action for fraud on the grounds there are insufficient facts to establish a claim.

 

The elements of fraud are: “(a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Charnay v. Cobert (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 170, 184.) In California, fraud, including negligent misrepresentation, must be pled with specificity. (Small v. Fritz Companies, Inc. (2003) 30 Cal.4th 167, 184.)

 

Defendant argues the fraud claim fails for several reasons. First, the allegations of fraud in the FAC comingle allegedly fraudulent statements and falsified evidence offered during separate litigation proceedings (Plaintiffs’ counsel has filed about 20 of these lawsuits against Defendant Global Integrity Realty Corporation) with the allegedly fraudulent statements made to induce Plaintiffs to submit the allegedly ICRAA-defective rental applications. Moreover, the FAC does not contain a specific statement of any misrepresentation, or even a description of the content of the alleged misrepresentations. Instead, it reiterates a broad general and vague statement that Defendant made “statements and representations” to Plaintiffs, in “application materials and related communications, concerning the tenant-screening process . . .”  Moreover, the allegations in the FAC do not show a “reasonable probability” that Defendant’s alleged failure to accurately represent that the rental application fully complied with ICRAA would be material enough to induce reasonable people in Plaintiffs’ positions to refuse to pay to complete a rental application and forgo entering into a lease agreement.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues the statutory privilege afforded to statements made in judicial proceedings does not shield a defendant from liability relating to statements made outside of judicial proceedings.  Litigation privilege also does not apply to causes of action based on statutory entitlements that are more specific than the litigation privilege and the two are irreconcilable. (See People v. Persolve, LLC (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 1267 [finding no litigation privilege barring unfair competition law claims based on conduct specifically prohibited by the Rosenthal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act].)

 

Regarding the litigation privilege, the court finds the allegations unclear. Reading these allegations, it is unclear whether these are misrepresentations made in pre-litigation or during judicial proceedings. The first part reads as pre-litigation misrepresentations (compliance with the ICRAA) and the remaining allegations deal with "written communications" after suit was filed and the verified discovery responses, which are classic litigation-related communications. Moreover, it is unclear what the misrepresentations were here that actually induced conduct on the part of the plaintiffs.

 

Based on the foregoing, the demurrer to the seventh cause of action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Motion to Strike

 

Defendant moves to strike portions of Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint regarding punitive damages.

 

Motions to strike are used to reach defects or objections to pleadings that are not challengeable by demurrer (i.e., words, phrases, prayer for damages, etc.).  (See CCP §§435, 436 & 437.)  A motion to strike must be filed within the time allowed to respond (e.g., 30 days after service of the complaint or cross-complaint) unless extended by court order.  Where there are grounds for a demurrer and a motion to strike, they must be filed together and noticed for hearing at the same time.  (CCP §435(b)(3) and CRC Rule 329.)  A motion to strike can be made to strike irrelevant, false or improper matter inserted in any pleading or to strike any pleading or part thereof not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule or order of the court.  (CCP §436.)  

 

Punitive Damages are recoverable where the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice, express or implied. (Civ. Code §3294.) “Something more than the mere commission of a tort is always required for punitive damages. There must be circumstances of aggravation or outrage, such as spite or malice, or a fraudulent or evil motive on the part of the defendant, or such a conscious and deliberate disregard of the interests of others that his conduct may be called willful or wanton.” (Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890, 894.) 

 

Defendant argues the allegations at most are general and conclusory. The FAC alleges (1) “deliberate planned violations of the ICRAA;” (2) “Defendants’ conduct and violations of the ICRAA was and is willful and grossly negligent;” and (3) “Defendants acted in deliberate or reckless disregard of their obligations and the rights of the Plaintiffs.” (FAC ¶24, 25.) Defendant maintains these allegations do not establish the key prerequisites for punitive damages: “oppression, fraud, or malice.”

 

The court finds the allegations sufficient to support a claim for punitive damages. Plaintiff has alleged Defendant knew of and disregarded its obligations. As noted by Defendant, punitive damages are available under the ICRAA cause of action. The punitive damages could also survive with a properly pleaded fraud claim.

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion to strike is DENIED.
