DEPARTMENT 515 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV00899    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion to Quash Service of Summons

The Motion to Quash Service of Summons is granted.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Laura Aguirre (Plaintiff) filed this action against MV Transportation, Inc. (MV), alleging that Plaintiff was injured on May 26, 2022 at premises MV owned, maintained, and operated. Plaintiff later filed an amendment to the complaint substituting La Shawn Evans (Evans) for Doe 1, and a further amendment correcting the spelling of her name.

The causes of action are: (1) Premises Liability; and (2) General Negligence.

Specially appearing Defendant Evans filed a Motion to Quash Service of Summons. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

“A defendant, on or before the last day of his or her time to plead or within any further time that the court may for good cause allow, may serve and file a notice of motion ... [t]o quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over him or her.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a)(1).) When a defendant challenges jurisdiction on that ground, “the burden is on the plaintiff to prove the existence of jurisdiction by proving, inter alia, the facts requisite to an effective service.” (Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1440.)

Personal service is not the only route by which a court acquires jurisdiction over a defendant. “A general appearance by a party is equivalent to personal service of summons on such party.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 410.50, subd. (a).) A defendant appears by answering, demurring, filing a notice of motion to strike, filing a notice of motion to transfer, moving for reclassification, giving the plaintiff written notice of appearance, or through an attorney’s notice of appearance. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1014.) That list is not exclusive. What controls is “whether defendant takes a part in the particular action which in some manner recognizes the authority of the court to proceed.” (Hamilton v. Asbestos Corp. (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1127, 1147, quoting Sanchez v. Superior Court (1988) 203 Cal.App.3d 1391, 1397.) “If the defendant raises an issue for resolution or seeks relief available only if the court has jurisdiction over the defendant, then the appearance is a general one.” (Factor Health Management v. Superior Court (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 246, 250 (Factor Health).)

The service of process statutes “are now to be liberally construed to effectuate service and uphold jurisdiction if actual notice has been received by the defendant.” (Bein v. Brechtel-Jochim Group, Inc. (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 1387, 1392.) That rule extends to substituted service. (Ibid.)

 

ANALYSIS

I. General Appearance

Plaintiff argues that Evans submitted to the Court’s jurisdiction before she filed this Motion. Plaintiff points to Evans’s appearance for her deposition in this action, to counsel’s statement on the record that he represented her, and to counsel’s later agreement that her name could be corrected without a noticed motion.

A general appearance requires participation in the action that recognizes the court’s authority to proceed. In Hamilton, the defendant was served with a motion to consolidate two actions for all purposes, did not oppose it, and then took part in the consolidated trial. The Supreme Court held that it had generally appeared in the action in which it had never been served. (Hamilton, supra, 22 Cal.4th at pp. 1148-1149.) In Sanchez, by contrast, the defendant appeared and testified at depositions in an action consolidated for trial with a second action in which he had never been served. The Court of Appeal held that those activities “cannot reasonably be construed as a general appearance” in the unserved action. (Sanchez, supra, 203 Cal.App.3d at p. 1399.)

Evans gave her testimony in this action rather than in a companion case. “‘[T]he mere physical presence of a party or of his attorney in the court room during the trial, or at the taking of depositions where there is no participation, or the presence as a witness of a party over whom jurisdiction has not been acquired, will not of itself constitute an appearance in the action.’” (Slaybaugh v. Superior Court (1977) 70 Cal.App.3d 216, 223-224.) Evans was present as a witness, and the attempted service postdated the deposition by three weeks.

Here, Evans did not take part in this action in a manner recognizing the Court’s authority to proceed. She attended a deposition Plaintiff took and gave testimony. (Navi Decl., ¶¶ 3, 5.) Before filing this Motion she filed nothing with the Court, initiated no discovery, and asked the Court for no relief. Nor is this Motion itself a general appearance. (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (d).)

Plaintiff relies on counsel’s statement at the outset of the deposition that he was “representing defendant MV Transportation and representing the witness here in deposition today.” (Navi Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 2.) Counsel’s statement identified a client. Counsel raised no issue for the Court to resolve, and he sought no relief the Court could grant only with jurisdiction over Evans. (Factor Health, supra, 132 Cal.App.4th at p. 250.) Counsel gave no notice of appearance for Evans. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1014.) He also declined to accept service of the summons and complaint on her behalf. (Navi Decl., ¶ 7.)

Nor did counsel’s agreement that Plaintiff could correct Evans’s name without a noticed motion recognize the Court’s authority over Evans. Counsel also advised that his office would represent Evans after the correction. (Navi Decl., ¶ 7.) Neither act asked the Court for anything. Plaintiff filed that amendment, and Evans took no part in it.

Plaintiff’s reliance on Creed v. Schultz (1983) 148 Cal.App.3d 733 is misplaced. There, the party contesting jurisdiction served a notice of deposition and a demand for production of documents. The court held that act a general appearance. (Id. at p. 740.) The Sanchez court distinguished Creed on the ground that the defendant before it “did not initiate any discovery” in the unserved action. (Sanchez, supra, 203 Cal.App.3d at p. 1398.) Evans noticed no deposition and served no discovery in this action.

Therefore, the Court concludes that Evans did not generally appear in this action. That conclusion disposes of Evans’s further contention that she attended the deposition under compulsion of a notice served on her employer. Plaintiff must establish that service on her was valid.

II. Substituted Service

Evans contends that she was never served, because the papers were left at a unit where she has never lived and with a person who is not a member of her household.

A summons may be served by personal delivery of a copy of the summons and of the complaint to the person to be served. (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.10.) Where personal delivery cannot be accomplished, Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20, subdivision (b) provides:

“If a copy of the summons and complaint cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to the person to be served ... a summons may be served by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the person’s dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge of their office, place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, at least 18 years of age, who shall be informed of the contents thereof, and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and of the complaint by first-class mail, postage prepaid to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left. Service of a summons in this manner is deemed complete on the 10th day after the mailing.”

The parties offer conflicting accounts of where Evans lived and of who received the papers. The proof of service states that the papers were left at 5891 Miramonte Boulevard with a person identified as a “Co-occupant.” It recites that the papers were left with a competent member of the household at the dwelling house or usual place of abode of the party. (Dankert Decl., Ex. 1.) Evans states that she has never lived at that address, and that it “has never been [her] dwelling house.” She states that her residence is 5891½ Miramonte Boulevard, that the two are separate units among six at the structure, that other individuals reside at the 5891 unit, and that no one else lived in her unit when service was attempted. (Evans Decl., ¶¶ 5-8.)

The return of a registered process server “establishes a presumption, affecting the burden of producing evidence, of the facts stated in the return.” (Evid. Code, § 647; see American Express Centurion Bank v. Zara (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 383, 390.) A presumption of that kind requires the trier of fact to assume the presumed fact “unless and until evidence is introduced which would support a finding of its nonexistence, in which case the trier of fact shall determine the existence or nonexistence of the presumed fact from the evidence and without regard to the presumption.” (Evid. Code, § 604.) Even assuming this return carries that presumption, Evans’s declaration would support a finding that she did not live at 5891 Miramonte Boulevard and that no member of her household received the papers. Therefore, the Court decides those questions on the evidence.

The Court finds that Evans resided at 5891½ Miramonte Boulevard when service was attempted, and that no one else lived in that unit. Evans has personal knowledge of where she lives and of who lives with her. (Evans Decl., ¶¶ 5-8.) Notably, Plaintiff’s own evidence places Evans at 5891½ Miramonte Boulevard, because she gave that address under oath at the deposition Plaintiff took three weeks before the attempted service. (Navi Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. 2.) Plaintiff offered no basis for the return’s description of the recipient as a co-occupant, and no declaration from the process server or from anyone else addressing the unit or the household.

Here, Plaintiff did not effect substituted service under Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20, subdivision (b). First, 5891 Miramonte Boulevard was not Evans’s dwelling house or usual place of abode. Second, the person who received the papers at a different unit was not a competent member of Evans’s household. Finally, the attempts recorded in the affidavit of reasonable diligence were all made at that same address. (Dankert Decl., Ex. 1.) “‘“Ordinarily, ... two or three attempts at personal service at a proper place should fully satisfy the requirement of reasonable diligence and allow substituted service to be made.” [Citation.]’” (Bein, supra, 6 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1391-1392.) Attempts at a unit where Evans has never lived were not attempts at a proper place. Thus, Plaintiff did not show that a copy of the summons and complaint could not with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to Evans.

Plaintiff’s reliance on Bein and Ellard is misplaced. In Bein, the process server left the papers with the gate guard the defendants had authorized to control access to them and to their residence. The Court of Appeal held the guard a competent member of the household, because his relationship with the defendants made it more likely than not that he would deliver the papers to them. (Bein, supra, 6 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1393-1394.) In Ellard, the papers were left with the manager of the commercial mail facility the defendants used as their usual mailing address, one of the locations Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20, subdivision (b) identifies. (Ellard v. Conway (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 540, 545-547.) In both cases the papers went to a location the statute identifies, and to a person whose connection to the defendants made delivery to them likely. Here the papers went to a unit that is not Evans’s residence, and to a person the record does not connect to her.

Moreover, the liberal construction Plaintiff invokes applies only where the defendant has received actual notice. The Bein defendants did not claim that they failed to receive notice of the service. (Bein, supra, 6 Cal.App.4th at p. 1394.) Evans states that she was never personally served with the summons and complaint. (Evans Decl., ¶ 3.) Her counsel states that the proof of service was never served on his office and came to his attention only on review of the Court’s docket. (Dankert Decl., ¶ 3.) Plaintiff submitted no evidence that the papers left at the 5891 unit reached Evans.

Plaintiff correctly observes that Taylor-Rush arose on a different form of service. That case involved service by mail on an out-of-state defendant under a statute requiring evidence of actual delivery. (Taylor-Rush v. Multitech Corp. (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 103, 110.) However, Evans cites the case for a narrower proposition, and the posture makes no difference to it: “A defendant is under no duty to respond to a defectively served summons. The notice requirement is not satisfied by actual knowledge of the action without service conforming to the statutory requirements, which are to be strictly construed.” (Id. at p. 111.) Evans knew of this action. (Reply, at p. 6:24.) That knowledge does not substitute for service conforming to the statute.

Plaintiff therefore failed to prove the facts requisite to effective service.

That conclusion also answers Plaintiff’s argument from the Motion’s timing. Plaintiff concedes that the procedural history recited in the Opposition does not independently establish valid service. (Opp., at p. 9:25-26.) Evans’s time to plead never began. No default could therefore be entered against her. (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (d).) MV, not Evans, filed the July 21, 2026 ex parte application to continue trial and to advance the hearing on this Motion. In any event, no act by a party who makes a motion under section 418.10 constitutes an appearance unless the Court denies the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (e)(1).) Evans’s delay in filing this Motion, unaccompanied by conduct recognizing the Court’s authority, did not cure the defect in service.

Plaintiff asks in the alternative that the Court permit an additional opportunity to serve Evans at 5891½ Miramonte Boulevard. (Opp., at p. 10:9-12.) An order quashing service does not dismiss the action. Plaintiff may serve Evans at the correct address without leave of court. The Court therefore denies the alternative request.

Accordingly, the Motion to Quash Service of Summons is granted.

 

CONCLUSION

The Motion to Quash Service of Summons is granted.


Case Number: 24STCV18995    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Petition to Confirm Minor’s Compromise.

The hearing on the Petition is continued to a date to be set at the hearing.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Bianca Surmeh Salvatori (Petitioner) and Plaintiff Elle Alessandra Salvatori (Claimant), a minor appearing through Petitioner as guardian ad litem, filed this action against S-Oaks Blvd Apartments Owner, LLC and Mill Creek RESIDENTIAL TRUST, LLC (collectively, “Defendants”), alleging that Defendants failed to maintain the apartment Plaintiffs rented from them in habitable condition.

The causes of action are: (1) Breach of Implied Warranty of Habitability; (2) Negligence; (3) Negligent Hiring, Retention, and Supervision; (4) Breach of Contract; (5) Breach of the Covenant of Quiet Enjoyment; (6) Nuisance; (7) Violation of Business and Professions Code Section 17200; (8) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress; (9) Negligent Infliction of Emotional Distress; (10) Fraudulent Misrepresentation; and (11) Negligent Misrepresentation.

Petitioner filed a Petition for Expedited Approval of Compromise of Claim or Action for Minor.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A guardian ad litem appearing for a minor has power to compromise the minor’s claim in a pending action “with the approval of the court in which the action or proceeding is pending.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 372, subd. (a)(3).) In approving a minor’s compromise, the court’s “primary concern is whether the compromise is sufficient to provide for the minor’s injuries, care and treatment.” (Goldberg v. Superior Court (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1378, 1382.) A petition for that approval “must be verified by the petitioner and must contain a full disclosure of all information that has any bearing upon the reasonableness of the compromise, covenant, settlement, or disposition.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950.) Where each condition listed in rule 7.950.5(a) exists, the petitioner may satisfy those disclosure requirements by submitting a completed form MC-350EX. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950.5(a).)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Settlement Amount

Petitioner asks the Court to approve a gross settlement of $2,750 for Claimant, to be paid without deduction for medical expenses, attorney fees, or costs. (Pet., items 11a, 17.) Petitioner represents having made a careful and diligent inquiry into the facts and circumstances of the incident, the responsibility for it, and the nature and extent of Claimant’s injuries. (Pet., item 10.) Petitioner identified no injury requiring care and no medical expenses. (Pet., items 7-8, 13a.)

Here, the Court finds that $2,750 is a reasonable compromise of Claimant’s claims. The amount is typical of settlements in the many similar cases before this Court involving minors who lived in uninhabitable conditions, suffered no physical injury, and required no medical treatment.

The Court also finds the apportionment reasonable. Under the Settlement Agreement and Release (Settlement Agreement), Defendants must pay $27,500 to Plaintiffs and waive any balance remaining on Plaintiffs’ tenant account. (Pet., Ex. 11c, at p. 3.) Petitioner proposes that $24,750 go to Petitioner and $2,750 go to Claimant. (Pet., item 12b(3).) Plaintiffs allege that Petitioner signed the lease and that Claimant was four years old when the tenancy began. (Compl., ¶¶ 8, 11.) Thus, the claims tied to the lease and the tenant account belong to Petitioner. Therefore, Claimant’s 10% share of the cash payment reflects the difference between the two Plaintiffs’ claims.

II. Attorney Compensation

Where an attorney assisted the petitioner, the petition must disclose the fees the attorney has received or expects to receive and the identity of the person who paid or is expected to pay them. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.951(4)-(5).) The petition must also disclose “[t]he terms of any agreement between the petitioner and the attorney.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.951(6).)

Here, Petitioner disclosed that counsel has received or expects to receive $9,625 from Petitioner, and requested no attorney fees from Claimant’s share. (Pet., items 17c, 18b.) Those entries satisfy rule 7.951(4) and (5). However, Petitioner did not disclose the terms of the fee agreement, as rule 7.951(6) separately requires.

III. Disposition of Proceeds

Petitioner requests that Claimant’s $2,750 be paid to a parent of Claimant under Probate Code sections 3401 and 3402, without bond. (Pet., item 19b(4).) Where the balance does not exceed $5,000, the Court may order that it “be paid or delivered to a parent of the minor, without bond, upon the terms and under the conditions specified in Article 1 (commencing with Section 3400) of Chapter 2.” (Prob. Code, § 3611, subd. (e).) Under Probate Code section 3401, money belonging to a minor who has no guardian of the estate “may be paid or delivered to a parent of the minor entitled to the custody of the minor to be held in trust for the minor until the minor reaches majority if the requirements of subdivision (c) are satisfied.” (Prob. Code, § 3401, subd. (a).)

Subdivision (c) provides, “This section applies only if both of the following requirements are satisfied: [¶] (1) The total estate of the minor, including the money and other property to be paid or delivered to the parent, does not exceed five thousand dollars ($5,000) in value. [¶] (2) The parent to whom the money or other property is to be paid or delivered gives the person making the payment or delivery written assurance, verified by the oath of such parent, that the total estate of the minor, including the money or other property to be paid or delivered to the parent, does not exceed five thousand dollars ($5,000) in value.” (Prob. Code, § 3401, subd. (c).)

Here, Petitioner meets the requirements of Probate Code section 3401. First, Petitioner is Claimant’s parent. (Application for Appointment of Guardian Ad Litem, filed Aug. 15, 2024, items 1a, 8b.) Second, Petitioner checked item 19b(4), which states that the “[v]alue of minor’s entire estate, including the money or property to be delivered, must not exceed $5,000,” and verified the Petition under penalty of perjury. (Pet., item 19b(4); Pet., at p. 7.) Because Petitioner verified the $2,750 to be delivered against that limit, the Court concludes that this satisfies subdivision (c).

However, Petitioner did not attach Attachment 19b(4), which must state the name and address of the parent and the money to be delivered. Nor did Petitioner state, in item 8b of the proposed order, the amount or method of disbursement. (Proposed Order, item 8b.)

IV. Other Corrections

Petitioner identified Petitioner’s relationship to Claimant in item 1 only as guardian ad litem and did not check the box for parent. (Pet., item 1.)

Petitioner also checked item 4a, which states that Claimant’s claim is not the subject of a pending action. (Pet., item 4.) However, Claimant is a plaintiff in this action. Thus, item 4b applies to Claimant’s claim.

Accordingly, the Court continues the hearing on the Petition to a date to be set at the hearing. No later than 9 court days before the continued hearing, Petitioner must file an amended Petition, or a supplemental verified declaration, that (1) discloses the terms of Petitioner’s fee agreement with counsel; (2) attaches Attachment 19b(4); and (3) corrects items 1 and 4. Petitioner must also lodge a revised proposed order that states the method of disbursement and names the parent to whom the balance is to be paid.

 

CONCLUSION

The hearing on the Petition is continued to a date to be set at the hearing.


Case Number: 26STCV12858    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion to Strike

The Motion to Strike is denied.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Mercedes Moya and Noy Elbaz (Plaintiffs) filed this action against Katherine Calvert (Defendant), alleging that Defendant drove into their occupied parked vehicle while under the influence of alcohol or another intoxicating substance.

The causes of action in the First Amended Complaint (FAC) are: (1) Motor Vehicle; and (2) General Negligence.

Defendant filed a Motion to Strike. Plaintiffs filed an Opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

The Court may “[s]trike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The grounds for a motion to strike “shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (a).) In construing a pleading, its allegations “must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) Accordingly, “judges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth.” (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)

ANALYSIS

I. Added Allegations

Defendant contends that the factual allegations the FAC added after the Complaint are of questionable veracity, and that their belated appearance underscores the conclusory character of the punitive damages demand. Plaintiffs argue that a motion to strike cannot resolve a dispute over the truth of what is pleaded, and that the FAC contradicts nothing in the Complaint.

The grounds for a motion to strike must appear on the face of the challenged pleading or in matter subject to judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (a).) Defendant disputes “the factual basis and veracity of these newly alleged matters.” (Margaryan Decl., ¶ 6.) However, counsel’s disbelief is not a defect on the face of the FAC, and the declaration offers no judicially noticeable matter concerning Defendant’s condition or her conduct at the scene. The Court therefore takes the FAC’s allegations as true for purposes of this Motion.

Nor does the amendment itself give a ground to strike. Under the sham pleading doctrine, a plaintiff may not avoid a defect by filing an amended pleading that omits harmful allegations or pleads facts inconsistent with the superseded one, absent a satisfactory explanation. (Deveny v. Entropin, Inc. (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 408, 425-426.) Applying the doctrine requires a comparison of the two pleadings, and the Court takes judicial notice of the superseded Complaint as a record of this court. (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (d).) Here, the FAC omits nothing adverse to Plaintiffs and contradicts nothing in the Complaint. Plaintiffs alleged in the Complaint that Defendant drove while under the influence of alcohol or another intoxicating substance and that she knowingly drove while her faculties were impaired. (Compl., Third Cause of Action, ¶¶ 1, 3.) The FAC pleads particulars of that same theory. Defendant identifies no earlier allegation that she was sober, that she was unaware of her impairment, or that she remained at the scene.

Defendant also states that the Complaint contained no allegation that she drove at an unsafe speed. (Margaryan Decl., ¶ 3.) Plaintiffs alleged in the Complaint that Defendant’s conduct violated Vehicle Code section 22350, and they quoted that section’s prohibition on driving at a speed greater than is reasonable or prudent. (Compl., Second Cause of Action.) Thus, the amendment history gives no ground to strike.

II. Punitive Damages

Defendant contends that the FAC pleads no specific facts showing malice or oppression, and that an allegation of impaired driving standing alone will not support punitive damages. Plaintiffs argue that the FAC alleges knowing impaired driving, observable indicia of impairment, a collision with an occupied parked vehicle, and a departure from the scene before a sobriety test could be administered.

Punitive damages are available in an action for the breach of an obligation not arising from contract “where it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) Malice means “conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Id., subd. (c)(1).) Likewise, oppression means “despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.” (Id., subd. (c)(2).) The adjective “despicable” is “a powerful term that refers to circumstances that are ‘base,’ ‘vile,’ or ‘contemptible.’” (College Hospital, Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 725.) The Legislature added that requirement in 1987. It operates as “a new substantive limitation on punitive damage awards,” so that absent an intent to injure the plaintiff, malice “requires more than a ‘willful and conscious’ disregard of the plaintiffs’ interests.” (Ibid.) “Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim.” (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166.)

In Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890, 892, the Supreme Court held that “the act of operating a motor vehicle while intoxicated may constitute an act of ‘malice’ under section 3294 if performed under circumstances which disclose a conscious disregard of the probable dangerous consequences.” A plaintiff proceeding on that basis “must establish that the defendant was aware of the probable dangerous consequences of his conduct, and that he wilfully and deliberately failed to avoid those consequences.” (Id. at pp. 895-896.) One who “wilfully consumes alcoholic beverages to the point of intoxication, knowing that he thereafter must operate a motor vehicle, thereby combining sharply impaired physical and mental faculties with a vehicle capable of great force and speed, reasonably may be held to exhibit a conscious disregard of the safety of others.” (Id. at p. 897.) A history of prior arrests, convictions, and mishaps may heighten the probability and foreseeability of an accident. However, the Supreme Court declined to treat those aggravating factors as “essential prerequisites to the assessment of punitive damages in drunk driving cases.” (Id. at p. 896.) The Supreme Court decided Taylor before the 1987 amendment. Despicability is therefore a separate question the FAC must also satisfy.

“Proof of negligence, even gross negligence, or recklessness is insufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages.” (Dawes v. Superior Court (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 82, 87.) “The risk of injury to others from ordinary driving while intoxicated is certainly foreseeable, but it is not necessarily probable.” (Id. at p. 89.)

Defendant reads Dawes to require driving of the kind alleged there. (Mot., at p. 7.) The driver in that case ran a stop sign and drove at more than 65 miles per hour through heavy pedestrian and bicycle traffic in a 35-mile-per-hour zone. (Dawes, supra, 111 Cal.App.3d at p. 86.) On those allegations the Court of Appeal concluded that “[t]he risk of injury to others from Mardian’s conduct under the circumstances alleged was probable.” (Id. at p. 89.) Notably, it then directed that the punitive damages allegations be reinstated. (Id. at p. 92.)

However, the Court of Appeal decided Dawes under Taylor and could not narrow it. In Dawes the manner of driving made the risk probable. In Taylor the probability came from the combination the Supreme Court described, “sharply impaired physical and mental faculties with a vehicle capable of great force and speed.” (Taylor, supra, 24 Cal.3d at p. 897.) The Supreme Court then declined to require aggravating circumstances beyond that combination. (Id. at p. 896.) Thus, a pleading that alleges knowing impaired driving alleges probable danger, and it need not allege a manner of driving as well.

Here, Plaintiffs allege facts sufficient to support the punitive damages demand. First, they allege that Defendant knew she had consumed alcohol or an intoxicating substance and knew that she would thereafter operate a motor vehicle on a public street. Second, they allege that they personally observed slurred and thickened speech, unsteady balance, and delayed, confused, or nonresponsive answers to simple questions immediately after Defendant drove into their stationary vehicle. Finally, they allege that they asked Defendant to remain until law enforcement arrived so that a sobriety test could be administered, that she refused, and that she drove away before officers arrived. (FAC, at p. 5.)

Those allegations plead the awareness and the deliberate choice Taylor requires. On Plaintiffs’ allegations, Defendant knew that she had impaired herself and that she would then drive, and she drove anyway. The observed speech, balance, and responses describe how far the impairment had gone by the time of the collision. Moreover, the refusal to remain for a sobriety test supports the inference that Defendant knew when she drove that she was impaired. The Court therefore concludes that the FAC alleges malice within the meaning of Civil Code section 3294, subdivision (c)(1).

Defendant also contends that the FAC pleads conclusions rather than facts. (Mot., at pp. 6, 8.) “Pleading in the language of the statute is not objectionable when sufficient facts are alleged to support the allegation,” and “‘[the] distinction between conclusions of law and ultimate facts is not at all clear and involves at most a matter of degree [Citations.].’” (Perkins v. Superior Court (1981) 117 Cal.App.3d 1, 6.) However, Plaintiffs do not rely on the statutory labels alone. They allege what Defendant knew before she drove, what they observed afterward, and what Defendant did at the scene.

Defendant further contends that the conduct alleged does not rise to the level of despicable conduct. Considering the FAC in whole and in context, Plaintiffs allege that Defendant consumed alcohol or another intoxicating substance knowing she would then drive on a public street, that she drove while visibly impaired, and that she struck a vehicle parked at the curb with both Plaintiffs seated inside it. A trier of fact could find that conduct base, vile, or contemptible.

Plaintiffs must ultimately prove malice by clear and convincing evidence. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) The question on this Motion is whether the FAC alleges facts that, if proved, would meet that standard. The Court concludes that it does.

Accordingly, the Motion to Strike is denied.

 

CONCLUSION

The Motion to Strike is denied.
