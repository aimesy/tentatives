MOTION TO COMPEL DISCOVERY RESPONSES (NOT FURTHER) AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS AGAINST DEBTOR EVA CEPEIDA AND HER COUNSEL

 

 

Relief Requested:

 

            The Court enter an order compelling defendant Eva Cepeida to respond within 10 days to Plaintiff’s first set of form interrogatories served on August 11, 2025. Plaintiff also requests the imposition of monetary sanctions against defendant Eva Cepeida and her counsel of record, Jacob Mojarro, in the amount of $1,675.00.  

 

 

Analysis

 

            Plaintiff Jose Rivera (“Plaintiff”) moves to compel Defendant’s responses to set one of Plaintiff’s form interrogatories (post-judgment) and requests the imposition of monetary sanctions in the sum of $1,675.00 against Defendant and her counsel of record.  

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

“The judgment creditor may propound written interrogatories to the judgment debtor . . . requesting information to aid in enforcement of the money judgment.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.020, subd. (a).) “Interrogatories served pursuant to this section may be enforced, to the extent practicable, in the same manner as interrogatories in a civil action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.020, subd. (c).)

 

A party must respond to interrogatories within 30 days after service. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.260, subd. (a).) If a party to whom interrogatories are directed does not provide timely responses, the requesting party may move for an order compelling responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (b).) “The party to whom the interrogatories are directed waives any right to exercise the option to produce writings . . . as well as any objection to the interrogatories, including one based on privilege or on the protection for work product . . . .” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).) There is no time limit for a motion to compel responses to interrogatories other than the cut-off on hearing discovery motions 15 days before trial. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2024.020, subd. (a), 2030.290.)   

 

Code Civ. Proc. § 2023.010(d) provides that a misuse of the discovery process is failing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery. Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010(h) states that a misuse of the discovery process includes making or opposing, unsuccessfully and without substantial justification, a motion to compel or limit discovery. A court may impose a monetary sanction against a party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process or any attorney advising such conduct under Code Civ. Proc. § 2023.030(a). A court has discretion to fix the amount of reasonable monetary sanctions. (Cornerstone Realty Advisors, LLC v. Summit Healthcare Reit, Inc. (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 771, 791.) 

 

 

B.     Defendant Has Provided Responses to the Discovery at Issue

 

In support of the motion, Chad Biggins (“Biggins”) provides a declaration. Attorney

Biggins declares, inter alia, that “[t]he discovery was served on 8-11-25 by mail as well as email . . . .” (Biggins Decl., ¶ 2; Exh. 1.) “To date, Plaintiff has not received the responses.” (Id., ¶ 3; Exh. 2.) Counsel has an hourly rate of $800 per hour which counsel states “has been awarded by the Court in numerous cases.” (Id., ¶ 4.) One (1) hour was spent preparing the motion. (Id.) One (1) hour is anticipated for the reply and appearing at the hearing on the motion. (Id.) Counsel attests that a filing fee of $75.00 was incurred. (Id.) Plaintiff requests monetary sanctions in the sum of $1,675.00. (Id.)

 

            In opposition, Attorney Jacob Mojarro (“Mojarro”) declares, inter alia, that responses to the discovery at issue were served on May 29, 2026. (Mojarro Decl., ¶ 3; Exh. 1.) Supplemental responses were served on June 9 and June 11, 2026 to certain form interrogatories. (Id., ¶¶ 4-5; Exhs. 2-3.) Counsel attests that the instant motion “is not for the purposes of any particular discovery need but rather to incur costs in retribution of the accusation of unethical conduct against Mr. Biggins.” (Id., ¶ 12.) “The Form Interrogatories served by Plaintiff’s were all contained within previous from interrogatories propounded by Plaintiffs and responded to by Cepeida on May 3, 2021. The only additional Form Interrogatories were 6.2 and 6.3 which related to personal injury and not applicable to these post-judgment proceedings.” (Id., ¶ 6.)

 

            The Court DENIES the request of Plaintiff to compel the responses of Defendant to the post-judgment form interrogatories at issue. Although served late, Defendant has now provided responses to the discovery at issue prior to the hearing. Due to the lack of a written reply brief, the Court assumes that Plaintiff does not take issue with the sufficiency of the responses.

 

            The Court, however, finds that the imposition of monetary sanctions is proper. Defendant has not provided a reason for the untimely discovery responses.

 

Therefore, the Court GRANTS IN PART the request for monetary sanctions in the reasonable amount of $460.00 (representing one (1) hour of work on the motion at the rate of $400.00 per hour plus the filing fee of $60.00). As stated in this Court’s October 28, 2025 order as to Plaintiff’s motion to compel discovery responses: “Plaintiff’s counsel alleges an hourly rate of $800 an hour with no justification other than that unspecified courts have recognized the rate before . . . [t]he Court credits a more reasonable rate of $400.” (10/28/25 Minute Order at p. 2.)

 

            Monetary sanctions in the sum of $460.00 are to be paid by Defendant and her counsel of record, Jacob Mojarro, jointly and severally to Plaintiff within 30 days of the date of this order.

 

Conclusion:

 

The Court GRANTS IN PART Plaintiff’s motion to compel responses to set one of Plaintiff’s form interrogatories and request for monetary sanctions.

 

            The Court DENIES the request of Plaintiff to compel Defendant’s responses to set one of Plaintiff’s form interrogatories.

 

            The Court GRANTS IN PART the request for monetary sanctions and award Plaintiff reasonable monetary sanctions in the sum of $460.00. The Court orders that Defendant and her counsel of record pay monetary sanctions in the sum of $460.00, jointly and severally, to Plaintiff within 30 days of the date of this order.

 


MOTION TO DISQUALIFY CHAD BIGGINS AS COUNSEL FOR PLAINTIFFS

 

 

Relief Requested:

 

            The Court enter an order disqualifying Chad Biggins and any associated firm from further appearing as counsel for Plaintiffs in this action, including all post-judgment enforcement, levy, renewal, and collection proceedings.    

 

Summary of Action:

 

This action arises from an alleged nuisance at a property located at 1203 E. Cesar E. Chavez Ave., Los Angeles, CA 90033.  

 

On August 17, 2020, plaintiff Progress Place filed a complaint against defendants Eva Cepeida (“Defendant”) and DOES 1-100, alleging causes of action for (1) Trespass and (2) Nuisance.

 

On December 29, 2020, plaintiffs Progress Place, Dora Gonzalez, Maria Lopez, Jose Rivera, Elizabeth Rivera, Rosa Rivera, Jeanette Rivera, and Angelica individually and on behalf of all others similarly situated filed a First Amended Complaint against Defendants alleging causes of action for (1) Trespass; (2) Public Nuisance; and (3) Private Nuisance.

 

On April 5, 2021, after hearing, the Court sustained the demurrer of Defendant to the First Amended Complaint with leave to amend.

 

On April 14, 2021, plaintiffs Dora Gonzalez, Maria Lopez, Jose Rivera, Elizabeth Rivera, Rosa Rivera, Jeanette Rivera, and Angelica Rivera filed the operative Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) against Defendant, Nick Joseph Estrada aka Nico Estrada Molina (“Estrada”), and DOES 2-100, alleging causes of action for (1) Trespass; (2) Public Nuisance; and (3) Private Nuisance.

 

The operative Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) alleges, inter alia, that defendant Estrada is unlawfully residing at 1203 E. Cesar Chavez Ave., Los Angeles, CA 90033 (the “Offending Property”) in a fifth-wheel trailer without a permit. (SAC, ¶ 8.) Defendant is the owner of the Offending Property and is knowingly allowing defendant Estrada to unlawfully reside at the Offending Property. (SAC, ¶ 9.) Defendants are alleged to have neglected the Offending Property “by . . . failing to clean up trash and debris, which has been trespassing onto Plaintiffs’ property on Progress Place.” (SAC, ¶ 10.)

 

On August 2, 2021, at the request of Plaintiffs, defendant Estrada was dismissed from this action without prejudice. (08/02/21 Request for Dismissal at p. 1.)

 

On November 3, 2021, after hearing, the Court sustained the demurrer of Defendant to the first cause of action in the SAC and overruled the demurrer of Defendant to the second and third causes of action in the SAC. (See 11/03/21 Minute Order.)

 

On September 12, 2022, Defendant filed an answer to the SAC. Also, on such date, non-jury trial in this action commenced.

 

On November 29, 2022, after hearing testimony and the presentation of evidence, the Court took the non-jury trial under submission.

 

On December 7, 2022, the Court issued a tentative decision entering judgment against Defendant “and in favor of plaintiffs Jose Rivera, Rosa Rivera, Angelica Rivera and Elizabeth Rivera in the total amount of $38,300.00.” (12/07/22 Minute Order at p. 1.)

 

On January 5, 2023, judgment was entered in favor of plaintiffs Jose Rivera, Rosa Rivera, Angelica Rivera, and Elizabeth Rivera in the sum of $38,300.00. (See 01/05/23 Judgment.)

 

On March 13, 2023, Rosa Herrera filed an appeal of the Court’s judgment.

 

On April 4, 2023, an amended notice of appeal was filed, which corrected the name of appellant Rosa Herrera to that of Defendant.

 

On April 24, 2023, Defendant filed a second amended notice of appeal.

 

On September 15, 2023, remittitur was issued and the March 13, 2023 appeal was dismissed. The remittitur indicates, inter alia, that “Respondent to recover costs on appeal.” (Remittitur Filed September 15, 2023 at p. 1.)

 

On May 27, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to disqualify Chad Biggins as counsel for Plaintiffs.

 

On June 10, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an opposition.

 

On June 17, 2026, Defendant replied.

 

Analysis

 

            Defendant moves to disqualify Plaintiffs’ counsel.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

“A motion to disqualify a party’s counsel may implicate several important interests.” (People ex rel. dept. of Corporations v. SpeeDee Oil Change Systems, Inc. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1135, 1144 (“SpeeDee”).) “[J]udges must examine these motions carefully to ensure that literalism does not deny the parties substantial justice.” (Ibid.) “Depending on the circumstances, a disqualification motion may involve such considerations as a client’s right to chosen counsel, an attorney’s interest in representing a client, the financial burden on a client to replace disqualified counsel, and the possibility that tactical abuse underlies the disqualification motion.” (Id. at p. 1145.) “A trial court’s authority to disqualify an attorney derives from the power inherent in every court [t]o control in furtherance of justice, the conduct of its ministerial officers, and of all other persons in any manner connected with a judicial proceeding before it, in every matter pertaining thereto.” (Ibid. [internal quotations omitted].) “Ultimately, disqualification motions involve a conflict between the clients’ right to counsel of their choice and the need to maintain ethical standards of professional responsibility.” (Ibid.) “The important right to counsel of one’s choice must yield to ethical considerations that affect the fundamental principles of our judicial process.” (Ibid.)

 

“A lawyer shall not, without informed written consent[] from each affected client and compliance with paragraph (d), represent a client if there is a significant risk the lawyer’s representation of the client will be materially limited by the lawyer’s responsibilities to or relationships with another client, a former client or a third person,[] or by the lawyer’s own interests.” (Prof. Conduct, Rule 1.7(b) [italics added].)

 

As stated in Rule of Professional Conduct, Rule 1.7(d):

 

“(d) Representation is permitted under this rule only if the lawyer complies with paragraphs (a), (b), and (c), and:

 

(1) the lawyer reasonably believes[] that the lawyer will be able to provide competent and diligent representation to each affected client;

 

(2) the representation is not prohibited by law; and

 

(3) the representation does not involve the assertion of a claim by one client against another client represented by the lawyer in the same litigation or other proceeding before a tribunal.”

 

(Prof. Conduct, Rule 1.7 (d).)

 

Rule of Professional Conduct, Rule 1.8.1 states as follows:

 

“A lawyer shall not enter into a business transaction with a client, or knowingly[] acquire an ownership, possessory, security or other pecuniary interest adverse to a client, unless each of the following requirements has been satisfied:

 

(a) the transaction or acquisition and its terms are fair and reasonable* to the client and the terms and the lawyer’s rule in the transaction or acquisition are fully disclosed and transmitted in writing* to the client in a manner that should reasonably* have been understood by the client;

 

(b) the client is either represented in the transaction or acquisition by an independent lawyer of the client’s choice or the client is advised in writing* to seek the advice of an independent lawyer of the client’s choice and is given a reasonable* opportunity to seek that advice; and

 

(c) the client thereafter provides informed written consent* to the terms of the transaction or acquisition and the lawyer’s role in it.”

 

(Prof. Conduct, Rule 1.8.1.)

 

B.     Evidence in Support of the Motion

 

In support of the motion, Defendant declares, inter alia, that since approximately 2025,

she has been engaged in settlement negotiations in an effort to satisfy the judgment in this case. (Declaration of Eva Cepeida Filed May 27, 2026 (“Cepeida Decl.”), ¶ 3.) Defendant declares that Mr. Biggins has increased the calculations for the total outstanding balance. (Id.) “Biggins refused to negotiate any fixed payment amounts or even a compromise for a full payoff before ever speaking to the Plaintiffs. Biggins negotiated as though any settlement payment would go directly to him, not the named Plaintiffs.” (Id.) Due to the difficulty negotiating with Mr. Biggins, Defendant “had a direct conversation with [p]laintiff Jose Rivera to discuss settlement of the judgment. During that conversation, Rivera directly told [Defendant] that the ‘attorney bought us out’ and he could not negotiate a settlement because the judgment ‘belongs to him’ and that Biggins ‘owns everything.’” (Id., ¶ 4.) Defendant “understood Rivera to mean that Biggins had purchased the judgment from Rivera and the other Plaintiffs and that Plaintiffs no longer had any authority over the judgment or its enforcement.” (Id.) Defendant indicates that she “then asked Rivera how much Biggins had paid the Plaintiffs to acquire the judgment. Rivera did not answer. I then asked Rivera whether Biggins had paid Plaintiffs somewhere between $15,000 and $20,000 for the judgment. Rivera responded by nodding his head, which [Defendant] understood as an affirmative response to [her] question.” (Id., ¶ 5.)

 

            Counsel for Defendant, Jacob Mojarro (“Mojarro”), also provides a declaration in support of the motion. Counsel states, inter alia, that “[b]ased on my review of the filings, correspondence, and conduct of Biggins in this litigation, it is my belief that Biggins has purchased or been assigned the judgment from the original Plaintiffs . . . and is now acting as the de facto judgment creditor.” (Declaration of Jacob Mojarro Filed May 27, 2026 (“Mojarro Decl.”), ¶ 2.) Biggins did not respond to the question of Mr. Mojarro during an April 2026 telephone discussion as to the ownership of the judgment; rather, Mr. Biggins stated that ownership of the judgment was not relevant. (Id., ¶ 3.) Mr. Biggins became defensive and defiant when Attorney Mojarro informed Mr. Biggins about the ethical implications of “his acquisition of a personal financial interest in the outcome of this litigation . . . .” (Id., ¶ 4.)

 

            In her declaration in support of the reply, Defendant declares, inter alia, that her attorney never coached or encouraged her to speak to plaintiff Jose Rivera. (Declaration of Eva Cepeida Filed June 17, 2026 (“Cepeida Reply Decl.”), ¶ 4.) Plaintiff Jose Rivera stated that Plaintiffs did not own the judgment and that Attorney Biggins bought Plaintiffs out. (Id., ¶ 6.) When asked how much Mr. Biggins paid for the judgment, plaintiff Jose Rivera indicated that he did not know because his daughters “took care of it.” (Id., ¶ 7.) Defendant attests that “Mr. Rivera made it perfectly clear that the Plaintiffs no longer own the judgment and that Chad Biggins had bought them out.” (Id., ¶ 9.)

 

C.     Evidence in Support of the Opposition

 

In support of the opposition, plaintiff Jose Rivera provides a declaration. Plaintiff Rivera

declares, inter alia, that the statements in the declaration of Defendant in support of the motion are false concerning that Plaintiffs were bought out by their counsel. (Declaration of Jose Rivera Filed June 10, 2026 (“Rivera Decl.”), ¶ 3.) Plaintiff Rivera states that he denies “that [he] ever told Cepeida that Biggins purchased the judgment or paid any amount to acquire it.” (Id., ¶ 4.) Plaintiff Rivera states that he “said words to the effect of ‘talk to Chad.’ I was not making any disclosure about the financial arrangement between me and my attorney. I was telling Cepeida to stop trying to talk to me about the case and call my lawyer. That is it.” (Id., ¶ 4.)

 

            Attorney Biggins states, inter alia, that during a telephone call in April 2026, which was during active settlement negotiations, “Mojarro raised the judgment-purchase theory as a specific accusation and explicitly threatened to file a State Bar complaint against [Biggins] unless [he] agreed to settlement terms favorable to his client.” (Declaration of Chad Biggins Filed June 10, 2026 (“Biggins Decl.”), ¶ 3.) Attorney Biggins “told Mojarro [his] fee arrangement was confidential and not relevant to [their] negotiations.” (Id.) Counsel attests that it was Attorney Mojarro who was being difficult during settlement negotiations. (Id., ¶¶ 4-5; Exh. 2.)

 

D.    Disqualification is Not Currently Warranted

 

Initially, the Court finds that Plaintiffs’ reliance on Great Lakes Construction, Inc. v.

Burman (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 1347 (“Great Lakes”) is proper. Great Lakes states the principle that “before the disqualification of an attorney is proper, the complaining party must have or must have had an attorney-client relationship with that attorney.” (Great Lakes, supra, 186 Cal.App.4th 1347, 1356.) Although Great Lakes arose in the context of joint representation and did not involve the alleged purchase of a client’s right to a judgment, the Court finds that standing is required to disqualify an attorney. (Great Lakes, supra, 186 Cal.App.4th 1347, 1350.)

 

            Defendant relies on SpeeDee, supra, 20 Cal.4th 1135 for the contention that the Court has inherent authority to control the conduct of its officers. (Memo. of Ps and As at p. 5:26-6:3.) However, as stated by the Great Lakes court, “a highly speculative and tactical interest does not meet the standing requirements.” (Great Lakes, supra, 186 Cal.App.4th 1347, 1359.) “[A] moving party must have standing, that is, an invasion of a legally cognizable interest, to disqualify an attorney.” (Id. at p. 1357.)

 

            Defendant’s reliance on Bauguess v. Paine (1978) 22 Cal.3d 626 and Limon v. Circle K. Stores Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 671 are also inapposite. Defendant has not presented a factually analogous case citation that addresses the present issue before this Court. “A case is not authority for a proposition not considered therein or an issue not presented by its own particular facts.” (McConnell v. Advantest America, Inc. (2023) 92 Cal.App.5th 596, 611.)

 

            The Court finds that Plaintiffs’ reliance on Code Civ. Proc. § 368.5 is inapposite to support the proposition that, even if Attorney Biggins owned the judgment at issue, such Code of Civil Procedure section expressly provides for transfer of the interest. (Opp’n at pp. 9-10.) Code Civ. Proc. § 368.5 does not address the issue of purchase of a client’s interest in a judgment by their attorney.

 

            Additionally, Plaintiffs’ citation to Fletcher v. Davis (2004) 33 Cal.4th 61 (“Fletcher”) is inapposite as such case did not address disqualification. Rather, Fletcher addressed whether an attorney must obtain a client’s consent in writing when attempting to secure payment of fees and costs by obtaining a charging lien against a client’s future recovery. (Fletcher, supra, 33 Cal.4th 61, 64.)

 

            Relevantly, the Court finds that Defendant is relying on speculation to warrant disqualification of Attorney Biggins. The Court references its recitation of the parties’ evidence from above and incorporates such recitation herein. There is a conflict in the evidence as to whether Attorney Biggins purchased the rights to the judgment from his clients. The declarations of Defendant and the declaration of her counsel are rooted in speculation as plaintiff Jose Rivera indicates that the statements of Defendant are false. (Rivera Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) Thus, Defendant has not shown standing.

 

Critically, Defendant is not a client of Mr. Biggins. Further, Defendant has not presented any admissible evidence that Mr. Biggins violated Rule of Professional Conduct, Rules 1.7 or 1.8.1. Mr. Biggins alludes to the instant motion being a retaliatory tactic for not agreeing to Mr. Mojarro’s settlement terms. (Biggins Decl., ¶ 3.) Further, none of the exhibits attached to either the opposition declaration of Mr. Biggins or the reply brief indicate that Mr. Biggins purchased the judgment from Plaintiffs.

 

Conclusion:

 

The Court  DENIES Defendant’s motion to disqualify Plaintiffs’ counsel.  




MOTION FOR ASSIGNMENT ORDER FOR RENTS PURSUANT TO CCP § 708.510 AND RESTRAINING ORDER PURSUANT TO CCP § 708.520; REQUEST FOR ACCOUNTING OF RENTS COLLECTED

 

 

Relief Requested:

 

            The Court enter an assignment order pursuant to CCP § 708.510 requiring Defendant to assign to Plaintiffs all rental income, rents, and other payments due or to become due from any tenant or occupant of the real property located at 1203 E. Cesar Chavez Avenue, Los Angeles, CA 90033 (APM 5714-011-028) (the “Property”), in an amount sufficient to satisfy the outstanding money judgment, until the judgment is paid in full; a restraining order pursuant to CCP § 708.520 prohibiting Defendant from assigning, transferring, dissipating, encumbering, or otherwise disposing of any rental income or right to payment from the Property pending further order of the Court; and an order requiring Defendant to provide a full accounting of all rents and other payments received from any tenant or occupant of the Property from the date of the judgment through the present, including the identity of all tenants, the amounts paid, and the dates received.

 

Analysis

 

 

A.    Evidentiary Objections

 

Plaintiffs object to the declarations of Defendant Eva Cepeida and Attorney Jacob

Mojarro submitted in support of the opposition. The evidentiary objections are not numbered. Moreover, Plaintiffs have only cited to the paragraphs sought to be stricken in the declarations and have not cited to the relevant page and line numbers.

 

            The Court OVERRULES Plaintiffs’ evidentiary objections to paragraph 3 of the declaration of defendant Eva Cepeida.

 

            The Court SUSTAINS Plaintiffs’ evidentiary objections to paragraph 4 of defendant Eva Cepeida’s declaration on the grounds of inadmissible compromise negotiation evidence (Evid. Code § 1152); paragraph 5 on the grounds of hearsay (Evid. Code § 1200); paragraph 8 on the grounds of speculation (Evid. Code §§ 702, 800); and paragraph 9 on the grounds of relevance (Evid. Code § 350).

 

            As to the declaration of Attorney Jacob Mojarro, the Court SUSTAINS Plaintiffs’ evidentiary objections to paragraph 2 therein on the grounds of speculation (Evid. Code §§ 702, 800) and relevance (Evid. Code § 350); paragraphs 3 and 4 on the grounds of inadmissible compromise negotiation evidence (Evid. Code § 1152); paragraph 5 on the grounds of hearsay (Evid. Code § 1200); and paragraph 7 on the grounds of relevance (Evid. Code § 350).

 

B.     Legal Standard

 

“Except as otherwise provided by law, upon application of the judgment creditor on noticed motion, the court may order the judgment debtor to assign to the judgment creditor or to a receiver . . . all of part of a right to payment due or to become due, whether or not the right is conditioned on future developments, including but not limited to . . . (2) Rents.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.510, subd. (a)(2).) “The notice of the motion shall be served on the judgment debtor. Service shall be made personally or by mail.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.510, subd. (b).)

 

Code Civ. Proc. § 708.510(c) states as follows:

 

“(c) Subject to subdivisions (d), (e), and (f), in determining whether to order an assignment or the amount of an assignment pursuant to subdivision (a), the court may take into consideration all relevant factors, including the following:

 

(1) The reasonable requirements of a judgment debtor who is a natural person and of persons supported in whole or in part by the judgment debtor.

 

(2) Payments the judgment debtor is required to make or that are deducted in satisfaction of other judgments and wage assignments, including earnings assignment orders for support.

 

(3) The amount remaining due on the money judgment.

 

(4) The amount being or to be received in satisfaction of the right to payment that may be assigned.”

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 708.510, subd. (c)(1)-(4).)

 

“A right to payment may be assigned pursuant to this article only to the extent necessary

to satisfy the money judgment.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.510, subd. (d).)

 

            “When an application is made pursuant to Section 708.510 or thereafter the judgment creditor may apply to the court for an order restraining the judgment debtor from assigning or otherwise disposing of the right to payment that is sought to be assigned. The application shall be made on noticed motion if the court so directs or a court rule so requires. Otherwise, it may be made ex parte.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.520, subd. (a).) “The court may issue an order pursuant to this section upon a showing of need for the order. The court, in its discretion, may require the judgment creditor to provide an undertaking.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.520, subd. (b).) “The court may modify or vacate the order at any time with or without a hearing on such terms as are just.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.520, subd. (c).) “The order shall be personally served upon the judgment debtor and shall contain a notice to the judgment debtor that failure to comply with the order may subject the judgment debtor to being held in contempt of court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.520, subd. (d).)

 

            “[A]n assignee of a right to payment pursuant to this article shall be deemed to be a bona fide assignee for value under terms of Section 955.1 of the Civil Code.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.530, subd. (a).) “The rights of an obligor are not affected by an order assigning the right to payment until notice of the order is received by the obligor.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 708.540.) An “obligor means the person who is obligated to make payments to the judgement debtor or who may become obligated to make payments to the judgment debtor depending upon future developments.” (Id.)

 

 

 

C.     Evidence in Support of the Motion

 

In support of the motion, Chad Biggins (“Biggins”) provides a declaration. Attorney

Biggins declares, inter alia, that Plaintiffs hold a money judgment against Defendant entered in this action on or about January 5, 2023. (Biggins Decl., ¶ 2.) Post-judgment interest continues to accrue, and additional costs have been incurred in connection with enforcement. (Id.) The judgment remains unsatisfied. (Id., ¶ 3.) Defendant has not made any voluntary payments and has not complied with the Court’s October 28, 2025 order compelling post-judgment discovery responses and imposing sanctions in the sum of $660.00. (Id.)

 

The Property is a commercial property and “[t]his is the same property that is the subject of the post-judgment discovery requests and the concurrent motion for sanctions.” (Id., ¶ 4.) Counsel attests that he has learned from multiple sources that a tenant is currently occupying the Property and paying rent to Defendant. (Id., ¶ 5.) However, counsel does not know the precise amount of rent being collected. (Id., ¶ 6.) Counsel indicates that the outstanding judgment balance is estimated to be over $60,000.00 in total, and the “rental income from the Property should be assigned to Plaintiff until the judgment is fully satisfied.” (Id., ¶ 7.)

 

D.    Evidence in Support of the Opposition

 

In opposition to the Motion, Defendant declares, inter alia, that the Property generates

rental income, which she uses to pay the mortgage and deed-of-trust obligations on the property, property taxes, insurance premiums, and necessary maintenance and repair costs. (Declaration of Eva Cepeida Filed May 22, 2026 (“Cepeida Decl.”), ¶ 3.) If the rental income is assigned to Plaintiffs, Defendant “would be unable to meet these obligations and would face the risk of default, foreclosure, and loss of the property.” (Id.) All outstanding discovery responses have been provided. (Id., ¶ 5.) As to the two deeds of trust that Attorney Biggins has sought to void by way of issue sanctions, the recorded documents are held by the entities that handled the relevant transactions. (Id., ¶ 6.) Defendant declares that she has not refused to satisfy the judgment. (Id., ¶ 7.) Rather, “there are ongoing disputes about the correct amount of the judgment balance, including disputes about post-judgment costs and fees claimed by Biggins.” (Id., ¶ 7.)

 

            In her supplemental declaration, Defendant states that the rents she has collected from the Property have been her only source of income for over ten years apart from a “portion of [her] ex-husband’s pension at approximately $2000 a month and [her] social security check at $803 per month.” (Supplemental Declaration of Eva Cepeida Filed June 12, 2026 (“Cepeida Suppl. Decl.”), ¶ 3.) As for her residential property, Defendant pays $1,400.00 per month for a mortgage contribution; spends approximately $375.00 on utilities and phone service; and spends around $1,000.00 per month combined for food and personal expenses. (Id.) Currently, the Property is vacant and there is no tenant. (Id., ¶¶ 4-6; Exhs. 1-2.) The current fair market rental value for the Property is $3,500.00. (Id., ¶ 4.) Defendant states that if the entire rental income from the Property is diverted to the judgment creditor, she “would face personal hardship, the risk of default and/or forfeiture of [her] real property interest.” (Id., ¶ 8.) Defendant states that she has provided responses to all outstanding discovery. (Id., ¶ 12.) Defendant states that she is “still willing and able to make payments on the judgment in the amount of $500 a month with an increase [to] $1000 a month upon the . . . Property being rented.” (Id., ¶ 14.)

 

            Attorney Mojarro states that on or about April 27, 2026, a check was issued to Attorney Biggins in the sum of $600.00 in satisfaction of the prior sanctions order entered in this case. (Mojarro Decl., ¶ 6.)

 

E.     The Requested Relief is Unwarranted at This Time

 

Initially, as to the issue of standing, the Court finds that such issue has been separately

addressed in connection with the motion to disqualify. The issue of standing is not relevant to this Court’s assessment of whether an assignment order should be entered. Moreover, Defendant has not presented sufficient admissible evidence that Attorney Biggins in fact owns the judgment at issue. 

 

            The Court references its recitation of the parties’ respective evidence from above and incorporates such recitation herein. The Court recognizes that the judgment is not satisfied, which is a fact that Defendant does not dispute. However, the entire premise of the Motion—that Defendant is currently receiving rent—has been disproven via the supplemental declaration of Defendant. Moreover, Attorney Biggins has attested to not knowing the precise rent amount collected. (Biggins Decl., ¶ 6.) The Court cannot order an assignment of rents at an accurate amount to satisfy the judgment if the moving party cannot ascertain the amount of rent currently being received. Relevantly, based on CCP § 708.510(c), the Court finds that an assignment order and the related relief requested via the Motion are not currently warranted. Defendant states that she would face personal hardship and have the “risk of default, or foreclosure and/or forfeiture of [her] real property interest” if the assignment order is instituted. (Cepeida Suppl. Decl., ¶ 8.) Also, Defendant is not attempting to avoid payment of the judgment. (Id., ¶ 13.)

 

Conclusion:

 

The Court DENIES Plaintiffs’ motion for assignment order, restraining order, and request for accounting of rents collected.

Analysis

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Vida Loca Entertainment LLC v. Moe Mostashari, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 25STCV12454, set for hearing on November 24, 2025. Plaintiff Giovani Vetironi LLC dba Vida Loca Entertainment (Plaintiff) moves to compel Defendants Moe Mostashari (Mostashari) and W.L.A. Flame International Inc. (Flame) to serve further responses to Plaintiff’s discovery requests. The requests to compel further responses are granted in part and denied in part as stated in this ruling. Plaintiff’s request for sanctions are denied given the Plaintiff’s failure to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or videoconference regarding each issue raised in the motions as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040.

 

A.    Meet and Confer Requirement

 

Before bringing a motion to compel further responses to a discovery request, the moving party must make efforts to meet and confer in good faith and submit a declaration attesting to those efforts. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.310, subd. (b)(2), 2030.300, subd. (b), 2033.290 subd. (b).) “A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Id., § 2016.040.) A discovery motion need not be denied automatically because the moving parties failed to meet and confer in good faith.¿(See Obregon v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 424, 434.) 

 

Plaintiff’s counsel testifies that on March 2, 2026, he emailed defense counsel “a detailed five-page meet-and-confer letter identifying the deficiencies in all six sets of responses and citing Code of Civil Procedure sections 2030.300(b), 2031.310(b), and 2033.290(b).” (Declarations of Alishan A. Jadhavji, filed May 14, 2026 (“Jadhavji Decl.”) ¶ 3; Exh. A – a copy of the meet and confer letter.) Thereafter, the parties’ counsel agreed in writing that defendants will serve supplemental responses by May 6, 2026, and Plaintiff’s deadline to file motions to compel further responses will be May 22, 2026. (Id. ¶ 4.) However, defendants had not served supplemental responses or produced responsive documents by their deadline of May 6, 2026, and before Plaintiff filed the instant motions on May 14, 2026. (Id. ¶ 5.) Therefore, Plaintiff filed the instant motions. (Id. ¶ 6.)

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff’s meet and confer efforts were insufficient for the following reasons. First, Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040 (“Section 2016.040”) now requires the meet and confer conference to take place in person, by telephone, or by video conference. Here, counsel only sent a letter. Second, the statute required Plaintiff to attempt to resolve “each issue” presented in the instant motions. With the exception of the motions to compel Flame’s further responses to special interrogatories and requests for production, the Court finds Plaintiff’s counsel’s meet and confer letter did not address all issues raised in the other motions. Plaintiff argues in reply papers that defense counsel never claimed not to understand which discovery requests were at issue. However, the burden is on the moving party, not the opposing party, to specify the issues that will be raised in the motion and attempt to informally resolve them with the responding party through the meet and confer process. That was not done for all motions.

 

An insufficient meet and confer conference is not grounds for denying the motion. Therefore, the Court will proceed on the merits as to Plaintiff’s request to compel further responses. However, the Court denies Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions due to the failure to meet and confer as required by Section 2016.040. The Court finds all other procedural arguments raised by defendants unpersuasive.

 

B.     Legal Standard

 

A party may move for an order compelling further responses to discovery if the party deems the responses evasive, incomplete, or contain objections that are without merit. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2033.290, subd. (a); 2030.300, subd. (a); 2031.310, subd. (a).)

 

A motion to compel further responses to requests for production “shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the inspection demand.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) If the moving party has shown good cause for the requests for production, the burden is on the objecting party to justify the objections. (Kirkland v. Sup.Ct (2002) 95 Cal. App.4th 92, 98.) 

 

A trial court retains the authority to hear a motion to compel further responses even if the responding party serves supplemental responses after the motion has been filed and before the hearing. (See e.g., Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 408–409 [“If a party provides an untimely interrogatory response that does not contain objections and that sets forth legally valid responses to each interrogatory, the untimely response might completely or substantially resolve the issues raised by a motion to compel responses under  section 2030.290. Even in such cases, however, the trial court retains the authority to hear the motion. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1030(a) [‘The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though ... the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed’].) Whether a particular response does resolve satisfactorily the issues raised by a motion is a matter best determined by the trial court in the exercise of its discretion, based on the circumstances of the case. In many cases involving untimely responses, the propounding party will take the motion off calendar or narrow its scope to the issue of sanctions. If the propounding party proceeds with the motion, however, the trial court has the discretion to rule on the motion. The trial court might compel responses without objection if it finds no legally valid responses have been provided to one or more interrogatories; it might deny the motion to compel responses as essentially unnecessary, in whole or in part, and just impose sanctions; it might treat the motion as one under section 2030.300 and either determine that further answers are required,14 or order the propounding party to ‘meet and confer’ (§ 2030.300, subd. (b)) and file a separate statement (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1020(a)(2), (c)); or it might take the motion off calendar, thereby requiring the propounding party to file a motion under section 2030.300”].)

 

C.    Motion to Compel Mostashari to Serve Further Responses to Special Interrogatories

 

Plaintiff’s motion initially moved for an order compelling Mostashari to serve further responses to Special Interrogatories (Set One) (“M-SROG”) Nos. 2, 4, 5, 7, 13, 14, 15, 16, 18, and 20. (Notice of M-SROG Motion, p. 2:6-9.) Mostashari served supplemental responses on June 10, 2026 (the “June 10 Responses”). (Opposition to M-SROG Motion, p. 4:18-19; Declaration of defense counsel Negar Tehrani, ¶ 5; Exh. A – a copy of the supplemental responses.) Plaintiff’s reply argues that the June 10 Responses to Nos. 2, 3, 7, 9, 11, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 and 20 are not code-compliant, and, therefore, the Court should enter an order compelling further responses to those M-SROGs.

 

The request to compel Mostashari’s further responses to M-SROG Nos. 3, 4, 5, 9, 11, 17, and 19 are denied.

 

Plaintiff’s motion did not initially move to compel further responses to M-SROG Nos. 3, 9, 11, 17, and 19. In addition, Plaintiff has not argued that the June 10 Responses for M-SROG Nos. 4 and 5 are insufficient. 

 

Therefore, the request to compel further responses to Nos. 3, 4, 5, 9, 11, 17, and 19 is denied.

 

Mostashari’s responses to M-SROG Nos. 2 and 15 were not code compliant.

 

No. 2 asked the defendant to state whether he sent a certain text message, while No. 15 asked him to state whether he authorized the issuance of IRS Form 1099-NEC to “Giovani Vetironi LLC.” In the June 10 Responses, the defendant claims that he lacks information to either admit or deny those interrogatories.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.220 requires: “(a) Each answer in a response to interrogatories shall be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits. [¶] (b) If an interrogatory cannot be answered completely, it shall be answered to the extent possible. [¶] (c) If the responding party does not have personal knowledge sufficient to respond fully to an interrogatory, that party shall so state, but shall make a reasonable and good faith effort to obtain the information by inquiry to other natural persons or organizations, except where the information is equally available to the propounding party.”

 

Mostashari’s response that he lacked information to answer M-SROG Nos. 2 and 15 was not accompanied by a statement that he made reasonable and good faith efforts to obtain the information requested in violation of the statute above.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further responses to Nos. 2 and 15 is granted.

 

The information requested by M-SROG No. 16 is not discoverable.

 

No. 16 asked the defendant to “identify” all bank accounts owned or controlled by him or Flame from which payments were made in connection with the underlying contract. The defendant objected in his June 10 Responses, claiming that the interrogatory seeks confidential financial and banking information, is overbroad, and is invasive of privacy.

 

            The Court finds that the information requested by No. 16 is not discoverable as it is unclear what Plaintiff is requesting. The M-SROGs defined the word “identify” only in terms of people and documents. (Jadhavji Decl., Exh. C – a copy of the M-SROGs, p. 3:4-8 [“‘IDENTIFY’ when used with respect to a PERSON, means to state the full name, last known address, email address, all known telephone numbers, and employment position. When used with respect to a DOCUMENT, means to state the document’s author(s), recipient(s), date, title or description, and the name and address of the present custodian”]; see also id. at p. 2:16-18[“‘PERSON’ means any natural person, firm, association, organization, partnership, business, trust, limited liability company, corporation, or public entity”]; id. at p. 2:19-27 [“‘DOCUMENT’ means a writing, as defined in California Evidence Code § 250, and includes the original or copy of any hand-written, typed, printed, graphic, symbolic, photographic or otherwise recorded matter of any kind. This definition includes, but is not limited to: correspondence; letters; memoranda; notes; diary, journal, notebook, calendar and appointment book entries; records and transcripts of conversations and conferences, including telephone conversations; reports; contracts and agreements; invoices; financial statements; ledgers; tax returns; electronic mail; text messages; photographs; videotapes; and electronic data of any kind”].) A bank account is neither a document nor a person as defined by Plaintiff.

 

            Therefore, the request to compel further response to M-SROG No. 16 is denied.

 

Mostashari’s response to M-SROG No. 18 is not straightforward.

 

No. 18 asked the defendant: “IDENTIFY all licenses, permits, or regulatory approvals YOU personally held or applied for in connection with the operation of W.L.A. FLAME INTERNATIONAL INC.’s establishment.” (June 10 Responses, p. 8:14:16.) Mostashari objected to the “interrogatory on the grounds it is vague as to time and overbroad as to whether it is in reference to the venture with Plaintiff or otherwise. Without waiving the foregoing objection, [he] contend[ed] that all permits and licenses required for the venture between Plaintiff and Defendants was Plaintiff’s obligation to maintain.” (Id. at p. 8:17-21.)

 

The Court agrees with the defendant that the interrogatory is overly broad because it is not limited to licenses, permits, or regulatory approvals relevant to the alleged venture between Plaintiff and the defendants. 

 

Nevertheless, as stated above, Section 2030.220(a) requires responses to an interrogatory to be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to M-SROG No. 18 is granted.

Mostashari’s response to M-SROG No. 20 is insufficient.

 

No. 20 asked the defendant to: “IDENTIFY all DOCUMENTS in YOUR possession, custody, or control relating to the negotiation, execution, performance, or termination of the CONTRACT.” The June 10 Responses stated: “Please refer to Responding Party’s document production.”

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.230 states: “If the answer to an interrogatory would necessitate the preparation or the making of a compilation, abstract, audit, or summary of or from the documents of the party to whom the interrogatory is directed, and if the burden or expense of preparing or making it would be substantially the same for the party propounding the interrogatory as for the responding party, it is a sufficient answer to that interrogatory to refer to this section and to specify the writings from which the answer may be derived or ascertained. This specification shall be in sufficient detail to permit the propounding party to locate and to identify, as readily as the responding party can, the documents from which the answer may be ascertained. The responding party shall then afford to the propounding party a reasonable opportunity to examine, audit, or inspect these documents and to make copies, compilations, abstracts, or summaries of them.”

 

Here, the defendant’s June 10 Responses failed to specify the writings from which the answer to M-SROG No. 20 may be derived or ascertained.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to M-SROG No. 20 is granted.

 

For those reasons, the motion to compel Mostashari’s further response to Plaintiff’s Special Interrogatories (Set One) is granted in part and denied in part as follows. The request to compel further responses to Nos. 2, 15, 18, and 20 is granted. The motion is otherwise denied.

 

D.    Motion to Compel Mostashari to Serve Further Responses to Requests for Admission

 

Plaintiff initially moved to compel Mostashari to serve further responses to Plaintiff’s Requests for Admission (Set One) (“M-RFA”), Nos. 6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12, 13, 15, and 16. (Notice of M-RFA Motion, p. 2:6-9.) Mostashari argues that Plaintiff’s meet and confer did not raise any issues with his responses to the M-RFAs. (Opposition to M-RFA Motion, pp. 4:21-5:18.) In reply, Plaintiff disputes the meet and confer letter failed to identify Plaintiff’s issues with the defendant’s responses and argues that further responses should be compelled.

 

Plaintiff did not meet and confer regarding M-RFA Nos. 6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 15 and 16.

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff’s meet and confer letter on March 2, 2026, only specified issues with M-RFA Nos. 12, 13, 14, and 19. (See Jadhavji M-RFA Decl., Exh. A – a copy of the letter, p. 2, Section III [“Internally Inconsistent Responses: Mostashari admits the text messages with Yasen are authentic (RFA 19) and admits authorizing payments (RFA 14), yet claims Yasen was an ‘unknown person’ (RFAs 12-13) and ‘lacks sufficient information’ about his own text message (RFA 7). These positions are irreconcilable. Additionally, WLA Flame denies the text message (RFA 4) while Mostashari authenticates those same messages (RFA 19)”].)

 

Therefore, the request to compel further responses to Nos. 6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 15, and 16 is denied due to Plaintiff’s failure to meet and confer regarding those M-RFAs. This leaves only Nos. 12 and 13 at issue.

 

The defendant’s denials of M-RFA Nos. 12 and 13 were code-compliant.

 

No. 12 asked the defendant: “Admit that YOU knew at the time of contract execution that Maftah Yasen was the managing member of Giovani Vetironi LLC.” (Jadhavji M-RFA Decl., Exh. D – a copy of the M-RFA Responses, Responses to M-RFA Nos. 13 and 14.) The defendant responded: “Denied. Responding Party contends that Maftah Yasen was an unknown person and that Responding Party believed he was conducting business with Giovani Vetironi.” (Ibid.) No. 13 asked the defendant: “Admit that all YOUR dealings under the CONTRACT were with Maftah Yasen.” (Ibid.) The defendant responded: “Denied. Responding Party contends that Maftah Yasen was an unknown person and that Responding Party believed he was conducting business with Giovani Vetironi.” (Ibid.)

 

The Court finds the defendant’s denials were code compliant. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.220, subd. (b) [“Each answer shall: [¶] (1) Admit so much of the matter involved in the request as is true, either as expressed in the request itself or as reasonably and clearly qualified by the responding party. [¶] (2) Deny so much of the matter involved in the request as is untrue. [¶] (3) Specify so much of the matter involved in the request as to the truth of which the responding party lacks sufficient information or knowledge”].) The fact that his denials may contradict another party’s responses or his own responses does not make his definitive denials to Nos. 12 and 13 not code-compliant.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further responses to M-RFA Nos. 13 and 14 is denied.

 

For the reasons set forth above, the motion to compel Mostashari’s further responses to Plaintiff’s Requests for Admission (Set One) is denied in its entirety.

 

E.     Motion to Compel Flame to Serve Further Requests for Admission

 

Plaintiff initially moved to compel Flame to serve further responses to Plaintiff’s Requests for Admission (Set One) (“F-RFA”), Nos. 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 17, 21, 22, 25, 26, and 28.

 

Flame argues that on May 28, 2026, it served verified responses to the F-RFAs. (Opposition to F-RFA Motion, p. 6:12-13.) Plaintiff has not filed a reply disputing that fact or arguing that the supplemental responses were insufficient.

 

Therefore, the motion to compel Flame’s further responses to Plaintiff’s Requests for Admission (Set One) is denied in its entirety.

 

F.     Motion to Compel Flame to Serve Further Responses to Special Interrogatories

 

Plaintiff initially moved to compel Flame to serve further responses to Special Interrogatories (Set One) (“F-SROG”), Nos. 1 through 25. (Notice of F-SROG Motion, p. 2:6-9.) Flame served supplemental responses to the F-SROGs on May 29, 2026. (Opposition to F-SROG Motion, p. 6:12-14; Tehrani Decl., ¶ 9; Exh. A – a copy of the supplemental responses.) In reply, Plaintiff argues that the supplemental responses to F-SROG Nos. 3, 9, 10, 16, 18, 21, 22, 23, 24, and 25 remain deficient. (Reply, p. 7:13-17.)

 

Flame’s response to F-SROG No. 3 is insufficient.

 

No. 3 asked Flame to state whether it received a draft contract in or around November 2023 that identified “Giovannie Vetironi LLC d/b/a Vida Loca Entertainment” as the contracting party. The defendant’s supplemental responses only state: “Moe Mostashari sent a contract to Plaintiff in November 2023.”

 

That response does not answer the question asked and, therefore, insufficient.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-SROG No. 3 is granted.

 

Flame’s response to F-SROG No. 9 is insufficient.

 

No. 8 asked the defendant to: “State the name, address, and relationship of every person to whom You issued payment under the CONTRACT.” The defendant stated in its supplemental response: “Giovani Vetironi LLC, Plaintiff.” Therefore, the F-SROG at issue (No. 9) asked: “For each payment made, state the date, amount, payee name, check number, and purpose.” Flame responded: “Responding Party provides all paystubs in its possession demonstrating payments made to Giovani Vetironi LLC. See Responding Party’s Document Production P. 00075-00084.”

 

Plaintiff argues that the response to F-SROG No. 9 is insufficient because: “[Code of Civil Procedure] Section 2030.230’s option to product writings requires ‘a specification of the writings from which the answer may be derived … in sufficient detail to permit the propounding party to locate and to identify’ them. A bare bates range is not a substantive answer.” (F-SROG Motion Reply, pp. 2:26-3:2.)

 

The Court finds Flame’s response to SROG No. 9 was not code-compliant. Section 2030.230 allows a response to an interrogatory to refer to a writing only if (1) “the answer to an interrogatory would necessitate the preparation or the making of a compilation, abstract, audit, or summary of or from the documents of the party to whom the interrogatory is directed …,” and (2) “if the burden or expense of preparing or making it would be substantially the same for the party propounding the interrogatory as for the responding party ….” Flame has not shown that was the case with regard to F-SROG No. 9. Second, the statute requires the specification of the writing to “be in sufficient detail to permit the propounding party to locate and to identify, as readily as the responding party can, the documents from which the answer may be ascertained.” Here, Flame fails to specify which writings within the documents produced answer the interrogatory’s request for the “purpose” of the payments.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-SROG No. 9 is granted.

 

Flame’s response to F-SROG No. 10 is insufficient.

 

No. 10 asked the defendant to state whether it “issued IRS Form 1099-NEC or other tax documents.” The defendant only answered that it does not recall, which is insufficient. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.220, subd. (c) [requiring that “[i]f the responding party does not have personal knowledge sufficient to respond fully to an interrogatory, that party shall so state, but shall make a reasonable and good faith effort to obtain the information by inquiry to other natural persons or organizations, except where the information is equally available to the propounding party”].)

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-SROG No. 10 is granted.

 

Flame’s response to F-SROG No. 16 is evasive.

 

No. 16 asked the defendant to identify “all permits or licenses relating to hookah or tobacco services.” The defendant responded that the “interrogatory … seek confidential regulatory records, information already in Plaintiff’s possession, custody or control, and/or information readily available to Plaintiff via third party sources.”

 

The Court finds Flame’s response evasive and that it fails to comply with Section 2030.2020 because it is unclear whether or not the defendant knows of the permits or licenses being requested in No. 16. To the extent the defendant does, the defendant is required to identify such documents. Flame’s confidentiality objection is unpersuasive; the interrogatory is not requesting the production of the permits or licenses, but only the identification of them, which is proper. (Cf. Smith v. Superior Court In and For San Joaquin County (1961) 189 Cal.App.2d 6, 12 [“It would seem if documents are discoverable …, and are not privileged, a preliminary interrogatory to ascertain the existence of such documents would be proper. … [For example, where a privilege objection is asserted] only the contents could be privileged not the fact of the document's existence”].)

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-SROG No. 16 is granted.

 

Flame’s response to F-SROG No. 18 is sufficient.

 

No. 18 asked the defendant to state whether it “operated hookah services during the term of the contract” at issue in this. The defendant responded: “Responding Party objects to this request as to validity of the contract and therefore any timeline of reference is vague and ambiguous. Subject to and without waiving the foregoing objections, Responding Party provides that Plaintiff sold hookah as part of his operations at the premises. Plaintiff was responsible to make sure his sales were permitted.”

 

The Court finds that response sufficient since Plaintiff failed to define what it meant by the “term of the contract.”

 

The request to compel further response to F-SROG No. 18 is denied.

 

Flame’s right to privacy objections to F-SROG Nos. 21 and 22 are outweighed by Plaintiff’s right to discovery.

 

Nos. 21 and 22 asked the defendant to state its gross revenue from December 1, 2023, through April 2024 (No. 21) and total revenue generated through hookah or bottle service (No. 22).

 

Plaintiff argues in reply: “Objection only on ‘confidentiality’ grounds. Flame is a corporation. California has long recognized that corporations do not possess the same constitutional privacy rights as natural persons, and any corporate confidentiality interest must be balanced against the relevance of and need for the discovery. (See Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552–557; Roberts v. Gulf Oil Corp. (1983) 147 Cal.App.3d 770, 791–795.) The information is directly relevant to damages and to Flame’s own contention that Plaintiff caused the ‘collapse’ of its hookah business (see Supp. Resp. No. 11).” (F-SROG Motion Reply, p. 3:13-20.)

 

“While corporations do have a right to privacy, it is not a constitutional right. The corporate right to privacy is a lesser right than that held by human beings and is not considered a fundamental right.” (SCC Acquisitions, Inc. v. Superior Court (2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 741, 756.) “Because the corporate privacy right is not constitutionally protected, the issue presented in determining whether [discovery requests] … infringe that right is resolved by a balancing test. The discovery's relevance to the subject matter of the pending dispute and whether the discovery ‘“appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence”’ is balanced against the corporate right of privacy.” (Ibid.)

 

The Court finds that the discovery requested by Plaintiff appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. Plaintiff’s SAC alleges that the parties entered into the contract at issue on or about December 1, 2023, in which they agreed that Plaintiff had the exclusive rights to provide hookah service and all other products within Flame’s establishment. (SAC, ¶¶ 20, 23.) The SAC further alleges that despite Plaintiff successfully generating revenue of $266,000 under the contract, the defendants attempted to prematurely and unilaterally terminate the contract in April 2024. (SAC, ¶¶ 2, 27.) “Defendants’ actions constitute[d] a clear and material breach of the Contract ….” (SAC, ¶ 29.) In turn, the defendant’s FAXC alleges, among other things, that Plaintiff was unjustly enriched as a result of the contract, and had engaged in illegal and unlicensed business operations. (FAXC, ¶¶ 71, 80.) Given those allegations, F-SROG Nos. 21 and 22’s request for gross revenue generated between December 1, 2023, through April 2024, and for hookah and bottle servicse is relevant.

 

The Court finds the defendant’s right to privacy in that information is outweighed by Plaintiff’s right to discovery of relevant information.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-SROG Nos. 21 and 22 is granted.

 

Flame’s response to F-SROG No. 23 was sufficient.

 

No. 23 asked the defendant to identify “all persons who performed promotional services other than Plaintiff.” Flame objected on the ground that the interrogatory “seeks confidential third-party business information.”

 

The Court sustains Flame’s objection, and Plaintiff’s reply does not convince the Court otherwise. (See F-SROG Motion Reply, p. 3:21-22 [arguing only that “Objection only on third-party ‘confidentiality’ grounds, again unaccompanied by any factual showing”].)

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-SROG No. 23 is denied.

 

Flame’s privileged objection to F-SROG No. 24 is sustained.

 

The SAC’s second cause of action alleges that the defendants materially breached the parties’ contract by failing to participate in contractually required mediation and arbitration process. (SAC, ¶¶ 30-32, 56.) Therefore, F-SROG No. 24 asked the defendant to state all fact supporting its refusal to participate in mediation or arbitration.

 

The defendant objected on the ground that the interrogatory seeks “legal conclusions and privileged strategy.”

 

While it is proper for an interrogatory to ask for the existence or identification of documents that are privileged or subject to other protections, it is a different matter entirely to seek the contents of those documents. (See Hernandez v. Superior Court (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 285, 293 [“In short, a responding party may object to an interrogatory that seeks privileged information by clearly stating the objection and the particular privilege invoked. But the existence of a document containing privileged information is not privileged. [Citations] Interrogatories may be used to discover the existence of documents in the other party’s possession. [Citation.] If an interrogatory asks the responding party to identify a document, an adequate response must include a description of the document. [Citation.] Thus, we agree with petitioners that a ‘privilege log’ is unnecessary with regard to answering interrogatories seeking the identification of documents” (italics in original)].) Here, FR-SROG No. 24 seeks privileged information.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-SROG No. 24 is denied.

 

Flame’s response to F-SROG No. 25 is not code-compliant.

 

No. 25 asked Flame to identify all documents or communications referring to Plaintiff as “Giovani Vetironi LLC.” Flame only responded: “Refer to Responding Party’s Document Production p. 00075-00084.”

 

The Court agrees with Plaintiff that Flame’s response to No. 25 was not code-compliant for the same reasons the Court has found the same with regard to No. 9 above.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-SROG No. 25 is granted.

 

For the reasons set forth above, the motion to compel Flame’s further responses to Plaintiff’s Special Interrogatories (Set One) is granted in part and denied in part as follows. The request is granted as to Nos. 3, 9, 10, 16, 21, 22, and 25. The motion is otherwise denied.

 

G.    Motion to Compel Flame to Serve Further Responses to Requests for Production of Documents

 

Plaintiff initially moved to compel Flame to serve further responses to Requests for Production of Documents (Set One) (“F-RPD”), Nos. 1 through 25. (Notice of F-RPD Motion, p. 2:7-9.) Flame served supplemental responses to the F-RPDs on May 29, 2026. (Opposition to F-RPD Motion, p. 7:13-14; Tehrani Decl., ¶ 9; Exh. A – a copy of the supplemental responses.) In reply, Plaintiff argues that the supplemental responses to F-RPD Nos. 5, 9, 10, 12, 13, 14, 15, 18, 19, 20, 21, and 22 remain deficient. (Reply, p. 7:13-17.)

 

F-RPD No. 5 seeks privileged tax information.

 

The request asked the defendant to produce: “All IRS Forms 1099, 1099-NEC, or other tax documents issued in connection with the CONTRACT.” Flame objected to the F-RPD as asking for “confidential tax records beyond what is relevant or proportional,” and, without waiving that objection, stated that it does not recall issuing the tax forms. In its reply, Plaintiff argues that it is insufficient for the defendant to claim that it does not recall. (F-RPD Motion Reply, p. 2:20-28.)

 

Tax returns are the subject of a statutory privilege against forced disclosure. (See Webb v. Standard Oil Co. of Cal. (1957) 49 C.2d 509; Schnabel v. Superior Court (1993) 5 C.4th 1117, 719-720 (Schnabel) (collecting cases).

 

“The privilege is not absolute… the privilege is waived or does not apply in three situations: “(1) there is an intentional relinquishment (Crest Catering Co. v. Superior Court (1965) 62 Cal.2d 274, 278), (2) the 'gravamen of [the] lawsuit is so inconsistent with the continued assertion of the taxpayer's privilege as to compel the conclusion that the privilege has in fact been waived' (Wilson v. Superior Court, supra, 63 Cal.App.3d at p. 830), or (3) a public policy greater than that of confidentiality of tax returns is involved (Miller v. Superior Court, supra, 71 Cal.App.3d at p. 149).”” (Schnabel, supra, 5 C.4th at 721.) “A trial court has broad discretion in determining the applicability of a statutory privilege.” (Weingarten v. Superior Court (2002) 102 C.A.4th 268 (Weingarten).) The third exception requires the court to balance the strength of the public policy in favor of tax return confidentiality against other public policies at play in any given case. The exception “is narrow and applies only “when warranted by a legislatively declared public policy.”” (Weingarten, supra, 102 C.A.4th at 274 (quoting Schnabel, supra, 5 C.4th at 721.)

 

Here, Plaintiff has not shown that any of the three situations above apply to the tax documents requested.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-RPD No. 5 is denied.

 

F-RPD No. 9 is overly broad.

 

The Court sustains Flame’s overbroad objection to No. 9, which requested all documents relating to any hookah services provided at its establishment from January 2023 to the present.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-RPD No. 9 is denied.

 

Flame’s response to F-RPD No. 10 is insufficient.

 

The RPD asked the defendant to produce all permits, licenses, or regulatory approvals relating to hookah or tobacco services. Flame responded: “Responding Party objects to this Request to the extent it seeks confidential regulatory materials. Subject to and without waiving this objection, Responding Party responds as follows: Plaintiff should have possession of all such documents as pertaining to the alleged contract.”

 

The Court finds Flame’s objection is unclear, and the alternative response is not code compliant with Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.210, subdivision (a), which requires a responding party to state that it either: (1) will comply with the particular demand, or (2) lacks the ability to comply with the demand.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-RPD No. 10 is granted.

 

Plaintiff has failed to show good cause justifying further response to F-RPD No. 12.

 

The request asked the defendant to produce all documents “relating” to compliance with Los Angeles Municipal Code § 46.90.1. The defendant responded that the request was vague and sought legal conclusions. In its reply, Plaintiff disputes that contention, arguing that “Flame’s own supplemental responses to Special Interrogatory No. 11 rely on LAPD/regulatory enforcement as the central justification for terminating the contract; documents reflecting that compliance are squarely relevant.” (F-RPD Motion Reply, p. 3:14-16.)

 

A motion to compel further responses to requests for production “shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the inspection demand.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) 

 

Here, Plaintiff has failed to do so as to F-RPD No. 12. The Court also finds the request vague as it is unclear what Plaintiff meant by documents “relating to compliance” with the ordinance.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-RPD No. 12 is denied.

 

Flame’s privacy objections to F-RPD Nos. 13 and 14 is overruled.

 

No. 13 asked Flame to produce: “All financial records showing gross revenue generated at YOUR establishment from December 1, 2023 through April 2024.” No. 14 asked for financial records showing revenue generated through hookah or bottle services from December 1, 2023, through April 20224. Flame responded: “Responding Party objects to this Request as overbroad, confidential, commercially sensitive, and subject to privacy rights.”

 

The Court finds the F-RPDs are not overly broad as they are limited to the period Plaintiff’s SAC alleges the contract at issue in this case was executed and breached. The Court also finds Flame’s privacy concerns are outweighed by Plaintiff’s right to discover relevant information.

 

Nevertheless, Plaintiff is not entitled to financial records that do not pertain to the services it rendered for the defendant.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-RPD Nos. 13 and 14 is granted, but limited to financial records showing revenue from the services that Plaintiff rendered under the contract at issue in this case.

 

Flame’s overbroad and privacy objections to F-RPD No. 15 are sustained.

 

F-RPD No. 15 asked Flame to produce: “All contracts or agreements with other promotional service providers from January 1, 2023 to the present.” The defendant responded: “Responding Party objects to this Request as overbroad, confidential, commercially sensitive, and subject to privacy rights.”

 

The Court sustains Flame’s overly broad and privacy objections, and finds Plaintiff has failed to show good cause for justifying production of documents responsive to No. 15.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-RPD No. 15 is denied.

 

Flame must produce a privilege log in response to F-RPD No. 18.

 

No. 18 asked Flame to produce all documents reflecting communications with its attorney or any third party concerning this lawsuit. Flame objected on the grounds of attorney-client privilege and work product doctrine.

 

The Court agrees with Plaintiff that Flame must produce a privilege log in response to No. 18. (See Catalina Island Yacht Club v. Superior Court (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1130 [“A privilege log must identify with particularity each document the responding party claims is protected from disclosure by a privilege and provide sufficient factual information for the propounding party and court to evaluate whether the claim has merit.  [Citations.]  The precise information required for an adequate privilege log will vary from case to case based on the privileges asserted and the underlying circumstances.  In general, however, a privilege log typically should provide the identity and capacity of all individuals who authored, sent, or received each allegedly privileged document, the document’s date, a brief description of the document and its contents or subject matter sufficient to determine whether the privilege applies, and the precise privilege or protection asserted”].) 

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-RPD No. 18 is granted.

 

Flame’s response to F-RPD Nos. 19 and 20 is insufficient.

 

No. 19 asked the defendant to produce: “All photographs, videos, or other media depicting hookah services from December 1, 2023 to the present.” No 20 asked for: “All marketing or promotional materials referencing hookah, Vida Loca Entertainment, or similar services.” Flame provided the following, same response to those F-RPDs: “Responding Party objects to this Request as vague and overbroad and that such documents are in Plaintiff’s possession.”

 

The Court finds the objections meritless, and agrees with Plaintiff that the defendant’s responses were not code compliant. (See Code Civ. Proc. 2031.210, subd. (a) [requiring the a response to state any of the following: “(1) A statement that the party will comply with the particular demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling by the date set for the inspection, copying, testing, or sampling pursuant to paragraph (2) of subdivision (c) of Section 2031.030 and any related activities. [¶] (2) A representation that the party lacks the ability to comply with the demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling of a particular item or category of item. [¶] (3) An objection to the particular demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling.”

 

Therefore, the request to compel further responses to F-RPD Nos. 19 and 20 is granted.

 

Flame’s response to F-RPD Nos. 21 and 22 is insufficient.

 

Nos. 21 and 22 asked Flame to produce its articles of incorporation, bylaws, operating agreements, and amendments (No. 21), and all documents identifying officers, directors, shareholders, or owners from January 1, 2023 to the present (No. 22). Plaintiff objected on the ground that the requests sought confidential ownership or governance information beyond what was relevant. Plaintiff argues that those documents “go to who actually owns and operates [Flame] – directly relevant given that the identity of the contracting parties is central to this case.” (F-RPD Motion Reply, p. 4:17-19.)

 

The Court finds Plaintiff has failed to “set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the inspection demand.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) Specifically, Plaintiff fails to cite to the allegations in the pleadings showing that the identity of the defendants is in dispute, and explain how the documents requested in the F-RPDs are relevant to prove that fact.

 

Therefore, the request to compel further response to F-RPD Nos. 21 and 22 is denied.

 

For the reasons set forth above, the motion to compel Flame’s further responses to Plaintiff’s Requests for Production of Documents (Set One) is granted in part and denied in part. The request to compel further responses to Nos. 10, 13 (as limited in this ruling), 14 (as limited in this ruling), 18, 19, and 20 is granted. The motion is otherwise denied.

Case Number: 25STCV19367    Hearing Date: June 24, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO COMPEL INITIAL DISCLOSURES PURSUANT TO CALIFORNIA CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE § 871.26

 

Relief Requested:

 

             The Court order Defendant FCA US LLC to comply with California Code of Civil Procedure § 871.26 by producing all documents required under subdivisions (h)(6), (h)(7), (h)(8), (h)(9), (h)(10), (h)(12), and (h)(13) and impose sanctions against Defendant.

 

Summary of Action:

 

            Plaintiff commenced the action on June 27, 2025. This is a standard Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty action. Plaintiff filed the present motion on May 26, 2026. Defendant has not filed an opposition. On June 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed a notice of non-opposition.

 

Analysis

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 applies to “a civil action seeking restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle pursuant to Section 871.20.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (a).) “Within 60 days after the filing of the answer or other responsive pleading, all parties shall, without awaiting a discovery request, provide to all other parties an initial disclosure and documents pursuant to subdivisions (f), (g), and (h).” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (b).)

 

            “The defendant or manufacturer shall provide the following documents to all other parties pursuant to the timelines prescribed in subdivision (b): … (6) Motor vehicle information reports, including build documentation, component information, and delivery details; (7) Entire warranty transaction history for the motor vehicle; (8) Listing of required field actions applicable to the motor vehicle; (9) Published technical service bulletins (”TSBs”) for the same make, model, and year reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle; (10) Published information service bulletins (“ISBs”) for the same make, model, and year reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle; … (12) Warranty policies and procedure manuals; (13) Service manuals reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle…” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (h).)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Here, Defendant demurred to Plaintiff’s complaint on October 27, 2025, which was Defendant’s first responsive pleading filed in this action. Therefore, Defendant’s initial disclosures were due by December 26, 2025, sixty (60) days following Defendant’s demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (b).) Defendant failed to provide complete and compliant disclosures. (Rucker Decl., ¶ 4.) Plaintiff sent a meet and confer letter to Defendant on May 18, 2026, but Defendant did not cure the deficiencies in its mandatory disclosures. (Rucker Decl., ¶ 5; Exh. 2.)

 

Therefore, the motion to compel initial disclosures filed by Plaintiff is granted.

 

C.     Sanctions

 

“Unless the party failing to comply with this section shows good cause, … a court shall impose sanctions as follows: (1) A one-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($1,500) sanction against the plaintiff’s attorney or two-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($2,500) sanction against the defense attorney respectively, paid within 15 business days for failure to comply with the document production requirements as prescribed in subdivision (b)….” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (j).)

 

            Here, Defendant fails to provide an opposition to this motion demonstrating good cause. Therefore, the Court must impose sanctions in the amount of $2,500, payable within 15 business days for failure to comply with the document production requirements.

 

Conclusion:

 

            Plaintiff’s motion to compel initial disclosures is granted. The Court awards Plaintiff $2,500 in sanctions to be paid by Defendant’s counsel to Plaintiff’s counsel within 15 days.
