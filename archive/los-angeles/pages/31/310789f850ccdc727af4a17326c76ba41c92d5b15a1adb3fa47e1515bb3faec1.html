LEGAL STANDARD 

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (CCP §§ 430.30, 430.70.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732, internal citations omitted.) 

 

A special demurrer for uncertainty is disfavored and will only be sustained where the pleading is so bad that defendant cannot reasonably respond—i.e., cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him/her. (CCP § 430.10(f); Khoury v. Maly’s of Calif., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Moreover, even if the pleading is somewhat vague, “ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Ibid.)  

 

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof. (CCP § 435(b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1322(b).) The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (CCP §§ 436(a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].) 

 

“Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given.” (Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227.) It is an abuse of discretion for the court to deny leave to amend where there is any reasonable possibility that plaintiff can state a good cause of action. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) The burden is on plaintiff to show¿in what manner¿plaintiff can amend the complaint, and¿how¿that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading.¿(Id.) 

 

ANALYSIS

 

Defendant Evelen Guirguis demurs to each cause of action of Plaintiffs Usama Kamel, Jehan Bekhet, Maria Abdelmesseh, and Amy Abdelmesseh’s Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”), and moves to strike allegations regarding punitive damages, agency, and emotional distress.

 

Defendant Mena Guirguis demurs to each cause of action the SAC, and moves to strike allegations regarding punitive damages, agency, and emotional distress.

 

Defendants Xavier Jones and 12422 Pacific LLC demurs to each cause of action of the SAC.

 

Pertinent Allegations

 

Plaintiffs are tenants of the subject Property, 12422 Pacific Ave., #1, Los Angeles, 90066. (SAC ¶ 1.) Evelen and Mena Guirguis are the former owners and/or managers of the Subject Property. (SAC ¶ 2.) 12422 Pacific LLC is the current owner of the Property. (Id.) Jones is the managing agent and sole owner of the LLC. (Id.)

 

According to the SAC, the Property suffered from defects that rendered it untenantable, including defective plumbing, defective faucet, and a corroded sink stopper. (SAC ¶ 7.) For example, in 2022, the ceiling in the bathroom collapsed. (SAC ¶ 18.) Despite notice to Guirguis Defendants, Plaintiffs remained without access to any bathroom for weeks. (Id.) When Guirguis Defendants finally effectuated repairs, the repairs were improper and inadequate. (Id.) Plaintiffs’ sole bathroom continues to suffer from bubbling in the walls, trapped moisture which causes mold, dilapidated and broken bathroom fixtures, and faulty plumbing which causes consistent, unnoticed water shutoffs. (Id.) Plaintiffs also allege that the heating and cooling appliance were not maintained, and are not sure whether they are even operable. (SAC ¶ 19.) Defendants had written, verbal and constructive notice of these issues. (SAC ¶20.) This includes notice via an inspection of the Property prior to the sale of the building to the LLC. (Id.) Defendants refuse to repair, and instead mounted a campaign of intimidation and harassment to inconvenience Plaintiffs and ultimately force Plaintiffs to abandon their home. (SAC ¶¶ 21.) Defendants knowingly intended to interfere with Plaintiffs’ use and enjoyment of the Property. (SAC ¶ 22.)

 

Defendants have engaged in a “harassment campaign” against Plaintiffs, including failing to repair the rental unit after being provided with written notice. (SAC ¶¶ 9, 15, 21.) Guirguis Defendants attempted to physically strike Usama. (SAC ¶ 11.) Guirguis Defendants arbitrarily forbade outdoor grilling in a spacious yard and threatened to remove and confiscate any offending personal property belonging to Plaintiffs. (SAC ¶ 12.) Guirguis Defendants removed the side fences from the Property, thus permitting unfettered, unobstructed public access to Plaintiffs’ private yard, causing a substantial safety concern and causing the loss of Plaintiffs’ pet dog. (SAC ¶ 13.) Jones appeared at the Property without notice to attempt to remove an awning structure comprising an essential part of Plaintiff’s leased unit. (SAC ¶ 14.) He became aggressive and hostile in the act, threatening to use their tools as weapons once Plaintiffs intervened. (Id.) Defendants have repeatedly intimidated, frightened assaulted or threatened to assault, and attempted to coerce Plaintiffs into leaving the Property. (SAC ¶ 23.)

 

Defendants over-collected rent. (SAC ¶ 8.) Jones and the LLC knowingly demanded more rent than what was legally owed. (SAC ¶ 16.) Jones and the LLC also refused to accept Plaintiffs' rent and demanded Plaintiffs take a buyout of their unit. (SAC ¶ 17.)

 

First and Second Causes – Habitability

 

The elements of negligence are: 1) a legal duty owed to plaintiffs to use due care; 2) breach of duty; 3) causation; and 4) damage to plaintiff.¿ (County of Santa Clara v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 292, 318.)¿¿A landlord has a duty to ensure that a leased premises is maintained in a habitable condition- that is, the landlord will maintain “bare living requirements.”¿ (Green v. Superior Court (1974) 10 Cal.3d 616, 637.)

 

To establish a breach of the implied warranty of habitability, a plaintiff must allege (1) “the existence of a material defective condition affecting the premises’ habitability,” (2) “notice to the landlord of the condition within a reasonable time after the tenant’s discovery of the condition,” (3) “the landlord was given a reasonable time to correct the deficiency, and” (4) “resulting damages.” (Erlach v. Sierra Asset Servicing, LLC (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1281, 1297.) "The implied warranty of habitability recognizes 'the realities of the modern urban landlord-tenant relationship' and imposes upon the landlord the obligation to maintain leased dwellings in a habitable condition throughout the term of the lease." (Peterson v. Superior Court (1995) 10 Cal.4th 1185, 1204.) "The implied warranty of habitability… gives a tenant a reasonable expectation that the landlord has inspected the rental dwelling and corrected any defects disclosed by that inspection that would render the dwelling uninhabitable. The tenant further reasonably can expect that the landlord will maintain the property in a habitable condition by repairing promptly any conditions, of which the landlord has actual or constructive notice, that arise during the tenancy and render the dwelling uninhabitable. A tenant injured by a defect in the premises, therefore, may bring a negligence action if the landlord breached its duty to exercise reasonable care." (Id. at 1205-1206, fns. omitted.)

 

The SAC states causes of action against Defendants for breach of the warranty of habitability and for negligence based on the breach of that same duty. Defendants allegedly breached their duties as landlords to provide Plaintiffs with a safe, habitable, and tenantable dwelling by failing to maintain the Property in a habitable condition. (SAC ¶¶ 27-28, 33-35.) The SAC alleges persisting habitability issues, including with mold, plumbing, and consistent, unnoticed water shutoffs. (SAC ¶ 18.) Despite notice, Defendants have refused to repair those defects, instead mounting a campaign of intimidation and harassment to inconvenience Plaintiffs and force them to abandon the Property. (SAC ¶¶ 18-21.) As a result of the habitability issues, Plaintiffs suffered damages, including economic and non-economic damages. (SAC ¶¶ 29, 36.)

 

Jones separately argues that he has no duty to Plaintiffs, since he is not alleged to be the direct landlord of the Property. The specific facts alleged establish he is the managing agent and sole owner of the LLC, and that the LLC owns the Property. (SAC ¶ 2.) He notes that he would not be liable unless he authorizes, directs, or in some meaningful sense actively participates in wrongful conduct. (See Frances T. v. Village Green Owners Assn. (1986) 42 Cal.3d 490, 503 [corporate directors generally cannot be held vicariously liable for the corporation's torts; the individual officer or director will be immune unless he authorizes, directs, or in some meaningful sense actively participates in the wrongful conduct].) The SAC alleges Jones’s direct participation in the allegedly wrongful conduct. Jones allegedly had written, verbal and constructive notice of the habitability issues, yet failed to correct those issues. (SAC ¶ 20.) The habitability issues persist through the change of ownership. (SAC ¶18.) Additionally, as to the rent collection and harassment allegations, LARSO has an expansive definition of landlord as including any person “having any legal or equitable right of ownership or possession or the right to lease or receive rent,” or the “representative” of an owner or lessor. (LAMC §§ 45.32, 151.02.) Jones meets these definitions by having an equitable right of ownership via his sole ownership of the LLC as well as being a representative of the LLC.

 

Accordingly, the demurrers are OVERRULED as to the first and second causes of action.

 

Third and Fourth Causes – LARSO Harassment

 

LAMC section 45.33 defines tenant harassment as “a landlord’s bad faith conduct directed at a specific tenant or tenants that causes the latter detriment or harm.” The definition expressly includes reducing or eliminating, or threatening to reduce or to eliminate, housing services required by a lease, contract or law. (LAMC § 45.33(1).) The definition includes the failure “to perform timely and complete necessary repairs and maintenance required by Federal, State, County, or local housing, health, or safety laws.” (LAMC § 45.33(2).) The definition also includes “[t]hreatening a tenant, or their guests, by word or gesture, with physical harm.” (LAMC § 45.33(6).) It also includes “[r]efusing to acknowledge, facilitate, or accept receipt of lawful rent payments or rental assistance payments as set forth in the lease agreement or as established by the usual practice of the parties or applicable law.”¿(LAMC § 45.33(9).) It also broadly covers “[o]ther repeated acts… of such significance as to substantially interfere with or disturb the comfort, repose, peace or quiet of a tenant(s) and that cause, are likely to cause, or are committed with the objective to cause a tenant(s) to surrender or waive any rights in relation to such tenancy.” (LAMC § 45.33(17).)

 

The SAC alleges Defendants engaged in tenant harassment. (SAC ¶ 40.) As discussed, Defendants willfully failed to perform and timely complete necessary repairs and maintenance with the intent to have Plaintiffs abandon the Property. (SAC ¶¶ 18-22.) Defendants have repeatedly intimidated, frightened, assaulted or threatened to assault, and attempted to coerce Plaintiffs into leaving the Property. (SAC ¶ 23.) Guirguis Defendants attempted to physically strike Usama. (SAC ¶ 11.) Jones attempted to remove a material awning structure from the Property, and became aggressive and threatened to use tools as weapons once Plaintiffs intervened. (SAC ¶ 14.) Defendants reduced or threatened to reduce housing services material to the lease. Guirguis Defendants forbade outdoor grilling and threatened to remove and confiscate personal property belonging to Plaintiffs. (SAC ¶ 12.) Guirguis Defendants removed the side fences from the Property causing a substantial safety concern. (SAC ¶ 13.) Jones and the LLC refused to accept Plaintiffs' lawful rent and pressured Plaintiffs to leave. (SAC ¶ 17.)

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is OVERRULED as to the third and fourth causes of action.

 

Fifth Cause – Overpayment of Rent

 

LAMC § 151.04 states that “[i]t shall be unlawful for any landlord to demand, accept or retain more than the maximum adjusted rent permitted pursuant to this chapter or regulation or orders adopted pursuant to this chapter.” LAMC § 151.07(A) provides the formulae for calculation of the maximum adjusted rent. Notably, a successor landlord may be held liable for maintaining a prior landlord’s unlawful rent increases. (Baychester Shopping Center, Inc. v. San Francisco Residential Rent Stabilization and Arbitration Bd. of City and County of San Francisco (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1000, 1008-09.) The SAC alleges that Defendants charged rent in violation of LARSO. (SAC ¶¶ 8, 16, 47.) The SAC further alleges that Jones himself demanded excess rent. (Id.)

 

Accordingly, the demurrers are OVERRULED as to the fifth cause of action with leave to amend.

 

Sixth Cause – Quiet Enjoyment

 

Every lease includes a covenant of quiet possession and enjoyment. (Erlach, supra, 226 Cal.App.4th at 1299.) The¿necessary elements¿for breach of the covenant of quiet¿enjoyment¿are: (1) a lease agreement between plaintiff and defendant; (2) the absence of language contrary to the implied covenant that tenant shall have quiet¿enjoyment¿and possession; (3) an act or omission of the landlord, or anyone claiming under the landlord, which substantially interferes with a tenant’s¿right to use and enjoy the premises for purposes contemplated; and (4) an applicable remedy.¿(Andrews v. Mobile Aire Estates¿(2005) 125 Cal.App.4th 578, 588-591.) A plaintiff may alternatively terminate the lease, surrender the property and sue for damages, or elect to stand upon the lease and sue for damages as well as for injunctive relief. (Id. at 590.)

 

The allegations of harassment and intentional failure to maintain habitability establish that Defendants substantially interfered with Plaintiffs’ use and enjoyment of the Premises. To reiterate, despite actual notice of the habitability issues, Defendants refused to repair the defects in order force Plaintiffs to abandon their home. (SAC ¶¶ 20-22.) Defendants attempted to assault at least one plaintiff. (SAC ¶ 11.) Defendants reduced or threatened to reduce housing services material to the lease. (SAC ¶¶ 11-14, 23.) Such acts may be considered a substantial interference with Plaintiffs’ lease.

 

Accordingly, the demurrers are OVERRULED as to the sixth cause of action.

 

Motion to Strike – Emotional Distress

 

            Plaintiffs may recover emotional distress damages in tort based on the above discussed allegations. The negligent infliction of emotional distress, including anxiety, worry and discomfort, is compensable without physical injury in cases involving the tortious interference with property rights. (Erlach, supra, 226 Cal.App.4th at 1299.) This includes circumstances where the failure to repair a premises renders the premises uninhabitable. (Id.) Therefore, the motion to strike is DENIED.

 

MTS – Agency

 

General allegations of agency are sufficient for pleading purposes. (Brown v. USA Taekwondo (2019) 40 Cal.App.5th 1077, 1106.) The ultimate facts of agency are pled. (SAC ¶¶ 2, 4, 11.) Accordingly, the motion to strike is DENIED.

 

MTS – Punitive Damages

 

Section 3294 defines malice as conduct “intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff,” or “despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” Despicable is a powerful term used to describe circumstances that are “base,” “vile,” or “contemptible.” (Coll. Hosp., supra, 9 Cal.4th 704, 726.) The statute “plainly indicates that absent an intent to injure the plaintiff, “malice” requires more than a “willful and conscious” disregard of the plaintiffs' interests. The additional component of “despicable conduct” must be found.” (Id.)

 

Courts have permitted recovery of punitive damages on claims for tortious breach of the implied covenant of quiet enjoyment. (Ginsberg v. Gamson (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 873; Hjelm v. Prometheus Real Estate Group Inc. (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 1155; Stoiber v. Honeychuck (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 903, 919.) For example, in Stoiber, the court found that a plaintiff could recover punitive damages for numerous persistent uninhabitable conditions of which she repeatedly notified the landlord defendants. (Id. at 921.) The plaintiff alleged specific uninhabitable conditions, including heavy cockroach infestation, broken interior walls, broken deteriorated flooring on front porch, falling ceiling, deteriorated, overfused electrical wiring, lack of proper plumbing connection to sewage system in bathroom, sewage under bathroom floor, leaking roof, broken windows, and fire hazard. (Ibid.) Despite notice, the defendants knowingly, intentionally and willfully failed to correct the defective conditions by not even attempting to repair the defects, except on one occasion where they sent a plumber in response to a complaint regarding the toilet. (Id. at 913.)

 

The SAC alleges that the Guirguis Defendants engaged in knowing, intentional and willful conduct designed to harm Plaintiffs. Despite actual notice of the habitability issues, including consistent and unnoticed water shutoffs, Guirguis Defendants refused to repair the defects in order force Plaintiffs to abandon their home. (SAC ¶¶ 11-22.) The allegations establish an intent to injure Plaintiffs by forcing them to move. Moreover, there would be a dispute of fact as to whether the alleged conduct was despicable. Thus, the claim for punitive damages are well-pled against Mena and Evelen.

 

Accordingly, the motion to strike is DENIED.

Case Number: 25SMCV06023    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           National Right to Work Legal Defense Foundation v. University of California at Los Angeles, et al.

CASE NO.:                25SMCV06023

MOTIONS:               Motion for a Protective Order;

                                    Motion to Compel Further Responses (RPDs, SROGs); and

                                    Motion to Compel Deposition

HEARING DATE:   6/30/2026

 

 

Legal Standard

 

            In the absence of contrary court order, a civil litigant’s right to discovery is broad. “[A]ny party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action . . . if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” (CCP § 2017.010; see Davies v. Superior Court (1984) 36 Cal.3d 291, 301.) Section 2017.020(a) vests the Court with authority to limit the scope of discovery if the burden, expense, or intrusiveness of the discovery sought “clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.”  Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) § 2019.030 directs the Court to consider the needs of the case, amount in controversy, and the importance of the issues at stake in the litigation, and to consider whether the discovery being sought is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative, or is obtainable by a more convenient or less expensive or less burdensome way, when deciding whether to restrict the frequency of extent of use of an authorized discovery method.

 

Protective Orders

 

            For good cause shown, the Court may enter a protective order to prevent unwarranted annoyances, embarrassment, oppression, undue burden, and expense in discovery. (Fireman's Fund Ins. Co. v. Sup. Ct. (1991) 233 Cal.App.3d 1138, 1141; CCP §§ 1987.1, 2017.020(a), 2025.420(b).) 

 

Compel Further Discovery Responses

 

            CCP section 2030.220(a) requires that “[e]ach answer in a response to interrogatories shall be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.” Pursuant to CCP section 2030.300, a party may move to compel further responses to a form interrogatory if the other party’s answer is “evasive or incomplete.” The responding party has the burden of justifying any objections. (Coy v. Superior Court (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220-221.)

 

            A motion to compel further responses to a demand for inspection or production of documents may be brought based on: (1) incomplete statements of compliance; (2) inadequate, evasive or incomplete claims of inability to comply; or (3) unmerited or overly generalized objections. (CCP, § 2031.310(c).) A motion for order compelling further responses “shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (CCP § 2031.310(b)(1).) Absent a claim of privilege or attorney work product, the moving party meets its burden of showing good cause by a fact-specific showing of relevance. (See Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98.) If the moving party has shown good cause for the RPDs, the burden is on the objecting party to justify the objections. (Ibid.) 

 

Notice of the motion must be provided within 45 days of service of the response, or any supplemental response, or on or before any specific later date to which the parties have agreed.  (CCP § 2031.310(c).) This 45-day deadline runs from the date the verified response is served, and is extended for service by mail in accordance with C.C.P. Sections 1010.6(a)(4) and 1013(a). (Ibid.) This is a jurisdictional requirement. (Vidal Sassoon, Inc. v. Superior Court (1983) 147 Cal.App.3d 681, 685.)

 

The moving party must demonstrate a “reasonable and good faith attempt” at an informal resolution of each issue presented. (CCP §§ 2016.040, 2031.310(b)(2).)

 

Motions to compel further responses must always be accompanied by a separate statement containing the requests and the responses, verbatim, as well as reasons why a further response is warranted. (CRC, Rule 3.1345(a).)

 

Compel Deposition Attendance

 

Service of a proper deposition notice obligates a party or “party-affiliated” witness (officer, director, managing agent or employee of party) to attend and testify, as well as produce any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing for inspection and copying. (CCP § 2025.280(a).) If, after service of a deposition notice, a party deponent fails to appear, testify, or produce documents or tangible things for inspection without having served a valid objection under CCP § 2025.410, the deposing party may move for an order compelling attendance, testimony, and production. (CCP § 2025.450(a).) The motion must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration, or, when a party deponent fails to attend the deposition, the motion must also be accompanied by a declaration stating that the moving party has contacted the party deponent to inquire about the nonappearance. (CCP § 2025.450(b)(2).) If the deposition notice included a request for production of documents, the motion to compel attendance must also show good cause to justify the production. (CCP § 2025.450(b)(1).) 

 

The motion to compel must be “made no later than 60 days after the completion of the record of the deposition.” (CCP § 2025.480(b).) This time limit also applies to motions based on a deposition subpoena for production of documents or a business records subpoena. The 60-day time limit runs from the date objections are served because the deposition record is then complete. (Rutledge v. Hewlett-Packard Co. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 1164, 1192.) 

 

The California Public Records Act

 

            Pursuant to Government Code section 7921, et seq., citizens have a right to access government records. In enacting the California Public Records Act (“CPRA”), the Legislature declared that “access to information concerning the conduct of the people's business is a fundamental and necessary right of every person in this state.” (Gov. Code § 7921.000; see also County of Los Angeles v. Superior Court (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 57, 63.) Article 1, Section 3(b) of the Constitution affirms that “[t]he people have the right of access to information concerning the conduct of the people’s business.” The Constitution mandates that the CPRA be “broadly construed,” while any statute “that limits the right of access” must be “narrowly construed.” (Nat’l Lawyers Guild v. City of Hayward (2020) 9 Cal.5th 488, 507.) To establish an agency has a duty to disclose under the CPRA, the petitioner must show that: (1) the record qualifies as a public record; and (2) the record is in the possession of the agency. (Anderson-Barker v Sup. Ct. (2019) 31 Cal.App.5th 528, 538.) An agency has constructive possession of records if it has the right to control the records, i.e. the power or authority to manage, direct, or oversee the records, either directly or through another person. (Id. at 538-540.) The agency must enumerate or describe the records discovered, identify exemptions applying to any enumerated or described records, and disclose the remaining records. (Haynie v. Superior Court (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1061, 1072; see ACLU of Northern Cal. v. Sup. Ct. (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 55, 83 [“Because the agency has full knowledge of the contents of the withheld records and the requester has only the agency's affidavits and descriptions of the documents, its affidavits must be specific enough to give the requester ‘a meaningful opportunity to contest’ the withholding of the documents.”].) The agency bears the burden of showing that a specific exemption applies, and such exemptions are narrowly construed. (Sacramento County Employees’ Retirement System v. Sup. Ct. (2013) 195 Cal.App.4th 440, 453.)

 

Discovery in CPRA

 

The Civil Discovery Act applies to CPRA proceedings, which fall within the definition of a “special proceeding of a civil nature.” (City of Los Angeles v. Sup. Ct. (2017) 9 Cal.App.5th 272, 284-288.) Nonetheless, the right to discovery “remains subject to the trial court’s authority to manage and limit discovery as required.” (Id. at 288.) The Court of Appeal explained:

 

[T]he CPRA is intended to “permit the expeditious determination” of a narrow issue: whether a public agency has an obligation to disclose the records that the petitioner has requested. [Citations.] Although many CPRA cases are likely to involve questions of law based on undisputed facts (including, for example, whether a particular type of record is subject to a disclosure exemption), other cases will require the court to make factual findings based on conflicting evidence. In some such cases, discovery may be necessary to test the agency’s assertion that it does not have an obligation to disclose the records at issue. [Citation.] When a party does seek to compel discovery (or seeks a protective order from a discovery request), the trial court must determine whether the discovery sought is necessary to resolve whether the agency has a duty to disclose, and to additionally consider whether the request is¿justified given the need for an expeditious resolution.

 

(Id. at 289.) 

 

            The Court of Appeal has reiterated that pretrial discovery in a CPRA action is limited to the “narrow issue of whether the agency has a duty of disclosure” and that the trial court has discretion to determine whether discovery furthers that purpose. (County of San Benito v. Superior Court (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 243, 255.) The Court also limited the right to discovery in multiple respects. First, a petitioner may not serve requests for production “of the very documents sought by the underlying public records request.” (Ibid.) Second, a petitioner may not serve interrogatories “calling for the [respondent] to generate new substantive content that is beyond the scope of discovery relevant to the merits of a Public Records Act enforcement proceeding as a means to collect information that is not subject to disclosure under the Act.” (Ibid.) For example, questions concerning the substance of documents, rather than the process for searching for those documents, would exceed the scope of pretrial discovery in a CPRA action. (Id., at 265.) Information concerning an agency’s “reasons for taking a certain action” in an underlying investigation is irrelevant to the CPRA and does not relate to whether a search process was reasonable. (Id., at 267.)

 

            "When assessing motions to compel discovery (or motions seeking a protective order) in CPRA proceedings, the trial court has discretion to consider whether the petitioner has made an adequate showing that the discovery is likely to aid in the resolution of the particular issues presented in the proceeding." (City of Los Angeles v. Superior Court (2017) 9 Cal.App.5th 272, 290.) Under persuasive federal authorities, “when the government has provided a detailed factual basis in support of its decision to withhold documents (generally through affidavits), discovery is warranted only if the plaintiff ‘make[s] a [sufficient] showing of bad faith,’ or is able to provide ‘tangible evidence’ that the records have been improperly withheld.” (Id., citing Wood v. F.B.I. (2d Cir. 2005) 432 F.3d 78, 85; Wheeler v. C.I.A. (D.D.C. 2003) 271 F.Supp.2d 132, 141.) Thus, discovery “may be appropriate when the plaintiff can raise sufficient question as to the agency's good faith in processing or in its search [citation], it is improper when sought for the bare hope of falling upon something that might impugn the agency's evidence.” (Id., quotations omitted.)

 

Analysis

 

            The instant hearing regards three separate discovery motions.

 

            Plaintiff National Right to Work Legal Defense Foundation, Inc. moves for an order compelling Defendants University of California, Los Angeles, and The Regents of the University of California to provide further responses to Plaintiff’s First Set of Requests for Production of Documents (“RPD”) nos. 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 11, 15, 16, 23, and 25, and Special Interrogatories (“SROG”) nos. 1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 16, and 18.

 

            Second, Defendants move for a protective order providing relief from Plaintiff’s discovery requests under CCP sections 2023.090(a) and 2031.060(a).

 

            Finally, Plaintiff also moves to compel the deposition of Rachel Pounds, a Senior Public Records Analyst for Defendant UCLA.

 

Compel Furthers and Protective Order

 

            The Court tentatively grants the motions to compel further responses to RPD nos. 1, 2, 3, 5 and 25, and SROG nos. 1, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 16, as they are reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence concerning the limited issues presented in this CPRA action.

 

            A central dispute of this action is whether Defendants performed an adequate search of their records. The Complaint alleges that Defendants are illegally withholding public records past statutory deadlines, without justification or citing an exemption. (Compl., ¶¶ 1-2.) The CPRA requests records relating to Defendants' 2024 Private Equity Research Strategy Conference and Defendants' 2018-2024 Strategic Labor Research Conferences. (Compl., Exs. 1-2.) More specifically, the requests sought all documents presented, distributed, or used at the conference itself and any recordings of the presentation; as well as any draft materials, any communication relating to the materials or the conferences, and any lists of presenters or participants. (Compl., ¶¶ 8-9.) Defendants agreed to produce documents. (Compl., ¶¶ 11-12, 14.) Eventually, Defendants produced some documents, but the production consisted almost entirely of publicly available municipal financial reports as well as scattered travel itineraries, a handful of generic policies concerning honoraria, and a short video taken at a conference. (Compl., ¶ 16.) The documents appear unrelated to the conference presentations, research materials, attendance lists, expense reports, or communications, and Defendants proffered no explanation why such published municipal materials were somehow responsive. (Id.) The production suggests that there is a cache of existing and withheld responsive documents. (Compl., ¶ 17.) Plaintiff alleges that Defendants' purported "review" process is pretextual and conducted in bad faith. (Compl., ¶ 18.)

 

            Agencies are generally obliged to disclose records they can locate “with reasonable effort.” (City of San Jose v. Superior Court (2017) 2 Cal.5th 608, 627.) “Reasonable efforts do not require that agencies undertake extraordinarily extensive or intrusive searches, however.” (Ibid.) In general, the scope of an agency’s search for public records “need only be reasonably calculated to locate responsive documents.” (Ibid.) Courts have permitted discovery assessing the reasonableness of the agency’s efforts to collect documents in response to a public records request. (San Benito, supra, 96 Cal.App.5th at 265-266.) “This information could include the identity of the individuals participating in the investigation, the time period during which the investigation was completed, and the means by which the participants communicated with each other. Such information could go to the reasonableness of the custodians the public entity identified, the repositories searched, and the temporal scope of the search.” (Id. at 265.)

 

            The Court will not require Plaintiff to demonstrate Defendants’ bad faith, or evidence that Defendants have improperly withheld documents. While City of Los Angeles, supra, 9 Cal.App.5th at 290, suggests that the court may require a plaintiff demonstrate an agency’s bad faith or other evidence that records are improperly withheld before discovery, more recent case law suggests that discovery into the scope of a search is generally permissible. (San Benito, supra, 96 Cal.App.5th at 265-266.) Also, Defendants are not necessarily withholding documents in bad faith. Rather, the main issue is whether Defendants conducted a reasonable search. Information on those search efforts is directly relevant this central issue. Under such circumstances, Plaintiff does not need to present affirmative evidence of bad faith or improper withholding.

 

            RPDs

 

            Responses to RPD nos. 1, 2, 3, 5 and 25 will establish the reasonableness of Defendants’ search efforts. RPD no. 1 asks for documents evidencing the searches conducted in response to Plaintiff’s requests. RPD no. 2 requests documents reflecting the review, approval, or decision-making process relating to the production of records. RPD no. 3 asks for documents upon which Defendants rely to support their contentions in affirmative defenses to the Complaint that they fully complied with the CPRA. RPD no. 5 asks for documents exchanged with or maintained by any third party or vendor relating to the search for, production of, or withholding of responsive records. RPD no. 25 asks for Defendants’ retention policy. (See San Benito, supra, at 258 [approving discovery seeking information about county's record-retention policies].) These documents requests will illuminate whether Defendants undertook search efforts that were reasonably calculated to locate any responsive documents, and whether Defendants have retained responsive, but unproduced, documents.

 

            RPD nos. 6, 10, 11, 15 and 16 go beyond the narrow issues presented in a CPRA action. RPD no. 6 seeks documents regarding the “creation and distribution” of the litigation hold, which would not show whether Defendants has a duty of disclosure. RPD no. 10 and 15 pertain to communications which relate to CPRA requests received from Cogency. Cogency’s request is not at issue, and will not demonstrate whether Defendants have a duty of disclosure to Plaintiff’s subject request. RPD no. 11 and 16 are overbroad, as they request any communications which relate at all to Plaintiff’s request, which goes well beyond documents which will help resolve whether Defendants conducted a reasonable search or whether Defendants have a duty of disclosure. Therefore, the burden, expense and intrusiveness of responding to such requests outweigh the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.

 

            SROGs

 

            Similarly, SROG nos. 1, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 16 will also establish the reasonableness of Defendants’ search efforts. SROG no. 1 seeks the identity of the persons with personal knowledge of the facts stated in Defendants’ answer. SROG no. 5 asks for Defendants to describe the process used to respond to Plaintiff’s requests, including “each step taken from receipt of the request through final production or withholding of records”; “(a) intake and initial review procedures; (b) identification of custodians, departments, and data sources searched; (c) search methodologies employed; (d) internal review, approval, and decision-making steps; and (e) final production or withholding determinations” and identification of all documents that “evidence this process” and all persons who “participated in or had responsibility for any portion of the process.” SROG no. 7 seeks details on the search terms, queries, or parameters used for the search. SROG no. 8 seeks any steps taken to verify and validate that the searches conducted. SROG no. 16 seeks information on all steps taken to identify and obtain responsive records from third parties. 

 

            SROG no. 6 asks for identification of each person Defendants requested or suggested to participate in or to assist or consult in searching, which pertains to Defendants’ search parameters. SROG no. 6, however, is overbroad since it seeks information on individuals Defendants “requested” or “suggested” to participate in the search, rather than those people that actually assisted in the search. The Court limits SROG no. 6 to individuals that assisted in the search.  SROG nos. 3 and 18 are overbroad and will not aid in resolving the narrow issues of whether the agency has a duty of disclosure, including whether Defendants conducting reasonable search efforts. SROG no. 3 seeks the identity of played any role in responding to Cogency’s and/or Plaintiff’s requests. Seeking information on Cogency’s request is overbroad, since information on that request will not aid determining whether Defendants complied with Plaintiff’s request. SROG no. 18 seeks information on distribution of and compliance with Plaintiff’s litigation hold. Information concerning Defendants’ compliance with a purported litigation hold does not relate to the CPRA request or the reasonable search efforts for said request. Therefore, the burden, expense and intrusiveness of responding to such interrogatories outweigh the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.

 

            Defendants otherwise do not attempt to substantiate their other objections, such as objections based upon the attorney-client privilege.

 

            Additionally, the Court will issue a protective order limiting discovery as to the topics in the denied RPDs and SROGs as discussed above. Defendants are ordered to prepare a proposed protective order consistent with this ruling, meet and confer with Plaintiffs, and then submit the order within 10 days.

 

            Accordingly, the motion to compel further responses is GRANTED as to RPD nos. 1, 2, 3, 5 and 25, and SROG nos. 1, 5, 6 (as modified), 7, 8 and 16, and DENIED as to the remainder.  Further responses are ordered within 30 days.

 

Deposition

 

            Plaintiffs demonstrate proper service of the deposition notice on the party affiliated witness, Ms. Pounds. (Oster Decl., ¶ 2.) Defendants did not properly object to the deposition notice, or otherwise move to quash the notice. However, the Court agrees that some of Defendants’ arguments in support of their requested protective order would equally apply to any depositions conducted in this action. As discussed, relevant deposition topics would concern Defendants' response to Plaintiff's CPRA requests, including Defendants' preservation activities, search efforts, and the factual basis for Defendants' assertion that their production was complete. The deposition must not delve into manifestly irrelevant topics, such as Cogency’s request(s) or Plaintiff’s litigation hold.

 

            Accordingly, the motion to compel deposition is GRANTED.

 

Sanctions

 

Sanctions are generally mandatory against any party that unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a further response, “unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (See, e.g., CCP, § 2030.300(d).)

 

Plaintiff failed to notice or substantiate their request for monetary sanctions.

 

Defendants were also substantially justified in their objections. Some of the RPDs and SROGs went beyond the appropriate scope of discovery. Additionally, there is persuasive federal caselaw justifying denial of discovery absent Plaintiff’s showing of bad faith or other tangible evidence that records have been improperly withheld. (City of Los Angeles, supra, 9 Cal.App.5th at 292.) Defendants were also substantially justified in their opposition to the deposition, since the proper scope of the topics at deposition was not certain.

 

            Accordingly, the Court declines to impose sanctions.

Legal Standard

 

Forum Non Conveniens

 

“Forum non conveniens is an equitable doctrine invoking the discretionary power of a court to decline to exercise the jurisdiction it has over a transitory cause of action when it believes that the action may be more appropriately and justly tried elsewhere. [Citations].” (Stangvik v. Shiley, Inc. (1991) 54 Cal.3d 744, 751.) The doctrine has been codified in Code of Civil Procedure section 410.30. “The inquiry is whether ‘in the interest of substantial justice an action should be heard in a forum outside this state.’ ” (Animal Film, LLC v. D.E.J. Productions, Inc. (2011) 193 Cal.App.4th 466, 471.)

 

Where plaintiff is a California resident for purposes of forum non conveniens, there is a strong presumption in favor of plaintiff's choice of forum. (Stangvik, supra, 54 Cal.3d at 754.) A nonresident plaintiff's choice of forum is entitled to “due deference” under all circumstances, but not a strong presumption of appropriateness. (National Football League v. Fireman's Fund Ins. Co. (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 902, 929-930.)  

 

In a contract dispute where the contract contains a forum selection clause “a threshold question in a forum non conveniens motion is whether a forum selection clause is mandatory or permissive.” (Animal Film, Inc., supra, 193 Cal.App.4th at 471.) Because California favors enforcement of mandatory forum selection clauses, they will be given effect absent a showing by the party resisting the motion that enforcement of such clauses would be unreasonable. (Quanta Computer Inc. v. Japan Communications Inc.¿(2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 438, 445.) “The factors that apply generally to a forum non conveniens motion do not control in a case involving a mandatory forum selection clause. Where there is a mandatory forum selection clause, ‘the test is simply whether application of the clause is unfair or unreasonable, and the clause is usually given effect. Claims that the previously chosen forum is unfair or inconvenient are generally rejected. A court will usually honor a mandatory forum selection clause without extensive analysis of factors relating to convenience.” (Ibid. [citations and internal quotation marks omitted].) 

 

Compel Arbitration

 

Under California and federal law, public policy favors arbitration as an efficient and less expensive means of resolving private disputes. (Moncharsh v. Heily & Blase (1992) 3 Cal.4th 1, 8-9; AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion (2011) 563 U.S. 333, 339.) Accordingly, whether an agreement is governed by the California Arbitration Act (“CAA”) or the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), courts resolve doubts about an arbitration agreement’s scope in favor of arbitration.  (Moncharsh, supra, 3 Cal.4th at 9; Comedy Club, Inc. v. Improv West Assocs. (9th Cir. 2009) 553 F.3d 1277, 1284; see also Engalla v. Permanente Med. Grp., Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 971-972 [“California law incorporates many of the basic policy objectives contained in the Federal Arbitration Act, including a presumption in favor of arbitrability [citation] and a requirement that an arbitration agreement must be enforced on the basis of state law standards that apply to contracts in general”].) “[U]nder both the FAA and California law, ‘arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.’ ” (Higgins v. Superior Crout (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1247.) 

 

“Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2 requires a trial court to grant a petition to compel arbitration if the court determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists.” (Avery v. Integrated Healthcare Holdings, Inc. (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 50, 59, quotations omitted.) Accordingly, “when presented with a petition to compel arbitration, the court’s first task is to determine whether the parties have in fact agreed to arbitrate the dispute.”  (Ibid.) A petition to compel arbitration is in essence a suit in equity to compel specific performance of a contract. (Id. at 71.) As with any other specific performance claim, “a party seeking to enforce an arbitration agreement must show the agreement’s terms are sufficiently definite to enable the court to know what it is to enforce.” (Ibid. [internal citations omitted].) “Only the valid and binding agreement of the parties, including all material terms well-defined and clearly expressed, may be ordered specifically performed.” (Ibid.) An arbitration agreement “must be so interpreted as to give effect to the mutual intention of the parties as it existed at the time of contracting, so far as the same is ascertainable and lawful.” (Civ. Code, § 1636.) The language of the contract governs its interpretation if it is clear and explicit. (Civ. Code, § 1368.) If uncertainty exists, “the language of a contract should be interpreted most strongly against the party who caused the uncertainty to exist.” (Civ. Code, § 1654.)  

 

The party seeking to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence. (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 972.) It would then be plaintiff’s burden, in opposing the motion, to prove by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to her opposition. (See Ibid.) “In these summary proceedings, the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Ibid.) As with any contract, mutual assent or consent is necessary for the formation of a valid arbitration agreement. (Civ. Code, §§ 1550, 1565.) “Consent is not mutual, unless the parties all agree upon the same thing in the same sense.” (Civ. Code, § 1580.)

 

Pro Hac Vice

 

California Rules of Court (“CRC”), rule 9.40 provides that an attorney in good standing in another jurisdiction may apply to appear pro hac vice in this State by way of written application upon due notice to all interested parties, as well as service on the State Bar in San Francisco with payment of a reasonable fee, so long as that attorney is not a resident of California, does not work in California and does not perform regular or substantial business, professional or other activities in the State.  

 

The written application must provide the following information: (1) applicant attorney’s residence and office addresses; (2) the courts to which the applicant attorney has been admitted and dates of admission; (3) a representation that the attorney applicant is a member in good standing in the courts of admission and is not currently suspended or disbarred in any court; (4) the title of each court and action in which the applicant attorney has appeared pro hac vice in this State in the preceding two years, if any; and (5) the name, address, and telephone number of the active California State Bar member who is counsel of record in the local action. (CRC Rule 9.40(d).) 

 

ANALYSIS

 

Defendants Primerica Financial Services, LLC, Primerica Life Insurance Company, PFS Investments Inc., and Primerica Client Services, Inc. (“Primerica” or “Defendants”) moves for an order dismissing Plaintiff Experior Financial Group Inc.’s complaint because California is an inconvenient forum and the action may only proceed in the state of Georgia. (CCP §§ 410.30, 418.10.) Alternatively, Defendants move to compel Plaintiff’s claims to arbitration.

 

Defendants also move for permission for its counsel Meredith N. Palmer and Christopher B. Trowbridge, to appear as counsel pro hac vice on their behalf.

 

Related Case

 

Defendants note that, prior to filing the instant action, Plaintiff had filed another, virtually identical action with one of Primerica’s former agents subject to these agreements, Pedro Gonzalez. (Gonzalez v. Primerica Financial Services, LLC, Case No. 25STCV29196.) The Gonzalez action was in Dept. 507 at Stanley Mosk Courthouse. Plaintiff dismissed the Gonzalez action in response to Defendants filing a similar motion in that action, the same day that Plaintiff filed this action. Facially, the two actions appear related within the meaning of CRC 3.300(a)(1)-(2), as they each involve the Plaintiff and Defendants, arise from identical events, seek identical relief, and would resolve substantially identical questions of law and fact. Thus, this action would likely be transferred to Dept. 507. No party has filed a notice of related case, despite an affirmative duty to do so. (CRC Rule 3.300(b), (h).)

 

Plaintiff is ordered to promptly file a notice of related case in both actions and request Dept. 507 rule on the notice of related case. The Court will continue this motion to allow a ruling on whether these cases are related by Dept. 507, as that department has the earlier numbered case. If Dept. 507 deems the cases related, then that department should rule on the instant, potentially dispositive, motion. 

 

Accordingly, the Court will continue Defendants’ motions to ______________, which will allow sufficient time for the other department to rule on the notice of related case.

 

Pro Hac Vice

 

The applications meet the requirements of CRC Rule 9.40. Defendants’ motions for counsel to appear pro hac vice are GRANTED.
