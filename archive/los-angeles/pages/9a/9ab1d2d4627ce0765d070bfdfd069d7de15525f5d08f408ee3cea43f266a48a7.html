23VECV03417 Lingard v Sober Souls

FOR THE FSC 8/11/2026

COUNSEL, PARTIES, AND WITNESSES ARE REQUIRED TO COMPLY WITH ALL LOCAL RULES BUT THE COURT REITERATES THESE SPECIFIC LOCAL RULES:

3.183 OBJECTIONS TO CLOSING ARGUMENT Any objection to a closing argument should be argued outside the jurors' hearing at the side bench. If the trial judge is uncertain about whether there has been a misstatement of the evidence in final argument, the jurors may be advised to consult their own memories and notes.

3.112 EFFECT OF ASKING ANOTHER QUESTION Counsel must not repeat the witness' answer to the prior question before asking another question. Counsel must wait until the witness has completed the answer before asking another question. If counsel asks a new question before the witness answers the pending question or an objection thereto is ruled upon, counsel will be deemed to have withdrawn the earlier question.  This applies to voir dire.

3.158 USE OF DEPOSITIONS, INTERROGATORIES AND REQUESTS FOR ADMISSION Before reading into evidence any portion of any deposition, interrogatory or request for admission, or showing any video deposition, counsel must obtain leave of court and must then advise the court and opposing counsel which pages and lines of the deposition or the numbers of the interrogatories or requests for admission are to be read. Prior to such reading, opposing counsel must be given a reasonable opportunity to read the same and interpose any objections thereto. If counsel intends to read multiple interrogatories into evidence, that counsel should make extracts of the pertinent portions of questions and answers and furnish sufficient copies for adversaries and the court before reading. Those extracts should be prepared in a form so that the question answered immediately precedes the answer and the date or other description of the particular set of interrogatories appear on the extract. The same procedure will apply to requests for admissions and responses thereto.

3.120 POLICY AGAINST INDICATION AS TO TESTIMONY Persons in the courtroom, including the parties and counsel, must not indicate, by facial expression, shaking of the head, gesturing, shouts, or other conduct their disagreement with or approval of testimony or other evidence. Counsel must so instruct parties they represent, witnesses they call, and any persons accompanying them.

3.125 OFFERS TO STIPULATE TO BE OUT OF JURY'S HEARING Offers to stipulate must be made outside the hearing of jurors. While the court is in session, counsel wishing to stipulate must first obtain leave of court and then confer with other counsel outside the hearing of the jurors.  Do not solicit a stipulation in front of the jury.

3.131 COUNSEL'S DEMONSTRATING OR DISPLAYING REPRESENTATIONS OF TESTIMONY TO THE JURY DURING PRESENTATION OF EVIDENCE Without the consent of the trial judge, counsel may not, during presentation of evidence, summarize, diagram, calculate, or outline testimony or evidence in a manner displayed to the jury. This includes, but is not limited to, using a chalkboard, paper on a bulletin board, overhead projection device, computer or other word processing device to be projected onto a screen, or any other technology.

3.47 MULTIPLE COUNSEL – MOTIONS In the absence of permission by the court, only one attorney for a party may present oral argument on a motion, application, or issue. Upon the request of a party, the court may, in its discretion, allow more than one attorney per party to argue. In making the request, counsel may present the issue of providing an opportunity for a less experienced attorney.

3.96 ADDRESSING OTHERS During trial, counsel must not exhibit familiarity with witnesses, parties or other counsel, nor address them by use of first names (except [minor] children). 

 

TENTATIVE RULING ON MOTIONS IN LIMINE

 

All rulings on motions in limine apply to all counsel, all parties, and all witnesses.  Counsel are responsible for advising their clients and witnesses of the rulings.  The court recently had a mistrial in which a witness violated a motion in limine and upon questioning, the witness had not been told of the ruling, resulting in sanctions on that counsel.  Please be sure your clients and witnesses know the rulings and comply.

DEFENDANTS’ MOTIONS IN LIMINE

MOTION IN LIMINE NO. 1 FOR AN ORDER TO PRECLUDE PLAINTIFFS’ COUNSEL, PLAINTIFFS, AND/OR ANY OF PLAINTIFFS’ WITNESSES FROM MAKING ANY “GOLDEN RULE,” OR IN ITS MORE MODERN INCARNATION, “REPTILE THEORY” ARGUMENT: Grant. There shall be no questions or argument suggesting that this case refers to “community” standards or may have a direct effect on the community or the rights of jurors.  Nor shall there be any questions or argument that the jurors are to imagine themselves in the shoes of the plaintiff.  Please review below: Allen v. Patel 117 Cal.App.5th 432, 2025 WL 3718739 (4th Dist. Ct. App. 2025).  This is controlling law.

“1. Golden Rule Violation

Before the trial, the trial court heard defendant’s MIL 1, in which defendant requested that the trial court exclude evidence and arguments based on the golden rule and reptile theory, The trial court initially deferred ruling on MIL 1, as to the golden rule, until it became an issue during the trial. Later in the trial, the trial court granted defendant’s objection to Blue’s closing argument on the ground it violated the golden rule.

During closing argument, when asking the jury to compensate Allen for her injuries, Blue requested the jury to imagine the following scenario: “There is a big limousine that drives up into the parking lot, and it parks right next to the U-Haul. And out of that limousine comes a person really well dressed, perfect suit, perfect socks, perfect shoes. Six other people and they all have black briefcases, shiny black briefcases. They walk down the corridor to Room 163, and they knock on the door and get past Mr. Mejia and Mr. Patel. And they all walk in and open the door. . . . [¶] First guy, the main guy comes, and he has this big shiny briefcase. And he sees Ms. Allen, and he opens [h]is briefcase. There is a crisp $100 bill, all of the briefcases, $6,000,000. And he shows it. . . . He says Ms. Allen, this is for you. You can have every one of these. You can do whatever you want with these. It’s yours. Take it for the rest of your life.” Defendant objected at this point, stating “[g]olden rule.” The trial court overruled the objection.

Blue continued: “So the man closes the briefcase and puts it down and says to Ms. Allen ‘You can have that, but I’m going to want something. I’m going to take every single thing that matters to you. I’m going to take your husband and all that love that you have enjoyed. I’m going to take your dignity. I’m going to take your calm. I’m going to take everything that matters to you. You will never have that again ever. And I’m going to leave this briefcase outside the door because you don’t have a choice. [¶] Now, in the beginning of this case I had mentioned compensation is an equal fair value.” Defendant again objected, stating “[g]olden rule.” The trial court sustained the objection and instructed the jury not to consider the discussion regarding “the briefcase and the money inside.”

The trial court concluded during the MNT hearing and in its statement of decision, that Blue violated the court’s order not to violate the golden rule, when Blue made the above analogy. In effect, Blue asked the jury if it would accept $6,000,000 in exchange for being put in Allen’s position. A “golden rule” argument is one where “counsel asks the jurors to place themselves in the plaintiff’s shoes and to award such damages as they would ‘charge’ to undergo equivalent pain and suffering.” (Beagle v. Vasold (1966) 65 Cal.2d 166, 182, fn. 11; see Cassim, supra, 33 Cal.4th at p. 797.)

Making a golden rule argument is improper because “[t]he jury must impartially determine pain and suffering damages based upon evidence specific to the plaintiff, as opposed to statistical data concerning the public at large. The only person whose pain and suffering is relevant in calculating a general damage award is the plaintiff. How others would feel if placed in the plaintiff’s position is irrelevant.[] It is improper, for example, for an attorney to ask jurors how much ‘they would “charge” to undergo equivalent pain and suffering.’ [Citation.]” (Loth v. Truck-A-Way Corp. (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 757, 764-765, fn. omitted.) A golden rule argument, “‘in effect asks each juror to become a personal partisan advocate for the injured party, rather than an unbiased and unprejudiced weigher of the evidence. Finally, it may tend to induce each juror to consider a higher figure than he otherwise might to avoid being considered self-abasing.’” (Id. at p. 765, quoting Neumann v. Bishop (1976) 59 Cal.App.3d 451, 484- 485.)

Here, Blue violated the golden rule during closing argument by suggesting that when considering the amount of fair value compensation Allen should receive for her losses, the jurors should imagine what it would be like to be forced to accept the loss of “every single thing that matters” to them in exchange for a briefcase full of money.

Reptile Theory

Blue also committed misconduct by violating the reptile theory doctrine during closing argument. The court made it clear during the hearing on MIL 1 that reptile theory arguments were improper. Reptile theory arguments attempt to appeal to a jury’s emotions by arguing a defendant’s conduct threatens the community’s safety. (Regalado v. Callaghan (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 582, 599.) A violation of the reptile theory occurs when counsel asks jurors to base their decisions on concerns about their own safety and the safety of the community rather than the applicable standard of care. (Id. at pp. 598- 30 599 [closing argument remarks telling jury its function was to keep the community safe were improper]; Cassim, supra, 33 Cal.4th at p. 796 [arguments appealing to the self-interests of jurors are improper because they tend to undermine juror impartiality.].)

Blue began his opening statement by stating, “I’m scared. I couldn’t sleep last night because I’m scared. It’s an enormous obligation to represent somebody who cannot represent themselves. . . . I’m shaking. My knees are shaking. . . . It’s a privilege of a lifetime . . . when you believe in somebody. My fear is that I have not done a good enough job to represent that person the way that person deserved to be represented that I have failed. And at some point, I’m going to hand this case . . . to you for you to take this case for me. And that scares me to death.”

Blue then told the jury it had “an opportunity to make a difference. We go through life, try to set an example, try and do the right thing. Maybe somebody will see. Maybe our children will see. Maybe our students will see. But seldom do we have an opportunity to defend our community, to champion our community, to stand as the last bastion for what we will tolerate for our community. Will we tolerate men coming into a room with a vulnerable woman, the most vulnerable of us and put their hands on her? Will we tolerate that? That decision is going to be your decision in just a few moments.”

Blue noted that “we are stuck with a money justice system. . . . Mr. Patel will not serve a day in jail aside from any potential charges that may be filed against him in criminal court, but that’s not for us. [¶] Here in this civil justice system, Mr. Patel will not serve a day in jail. Judge Sachs will not call the deputies to have him escorted. The  deputies won’t take Mr. Patel out to the public square and have him flogged. Mr. Patel will not be escorted to the front of the courthouse to wear a sign that says, ‘Shame on me.’ None of that is going to happen. It’s just money, dirty money.” Blue added that “all we have to ask for is money.”

Blue further told the jury: “We have the power to decide what is right. We have the power to decide what is just. We have the power to claim to the world that in this community a person cannot put their hands on another person. We have that power, and we can proclaim it. . . . [¶] So ultimately the power is yours. And that power extends into the future to protect the innocent of your community. It’s up to you. Do you want to protect the most vulnerable, the innocent? Do you want to protect the members of your community from someone just putting their hands on you?” Blue added that Allen’s attempt to stay at the Hotel without paying rent “doesn’t give a person a right to put their hands on her. . . . It’s up to you. It’s up to you to exercise that power. It’s up to you. And the opportunities are so few, so few to go and apply that power to make a change to make a real change to protect your community. And in so doing I believe that in so doing I believe fulfill your destiny. It’s why we are here. These opportunities are so few. We ought not to waste them. Fate chooses a precious few.”

Blue also told the jury that the court reporter was recording everything “because it’s a historical record. It’s a record to show what happened here on this day in this courtroom in this community. Your grandchildren’s grandchildren will be able to come here and see what happened on this day. You can see the defenders of a community tell a person you cannot put his hands on a member of the community . . . .” We conclude the trial court reasonably found during the MNT hearing and in its statement of decision that Blue violated the reptile theory by telling the jury it had “the power to claim to the world that in this community a person cannot put hands on another person.” Blue’s closing arguments improperly urged the jury to enter a verdict against defendant as a way of protecting the community from harm, in violation of the reptile theory. “

Case Number: 25VECV03017    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: T

25VECV03017 Sargsyan v Tatoyan

[TENTATIVE] ORDER: Defendant Krikor Tatoyan, M.D. Motion to Strike Portions of the First Amended Complaint is GRANTED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND.  Defendant Krikor Tatoyan, M.D. is to file an Answer to the First Amended Complaint within 10 days.

Introduction

Defendant Krikor Tatoyan, M.D. (Defendant) moved to strike portions of Plaintiff Kristine Grigoryan’s (Plaintiff) First Amended Complaint (FAC).  Defendant placed into issue the prayer for non-economic damages in the first cause of action (COA) for negligence (survival action) to the extent that it seeks pre-death pain, suffering, and emotional distress damages and the allegation as to Decedent Anna Sarsyan’s (Decedent) “great mental, physical, and nervous pain and suffering” alleged in FAC paragraph 28.

Discussion

Defendant asserted that the statute that permitted pre-death general damages expressly limited the remedy to actions filed before January 1, 2026.  (Code Civ. Proc., sec. 377.34(b).)  Defendant asserted that Plaintiff’s request for pre-death general damages is no longer permitted because Plaintiff’s survival action and wrongful death claim was alleged for the first time in the FAC, which was filed on May 14, 2026, because Decedent passed away on February 21, 2026.  (FAC par. 34.)  Plaintiff asserted that Defendant misinterprets the statute and argued that the pre-death general damages claim is properly alleged because the statute does not reference a specific COA (i.e., survival COA).  Plaintiff asserts that the statute should be read, based upon its plain language, and argues that pre-death general damages can be alleged based upon when the “action or proceeding” was filed.  The instant action/proceeding was filed on May 30, 2025.  However, the Court disagrees with Plaintiff’s interpretation of the statute because Plaintiff’s interpretation is limited to the words “action or proceeding”.  The complete phrase in the statute’s subdivision (b) states: “in an action or proceeding by a decedent’s personal representative or successor in interest on the decedent’s cause of action (italics added)”.  (Code Civ. Proc., sec. 377.34(b).)  Plaintiff’s interpretation of the subdivision attempts to broaden the phrase of “action or proceeding” by ignoring the phrase that comes directly after it – “by a decedent’s personal representative or successor in interest”.  The filing of the Complaint on May 30, 2025, was brought by then-living Anna Sargsyan, as an individual.  The Complaint was not filed  by Anna Sargsyan’s successor in interest, Kristine Grigoryan, as is pled in the FAC.  Kristine Grigoryan in her capacity as Decedent’s successor in interest did not exist when the Complaint was filed because Decedent was still alive.  Because the FAC was filed by Kristine Grigoryan, as Decedent’s successor in interest, after January 1, 2026, Decedent’s pre-death general damages are untimely filed per Code of Civil Procedure, section 377.34(b).  The Court finds Defendant’s interpretation of the statute to be persuasive.

Plaintiff then argued that the survival claim relates back to the filing of the Complaint.  However, the Court disagrees with Plaintiff.  Preliminarily, Plaintiff failed to present any legal authority to support the contention that the relation back doctrine applies to the statute.  Even if considered, the relation back doctrine does not apply to the facts of this case when read in conjunction with the statute.  The statute expressly requires the survival action to be “filed” by Decedent’s successor in interest before January 1, 2026.  Relating back the FAC to the original Complaint would not change the fact that Decedent filed the Complaint while she was alive and Plaintiff, as the successor in interest, did not exist when the Complaint was filed, specifically because Decedent was alive.  This survival action is outside the bounds identified in the subdivision (b) of the statute. 

The motion to strike is GRANTED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND. 

IT IS SO ORDERED, CLERK TO GIVE NOTICE.

26VECV02454 TORRES V PERIMETER SECURITY

[TENTATIVE] ORDER: Defendant Total Quality Logistics, LLC’s Demurrer to the Complaint is SUSTAINED WITH 10 DAYS LEAVE TO AMEND as to the fifth cause of action and otherwise OVERRULED.  Leave to amend is limited to curing the pleading defect in the fifth cause of action and not to add new parties or causes of action.

Introduction

Defendant Total Quality Logistics LLC (Defendant) demurred to Plaintiff Jose Esteban Medina Torres’ (Plaintiff) Complaint.  The demurrer placed into issue the four causes of action alleged against Defendant: the first COA for negligence, the fourth COA for negligent undertaking, the fifth COA for negligence per se, and the sixth COA for negligent selection, retention, and supervision of motor carrier/broker negligence. 

Discussion

Defendant, a broker[1], argued that all four COAs are preempted by the Federal Aviation Administration Authorization Act (FAAAA) (49 U.S.C. sec. 14501(c)(1).)  The operative test in California is whether the state claim (1) relates to a broker's prices, routes, or services with respect to the transportation of property (if so, it is preempted), and (2) if preempted, whether it concerns motor vehicle safety.  (Casarez v. Irigoyen Farms, Inc. (2025) 114 Cal.App.5th 873;  Miller v. C.H. Robinson Worldwide, Inc. (9th Cir. 2020) 976 F.3d 1016, cert. denied, 142 S.Ct. 2866 (2022).)  Plaintiff, a motor carrier,[2] alleged that he was injured while Co-Defendant Perimeter Security Group, LLC (PSG) (i.e., the shipper) was loading the tractor-trailer.  Plaintiff alleged that Defendant is also liable for the injury because of Defendant’s affirmative act during PSG’s loading process.  Defendant, a freight broker, “is in the business of arranging freight transportation for compensation between shippers (i.e., PSG) and motor carriers (i.e., Plaintiff)”.  (Compl. par. 6.)  During the loading of the cargo, the responsibility of PSG, Plaintiff alleged that Defendant told PSG to “just proceed” with the loading of Plaintiff’s tractor-trailer.  PSG allegedly contacted Defendant during the loading process to notify Defendant of the unsafe condition of the load and to request a different motor carrier.  (Compl. par. 24-26.)  Despite this notice from PSG, Defendant told PSG to proceed with loading Plaintiff’s motor carrier.  The FAAAA preempts state negligence claims related to the “price, route, or service” of a “broker … with respect to the transportation of property”. (49 U.S.C. sec. 14501(c)(1).)  The “price, route, or service” of a freight broker is the “arranging” or selection of a motor carrier.  Plaintiff’s claim of injury is within the scope of a freight broker’s business – arranging/selecting the motor carrier (i.e., Plaintiff) – because Defendant chose Plaintiff and no other motor carriers were available when PSG requested a different motor carrier.  Because Plaintiff’s claim against Defendant involved the selection of the motor carrier (i.e., Plaintiff), Plaintiff’s claim is preempted under the FAAAA. 

However, Plaintiff asserted that the Safety Exception in the FAAAA applies.  (49 U.S.C. sec. 14501(c)(2)(A).)  The Safety Exception provides that the preemption provision "shall not restrict the safety regulatory authority of a State with respect to motor vehicles."  In the Complaint, Plaintiff alleged additional facts to plead that PSG notified Defendant of the unsafe conditions of the load; Defendant telling PSG to “just proceed”; and Plaintiff being “physically struck, crushed, impacted and/or violently engaged by cargo, cargo-securement equipment (including straps), vehicles, equipment, and/or personnel involved in the loading”.  Plaintiff’s claim also involves the safety of the load.  Because the safety of the load is alleged to be at issue, the Court finds that the Safey Exception applies and Plaintiff’s claim is outside the preemption.

Defendant’s argument as to preemption is not persuasive.

Defendant asserted that the first COA for negligence failed to allege sufficient facts as to duty.  Defendant argued that they are the freight broker and did not own, control, operate, or possess PSG’s premise or the cargo loading process at PSG’s facility.  However, the claim against Defendant is not based upon any premise liability/dangerous condition of the property but is based upon Defendant’s control.  Plaintiff alleged that PSG notified Defendant of an unsafe condition as to the load.  Plaintiff alleged that PSG requested a different motor carrier but none was available and Defendant exercised control by telling PSG to “just proceed”.  Defendant’s argument ignores the fact allegation as to their alleged control, not of the premise/facility, but the loading process that involved an unsafe condition.  Because of these facts, there are sufficient facts to plead a duty of care grounded upon Defendant’s control over the loading process and Defendant’s role in the injury that go beyond their capacity as a mere freight broker.

The demurrer to the first COA for negligence is OVERRULED.

Defendant asserted that there are insufficient facts alleged to support the fourth COA for negligent undertaking.  Defendant again argued that they have no duty as to PSG’s premise or workplace safety/loading process/protection of invitees because Defendant is a freight broker and the phone call was from PSG was a “routine logistical discussion”.  As reviewed above, the fact allegations are sufficient to plead that Defendant stepped outside of their role as a freight broker and/or their service of selecting/arranging a motor carrier.  Further, Defendant’s characterization of the telephone call between PSG and Defendant lacks any supportive facts within the four corners of the Complaint to show that the phone call was a “routine logistical discussion”.  Because the argument would rely upon facts outside the pleading, the argument is not persuasive for a demurrer. 

Defendant asserted that the theory of negligent undertaking failed to plead facts as to each pleading element.  To plead negligent undertaking, Plaintiff must allege: (1) That defendant, voluntarily or for a charge, rendered services to a third person; (2) That these services were of a kind that defendant should have recognized as needed for the protection of plaintiff; (3) That defendant failed to exercise reasonable care in rendering these services; (4) That defendant’s failure to exercise reasonable care was a substantial factor in causing harm to plaintiff; and (5)(a) That defendant’s failure to use reasonable care added to the risk of harm; or (5)(b) That defendant’s services were rendered to perform a duty that the third person owed to third persons including  plaintiff; or (5)(c) That plaintiff suffered harm because of third person or  plaintiff relied on  defendant’s services.  (CACI no. 450C.)  Defendant again argued that the alleged undertaking (i.e., the phone call between PSG and Defendant and the “just proceed” comment) was merely a routine logistical discussion and not an undertaking.  However, the facts alleged in the Complaint do not support Defendant’s characterization of the phone call.  The phone call is alleged to be a request from the shipper for a different motor carrier because the cargo to be loaded could not safely be placed upon Plaintiff’s tractor-trailer.  Whether the phone call/direction from Defendant was “routine” or an administrative call cannot be determined based upon the facts alleged in the Complaint and would require a review of additional facts outside the four corners of the Complaint.  The argument, again, is improper for the pleading stage.  The facts alleged in the Complaint are sufficient to plead an undertaking. 

Defendant argued that there are insufficient facts alleged to plead that Defendant’s services were of a kind that Defendant should have recognized as needed for the protection of Plaintiff.  However, Plaintiff expressly alleged that PSG expressly notified that the loading of the cargo on Plaintiff’s carrier was unsafe.  The fact allegation is sufficient to plead facts to show that Defendant should have recognized that the service of selecting another motor carrier was needed for the protection of Plaintiff. 

Defendant argued that there are insufficient facts alleged to identify any negligent performance of the undertaking.  However, the facts alleged that Defendant’s service was to select the motor carrier and implicit within that service is that the correct or appropriate motor carrier would be selected for the cargo to be loaded upon.  PSG notified Defendant that the conditions of the loading of the cargo on the motor carrier were unsafe and it is inferred that the incorrect motor carrier was chosen by Defendant.  The improper selection of the motor carrier and/or failure to send a substitute motor carrier is sufficient fact pleading to allege negligent performance of the undertaking.

Defendant argued that the last pleading element is not sufficiently supported by fact pleading.  However, Plaintiff expressly alleged that the failure to send a substitute motor carrier and Defendant’s instruction to PSG to proceed with loading of the cargo increased the risk of harm due to the unsafe condition of the load.  The alleged failure to substitute the motor carrier is sufficient to plead Defendant’s failure to exercise reasonable care and the failure is alleged to have increased the risk of harm since the unsafe loading of the cargo allegedly injured Plaintiff.  Defendant argued that the increase in the risk of harm is attributable to PSG.  However, the issue of whether the alleged increase of harm is attributable to Defendant or PSG is an issue not proper for a demurrer because analysis would require review of facts outside the four corners of the pleading.  The arguments against the fourth COA are unpersuasive and Plaintiff sufficiently pled supportive facts to plead negligent undertaking.

The demurrer to the fourth COA is OVERRULED.

Plaintiff submitted to Defendant’s arguments against the fifth COA for negligence per se and Plaintiff requested leave to amend.

The demurrer to the fifth COA is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

Defendant asserted that the sixth COA for negligent selection, retention, and supervision of motor carrier/broker negligence failed to plead a duty owed and that Defendant is only a freight broker without any duties related to PSG’s premise or the safety of PSG’s loading process or control over the selection of the motor carrier/Plaintiff.   The argument presented is the same argument reviewed under the first COA for negligence.  The argument was not persuasive under the first COA and is not persuasive against the sixth COA based upon the same analysis provided above.  The allegations as to the sixth COA are sufficiently plead with supportive facts.

The demurrer to the sixth COA is OVERRULED.

IT IS SO ORDERED, CLERK TO GIVE NOTICE.

[2] “Motor Carrier” is defined as “a person providing motor vehicle transportation for compensation”.  (49 U.S.C. sec. 13102(14).)
