[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING MOTION TO VACATE ORDER

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

June 25, 2026

 

On March 18, 2024, Plaintiff filed this action against Defendants Lori Abramowitz (as Trustee for the Gross Family Children’s Trust II), Maxx Abramowitz, and Adam Levin.[1]

On May 8, 2026, Defendants filed an amended motion to vacate the Court’s March 27, 2026 Minute Order granting Plaintiff’s discovery motions and imposing $12,350 in monetary sanctions.

DISCUSSION

Defendants argue that the March 27 Order must be vacated because Maxx and Adam were not served with the motions to compel discovery, the notice of hearing, and Plaintiff’s Notice of Non-Opposition.  (Motion at p. 6.)  All Defendants were not served with the March 27, 2026 Order.  (Ibid.)

A.        Relevant Timeline

On December 9, 2025, the Court granted Attorney Reynolds’s unopposed motions to be relieved as counsel for Defendants.  The Court also scheduled an April 6, 2026 Order to Show Cause Re: Striking the Answer of Lori as Trustee and Entering Its Default for Failure to Retain New Legal Representation and an Order to Show Cause Re: Dismissal of Cross-Complainant.

On December 15, 2025, Attorney Reynolds filed a Proof of Service of the Court’s orders on each Defendant at their last-known addresses.  Lori was served by mail at 6812 Willow Wood Dr., Apt. 802; Maxx was served by mail at 6812 Willow Wood Dr., Apt. 802; and Adam was served by mail at 2 Catamaran St., Apt. 1.  Each Defendant was also served by mail “c/o Oreva Capital” at 10990 Wilshire Boulevard, 18th Floor and by email.

On December 24, 2025, Plaintiff filed six discovery motions (two for each Defendant).  The Proofs of Service reflect service by mail at each Defendant’s address that was provided by Attorney Reynolds.

On March 27, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s six unopposed motions to compel discovery responses and imposed sanctions in the amount of $12,350.00.

On April 6, 2026, the day of the OSCs, Defendants filed Substitutions of Counsel, substituting their self-representation for new representation by Attorney Peake.  The same day, for unreleased reasons, the Court continued the OSCs.

On May 4, 2026, Defendants filed a motion to vacate the March 27, 2026 Order, and on May 8, 2026, they filed an amended motion.

B.        It Is Undisputed That Lori’s Address Is Correct.

Although Lori contends that she “was not served with and never received any of Plaintiff’s December 24, 2025 motions to compel discovery responses or requests for monetary sanctions” and was not served with notice of the hearing, Defendants admit that Lori’s address is correct.  (Lori Decl. ¶¶ 4-5, 8-14; see Motion at pp. 3-4.)  Accordingly, Plaintiff’s service of the motions by mail at that address was proper.

Only Maxx and Adam argue that the addresses provided by Attorney Reynolds are “stale” and unconfirmed, rendering any service ineffective.  (See Motion at p. 5.)

C.        There Is No Due Process Problem as to Lori as Trustee.

Defendants argue that the March 27 Order should be vacated on due process grounds because Lori, as a trustee, could not appear pro se and could not oppose the discovery motions.  (Motion at pp. 10-11.)

The Court’s December 9, 2025 minute order relieving counsel explained that Lori, as trustee, would need to retain new counsel.  The signed Form MC-053 order also warned that a trustee “may not in most cases represent yourself,” and a trustee “SHOULD IMMEDIATELY SEEK LEGAL ADVICE REGARDING LEGAL REPRESENTATION.  Failure to retain an attorney may lead to an order striking the pleadings or to the entry of a default judgment.”  Lori does not deny receiving notice of these orders.

Lori had approximately three and a half months to retain new counsel for the trust before the March 24, 2026 hearing on the discovery motions and the Court’s March 27 Order.  She did not do so.

D.        Maxx and Adam Were Served at Their “Current” Addresses Under the Rules of Court.

Defendants observe that the order granting counsel’s withdrawal mandates, “If the client’s current address is known, service on the client must hereafter be made at that address unless otherwise ordered in item 13.  If the current address is not known, service must be made according to Code of Civil Procedure section 1011 (b) and rule 3.252 of the California Rules of Court.”  (Motion at pp. 4-5; see Reply at pp. 2-3.)

Under those service rules, “[i]f the party’s residence is not known, any attempt of service pursuant to this subdivision may be made by delivering the notice or papers to the clerk of the court, for that party.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1011, subd. (b)(3).)  The notice or papers “must be enclosed in an envelope addressed to the party in care of the clerk or the judge,” with the following information included on the back of the envelope: “Service is being made under Code of Civil Procedure section 1011(b) on a party whose residence address is unknown.”  (California Rules of Court, rule 3.252.)

As Plaintiff notes, the distinction between “current” and “last known” addresses is set forth in the Rules of Court for motions to be relieved as counsel.  (Motion at p. 7.)  As used in at context, “‘current’ means that the address was confirmed within 30 days before the filing of the motion to be relieved.”  (California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362(d).)

Attorney Reynolds’s declarations in support of his Motions to Be Relieved as Counsel (filed on October 28, 2025) explained his efforts to confirm Defendants’ contact information and locate a current address.  He conducted Westlaw Public Records searches that were “re-run and updated on 10/23/25,” and he was “unable to locate more current addresses or phone numbers after making reasonable efforts to do so within 30 days before filing of the motion.”  He also stated that he would (1) serve Defendants by email “at the same email addresses they have used to correspond with counsel over the course of counsel’s representation of Defendants,” (2) serve them by mail at their business address at Oreva Capital, and (3) notify Defendants about the motion via telephone.  Thus, through his declaration, Attorney Reynolds did confirm that Defendants’ “last known” addresses were their “current” addresses under the Rules of Court, “confirmed within 30 days before the filing of the motion to be relieved.”[2]  (California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362(d).)

After counsel’s thorough service of the December 9, 2025 orders relieving counsel (with the information also communicated by mail at their business address, by email, and by telephone), no Defendant denies receiving notice of the December 9, 2025 order.  Furthermore, all Defendants filed Substitutions of Counsel on the date of the OSCs that were set via that order, indicating that they were aware of the deadline for Lori retaining counsel as trustee—again demonstrating that Defendants received notice of the December 9, 2025 orders.

Thereafter, after counsel’s withdrawal, it was Defendants’ duty to inform the Court and all parties of changes to their contact information.  (California Rules of Court, rule 2.200.)  “The ‘person to be served’ has the burden of notifying the court of any change of address, and failure so to do does not enable him to claim improper notice.”  (Bethlahmy v. Customcraft Industries, Inc. (1961) 192 Cal.App.2d 308, 310.)

In sum, Attorney Reynolds provided Defendants’ contact information when he sought to be relieved as counsel.  Defendants received notice of the order granting that motion.  Defendants did not update their contact information any time thereafter.  Plaintiff served his discovery motions on Defendants at their mailing addresses that were on file in this action.

Defendants have not demonstrated that they are entitled to their requested relief.

E.        Defendants Are Granted a Thirty-Day Extension to Comply with the March 27 Order.

Defendants observe that the Court Clerk incorrectly served the March 27 Order on Defendants’ former counsel via electronic service.  (Motion at pp. 9-10.)  Defendants were not individually served by mail.  Accordingly, because Defendants did not receive proper notice of the March 27 Order, the Court will extend those deadlines.

Defendants are ordered to provide supplemental responses to (1) Form Interrogatory Nos. 9.2, 15.1, and 17.1; and (2) Requests for Production Nos. 1, 2, and 4 within thirty days of this order.  Defendants are also ordered to pay sanctions in the sum of $12,350.00, jointly and severally, to Plaintiff by within thirty days of this order.

CONCLUSION

The motion to vacate is DENIED.

Defendants are ordered to comply with the Court’s March 27, 2026 Order within thirty days.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING DEMURRER; GRANTING MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

June 25, 2026

 

On October 23, 2025, Plaintiff Irvin Singh filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”) against Defendant General Motors LLC arising from Plaintiff’s purchase of an allegedly defective vehicle.

On November 11, 2025, Defendant filed a demurrer to the fifth cause of action for Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment and a motion to strike punitive damages.

DEMURRER

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true.  (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.)

A.        The Fifth Cause of Action is Not Facially Barred by the Statute of Limitations.

Defendant argues that the fifth cause of action is barred by the three-year statute of limitations.  (Demurrer at pp. 8-10; see Code Civ. Proc., § 338, subd. (d).)

To sustain a demurrer on the statute of limitations, the running of the statute must appear clearly and affirmatively on the face of the complaint.  (Geneva Towers Ltd. Partnership v. City of San Francisco (2003) 29 Cal.4th 769, 781.)  “‘[I]t is not enough that the complaint might be time-barred.  [Citation.]’”  (Ibid.)

Plaintiff alleges that “the running of the limitation periods have been tolled by, inter alia, the following doctrines or rules: equitable tolling, the discovery rule, equitable estoppel, the repair rule, and/or class action tolling (e.g., the American Pipe rule).”  (FAC ¶ 22.)

Plaintiff does not allege any facts for equitable tolling, equitable estoppel, class action tolling so these cannot be bases for tolling.  (See Demurrer at pp. 8-10.)  Accordingly, only the repair rule and Plaintiff’s delayed discovery of Defendant’s concealment is at issue.

“The general rule is that the statute of limitations begins to run when the wrongful act is done, even though the plaintiff is ignorant of his or her cause of action or of the identity of the wrongdoer.”  (Snow v. A. H. Robins Co. (1985) 165 Cal.App.3d 120, 127.)  The running of the statute of limitations may be postponed on certain causes of action until the time of discovery of the facts.  (Ibid.)  “With respect to actions based on fraud, the statute of limitations is tolled whenever plaintiff is able to show the defendant fraudulently concealed facts which would have led him to discover his potential cause of action.”  (Id. at pp. 127-128.)

Plaintiff alleges that on December 27, 2016, he entered into a warranty contract with Defendant.  (FAC ¶ 6.)  Before Plaintiff purchased the vehicle, Defendant “was well aware and knew that the 8-speed Transmission installed on the Vehicle was defective but failed to disclose this fact to Plaintiff prior to and at the time of sale and thereafter.”  (FAC ¶ 60.)  “Plaintiff discovered Defendant’s wrongful conduct alleged herein shortly before the filing of the complaint” on December 30, 2024.  (FAC ¶ 23.)

The FAC does not facially show that the statute of limitations bars this cause of action.  It is not clear from the face of the FAC when Plaintiff should have discovered that Defendant concealed information about the vehicle’s defects.

The demurrer is overruled on this ground.

B.        The Fifth Cause of Action Does Not Allege Fraud with Specificity.

Defendant argues that the fifth cause of action does not allege sufficient facts, including a duty to disclose.  (Demurrer at pp. 10-13.)

Fraud based on concealment requires that “(1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.”  (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 310-311 (Bigler-Engler).)

An essential element of intentional concealment includes the duty to disclose, which must be based upon a transaction, or a special relationship, between plaintiff and defendant.  (Id. at p. 314.)  “There are ‘four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.’”  (Id. at p. 311.)  “[O]ther than the first instance, in which there must be a fiduciary relationship between the parties, ‘the other three circumstances in which nondisclosure may be actionable presuppose[ ] the existence of some other relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise. . . . “[W]here material facts are known to one party and not to the other, failure to disclose them is not actionable fraud unless there is some relationship between the parties which gives rise to a duty to disclose such known facts.”  [Citation.]’  [Citation.]”  (Hoffman v. 162 North Wolfe LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 1178, 1187 (Hoffman).)  “Even under the strict rules of common law pleading, one of the canons was that less particularity is required when the facts lie more in the knowledge of the opposite party.”  (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn., Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1256, 1384.)

Plaintiff alleges that Defendant knew about the engine defect before the purchase.  (E.g., FAC ¶¶ 59-60; see FAC ¶ 61 [describing the defect].)  Because of the defect, “has suffered and will continue to suffer actual damages.”  (FAC ¶ 71.)  Plaintiff did not know about these defects and problems, and Defendant, who had superior knowledge of the defect, did not disclose the defects when Plaintiff purchased the vehicle.  (E.g., FAC ¶¶ 60, 64-67.)  The allegations are specific enough to allege the information that was concealed and the danger posed.  (See Jones v. ConocoPhillips Co. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1187, 1199-1200.)

However, Plaintiff does not allege facts showing the existence of a relationship between the parties that gives rise to a duty to disclose facts.  (See Hoffman, supra, 228 Cal.App.4th at p. 1187.)  Plaintiff alleges only that Defendant “was in a superior position from various internal sources to know (or should have known) the true state of facts about the material defects contained in vehicles equipped with the 8-speed Transmission.”  (FAC ¶ 66.)  There are no facts showing why Defendant’s knowledge was exclusive or that Defendant intended to defraud.  Indeed, Plaintiff alleges that there were “early consumer complaints about the Transmission Defect,” suggesting that the knowledge was in fact public.  (FAC ¶¶ 62, 66.)

Moreover, in a transaction that does not involve fiduciary or confidential relations, the “transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.”  (Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at pp. 311-312.)  Plaintiff alleges no such direct transaction with and direct concealment by Defendant.

The demurrer is sustained on this ground.

MOTION TO STRIKE

The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subds. (a)-(b).)

Defendant moves to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages.  A plaintiff can recover punitive damages in tort cases where “the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.”  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).)  “The mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages.  [Citation.]  Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim.  [Citation.]”  (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166, fn. omitted.)  Additionally, a plaintiff is not entitled to recover both punitive damages and civil penalties under Song-Beverly.  (Troensegaard v. Silvercrest Industries, Inc. (1985) 175 Cal.App.3d 218, 228.)

The Court concurrently sustains the demurrer to the fraud cause of action, and there are no other specific facts showing oppression or malice.

The motion to strike is granted.

CONCLUSION

The demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED.

The motion to strike is GRANTED.

Plaintiff has already had an opportunity to amend, and he has not shown how else he can remedy the FAC’s deficiencies.  Accordingly, no further leave to amend is granted.

Defendant is ordered to file an answer to the remainder of the FAC within 10 days.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

On March 6, 2025, Plaintiff Jeffery Stephens filed this action against Defendants SRO Combo Apartments LLC and Single Room Occupancy Housing Corporation.

On August 22, 2025, Defendants filed a motion to strike.

The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subds. (a)-(b).)

Defendants seek to strike paragraphs 52 and 67, as well as the prayer for exemplary and punitive damages.  A plaintiff can recover punitive damages in tort cases where “the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.”  (Civ. Code § 3294, subd. (a).)  “The mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages.  [Citation.]  Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim.  [Citation.]”  (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166, fn. omitted.)  A corporate employer can be liable for punitive damages only when an officer, director, or managing agent of the corporation authorized or ratified the wrongful conduct or was personally guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (b).)  The facts about exposed wires, an inope4rable toilet, broken doors, cockroaches, bedbugs, standing gray water, broken smoke detectors, feces in common areas, mold, and other conditions—if proven—would support a finding of oppression or malice.  (Complaint ¶¶ 16-24.)  Allegations that Defendants knew of the conditions and failed to act—particularly through notice from the Los Angeles Housing Department (see Complaint ¶¶ 16-17, 29-30)—could also support a finding that Defendants’ agents ratified the conduct.  The motion is denied for these items.

Defendants also seek to strike paragraph 53 and the prayer for attorney fees.  Defendants argue that because Plaintiff “did not attach a copy of the operative Lease Agreement, it is not possible to determine whether he was required to mediate or arbitrate disputes before filing this action, whether attorney fees were capped at a specific amount, or whether Plaintiff forfeited his entitlement to attorney fees when he filed this lawsuit.”  Plaintiff alleges, “Subject to the terms of Plaintiff’s lease and/or applicable law, Plaintiff is entitled to an award of attorney’s fees and costs.”  (Complaint ¶ 53.)  If the lease does not provide for attorney fees, then as a matter of law, they will not be recoverable pursuant to the contract.  But there is no harm or prejudice in Plaintiff alleging the possibility of contractual attorney fees.  Moreover, attorney fees are available for Plaintiff’s claim under Civil Code section 1942.4.  The motion is denied for these items.

The motion to strike is DENIED.

Defendants are ordered to file an answer within 10 days.
