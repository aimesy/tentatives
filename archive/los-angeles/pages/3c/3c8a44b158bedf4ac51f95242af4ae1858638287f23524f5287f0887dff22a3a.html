Tentative Ruling

Tallen & Tyce Development, Inc. v. L.K. Plumbing & Heating Inc., et al., Case No. 24SMCV01259

Plaintiff’s Motion for Determination of Good Faith Settlement

Hearing Date: September 22, 2026

Background

This is a construction defect case. Plaintiff Tallen & Tyce Development, Inc. (“Tallen & Tyce”) sues Defendants L.K. Plumbing & Heating Inc. (“L.K.”) for negligence, breach of express and implied warranty, strict liability, and breach of contract, alleging that L.K.’s work on a construction project at 1033 Somera Road caused property damage.

On March 18, 2024, Tallen & Tyce filed the complaint.

On May 16, 2024, L.K. filed a cross-complaint for indemnity against Plug It In Systems, Inc. (“Plug It In”), Arbuckle Electric Motors, Inc., Conery Manufacturing, Inc, and Zoeller Pump Company.

On August 12, 2024, Plug It In filed a cross-complaint for indemnity against Moes 1-50.

On August 26, 2026, Tallen & Tyce filed this motion for determination of good faith settlement between Tallen & Tyce, Plug It In, Arbuckle Electric Motors, Inc., and Conery Manufacturing, Inc. On September 9, 2026, L.K. filed opposition. On September 15, 2026, Tallen & Tyce filed a reply.

Arguments

Tallen & Tyce propose a settlement with Plug It In, Arbuckle Electric Motors, Inc., Conery Manufacturing, Inc., and Zoeller Pump Company (“settling defendants”) for $200,000. Plug It In will contribute $100,000, Arbuckle Electric Motors, Inc. will contribute $50,000, Conery Manufacturing will contribute $30,000 and Zoeller Pump Company will contribute $20,000. Tallen & Tyce argue that this settlement was reached in good faith and that it is proportionate to the settling defendants’ liability for Tallen & Tyce’s total estimated damages of $596,577.05. (Motion for Determination of Good Faith Settlement, p. 5.) Tallen & Tyce argues that this action arises out of flooding that occurred at the subject property on February 3, 2024 as the result of a rain storm. The day before the rain, L.K. replaced water pumps and Plug It In sent an electrician to confirm functionality. Tallen & Tyce argues that the damage was done either because the pumps failed or the electrical work on the pumps failed.

L.K. does not oppose Tallen & Tyce’s settlement with Arbuckle Eelctric Motors, Inc., Conery Manufacturing, Inc., or Zoeller Pump Company. L.K. only opposes the settlement with Plug It In. In opposition, L.K. argues that Plug It In is potentially entirely liable for Tallen & Tyce’s damages because L.K. replaced the pumps and verified that they were working On February 2, 2024. L.K. argues that Tallen & Tyce’s evidence that Plug It In made electrical repairs on the pumps before the rain started is self-serving because there are no independent witnesses who would confirm that Plug It In made it to the job site that afternoon. L.K. argues that Plug It In was supposed to come earlier in the day, while L.K. was still at the site, and never appeared. L.K. argues that its expert testimony shows that Plug It In is liable.  

In reply, Tallen & Tyce argues that Plug It In’s contribution to the settlement is not wildly disproportionate and that its evidence is not one-sided. Tallen & Tyce argue that a Plug It In electrician arrived at the property after L.K. left, so  both sides have evidence that the other side was responsible for the flooding. Considering the damages at issue, Tallen & Tyce argues that the Tech-Bilt factors favor a determination of good faith settlement.

Motion for Determination Good Faith Settlement

In any action in which two or more defendants are joint tortfeasors, a “good faith” settlement by one of these defendants with the plaintiff bars the other defendants from seeking contribution from the settling defendant.¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 877.) The settlement shall also “reduce the claims against the others” by the amount stipulated in the settlement.¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 877(a).)¿ Any of the parties in such an action is “entitled to a hearing on the issue of the good faith of a settlement entered into by the plaintiff” and the alleged joint tortfeasor, “upon giving notice in the manner provided in subdivision (b) of Section 1005.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 877.6(a)(1).)¿¿¿ 

“The determination as to whether a settlement is in good faith is a matter left to the discretion of the trial court.” (Mattco Forge, Inc. v. Arthur Young & Co. (1995) 38 Cal.App.4th 1337, 1349.)¿ It may base its determination on affidavits, and other evidence admitted in its discretion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 877.6(b).) The factors to determine whether a settlement is in “good faith,” include, but are not limited to:  

[A] rough approximation of plaintiffs’ total recovery and the settlor's proportionate liability, the amount paid in settlement, the allocation of settlement proceeds among plaintiffs, and a recognition that a settlor should pay less in settlement than he would if he were found liable after a trial. Other relevant considerations include the financial conditions and insurance policy limits of settling defendants, as well as the existence of collusion, fraud, or tortious conduct aimed to injure the interests of nonsettling defendants. (Citation.) 

(Tech-Bilt, Inc. v. Woodward-Clyde & Associates (1985) 38 Cal.3d 488, 499 (Tech-Bilt).)¿ 

Opposing parties bear the burden of proof when arguing lack of good faith. (Ibid.)

Discussion

L.K. submits evidence that after the flood a neutral third party determined that a float switch electrical failure caused the flooding. LK submits evidence that when it left the property, the switch was functioning. (Muench Decl., ¶¶ 13-14.) Tallen & Tyce’s evidence in support of its motion shows that L.K. remains liable under its contract with Tallen & Tyce and that the failure is not clearly attributable to Plug It In, who also verified the electrical work before the rain started. (Marton Declaration, ¶ 11.)  

Of the proposed $200,000 settlement, ½ will be paid by Plug It In, and ½ will be paid by the other settling defendants. Though Plug It In’s share of the settlement represents only 1/5th of Tallen & Tyce’s claimed damages, Plug It In is contributing half of the total settlement, which correlates to their potential liability as compared to the other settling defendants. The proposed settlement is close to 40% of the claimed damages, which is a reasonable settlement amount in recognition that a settlor should pay less than if he were found liable at trial. There is also no evidence of collusion or tortious conduct during settlement negotiations or any financial condition that would show undue pressure to settle or bad faith. The only factor that cuts against granting good faith settlement is that Plut It In’s settlement contribution is only 1/10th of their liability insurance limit. However, no settlement in this matter would have reached Plug It In’s $1,000,000 policy limit—Tallen & Tyce’s total estimated damages are only 60% of that amount.

Conclusion

Tallen & Tyce’s Motion for Determination of Good Faith Settlement is GRANTED.

Swamp Capital, LLC v. Devon Wheeler, et al., Case No. 25SMCV03822

Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to Amend Complaint

Hearing Date: September 22, 2026

Background

 Plaintiff Swamp Capital, LLC (“Plaintiff”) sues defendants Wheeler (“Wheeler”), Handal, Erba Markets, Inc. (“Erba”), and Los Angeles Wellness Center (“LAWC”) (collectively, “Defendants”) for breach of contract, breach of fiduciary duty, fraud, and accounting. Plaintiff alleges it owned LAWC’s dispensary license and contracted to transfer ownership to defendants Wheeler, Handal and Erba for monthly minimum payments. Plaintiff alleges this agreement was memorialized via a memorandum of understanding (the “MOU”), which defendants breached by failing to make payments. On July 31, 2025, this Court ruled on Plaintiff’s ex parte TRO seeking to prevent defendants from exercising control over LAWC. Plaintiff asserted that a TRO was necessary to prevent waste and that defendants incurred tax liabilities due to mismanagement, resulting in a temporary suspension of LAWC’s license. The Court denied the ex parte TRO for lack of exigent circumstances and lack of evidence of probability of success on the merits. The Court found that an accounting must occur before a TRO can issue, and as the parties are exchanging financial documents, Plaintiff may bring a noticed motion for preliminary injunction, if appropriate.

The Court set an OSC re: preliminary injunction for September 8, 2025, and ordered the parties to provide plaintiff with full and transparent access to LAWC’s on site accounting records.

On September 8, 2025, the Court heard oral argument, but because the Court had not had an opportunity to review Plaintiff’s reply papers, the Court continued the matter to September 15, 2025. The Court also gave Wheeler permission to file a sur-reply to address arguments made at the September 8, 2025, hearing. On September 12, 2025, the Court granted a limited preliminary injunction to enforce the MOU but did not grant Plaintiff’s request for a preliminary injunction prohibiting misconduct. (Minute Order (09/12/2025), p. 10.)

On April 22, 2026, the Court denied Wheeler’s Motion to Modify Preliminary Injunction.

On April 30, 2026, Swamp Capital filed an amendment to the complaint naming the City of Los Angeles Department of Cannabis Regulation as a Defendant.

On June 30, 2026, the City of Los Angeles Department of Cannabis filed a demurrer to the complaint.

On August 25, 2026, Swamp Capital filed this Motion for Leave to Amend Complaint. As of September 16, 2026, the Court has not received opposition to this motion.

Motion for Leave to Amend

The trial court has discretion to allow amendments to pleadings in the furtherance of justice. (Code Civ. Proc. § 473, subd. (a)(1).) “Any judge, at any time before or after commencement of trial, in the furtherance of justice, and upon such terms as may be proper, may allow the amendment of any pleading.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 576.) “There is a policy of great liberality in permitting amendments to the pleadings at any stage of the proceeding.” (Berman v. Bromberg (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 936, 945 [citations omitted].) “An application to amend a pleading is addressed to the trial judge’s sound discretion.”  (Ibid. [citations omitted].) A judge may deny a motion for leave to amend where a moving party has been dilatory in seeking leave to amend and such delay has prejudiced the opposing party. (Hirsa v. Superior Court (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 486, 490 (“Hirsa”).) “It is difficult to understand how [a named defendant] can be prejudiced by amendment to add an additional theory of liability against it.” (Ibid.) 

“[I]t is an abuse of discretion to deny leave to amend where the opposing party [is] not misled or prejudiced by the amendment.” (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1048 (“Kittredge”).) Prejudice exists where the amendment would require delaying the trial, resulting in loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation. (Solit v. Tokai Bank, Ltd. New York Branch (1999) 68 Cal.App.4th 1435, 1448 (“Solit”).)  

A motion for leave to amend must include a separate declaration specifying: “(1) The effect of the amendment; (2) Why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) When the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) The reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier.” (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324(b).) A motion for leave to amend must “[i]nclude a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments.” (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324(a)(1).) Moreover, a motion to amend must “[s]tate what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed” to be deleted or added, if any, and must set forth the page, paragraph, and line number of such allegations. (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324(a)(2)-(3).)  

Discussion

Swamp Capital seeks leave to amend the complaint to allege facts that occurred or were discovered after it filed the original complaint, to substitute DOE defendants, and to add allegations against the City of Los Angeles Department of Cannabis Regulation. Swamp Capital argues that since it filed this action, Defendants have directed customers to other businesses, instituted unlawful detainer proceedings against Swamp Capital, forcing the dispensary to relocate, and refused to allow Swamp Capital to relocate the dispensary by withholding consent to the City of Los Angeles Department of Cannabis Regulation review of Swamp Capital’s permit application for the new location. Swamp Capital also seeks to add claims against the City of Los Angeles Department of Cannabis Regulation for their alleged failure to properly review the permit application without Defendants’ consent.

Swamp submits a declaration and the proposed amended complaint, satisfying the requirements of rule 3.1324. There is no evidence that the proposed amended complaint will cause prejudice. Leave to amend is granted.

Conclusion

Swamp Capital’s Motion for Leave to Amend is GRANTED.

The City of Los Angeles Department of Cannabis Regulation’s Demurrer is moot and off calendar.

Joseph W. Sherman v. John Oursland, Case No. 25SMCV06229

Defendant’s Motion to Deem RFAs Admitted

Hearing Date: September 22, 2026

Background

Plaintiff Joseph W. Sherman, in pro per, sues Defendants John Oursland, Jefferey Oursland, Kyle Brunick, Jonathon Gerber, Rosa Gutierrez, Dylan Finneran, Savannah Bruto, Gabriel Garcia, and Anthony Marinaccio for tenant harassment. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants harass him at the apartment he leases at 6 Seaview Terrace, Unit I, Santa Monica, CA, 90401.

On December 3, 2025, Plaintiff filed the complaint.

On June 18, 2026, Jeffery Oursland filed this Motion to Deem RFAs admitted.  As of September 16, 2026, the Court has not received opposition to this motion.

On July 14, 2026, Jeffery Oursland filed a Motion to Compel Response to Special Interrogatories. That Motion is set for hearing on December 15, 2026.

On July 16, 2026, Jeffery Oursland filed a Motion to Compel Response to Requests for Production. That Motion is set for hearing on October 22, 2026.

Motion to Deem Requests for Admission, Set One, Admitted

“Any party may obtain discovery . . . by a written request that any other party to the action admit the genuineness of specified documents, or the truth of specified matters of fact, opinion relating to fact, or application of law to fact. A request for admission may relate to a matter that is in controversy between the parties.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.010.)  “Within 30 days after service of requests for admission, the party to whom the requests are directed shall serve the original of the response to them on the requesting party, and a copy of the response on all other parties who have appeared . . . .”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.250(a).)

 

If a party to whom request for admissions are served fails to provide a timely response, the party to whom the request was directed waives any objections, including based on privilege or the work product doctrine. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(a).) The requesting party can move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the request be deemed admitted, as well as for monetary sanctions. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(b).) The court shall issue this order unless the party to whom the request was made serves a response in substantial compliance prior to the hearing on the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(c).)

Here, Jeffery Oursland served the Requests for Admission, Set One, on Plaintiff by certified mail on April 9, 2026. (Marinaccio Declaration, Exhibit B.) Jeffery Oursland’s return receipt shows that the RFAs were delivered to his apartment in Santa Monica, CA on April 10, 2026.  (Ibid.) Service was complete five days later, on April 15, 2026. As of this motion’s date of filing, Plaintiff has not responded to Jeffery Oursland’s RFAs. (Id. at ¶ 4.) Plaintiff has not filed opposition indicating that that he has produced responsive discovery since this motion was filed.

The Court therefore GRANTS Defendants’ motion to deem RFAs admitted.

Sanctions

Sanctions are mandatory against the party, the attorney, or both whose failure to serve a timely response to the request necessitated the motion to deem request for admissions as admitted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(c); see also Cal. Rules of Court R. 3.1348(a) [the court can award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party seeking to compel discovery even though no opposition was filed, the opposition was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed].)

Jeffery Oursland requests attorneys’ fees and the $60.00 filing fee for this motion as sanctions. Jeffery Oursland’s counsel bills at a rate of $415 per hour. (Marinaccio Decl., ¶ 5.) Counsel’s rate is reasonable. Jeffery Oursland requests fees for four hours preparing the motion and three hours reviewing opposition, preparing a reply, and attending the hearing on this motion. Jeffery Oursland also requests $15 for mailing costs, $30 in other costs, and $15 in parking costs. Three hours for preparing this motion is reasonable. Because this motion is unopposed, three hours for responding and attending the hearing is excessive; the Court finds one hour reasonable. Jeffery Oursland’s mailing and parking costs are reasonable, but the Court declines to grant sanctions for unspecified “other” costs. The Court GRANTS Jeffery Oursland $1,750 ($415 x 4 + $60 + $15 + $15) in sanctions.

Conclusion

Jeffery Oursland’s Motion to Deem RFAs Admitted is GRANTED. Jeffery Oursland is awarded $1,750 in sanctions.


Case Number: 26SMCV02990    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Darien Daniel v. Government Employees Insurance Co., et al., Case No. 26SMCV02990

Defendants’ Demurrer

Hearing Date: September 22, 2026

Background

Plaintiff Darien Daniel, in pro per, sues Defendants Government Employees Insurance Company d/b/a GEICO (“GEICO”); GEICO General Insurance Company; Shane Wheeler, Derek Davis, David Browne, Julee Thach-Tran, and James Turner for fraud, negligent misrepresentation, promissory estoppel, intentional infliction of emotional distress (IIED), intentional interference with prospective economic advantage, and unfair business practices. Plaintiff alleges that GEICO’s conduct following a July 21, 2023, car accident caused Plaintiff economic and professional losses. (Complaint, ¶ 3.)

On June 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed the complaint.

On June 12, 2025, the Court found this case not related to case number 25STCV21366.

On July 21, 2026, Defendants filed this demurrer. As of September 17, 2026, the Court has not received any opposition from Plaintiff.

Demurrer

In a demurrer proceeding, the defects in the complaint must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

Before filing a demurrer, parties must meet and confer in person or by phone. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41.)

Discussion

Meet and Confer

Defendants indicate that Plaintiff failed to respond to their meet and confer communications. (McDermott Decl., ¶ 2.) The meet and confer requirement is not satisfied, but that is not grounds for overruling the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41.)

Uncertainty

Defendants argue that the complaint is uncertain and fails to state a claim against Defendants GEICO General Insurance Company, Shane Wheeler, Derek Davis, David Browne, Julee Thach-Tran, and James Turner because Plaintiff makes no specific allegations about any of these individual defendants.

A demurrer for uncertainty lies where the pleading is uncertain, including where the pleading is ambiguous or unintelligible. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.10, subd. (f); Landau v. Salam (1971) 4 Cal.3d 901, 909.) To survive demurrer, a plaintiff must set forth the essential facts of his case with reasonable precision and with particularity sufficient to acquaint a defendant with the nature, source, and extent of his cause of action. (See Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal.App.3d 714, 719.)¿ 

Demurrers for uncertainty are disfavored. (Chen v. Benjamin (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 811, 822.) Nonetheless, a defendant must have information clear and specific enough to fully comprehend a plaintiff’s allegations and respond with appropriate denials and defenses. (See Committee On Children's Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp., supra, 35 Cal.3d at pp. 211–212.) 

Plaintiffs’ complaint does not satisfy the requirements of the Code of Civil Procedure or the Rules of Court and does not put GEICO General Insurance Company or the individual defendants on sufficiently clear notice of the facts on which their claims are based – or, indeed, of the precise nature of their claims. 

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer to the claims against GEICO General Insurance Company, Shane Wheeler, Derek Davis, David Browne, Julee Thach-Tran, and James Turner is SUSTAINED as uncertain.

Fraud

Defendants argue that the Complaint’s first four allegations against the GEICO are all species of fraud subject to the heightened pleading requirement for fraud claims, and that the complaint fails to plead Plaintiff’s reliance on Defendant’s actions with sufficient specificity. The Court agrees. Plaintiff’s claims for intentional misrepresentation, promissory fraud, fraudulent concealment, and negligent misrepresentation are all types of fraud.[1]

“The well-known elements of a cause of action for fraud or concealment are: (1) a misrepresentation, which includes a concealment or nondisclosure; (2) knowledge of the falsity of the misrepresentation, i.e., scienter; (3) intent to induce reliance on the misrepresentation; (4) justifiable reliance; and (5) resulting damages.” (Cadlo v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (2004) 125 Cal.App.4th 513, 519-520.)   

Plaintiffs alleging fraud must describe, with particularity, the specific statements defendants made that constituted fraud, or facts defendants omitted that constitute alleged fraud. Pleadings must allege facts as to “‘how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.’” (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 73. Accord Wald v. Truspeed Motorcars, LLC (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 378, 393; Morgan v. AT & T Wireless Services, Inc. (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1235, 1262. Cf. also Goldrich v. Natural Y Surgical Specialties, Inc. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th772, 783 [“conclusory allegations offer no facts at all and it is impossible to determine what was said or by whom or in what manner.”].)

Negligent misrepresentation occurs when a defendant (1) makes an untrue statement; (2) that defendant believes to be true, (3) without reasonable ground for the belief, (4) with intent to induce Plaintiff’s reliance on the representation; (5) plaintiff did rely, and (6) plaintiff was damaged. (Melican v. Regents of Univ. of Cal. (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 168, 182.)

Plaintiff’s allegations against GEICO are more specific than his allegations against the other defendants but nonetheless does not describe how he relied on Defendant’s alleged misrepresentations. Therefore, Plaintiff fails to state a claim for fraud or negligent misrepresentation.

Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiff’s fraud and negligent misrepresentation claims against GEICO is SUSTAINED.   

Promissory Estoppel

Defendants argue that Defendant’s promissory estoppel claim fails because it does not establish clear and unambiguous promise, reliance, or injury.

A complaint states a claim for promissory estoppel where it alleges facts showing that a promisor made a promise that it should reasonably expect would induce the promissee’s substantial action or forbearance, which does induce such action or forbearance, and injustice can be avoided only be enforcing the promise. (C & K Eng'g Contractors v. Amber Steel Co. (1978) 23 Cal.3d 1, 6; Cotta v. City and County of San Francisco (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1550, 1566; Lange v. Tig Ins. Co. (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 1179, 1185. See also Smith v. City & County of San Francisco (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 38, 48 [to allege promissory estoppel, pleadings must allege facts demonstrating such reliance on promises, beyond just conclusory allegations of reasonable reliance upon promises].)

The Court agrees with Defendants. Plaintiff’s promissory estoppel claim suffers from the same defect as his fraud claims: he fails to allege reliance with sufficient specificity to state a claim for promissory estoppel.

Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiff’s promissory estoppel claim is SUSTAINED.

Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

Defendants argue that Plaintiff fails to state a claim for IIED because he does not allege that Defendants’ conduct was extreme and outrageous.

To state a claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress, a complaint must plead facts showing that the defendant committed some extreme and outrageous act intentionally or with reckless disregard of the possibility of causing the plaintiff distress, and actually and proximately caused plaintiff’s severe emotional distress. (Grenier v. Taylor (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 471, 486.) Extreme and outrageous conduct is conduct that is so egregious that it is “utterly intolerable in a civilized community.” (Jackson v. Mayweather (2017) 10 Cal.App.5th 1240, 1266.)

Though Plaintiff alleges that GEICO acted poorly, his allegations that GEICO knew that Plaintiff was under financial stress and would rely on GIECO’s representations are not allegations of extreme and outrageous conduct that is “utterly intolerable.” (See Complaint, ¶¶ 106-110.) Absent any case law showing that such allegations can support a claim for IIED, the Court SUSTAINS Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiff’s complaint.

Intentional Interference with Prospective Economic Advantage

Defendants argue that Plaintiff does not allege that GEICO’s actions disrupted any prospective economic relationships because he fails to plead any existing economic relationships.

To state a claim for intentional interference with prospective economic relationship, a complaint must allege (1) an economic relationship between the plaintiff and a third party; (2) probability of future economic benefit; (3) defendants’ knowledge of that relationship; (4) defendant’s intentional acts designed to disrupt the relationship; (5) defendant engaged in an independently wrongful act in disrupting the relationship beyond just inducing disruption of economic advantage; (6) actual disruption of the relationship; and (7) economic harm to the plaintiff caused by the acts. (Roth v. Rhodes (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 530, 546 [as to interference with prospective economic advantage, the nature of particular allegations revealed a failure to allege an existing relationship beyond speculative future relations, and judgment on the pleadings was proper].)

Plaintiff’s allegations of economic relationships that Defendants’ conduct allegedly disrupted are extremely general: Plaintiff alleges that he had “work contracts, music production, operations/event work, and use of professional tools of trade,” but does not describe with whom he had those relationships or how GEICO’s conduct disrupted those relationships. Plaintiff does not state a claim for interference with prospective economic advantage.

Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiff’s Intentional Interference with Prospective Economic Advantage claim is SUSTAINED.

Violation of the UCL

There are three types of claims under Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200: claims for unlawful business practices, fraudulent business practices, and unfair business practices. Under any theory, the complaint must claim some unlawful, fraudulent, or unfair business practice. (People ex rel. Dept. of Motor Vehicles v. Cars 4 Causes (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 1006, 1016 [“An ‘unlawful’ practice requires violation of another statute, and a business practice may be ‘unfair’ even if not otherwise proscribed by statute as long as the practice is not expressly authorized by law.”]; In re Tobacco II Cases (2009) 46 Cal.4th 298, 328 [a claim for fraudulent business practices must allege individualized reliance on specific misrepresentations or false statements]; Cel-Tech Communications, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Co. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 163, 187 (“Cel-Tech”) [unfair conduct is conduct that threatens an incipient violation of an antitrust law or violates the policy or spirit of one of those laws].)

Pursuant to the Court’s analysis of Plaintiff’s fraud claims, above, Plaintiff does not allege fraudulent business practices. Plaintiff claims that GEICO’s conduct was unlawful because it was fraudulent. Since Plaintiff’s complaint does not state a claim for fraud, any claim for unlawful fraudulent conduct likewise fails. Next, Plaintiff alleges that “GEICO’s conduct was unfair because it used insurer-side control of funds, information, and communication channels to disadvantage a self-represented claimant after liability had been accepted.” (Complaint, ¶ 126.) This allegation is insufficient to describe unfair conduct under the UCL because it does not describe conduct that threatens a violation of antitrust law. (Cel-Tech, supra, 20 Cal.4th at 187.)

Defendants’ Demurrer to Plaintiff’s unfair or unlawful business practices is SUSTAINED.

Leave to Amend

Though this motion is unopposed, leave to amend is liberally granted. This is Plaintiff’s first complaint, so he may be able to allege sufficient detail to state a claim against Defendants if given leave to amend. Accordingly, the Court is not prepared to find that there is no reasonable possibility that Plaintiff will be able to state a claim in an amended complaint. Therefore, the Court sustains Defendants’ demurrer with leave to amend.

Conclusion

Defendants’ Demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.


[1] It is not settled law if a claim for negligent misrepresentation is subject to the heightened pleading standard for fraud, but Plaintiff’s negligent misrepresentation claim is virtually identical to his fraud claims in this case and similarly fails to describe any kind of reliance. (See dissent, B.L.M. v. Sabo & Deitsch (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 823, 845.)
