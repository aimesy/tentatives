DEPARTMENT D LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25NMWCV04440    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: D

LYDIA SERRATOS, et al. v. RIVIAN AUTOMOTIVE LLC, et al.

CASE NO.:  25NMWCV04440

HEARING:  9/17/2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#15

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant Rivian Automotive, LLC’s Motion to Compel is granted. 

 

Moving party(s) to give notice.

 

Defendant Rivian Automotive, LLC moves for an order (1) compelling Plaintiffs Lydia Serratos and Frank Aragon to arbitrate their claims in accordance with their arbitration agreement; and (2) staying this action pending the outcome of the arbitration. 

 

Background

 

Plaintiffs filed this action for violations of the Song Beverly Warranty Act on December 15, 2025 against Defendant Rivian Automotive LLC alleging (1) violation of Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Express Warranty; (2) violation of Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Implied Warranty; and (3) violation of Song-Beverly Act section 1793.2(b).

 

On June 9, 2026, Defendant Rivian filed the instant Motion to Compel Arbitration.  On September 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.  On September 10, 2026, Defendant filed a reply. 

 

Legal Standard

 

Under both the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”) and the California Arbitration Act (“CAA”), arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a contract.  (Winter v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943, 947.)  The party moving to compel arbitration must establish the existence of a written arbitration agreement between the parties.  (Code of Civ. Proc. § 1281.2.)  This is usually done by presenting a copy of the signed, written agreement to the court.  “A petition to compel arbitration or to stay proceedings pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 1281.2 and 1281.4 must state, in addition to other required allegations, the provisions of the written agreement and the paragraph that provides for arbitration.  The provisions must be stated verbatim, or a copy must be physically or electronically attached to the petition and incorporated by reference.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330.)  The moving party must also establish the other party’s refusal to arbitrate the controversy.  (Code of Civ. Proc. § 1281.2.) 

 

The Court shall order arbitration unless it determines that grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 1281.2.)  Under California law, and the Federal Arbitration Act, an arbitration agreement may be invalid based upon grounds applicable to any contract, including unconscionability, fraud, duress, and public policy, which would be matters for the court to decide.  (Sanchez v. Western Pizza Enterprises, Inc. (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 154, 165-166.)

 

Analysis

 

Defendant Rivian moves to compel arbitration of Plaintiffs’ complaint for violations of the Song Beverly Warranty Act based on the arbitration provisions contained in (1) the Warranty Agreement between Defendant Rivian and Plaintiffs; and (2) the lease agreement between non-party Rivian, LLC  and Plaintiff Serratos (the “Motor Vehicle Agreement”). 

 

Defendant Rivian argues that, to the extent Plaintiffs claim they were not parties to the Warranty Agreement, they may be compelled to arbitrate pursuant to the arbitration provision contained therein based on equitable estoppel, direct benefits estoppel and third party beneficiary law.  Defendant argues the Warranty Agreement’s arbitration provision requires that any issues of arbitrability be decided by the arbitrator.  As such, Defendant argues the Court may not reach the issue of whether the arbitration agreement applies here. 

 

Defendant Rivian acknowledges that it is not a party to the Motor Vehicle Agreement, but argues it is entitled to move for arbitration thereunder based on equitable estoppel and as a third party beneficiary.  Defendant Rivian argues that, like the Warranty Agreement, the Motor Vehicle Agreement contains an arbitrability provision requiring that issues of arbitrability be decided by the arbitrator.  Defendant Rivian argues the Court may not resolve the issue of whether Defendant Rivian can move to compel arbitration pursuant to the Motor Vehicle Agreement. 

 

          Motion not untimely

 

Plaintiffs oppose the motion on grounds that it is untimely under Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.5 subdivision (b).  However, section 1281.5 applies to actions enforce liens on works of improvement, as indicated by it title and the substance of subdivision (a).  Section 1281.5 is entitled “Waiver of right to arbitration; actions to enforce liens on works of improvement; notice of motion and motion to stay actions to pending arbitration.”  Section 1281.5 subdivision (a) provides, “Any person who proceeds to record and enforce a claim of lien by commencement of an action pursuant to Chapter 4 (commencing with Section 8400) of Title 2 of Part 6 of Division 4 of the Civil Code, does not thereby waive any right of arbitration the person may have pursuant to a written agreement to arbitrate, if, in filing an action to enforce the claim of lien, the claimant does either of the following….”

 

          FAA applies

 

Defendant argues the FAA applies based on the express language of the Motor Vehicle Agreement, which states “This Section, Dispute Resolution – Arbitration and Class Action Waiver, its subject matter, formation and enforceability will be governed by the Federal Arbitration Act as interpreted by the federal courts, and not any state law regarding arbitration.”  (Harden Dec., ¶5, Ex. A, p. 8, “Applicable Law.”)  The Warranty Agreement also contains an express statement that the FAA “shall govern the subject matter, formation, procedure, and enforcement of this section.”  (Harden Dec., Ex. B, ¶16, “Applicable Law.”)  “The FAA applies to contracts that involve interstate commerce (9 U.S.C. §§ 1, 2), but since arbitration is a matter of contract, the FAA also applies if it is so stated in the agreement.”  (Davis v. Shiekh Shoes, LLC (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 956, 963.) 

 

Defendant also engages in the “interstate commerce” analysis in footnote one of the motion.  “[T]he United States Supreme Court has identified three categories of activity that Congress may regulate under the commerce power: (1) the channels of interstate commerce, (2) the instrumentalities of interstate commerce and persons or things in interstate commerce, and (3) those activities having a substantial relation to interstate commerce. The party asserting FAA preemption bears the burden to present evidence establishing a contract with the arbitration provision affects one of these three categories of activity, and failure to do so renders the FAA inapplicable.”  (Carbajal v. CWPSC, Inc. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 227, 238.)

 

Defendant establishes that the Warranty Agreement involved interstate commerce.  The vehicle was manufactured in Normal, IL and shipped to California via interstate commerce channels.  (Harden Dec., ¶6.)  Harden claims the lease transaction took place over the Internet.  (Harden Dec., ¶¶2-5.) 

 

          Warranty Agreement

 

Defendant relies on the Warranty Agreement issued with the vehicle at the time of lease.  The Warranty Agreement issued by Defendant was incorporated by reference into the Motor Vehicle Agreement, which Plaintiffs signed.  The Warranty Agreement contains an express arbitration provision, which states that Plaintiffs and Rivian agree to “resolve all Disputes by binding arbitration conducted by the American Arbitration Associations (“AAA”) in accordance with the AAA Consumer Arbitration Rules, unless you and we agree otherwise.”  (Harden Dec., Ex. B, ¶16.) 

 

“‘Dispute’ is to be given the broadest possible meaning that twill be enforced and means any dispute, demand, claim, or controversy of any kind between you and Rivian, whether based in contract, tort, statute, demand, claim, or controversy of any kind between you and Rivian, including but not limited to any claim for…breach of express or implied warranties, or violations of consumer protection…laws, that arises out of or in any way relates to (1) this Agreement; (2) our relationship…; (3) the purchase, sale, condition, design, or manufacturing of the Vehicle; (4) Rivian products or services; (5) advertising and other communications between you and Rivian; and as applicable, (6) your credit application.” (Id.)

 

Defendant submits evidence that notice of the Warranty Agreement was provided to Plaintiffs in the Motor Vehicle Agreement, as well as a hyperlink to the Warranty Agreement.  (Harden Dec., ¶7.)  The Motor Vehicle Agreement was signed on June 13, 2024.  (Id. at ¶5.)  The Warranty Agreement references and incorporates the binding arbitration clause in the Motor Vehicle Agreement.  (Id. at Ex. B, p. 8.) 

 

Plaintiffs’ complaint is based on a dispute covered by the Warranty Agreement.  Plaintiffs expressly allege that express warranties accompanied the Motor Vehicle Agreement and the claims are based on breach of those express warranties.  (Complaint, ¶¶8, 22, 47-49.)  Plaintiffs also testify that they never opted out of the arbitration provision as allowed for therein.  (Hamblin Dec., ¶3.) 

 

Defendant satisfies its burden of establishing an applicable arbitration agreement with the Warranty Agreement.  (Kostandian v. American Honda Motor Co. (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 872, 881 (manufacturer satisfied its burden of establishing existence of applicable arbitration agreement based on arbitration provision contained in warranty booklet and evidence confirming plaintiff’s receipt of warranty booklet).)  The burden therefore shifts to the Plaintiffs to dispute the existence of an arbitration agreement with Defendant.

 

Plaintiffs argue there is no evidence that they agreed to the warranty guide.  Plaintiffs argue they never signed or otherwise expressly affirm his consent to the warranty guide.  Plaintiffs argue there is no evidence that they received the warranty guide until after purchase.  Plaintiffs argue their failure to opt out is insufficient to establish their consent to the arbitration provision contained in the Warranty Agreement.  Plaintiffs also argue warranties do not create obligations on the buyer, only the seller.

 

Plaintiffs fail to rebut Defendant’s showing that the arbitration provision contained in the Warranty Agreement applies.  Plaintiffs rely heavily on case law explaining the nature of warranty obligations.  (Daugherty v. Am. Honda Motor Co. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 824, 830; Weinstat v Dentsply Internat., Inc. (2010) 180 Cal.App.4th 1213, 1228.)

 

However, the obligation in question is the obligation to arbitrate contained in the  warranty, not the warranty obligation itself.  Plaintiffs fail to cite any authority holding that an arbitration provision in a warranty handbook is unenforceable as an issue of law.  In fact, the Court of Appeal in Kostandian found the defendant manufacturer established an applicable arbitration agreement based on the arbitration agreement contained in its warranty booklet and evidence that the plaintiff had received the owner’s manual and warranty information.  (Kostandian, supra, 120 Cal.App.5th at 882.)  The Court went on to find that the plaintiff failed to rebut the existence or applicability of the warranty booklet to his claims against the manufacturer.  The Court found the plaintiff never disputed the existence of the arbitration agreement with American Honda in the warranty booklet.  (Id. at 883.)  The Court rejected precisely the same arguments raised by Plaintiff attacking consent.  “While respondent here posits there was no mutual assent since he did not receive the warranty booklet until after the sale was completed, no evidence was presented supporting this assertion.  Respondent provided no declaration stating when he received the warranty booklet.”  (Id. at ¶884.)  Even if such a declaration were presented, the testimony would have been at odds with his signed acknowledgment of receipt and his complaint allegations that representations were made to him in the Manufacturer’s New Vehicle Limited Warranty.  (Id.)

 

Here, Plaintiffs failed to submit any evidence that they did not receive the Warranty Agreement until long after the sale.  Plaintiffs do not deny that the Motor Vehicle Agreement incorporated the Warranty Agreement by reference or that the Motor Vehicle Agreement set forth a hyperlink to the Warranty Agreement in paragraph 13.  (Harden Dec., Ex. A, ¶13.)  Plaintiffs undisputedly signed the Motor Vehicle Agreement.  Plaintiffs also allege that express warranties accompanied the sale of the vehicle to them.  (Complaint, ¶8.)  The evidence establishes Plaintiffs’ knowledge and receipt of the Warranty Agreement. 

 

For these reasons, Defendant establishes the existence of an applicable arbitration agreement between Defendant and Plaintiffs based on the Warranty Agreement.  Plaintiffs fail to rebut this showing.

 

Although the Motor Vehicle Lease Agreement is only signed by Plaintiff Lydia Serratos, Plaintiffs allege that they both entered into the Motor Vehicle Lease Agreement and both are suing on the express warranty issued by Defendant.  Plaintiffs are equitably estopped from denying the enforceability of the Warranty Agreement based on these facts. 

 

          Delegation Clause in Warranty Agreement

 

Defendant argues the delegation clause precludes the Court from reviewing whether the Warranty Agreement is enforceable and applicable to Plaintiff’s claims.  “When a dispute arises between parties to an arbitration agreement, the parties may disagree not only about the merits of the dispute but also about the threshold arbitrability question—that is, whether their arbitration agreement applies to the particular dispute.  The high court has recognized that parties may agree by contract that an arbitrator, rather than a court, will resolve threshold arbitrability questions as well as underlying merits disputes.  Such threshold or ‘gateway’ questions of arbitrability include whether the parties have agreed to arbitrate or whether their agreement covers a particular controversy.”  (Sandoval-Ryan v. Oleander Holdings LLC (2020) 58 Cal.App.5th 217, 222–223 (quoting Henry Schein, Inc. v. Archer and White Sales, Inc. (2019) -- U.S. – (139 S.Ct. 524, 527, 202 L.Ed. 2d 480).) 

 

“Parties to an arbitration agreement may agree to delegate to the arbitrator, instead of a court, questions regarding the enforceability of the agreement.  They can agree to arbitrate almost any dispute—even a dispute over whether the underlying dispute is subject to arbitration.”  (Tiri v. Lucky Chances, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 231, 241.)  “[A] party's challenge to the arbitration agreement does not invalidate the delegation clause, and therefore the arbitrator, and not a court, must consider any challenge to the arbitration agreement as a whole. Stated another way, Rent-A-Center acknowledges that while courts may consider enforceability challenges specific to delegation clauses, the arbitrator is to consider challenges to the arbitration agreement as a whole.”  (Id. at 240 (adopting analysis under FAA of delegation clause challenges versus challenges to arbitration agreement in Rent-A-Center, West, Inc. v. Jackson, 561 U.S. 63, 72 (2010).) 

 

“There are two prerequisites for a delegation clause to be effective. First, the language of the clause must be clear and unmistakable.  Second, the delegation must not be revocable under state contract defenses such as fraud, duress, or unconscionability.”  (Id. at 242.)

 

The arbitration agreement in the Warranty Agreement states, “[t]he arbitrator, and not any federal, state, or local court or agency, shall have exclusive authority to resolve all disputes arising out of or relating to the interpretation, applicability, enforcement, or formation of this Section, including but not limited to any claim that all or any party of this section is void or voidable.  The arbitrator shall also have exclusive authority to resolve all threshold issues of arbitrability, including whether a Dispute is subject to arbitration, issues relating to whether this Section is applicable, unconscionable, or illusory, and any defense to arbitration, including waiver, delay, laches, or estoppel.”  (Harden Dec., Ex. B, ¶16, p. 10.)

 

The delegation clause is clear and unmistakable and delegates issues concerning formation of the agreement to the arbitrator.  Plaintiffs also do not raise any state law defenses to the delegation clause apart from their claim that they never consented to the arbitration agreement contained in the Warranty Agreement.  Despite the broad form delegation clause delegating questions of formation to the arbitrator, it is the court that must determine the threshold question of whether the parties ever agreed to arbitrate “at all.”  (Mendoza v. Trans Valley Transport (2022) 75 Cal.App.5th 748, 772-777.) 

 

Plaintiffs are claiming that they never agreed to the arbitration agreement in the Warranty Agreement at all.  As such, the Court must determine the threshold issue of whether Plaintiffs ever agreed to the arbitration agreement in the Warranty Agreement.  For the reasons stated above, the Court finds sufficient evidence of Plaintiffs’ knowledge and consent to the Warranty Agreement, including the arbitration agreement therein. 

 

Defendant’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is granted based on the Warranty Agreement.  Defendant establishes the existence of an applicable arbitration agreement, including Plaintiffs’ consent thereto.  Plaintiffs fail to establish any grounds rendering the agreement unenforceable or that they did not consent to the arbitration provision. 

 

Conclusion

 

Defendant’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is granted.

LILLIAN AYALA v. CERRITOS COMMUNITY COLLEGE, ET AL.

CASE NO.:  25NWCV02058

HEARING:  09/18/2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#14

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant Cerritos Community College’s unopposed motion to deem requests for admission admitted against Plaintiff is GRANTED.

 

Defendant Cerritos Community College’s request for sanctions is GRANTED in the amount of $690, due 30 days from the date of this order.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant Cerritos Community College (Defendant) moves for an order deeming requests for admission admitted against Plaintiff Lillian Ayala (Plaintiff); monetary sanctions in the amount of $1,035.00.

 

Legal Standard

 

          Requests for Admission

 

“Any party may obtain discovery . . . by a written request that any other party to the action admit the genuineness of specified documents, or the truth of specified matters of fact, opinion relating to fact, or application of law to fact.  A request for admission may relate to a matter that is in controversy between the parties.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.010.)  “Within 30 days after service of requests for admission, the party to whom the requests are directed shall serve the original of the response to them on the requesting party, and a copy of the response on all other parties who have appeared . . . .”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.250(a).)

 

If a party to whom request for admissions are served fails to provide a timely response, the party to whom the request was directed waives any objections, including based on privilege or the work product doctrine.   (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(a).)  The requesting party can move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the request be deemed admitted, as well as for monetary sanctions.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(b).)  The court shall issue this order unless the party to whom the request was made serves a response in substantial compliance prior to the hearing on the motion.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(c).)

 

Sanctions

 

If the motion to compel is granted and the moving party properly requests monetary sanctions, the court “shall” impose sanctions against the party to whom the discovery was directed, unless it finds that the sanctioned party acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of sanctions unjust.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, subd. (c) [interrogatories]; 2031.300, subd. (c) [demands for inspection].)

 

In contrast, with respect to requests for admissions, monetary sanctions are mandatory against the party, the attorney, or both whose failure to serve a timely response necessitated the motion to deem the matters admitted, and the statute does not provide an exception for substantial justification.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c); see also Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1348, subd. (a).)

 

Analysis

 

On May 20, 2026, Defendant served initial discovery on Plaintiff, including Request for Admissions, Set Two. (McEwan Decl., ¶ 3; Exh. “A”.) Plaintiff failed to timely serve responses to Defendant’s Request for Admissions, Set Two, within 30 days as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.250(a). Counsel for Defendant sent Plaintiff’s counsel a meet and confer letter regarding the outstanding discovery responses on June 30, 2026. (McEwan Decl., ¶ 4; Exh. “B”.) Counsel for Defendant did not receive any response from Plaintiff’s counsel and, to date, no responses have been provided. (McEwan Decl., ¶ 5.)

 

Plaintiff's responses were due on or before June 19, 2026.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.260, subd. (a), 2031.260, subd. (a), & 2033.250, subd. (a).)  Plaintiff failed to timely serve verified responses, thereby waiving any objections to the discovery requests.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, subd. (a), 2031.300, subd. (a), & 2033.280, subd. (a).)

 

Plaintiff has not filed an opposition or otherwise disputed Defendant's showing that no timely verified responses were served.

 

Accordingly, Defendant's motion is GRANTED.

 

Sanctions

 

Defendant requests monetary sanctions against Plaintiff and his counsel, jointly and severally, in the amount of $1,035.00, based on 3.0 hours of attorney time at an hourly rate of $345.00.  Counsel represents that this includes 2.0 hours preparing the motion and an additional 1.0 hours to prepare the reply.  (McEwan Decl., ¶ 8.)

 

Here, as discussed above, the Court grants Defendant's motion to compel.  Accordingly, monetary sanctions are warranted.  In addition, with respect to the requests for admissions, monetary sanctions are mandatory where a party's failure to serve timely responses necessitates a motion to deem the matters admitted, absent a showing of substantial justification or other circumstances making the imposition of sanctions unjust.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).)

 

Given the straightforward nature of the motion to deem requests for admissions admitted, the Court finds that two hours is a reasonable amount of attorney time, as no substantive reply was necessary.  Accordingly, Defendant's request for monetary sanctions is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $690.00.

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s unopposed motion to deem requests for admission admitted against Plaintiff is GRANTED.

 

Defendant’s request for sanctions is GRANTED in the amount of $690.00, due 30 days from the date of this order.

DONNOVAN LEWERY v. DOWNEY’S BILLIARD, LLC, et al.

CASE NO.:  26NWCV01684

HEARING:  09/17/2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#16

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant HGG’s Motion to Expunge Lis Pendens is granted.  Plaintiff is ordered to pay Defendant $9,762.50 in fees. 

 

Moving party(s) to give notice.

 

Defendant HGG Firestone, LLC (“HGG”) moves to expunge the lis pendens recorded by Plaintiff Donnovan Lewery on May 18, 2026 againts HGG’s real property located at 8206-8214 Firestone Blvd., Downey, CA 90241 (the “Shopping Center”).

 

Background

 

Plaintiff obtained a judgment against Defendant Downey’s Billiard, LLC d/b/a Club DB Lounge (“Club DB”) in the amount of $750,000 in Case No. 19STCV30200 (“Prior Action”).  On May 13, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant action against Defendants Club DB, HGG, Hayk Gevorkyan (“Hayk”) and George Gevorkyan (“George”) for declaratory relief seeking an  order declaring HGG, Hayk and George to be the alter egos of Club DB and imposing the judgment from the prior action against them. 

 

On May 18, 2026, Plaintiff recorded a lis pendens with the Los Angeles County Recorder against HGG’s Shopping Center.  On July 21, 2026, HGG filed the instant motion to expunge lis pendens.  On September 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.  On September 10, 2026, HGG filed a reply.

 

Legal Standard

 

In a motion to expunge a notice of lis pendens, the claimant who filed the lis pendens has the burden of proof.  (Code of Civil Procedure, §405.30.)  Thus, that claimant, in opposing the motion to expunge the lis pendens, must demonstrate the following: (1) the action affects title to or right of possession of the real property described in the notice; (2) in so far as the said notice is concerned, the party recording the notice has commenced the action for a proper purpose and in good faith; and (3) the probable validity of the real property claim by a preponderance of the evidence.  (Hunting World, Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 22 Cal.App.4th 67, 70; Code of Civ. Proc., §405.30, et seq.)  The court must order the notice expunged if it finds the pleading on which the notice is based does not contain a real property claim (Code of Civil Procedure section 405.31) or the claimant has not established by a preponderance of the evidence the probable validity of the real property claim (Code of Civil Procedure section §405.32).  A claim has “probable validity” where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.  (Code of Civ. Proc., §481.190.)

 

In determining whether the action affects title to or right of possession of the real property described in the notice, “[w]hether real party's action is ultimately meritorious is not relevant to the determination of the propriety of his lis pendens.  The issue is simply whether the action as pleaded is one that affects title or possession of the subject property.”  (Urez v. Supr. Ct. (1987) 190 Cal.App.3d 1141, 1149.)

 

“Unlike most motions, the party opposing a motion to expunge bears the burden to show the existence of a real property claim.  In considering whether the burden has been met, the court engages in “a demurrer-like analysis.  The trial court and the reviewing court both review the complaint to determine if a real property claim has been properly pleaded.”  (Shoker v. Supr. Ct. (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 271, 277-278.)

 

“Where partners or joint venturers have agreed to buy a specific parcel of real property, and one partner or joint venturer, in breach of a fiduciary duty, wrongfully acquires it in his own name, the other partner or joint venturer may bring an action to impose a constructive trust and require the wrongdoing partner or joint venturer to convey the appropriate share of the legal title.  Assuming the truth of plaintiffs' allegations, as we must in this demurrer-like context, plaintiffs may be entitled to imposition of a constructive trust requiring the defendants to convey to plaintiffs the share of the legal title plaintiffs would have acquired had not the defendants breached their duties toward plaintiffs.”  (BGJ Associates, LLC v. Supr. Ct. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 952, 970.)

 

In Coppinger v. Superior Court (1982) 134 Cal.App.3d 883, 891, the court pointed out that an action to impose a constructive trust has been held to be one for the recovery of real property and therefore the action affects title to or possession of real property for purposes of the lis pendens expungement statute. (Coppinger, supra, 134 Cal.App.3d at 891.)

 

However, subsequent cases have disagreed with Coppinger’s expansive interpretation of what qualifies as a real property claim and have distinguished, limited, or explicitly rejected its reasoning.  (Urez, supra, 75 Cal.App.4th at 1149; BGJ Associates, supra, 75 Cal.App.4th at 968.)

 

In Urez, the plaintiff was a former lien holder and the court found no basis for a real property claim.  “It is essentially a fraud action seeking money damages with additional allegations urged to support the equitable remedies of a constructive trust or an equitable lien. Real party does not claim any ownership or possessory interest in the subject property. Rather, he seeks reinstatement or creation of a ‘beneficial’ interest in the property for the purpose of securing payment of money owed him under his defunct second trust deed.”  (Urez, supra, 75 Cal.App.4th at 1149.)

 

The court in BGJ Associates acknowledged that there was no straightforward answer to whether a claim for constructive trust can support a lis pendens, noting that an absolute rule either way failed to account for the reality that sometimes it may and other times it may not.  Thus, “what the Legislature has done in section 405.4 is to leave to the courts to determine in particular cases whether a claim supporting a constructive trust will justify the maintenance of a lis pendens…In determining this issue on a case-by-case basis, the courts have been restrictive because of well-known dangers that the lis pendens procedure can be abused to coerce a defendant to settle a claim. The courts have looked to the substance of the dispute to determine whether it is ‘essentially’ a fraud action seeking money damages, with constructive trust allegations ‘appended.’”  (Id. at 970-971.)

 

Applying these principles to the facts before it, the court in BGJ Associates held that no real property claim was alleged to support a lis pendens.  “In a case such as this where the pleading combines theories of liability for monetary damages and for a constructive trust, we hold that plaintiffs should not be able to maintain a lis pendens. The danger is too great that a lis pendens, which effectively renders the property unmarketable, will have the coercive effects condemned by the cases.”  (Id. at 971.)

 

The question of whether an action for constructive trust will support a lis pendens was discussed recently in Shoker.  “Some actions present easy questions under the statute. A buyer's suit seeking specific performance of a real property purchase and sale agreement is obviously a real property claim. On the other hand, an action for money damages alone is not a real property claim—even if it involves real property in some way. (BGJ Associates v. Superior Court (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 952, 967–968 [89 Cal.Rptr.2d 693] (BGJ Associates).) Despite the statute's straightforward language, it has never been entirely clear if a claim that seeks to impose a constructive trust on real property affects title to or possession of real property.”  (Shoker, supra, 81 Cal.App.5th at 278.) 

 

Shoker rejected the approach adopted in BGJ Associates, in which the court “looked past the plaintiffs’ real property claim to the broader ‘substance’ of the dispute” and concluded despite the allegation of a real property claim, “the case was essentially a fraud action seeking money damages, to which constructive trust allegations were merely appended.”  (Shoker, supra, 81 Cal.App.5th at 179.)  Shoker found BGJ Associates’ fear that the lis pendens procedure would be abused absent a test allowing the court to “look past” the real property claim was unfounded.  Shoker explained that the Legislature’s revision of the statutory scheme allowed for multiple avenues to limit abuse of the lis pendens scheme.  (Id. at 280.)  While rejecting BGJ Associates’ analysis, Shoker maintained that the propriety of a lis pendens in cases alleging constructive trust must be evaluated on a case-by-case basis and did not reject Urez’s distinction between actions alleging constructive trust where a lis pendens is appropriate and one where it is not:

 

“In reaching this conclusion, we note that we need not decide the continuing validity of the other cases in the Urez line. The case law distinguishes between two subtypes of constructive trust claims. First, there are constructive trust claims that are akin to fraudulent conveyance claims because they seek a constructive trust on real property that was itself wrongfully taken and, if successful on the merits, plaintiffs would regain title to or possession of the same property. On the other hand, there are claims seeking a constructive trust or equitable lien on different property, merely as a means to secure collection of a debt.  [¶] Here, we are dealing with the former type of constructive trust claim—the Shokers claim a present right to title in the same real properties they claim were wrongfully obtained by Phangureh….With the exception of BGJ Associates, the Urez line of cases deals with the latter use of constructive trust—where the plaintiff seeks to impose a constructive trust or equitable lien on real property only as a means to secure a debt.   To the extent any cases within the Urez line can be read as holding that a constructive trust claim can never constitute a real property claim under section 405.4, we respectfully disagree.”  (Shoker, supra, 81 Cal.App.5th at 282–283.)

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

Defendant HGG’s Request for Judicial Notice is granted pursuant to Evidence Code section 452 subdivision (c) and (d).  Exhibits 1 to 6 are filings and records of the California Secretary of State.  Exhibits 7 to 12 are records from the prior action and the instant action. 

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

Objection Nos. 1-22 overruled to the extent based on George Gevorkyan being a non-English speaker.  Plaintiff relies solely on George Gevorkyan’s use of an interpreter at the judgment debtor exam and his testimony that he speaks and writes very little English. 

 

Objection No. 2 based on relevance overruled.

 

Objection No. 22 based on improper legal opinion is sustained.

 

Analysis

 

Plaintiff’s complaint does not allege a real property claim that affects title or possession of property under Code of Civil Procedure section 405.31

 

Plaintiff’s complaint is a classic action for money judgment or a collection action with an incidental allegation of constructive trust.  Although Plaintiff is alleging a declaratory relief action against HGG, Plaintiff is seeking a declaration that (1) HGG is the alter ego of Club D and (2) HGG’s Shopping Center is therefore subject to the $750,000 personal injury judgment entered against Club G in the Prior Action.  There is no allegation that HGG fraudulently obtained or acquired the Shopping Center from Plaintiff.  There is no claim that Plaintiff has any right to title or possession of the Shopping Center.  The Shopping Center is only at issue as an asset from which the judgment in the Prior Action may be satisfied.  Plaintiff’s complaint fails allege a real property claim under Code of Civil Procedure section 405.31.  Plaintiff’s complaint is “an action for money damages alone” and it “is not a real property claim—even if it involves real property in some way.”  (Shoker, supra, 81 Cal.App.5th at 278; Urez, supra, 75 Cal.App.4th at 1149.)

 

Plaintiff argues his abstract of judgment against the assets of Club D will apply to the Shopping Center if the Court finds HGG to be the alter ego of Club D.  However, the mere fact that a judgment lien may issue against the Shopping Center does not transform this action from an action for money judgment alone into an action affecting title or possession of the Shopping Center under section 405.31.  If this were the rule, a lis pendens could be recorded in every action for money damages where the defendant’s assets include real property.  “A number of California courts have considered whether a complaint seeking the imposition of an equitable lien or other equitable security interest in real property constitutes a real property claim under the lis pendens statutes. The majority of these courts have concluded that a claim that seeks an interest in real property merely for the purpose of securing a money damage judgment does not support the recording of a lis pendens.”  (Campbell v. Superior Court (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 904, 912.)

 

Defendant HGG is entitled to attorney’s fees and costs pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 405.38

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 405.38, the prevailing party on a motion to expunge lis pendens is entitled to the reasonable attorney's fees incurred in making the motion, unless the other party acted with substantial justification.  The attorney's fees award is imposed against the party and not counsel.  (Doyle v. Sup.Ct. (Jacinth Develop., Inc.) (1991) 226 Cal.App.3d 1355, 1359.) 

 

The trial court has broad discretion to determine what constitutes a reasonable fee.    (PLCM Group, Inc. (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.)  “The trial court makes its determination after consideration of a number of factors, including the nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, the skill required in its handling, the skill employed, the attention given, the success or failure, and other circumstances in the case.”  (Melnyk v. Robledo (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 618, 623-624.)  If the Court reduces the requested attorney fees as unreasonable, it is not obligated to justify its decision by identifying specific time entries with which it finds fault.  (Id. at 625.)  To require otherwise would be inconsistent with the general rule that the trial court is entitled to take all circumstances into account when determining the reasonableness of attorney fees.  (Id.)  The trial court is therefore not bound by an attorney's evidence in support of his fees.  (Vella v. Hudgins (1984) 151 Cal.App.3d 515, 524.)

 

Defendant HGG requests fees in the amount of $9,762.50 based on 17.75 hours at a rate of $550 per hour.  Defendant HGG expended 14.25 hours in connection with preparing the motion and an anticipated 3.5 hours to review the opposition and prepare the reply.  Defendant HGG’s request is reasonable given the complexity of the motion and applicable law. 

 

Conclusion

 

Defendant HGG’s Motion to Expunge Lis Pendens is granted.  Plaintiff is ordered to pay Defendant $9,762.50 in fees within 30 days of this order.
