[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING IN PART MOTION TO TAX COSTS

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

July 28, 2026

On May 13, 2026, Plaintiffs Saerom Ha and Jason Ha filed a memorandum of costs.

On June 1, 2026, Defendants filed a motion to tax costs.

Defendants generally argue that Plaintiffs have not submitted “a single page of backup documentation (e.g., invoices, bills, or receipts) to support any of the requested costs.”  (Motion at p. 4.)  However, “[a] ‘verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of the propriety’ of the items listed on it, and the burden is on the party challenging these costs to demonstrate that they were not reasonable or necessary.”  (Adams v. Ford Motor Co. (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 1475, 1486 (Adams).)  “[I]f the correctness of the memorandum is challenged either in whole or in part by the affidavit or other evidence of the contesting party, the burden is then on the party claiming the costs and disbursements to show that the items charged were for matters necessarily relevant and material to the issues involved in the action.”  (Oak Grove School Dist. of Santa Clara County v. City Title Ins. Co. (1963) 217 Cal.App.2d 678, 699.)  In any event, Plaintiffs provided a spreadsheet of costs and copies of invoices with their Opposition.

Aside from arguments about the lack of documentation or items being listed under the wrong category, Defendants also challenge specific categories of costs as not being allowable or reasonable.  A prevailing plaintiff in a Song-Beverly action may recover all “costs and expenses . . . determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1794, subd. (d).)  “[I]t is clear the Legislature intended the word ‘expenses’ to cover items not included in the detailed statutory definition of ‘costs.’”  (Jensen v. BMW of North America, Inc. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112, 137 (Jensen).)

Defendants challenge the “repeated service of process to the same locations or for the starkly different costs incurred for such service.”  (Motion at p. 5.)  However, the costs for service were actually incurred, and they are reasonable.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(4).)

Defendants challenge Plaintiffs’ “unnecessary and excessive expenditure” of $2,750.00 for an expert who billed eleven hours and was not called to trial.  (Motion at p. 5.)  “The legislative history indicates the Legislature exercised its power to permit the recovery of expert witness fees by prevailing buyers under the [Song-Beverly] Act.”  (Jensen , supra, 35 Cal.App.4th at p. 138.)  As Plaintiffs further explain, their expert “is the counter-part for Defendant’s expert automotive witness,” and his “expert fees include time reviewing repair orders, recalls, service manuals, deposition transcripts, and technical service bulletins to prepare to give his opinions at deposition as well as educate Plaintiffs’ counsels on the technical information regarding the vehicle’s defects which needed to be presented at trial.”  (Opposition at p. 9.)  The Court finds that this cost is reasonable.

Defendants challenge Plaintiff’s $12,631.40 for “Other.”  Within this category, only a few items warrant further discussion.

Plaintiff’s counsel’s firm, “which is based in Los Angeles and consistently filed paperwork with the Court with a Los Angeles address, is now asking the Court to require [Defendants] to pay $10,665.02 in travel costs for a case that was heard in Los Angeles Superior Court.”  (Motion at p. 6.)  These costs, labeled “Travel,” include mileage, Ubers, flights, parking, meals, and snacks in additional to 11 nights of hotel stays that average $783.06 per night.[1]  (Opposition, Ex. A.)  Plaintiffs had Los Angeles-based counsel in addition to the trial counsel who flew from San Francisco.  Plaintiffs have not shown that it was reasonable or necessary to spend so much money on flights, other transportation, and lodging for out-of-town counsel to join local counsel—nor have Plaintiffs shown that it was reasonable or necessary for local counsel to also stay at a hotel.  The costs for counsel’s meals and snacks are also not reasonably charged to Plaintiffs case expenses.

Finally, $16.44 for “AHDMI adaptor for Trial (03.18.26)” is an overall office expense and was not reasonably incurred for just this action.

The motion is GRANTED IN PART.  The Court STRIKES $10,665.02 for “Travel” and $16.44 for “AHDMI adaptor for Trial (03.18.26).”

The motion is otherwise denied.  Plaintiffs shall recover $25,670.85 ($36,352.31 minus $10,681.46) in total costs and expenses.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING IN PART AND OVERRULING IN PART DEMURRER

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

July 28, 2026

 

On October 11, 2024, Plaintiff Austin Betters filed this action against Defendant Randel Management Service Inc., alleging (1) negligence, (2) negligent infliction of emotional distress (“NIED”), (3) bad faith, (4) breach of fiduciary duties, (5) breach of covenant of quiet enjoyment, and (6) negligence per se.

On June 3, 2025, Plaintiff filed an Amendment to Complaint, correcting Defendant’s name to R&E Management Inc.

On October 15, 2025, Defendant (identifying itself as R&E Management Company) filed a demurrer.[1]

DISCUSSION

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true.  (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.)

A.        The Complaint Properly Alleges Habitability Claims and Names a Defendant.

Defendant argues that “there is no lease attached to the Complaint, and no adequate description of the terms and certainly no fact alleged which connect R&E Management Co[.] to the alleged damages.”  (Demurrer at p. 3.)  Some of Plaintiff’s habitability claims arise from common law.  Pleading the contractual terms of the lease is not required.  Indeed, tenancy does not require or depend upon the existence of a formal lease agreement.  (See, e.g., Civ. Code, §§ 1943-1946.)

Additionally, Defendant argues that “[e]ven if there was evidence of an agreement (which there is not) between an alleged Landlord and the Plaintiffs, R&E MANAGEMENT COMPANY is not even the landlord at the Plaintiff’s alleged place of residence and owes no direct duty to Plaintiffs.”  (Demurrer at p. 4.)  This is a factual issue that requires evidence and cannot be resolved on demurrer.

The demurrer is overruled on this ground.

B.        Plaintiff Sufficiently Alleges NIED (Second Cause of Action).

Defendant argues, “Even if there was an alleged agreement (which there is not) between an alleged Landlord and the Plaintiffs, R&E MANAGEMENT COMPANY is not a legal entity and owes no duty to Plaintiffs.  As noted above, R&E MANAGEMENT COMPANY is not in privity with Plaintiffs in any shape or form. . . . Accordingly, the demurrer should be sustained because defendant R&E MANAGEMENT COMPANY should not have been a party to this action in the first place.  Naming a non-existent party automatically causes this cause of action to fail.”  (Demurrer at p. 4.)  This is not a proper ground for demurrer.

Defendant also argues that “[n]either a duty nor negligence nor causation has been properly pleaded.”  (Demurrer at p. 5.)  A cause of action for negligence requires (1) a legal duty owed to the plaintiff to use due care, (2) breach of that duty, (3) proximate causation, and (4) damages.  (Artiglio v. Corning Inc. (1998) 18 Cal.4th 604, 614; County of Santa Clara v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 292, 318 [citing Artiglio].)

Plaintiff alleges that an unresolved water leak caused mold and mushrooms to grow in his apartment.  (Complaint at p. 3, ¶¶ 1-2, 6-8.)  Plaintiff notified the landlord, but the issue remained unresolved, culminating in violations from the health department and a complete ceiling collapse.  (Complaint at p. 3, ¶¶ 4-5, 9-13.)  Even after repairs, the habitability issues remained.  (See Complaint at pp. 4-5, ¶¶ 15-40.)  “[A]s a direct result of the mold and the exposure to toxic particles, [Plaintiff] has incurred significant damages, including but not limited to expenses related to moving out, relocation, storage costs, and medical expenses, pain and suffering, and severe anxiety.”  (Complaint at p. 6, ¶ 42.)  Defendant “had a duty to prevent the danger to Subject Property and to Plaintiff and to prevent the aforementioned illness, emotional injury and homelessness to Plaintiff.”  (Complaint at p. 7, ¶ 12.)  This sufficiently alleges that Defendant breached its duty of care and caused Plaintiff’s damages.

The demurrer to the second cause of action is overruled.

C.        As Pleaded, Bad Faith is Not a Valid Cause of Action (Third Cause of Action).

Defendant correctly observes that “‘bad faith’ is not even a cause of action in its own right and Plaintiff must demonstrate how the alleged ‘bad faith’ actions contributed to an actual violation of law or code.  Just stating ‘bad faith’ as a cause of action is vague, ambiguous, and simply not a cause of action.”  (Demurrer at p. 5.)

Plaintiff contends that when “[l]iberally read, this states a claim for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, a recognized California cause of action,” and the allegations “support bad faith as both a contract and tort theory.”  (Opposition at p. 6.)

The demurrer to the third cause of action is sustained.

D.        Plaintiff Sufficiently Alleges a Fiduciary Relationship (Fourth Cause of Action).

Defendant argues that “the complaint fails to allege or provide proof of any written or oral agreement or any covenant between the parties creating fiduciary duties.”  (Demurrer at p. 5.)

A cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty requires (1) a fiduciary duty, (2) breach of the duty, and (3) damage caused by the breach.  (Charnay v. Cobert¿(2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 170, 182.)  “A fiduciary relationship is ‘ “ ‘any relation existing between parties to a transaction wherein one of the parties is in duty bound to act with the utmost good faith for the benefit of the other party.  Such a relation ordinarily arises where a confidence is reposed by one person in the integrity of another, and in such a relation the party in whom the confidence is reposed, if he voluntarily accepts or assumes to accept the confidence, can take no advantage from his acts relating to the interest of the other party without the latter’s knowledge or consent . . . . ’ ” ’ ”  (Wolf v. Superior Court (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 25, 29.)

Plaintiff alleges, “By virtue of the relationship between Plaintiff as lessee of the Premises and Defendant¿as the property management company . . . [Defendant] stands as a fiduciary to Plaintiff.”  (Complaint at p. 8, ¶ 20.)  This sufficiently alleges a fiduciary relationship.  A contract is not required.

The demurrer to the fourth cause of action is overruled.

E.        Plaintiff Sufficiently Alleges Breach of the Covenant of Quiet Enjoyment (Fifth Cause of Action).

As with the fourth cause of action, Defendant argues that the fifth cause of action “the complaint fails to allege or provide proof of any written or oral agreement or any covenant between the parties creating fiduciary duties, or any duties that would be owed,” and “[t]he non-existence of a covenant in this case makes claims of a breach of an alleged covenant vague and ambiguous.”  (Demurrer at p. 5.)

“In the absence of language to the contrary, every lease contains an implied covenant of quiet enjoyment, whereby the landlord impliedly covenants that the tenant shall have quiet enjoyment and possession of the premises.”  (Andrews v. Mobile Aire Estates (2005) 125 Cal.App.4th 578, 588.)

Plaintiff alleges the existence of a rental agreement between the parties, and “[i]mplied in the rental agreement between Defendants and Plaintiff is a covenant that the Defendants would not and will not interfere with Plaintiff's quiet enjoyment of the premises during the term of their respective tenancies.”  (Complaint at p. 9, ¶ 26.)  Defendant breached this covenant through its “failure and refusal to repair the alleged habitability violations and to maintain The Property in a habitable condition and in a condition consistent with the purpose for which it was rented.”  (Complaint at p. 9, ¶ 27.)

The demurrer to the fifth cause of action is overruled.

CONCLUSION

The demurrer to the third cause of action is SUSTAINED with 30 days’ leave to amend.

The demurrer is otherwise OVERRULED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

July 28, 2026

 

On September 19, 2025, Plaintiff Danielle Duitsman filed this action against Defendants The Cheesecake Factory Inc. and Alma Rabago.

The Complaint alleges (1) pregnancy discrimination in violation of in violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act (“FEHA”); (2) disability discrimination in violation of FEHA; (3) failure to accommodate a pregnancy-related condition in violation of FEHA; (4) failure to engage in the interactive process in violation of FEHA; (5) retaliation in violation of FEHA; (6) failure to prevent discrimination, harassment, and retaliation in violation of FEHA; (7) violation of California’s pregnancy disability leave law; (8) wrongful termination in violation of public policy; (9) intentional infliction of emotional distress; (10) negligent hiring, supervising, or retention; and (11) failure to provide rest breaks in violation of the Labor Code.

On March 23, 2026, Defendants filed a motion to compel arbitration.

DISCUSSION

When seeking to compel arbitration of a plaintiff’s claims, the defendant must allege the existence of an agreement to arbitrate.  (Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 219 (Condee).)  The burden then shifts to the plaintiff to prove the falsity of the agreement.  (Ibid.)  After the Court determines that an agreement to arbitrate exists, it then considers objections to its enforceability.  (Ibid.)

The Court must grant a petition to compel arbitration unless the defendant has waived the right to compel arbitration or if there are grounds to revoke the arbitration agreement.  (Ibid.; Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.)  Under California law and the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), an arbitration agreement may be invalid based upon grounds applicable to any contract, including unconscionability, fraud, duress, and public policy.  (Sanchez v. Western Pizza Enterprises, Inc. (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 154, 165-166.)

A.        Defendants Have Shown the Existence of An Arbitration Agreement.

On May 30, 2021, Plaintiff signed a Mutual Agreement to Arbitrate Claims.  (Motion at pp. 5-6; Lambert-Gaffney Decl., Ex. A [“Arbitration Agreement”].)  The parties agreed to arbitrate “any and all disputes or claims between the Company and me that arise out of or relate to my recruitment, employment or separation from employment with the Company (currently existing or which may arise in the future), including claims involving and/or against any current or former . . . agent or employee of the Company, . . . whether the disputes or claims arise under common law, in tort, in contract, or pursuant to a statute, regulation, or ordinance now in existence or which may in the future be enacted or recognized, . . . including, but not limited to . . . Claims for wrongful termination of employment, retaliation, violation of public policy, constructive discharge, infliction of emotional distress, . . . discrimination, negligence, . . . , and any other tort or tort-like causes of action relating to or arising from the employment relationship or the formation or termination thereof,” as well as other employment claims under state and federal law.

Plaintiff argues that the motion “has not met its burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that an agreement to arbitrate actually exists.”  (Opposition at p. 7.)  Defendants only need to “alleg[e] the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; see Condee, supra, 88 Cal.App.4th at p. 219.”  They have done so—and they have provided a copy of the written agreement.

Plaintiff also contends that she “do[es] not remember signing the alleged arbitration agreement and [she does] not recognize its contents.”  (Duitsman Decl. ¶ 16; Opposition at p. 8.)  Defendants’ custodian of records confirms that the Arbitration Agreement was in Plaintiff’s personnel file.  (Lambert-Gaffney Decl. ¶ 5.)  Importantly, Plaintiff does not argue that the written signature on the Arbitration Agreement is not hers.  “[A] petitioner is not required to authenticate an opposing party’s signature on an arbitration agreement as a preliminary matter in moving for arbitration or in the event the authenticity of the signature is not challenged.”  (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 846.)

Defendants have satisfied their burden of showing the existence of an agreement to arbitrate that covers Plaintiff’s claims.

C.        The Arbitration Agreement Satisfies the Armendariz Factors.

Arbitration agreements for FEHA claims must (1) provide for neutral arbitrators, (2) provide for more than minimal discovery, (3) require a written award, (4) provide for all of the types of relief that would otherwise be available in court, and (5) not require employees to pay either unreasonable costs or any arbitrators’ fees or expenses as a condition of access to the arbitration forum.  (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 102 (Armendariz).)  These requirements may apply to non-FEHA employment claims.  (See Pinela v. Neiman Marcus Group, Inc. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 227, 254 [applying the Armendariz factors in the context of claims under the Labor Code].)

The Arbitration Agreement provides for arbitration using a neutral arbitrator in accordance with the Employment Arbitration Rules and Mediation Procedures issued by the American Arbitration Association.  The arbitrator shall render a written award and opinion with the essential findings and conclusions upon which the award is based.  Discovery shall be conducted to the extent necessary to adequately arbitrate or defend any claims, including access to essential documents and witnesses, as determined by the arbitrator.  The company will bear the fees and expenses that are unique to arbitration.  Nothing precludes any type of relief that would otherwise be available in court.

Accordingly, the Arbitration Agreement satisfies Armendariz.

D.        The Arbitration Agreement Is Not Invalid Under the EFAA.

Plaintiff argues that the federal Ending Forced Arbitration of Sexual Assault and Sexual Harassment Act of 2021 (“EFAA”) invalidates the Arbitration Agreement.  (Opposition at pp. 4-7.)  Under the EFAA, no predispute arbitration agreement is valid or enforceable with respect to a case that relates to a sexual assault dispute or a sexual harassment dispute.   (9 U.S.C.A. § 402(a).)  “[T]he statute applies to the entire case, not merely to the sexual assault or sexual harassment claims alleged as a part of the case.  It is significant, moreover, that the statute does not require that the pendant claims arise out of the sexual assault or sexual harassment dispute; it is enough that the case relates to the sexual assault or sexual harassment claims.”  (Doe v. Second Street Corp. (2024) 105 Cal.App.5th 552, 577.)

“The term ‘sexual harassment dispute’ means a dispute relating to conduct that is alleged to constitute sexual harassment under applicable Federal, Tribal, or State law.”  (9 U.S.C.A. § 401(4).)  “Both the FEHA and title VII of the Federal Civil Rights Act [citation] prohibit employers from discharging or otherwise discriminating against a person on the basis of race in compensation, terms, conditions, or privileges of employment.  [Citations.]  Although the state and federal antidiscrimination legislation ‘differ in some particulars, their objectives are identical, and California courts have relied upon federal law to interpret analogous provisions of the state statute.  [Citations.]’”  (Clark v. Claremont University Center (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 639, 662.)  Borrowing from federal law, then, “[u]nwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favors, and other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature constitute sexual harassment when (1) submission to such conduct is made either explicitly or implicitly a term or condition of an individual’s employment, (2) submission to or rejection of such conduct by an individual is used as the basis for employment decisions affecting such individual, or (3) such conduct has the purpose or effect of unreasonably interfering with an individual’s work performance or creating an intimidating, hostile, or offensive working environment.”  (29 C.F.R. § 1604.11.)

Plaintiff argues that Defendants present “an overly narrow reading of what may constitute sexual harassment under current California law.”  (Opposition at p. 4.)  Plaintiff contends that she “was treated adversely, denied reasonable accommodation, and wrongfully terminated based on pregnancy, childbirth, and her related post-partum disabilities/medical conditions — conduct that constitutes sexual harassment under FEHA.”  (Id. at p. 5.)

However, under FEHA, “‘harassment’ because of sex includes sexual harassment, gender harassment, and harassment based on pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions.”  (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (j)(4)(C).)  The statute notably lists “sexual harassment” and “harassment based on pregnancy” as separate items.

The Complaint alleges that Defendants violated FEHA by “denying Plaintiff equal terms, conditions, and/or privileges of employment enjoyed by other employees; refusing to accommodate Plaintiff; failing to provide Plaintiff with legally required rest breaks while pregnant; failing to remedy or prevent discrimination; denying baby bonding leave; wrongfully terminating Plaintiff’s employment in violation of public policy.”  (Complaint ¶¶ 49, 62, 92.)

There are no facts about “unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favors, and other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature.”  (See 29 C.F.R. § 1604.11.)  Accordingly, Plaintiff has not “alleg[ed] conduct constituting a sexual harassment dispute or sexual assault dispute,” and the EFAA does not prevent arbitration.  (See 9 U.S.C.A. § 402(a).)

E.        There Is No Procedural Unconscionability.

For an arbitration agreement to be unenforceable as unconscionable, both procedural and substantive unconscionability must be present.  (Armendariz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 114.)  “[T]he more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.”  (Ibid.)

“The relevant factors in assessing the level of procedural unconscionability are oppression and surprise.”  (Orcilla v. Big Sur, Inc. (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 982, 997.)  “‘The oppression component arises from an inequality of bargaining power of the parties to the contract and an absence of real negotiation or a meaningful choice on the part of the weaker party.’”  (Abramson v. Juniper Networks, Inc. (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 638, 656.)  “The circumstances relevant to establishing oppression include, but are not limited to (1) the amount of time the party is given to consider the proposed contract; (2) the amount and type of pressure exerted on the party to sign the proposed contract; (3) the length of the proposed contract and the length and complexity of the challenged provision; (4) the education and experience of the party; and (5) whether the party’s review of the proposed contract was aided by an attorney.”  (Grand Prospect Partners, L.P. v. Ross Dress for Less, Inc. (2015) 232 Cal.App.4th 1332, 1348, fn. omitted.)  “The component of surprise arises when the challenged terms are ‘hidden in a prolix printed form drafted by the party seeking to enforce them.’”  (Ibid.)  “The adhesive nature of the employment contract requires [the court] to be ‘particularly attuned’ to [Plaintiff’s] claim of unconscionability [citation], but [the court] do[es] not subject the contract to the same degree of scrutiny as ‘[c]ontracts of adhesion that involve surprise or other sharp practices’ [citation].”  (Baltazar v. Forever 21, Inc. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 1237, 1245 (Baltazar).)

Plaintiff argues that the arbitration agreement is procedurally unconscionable because it is an oppressive contract of adhesion and “she was never given any indication of the legal ramifications of anything she signed.”  (Opposition at pp. 9-10.)  Arbitration agreements that are “take it or leave it” have some degree of procedural unconscionability.  (Ajamian v. CantorCO2e, L.P. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 771, 796.)  However, this Arbitration Agreement is a stand-alone six-page document clearly titled “Mutual Agreement to Arbitrate Claims.”  It includes (in bold font) instructions for opting out of the Waivers of Right To File Class, Collective, Representative or Joint Actions provision.  (Arbitration Agreement at p. 4.)  There is no language stating that the Arbitration Agreement is mandatory or that Plaintiff’s employment is contingent on her agreement to the terms.  Additionally, on the final page, above the signatures, the Arbitration Agreement states: (1) “THE PARTIES ACKNOWLEDGE AND AGREE THAT EACH HAS READ THIS AGREEMENT CAREFULLY AND UNDERSTAND THAT BY SIGNING IT, EACH IS WAIVING ALL RIGHTS TO A TRIAL OR HEARING BEFORE A JUDGE OR JURY OF ANY AND ALL DISPUTES AND CLAIMS SUBJECT TO ARBITRATION UNDER THIS AGREEMENT”; and (2) “I ACKNOWLEDGE THAT I HAVE CAREFULLY READ THIS AGREEMENT, THAT I UNDERSTAND ITS TERMS, THAT ALL UNDERSTANDINGS AND AGREEMENTS BETWEEN THE COMPANY AND ME RELATING TO THE SUBJECTS COVERED IN THE AGREEMENT ARE CONTAINED IN THIS AGREEMENT, AND THAT I HAVE ENTERED INTO THIS AGREEMENT VOLUNTARILY AND NOT IN RELIANCE ON ANY PROMISES OR REPRESENTATIONS BY THE COMPANY OTHER THAN THOSE CONTAINED IN THIS AGREEMENT ITSELF.  I FURTHER ACKNOWLEDGE THAT I HAVE BEEN GIVEN THE OPPORTUNITY TO AND HAVE BEEN ADVISED TO DISCUSS THIS AGREEMENT WITH MY PRIVATE LEGAL COUNSEL OR REPRESENTATIVE OF MY CHOOSING AND HAVE AVAILED MYSELF OF THAT OPPORTUNITY TO THE EXTENT I WISH TO DO SO.”  (Arbitration Agreement at p. 6.) 

Plaintiff has not shown any procedural unconscionability.

F.         There Is No Substantive Unconscionability.

Even if the Arbitration Agreement was a contract of adhesion and there was slight procedural unconscionability, Plaintiff has not shown any substantive unconscionability.

“‘Substantive unconscionability pertains to the fairness of an agreement’s actual terms and to assessments of whether they are overly harsh or one-sided.  [Citations.]  A contract term is not substantively unconscionable when it merely gives one side a greater benefit; rather, the term must be “so one-sided as to ‘shock the conscience.’”’  [Citation.]’”  (Carmona v. Lincoln Millennium Car Wash, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 74, 85.)

Plaintiff argues that “[r]equiring Plaintiff to exhaust Cheesecake’s internal complaint procedures before submitting claims to arbitration gives Cheesecake an explicit ‘free peek’ at Plaintiff’s claims.”  (Opposition at p. 12.)  Unlike the agreement in Plaintiff’s cited case, this agreement has no requirement to exhaust all internal dispute resolution procedures.  (Cf. Nyulassy v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 1267, 1282-1283.)  Instead, the parties agree to arbitrate claims “if either Party determines that the Company’s internal procedures for handling claims . . . have not resulted in a mutually acceptable resolution of the dispute.”  (Arbitration Agreement at p. 1.)

Plaintiff also argue that Defendants are “repeat players” with AAA, so “there is a severe risk . . . that if this matter is compelled to arbitration, the Arbitrator will have a strong interest in making biased rulings in favor of Defendants, including denial of additional and absolutely necessary discovery as well as the arbitration award itself, in order to continue receiving business from Defendants.”  (Opposition at pp. 12-13.)  This is pure speculation, and Plaintiff cites no authority for this being a ground for unconscionability.

In sum, there is no substantive unconscionability, and the Arbitration Agreement should not be invalidated.

CONCLUSION

Defendants’ motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED.  The entire action is STAYED pending the conclusion of the arbitration.

Plaintiff Roy Edward Thomas has served Form Interrogatories – General (Set One), Special Interrogatories (Set One), Request for Production of Documents (Set One), and Request for Admission (Set One).  Defendants Serrano Healthcare LLC and IHCM Management LLC never served responses.

On June 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed motions to compel responses and motions to deem the RFAs admitted.  Defendants did not file any oppositions.

Where a party fails to serve timely responses to discovery requests, the court may make an order compelling responses.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290 [interrogatories], 2031.300 [documents]; Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 403.)   A party that fails to serve timely responses waives any objections to the request, including ones based on privilege or the protection of attorney work product.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, subd. (a), 2031.300, subd. (a).)

When a party fails to timely respond to a request for admission, the propounding party may move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the requests be deemed admitted.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (b).)  The party who failed to respond waives any objections to the demand, unless the court grants them relief from the waiver, upon a showing that the party (1) has subsequently served a substantially compliant response, and (2) that the party’s failure to respond was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subds. (a)(1)-(2).)  The court shall grant a motion to deem admitted requests for admissions, “unless it finds that the party to whom the requests for admission have been directed has served, before the hearing on the motion, a proposed response to the requests for admission that is in substantial compliance with Section 2033.220.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).)

Defendants filed no oppositions to these motions and did not serve timely responses.  It does not appear that Defendants served substantially compliant responses prior to the hearing.

Accordingly, the motions are GRANTED.

Serrano Healthcare LLC is ordered to provide verified responses, without objections, to Form Interrogatories – General (Set One), Special Interrogatories (Set One), and Request for Production of Documents (Set One) within 30 days.

IHCM Management LLC is ordered to provide verified responses, without objections, to Form Interrogatories – General (Set One), Special Interrogatories (Set One), and Request for Production of Documents (Set One) within 30 days.

Request for Admission (Set One) is deemed admitted by Serrano Healthcare LLC.

Request for Admission (Set One) is deemed admitted by IHCM Management LLC.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING IN PART AND OVERRULING IN PART DEMURRER; DENYING MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

July 28, 2026

 

On December 22, 2025, Plaintiff Fabian Valenzuela filed this action against Defendants 1850 N. Whitley Inc., CIM Group L.P., SKR Group Inc., and others.

On February 5, 2026, Defendants 1850 N. Whitley Inc., CIM Group L.P., and SKR Group Inc. filed a demurrer to the second, fourth, fifth, and sixth causes of action.  They also filed a motion to strike.

DEMURRER

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true.  (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.)  “Because a demurrer challenges defects on the face of the complaint, it can only refer to matters outside the pleading that are subject to judicial notice.”  (Arce ex rel. Arce v. Kaiser Found. Health Plan, Inc. (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 471, 556.)

A.        The Second Cause of Action is Duplicative.

Defendants argue that the second cause of action for Tortious Breach of Implied Warranty of Habitability is duplicative of the first cause of action for contract-based Breach of Implied Warranty of Habitability, and the allegations are “insufficient as a matter of law to transform nonperformance of contractual duties into an independent tort.”  (Demurrer at pp. 6-7.)

In the rare contract cases where tort damages are available, “the duty that gives rise to tort liability is either completely independent of the contract or arises from conduct which is both intentional and intended to harm.”  (Erlich v. Menezes (1999) 21 Cal.4th 543, 552.)  “[A] warranty of habitability is implied by law in residential leases,” and Plaintiff does not allege any basis for tort liability beyond Defendants’ alleged breach of the implied terms of Plaintiff’s lease.  (Green v. Superior Court (1974) 10 Cal.3d 616, 637.)

The demurrer to the second cause of action is sustained.

B.        The Fourth Cause of Action Does Not Allege Outrageous Conduct.

“‘[T]o state a cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress a plaintiff must show: (1) outrageous conduct by the defendant; (2) the defendant’s intention of causing or reckless disregard of the probability of causing emotional distress; (3) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (4) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct.’  [Citation.]  ‘Conduct, to be ‘outrageous’ must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized society.’  [Citation.]”  (Huntingdon Life Sciences, Inc. v. Stop Huntingdon Animal Cruelty USA, Inc. (2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 1228, 1259.)  “While the outrageousness of a defendant’s conduct normally presents an issue of fact to be determined by the trier of fact [citation], the court may determine in the first instance, whether the defendant’s conduct may reasonably be regarded as so extreme and outrageous as to permit recovery.”  (Trerice v. Blue Cross of California (1989) 209 Cal.App.3d 878, 883.)  When a plaintiff does not suffer physical injury, the conduct must involve “extreme and outrageous intentional invasions of one’s mental and emotional tranquility.”  (Alcorn v. Anbro Engineering, Inc. (1970) 2 Cal.3d 493, 498.)

Defendants argue that Plaintiff alleges “at most, ordinary landlord-tenant disputes concerning maintenance and repairs and do not rise to the level of extreme and outrageous conduct that ‘exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.’”  (Demurrer at p. 7.)

Plaintiff’s claim is based on Defendants “intentionally failing to repair, maintain, exterminate and remediate the defective conditions in the Subject Property.”  (Complaint ¶ 55.)  The conditions included water leaks, mold, water stains, crumbling walls and ceilings, peeling paint, wood rot, poor plumbing drainage, and constant sewage odors.  (Complaint ¶ 13.)  Defendants’ failure to remedy these defects is not outrageous and so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized society.

The demurrer to the fourth cause of action is sustained.

C.        The Fifth Cause of Action Is Not Wholly Duplicative.

To prevail on a claim for private nuisance, a plaintiff must prove (1) an interference with his use and enjoyment of his property; (2) “that the invasion of the plaintiff’s interest in the use and enjoyment of the land was substantial, i.e., that it caused the plaintiff to suffer ‘substantial actual damage’”; and (3) that the interference was unreasonable, “i.e., it must be ‘of such a nature, duration or amount as to constitute unreasonable interference with the use and enjoyment of the land.’”  (San Diego Gas & Electric Co. v. Superior Court (1996) 13 Cal.4th 893, 938.)

Defendants argue that the private nuisance claim “merely repackages alleged failures to repair” and is duplicative.  (Demurrer at pp. 7-8.)  But the legal claims are distinct, and Plaintiff is permitted to plead alternative theories of liability.

The demurrer to the fifth cause of action is overruled.

D.        The Sixth Cause of Action Alleges a Legal Basis for Unlawful Practices.

California’s Unfair Competition Law (“UCL”) includes any unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business act or practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue, or misleading advertising.  (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200.)  The UCL embraces “anything that can properly be called a business practice and that at the same time is forbidden by law.”  (Cel-Tech Communications, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Co. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 163, 180.)  “By proscribing any unlawful business practice, section 17200 borrows violations of other laws and treats them as unlawful practices that the unfair competition law makes independently actionable.”  (Ibid.; see Klein v. Earth Elements, Inc. (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 965, 969 [“Virtually any law can serve as the predicate for a section 17200 action.”].)

Plaintiff has sufficiently pleaded several violations of landlord-tenant law that can serve as bases for this claim.  (See Complaint ¶¶ 68-69.)  Accordingly, Plaintiff has stated a claim under the UCL.

The demurrer to the sixth cause of action is overruled.

MOTION TO STRIKE

The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subds. (a)-(b).)

Defendants move to strike Paragraphs 45, 52, 57, and 65, as well as the prayer for punitive damages.  A plaintiff can recover punitive damages in tort cases where “the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.”  (Civ. Code § 3294, subd. (a).)  “The mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages.  [Citation.]  Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim.  [Citation.]”  (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166, fn. omitted.)  A corporate employer can be liable for punitive damages only when an officer, director, or managing agent of the corporation authorized or ratified the wrongful conduct or was personally guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (b).)  The facts about water leaks, mold, rodent infestation, and other conditions, if proven, would support a finding of oppression or malice.  Allegations that Defendants knew of the conditions and failed to act—with conditions persisting for over four years (see Complaint ¶¶ 18-27)—could also support a finding that Defendants’ agents ratified the conduct.  The motion is denied for these items.

Defendants move to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for attorney fees because “Plaintiff does not allege the existence of any lease provision entitling him to attorneys’ fees, nor does he identify any statute authorizing fee recovery for the tort-based causes of action asserted.”  (Motion at p. 9.)  Plaintiff prays for “reasonable attorney’s fees to the extent provided for by Plaintiff’s lease and/or applicable law.”  The Complaint attaches a copy of the lease as Exhibit A.  Defendants also argue that “Plaintiff does not establish that CIM Group, L.P. or SKR Group, Inc. signed the lease or assumed contractual fee obligations under it,” and Plaintiff does not “identify any independent statute authorizing attorney's fees under the negligence, IIED, private nuisance, or UCL causes of action as pleaded.”  (Reply at p. 7.)  If the contract does not reach the moving Defendants, or if there is no applicable law awarding attorney fees for a particular cause of action, then attorney fees will not be available.  At the pleading stage, however, there is no basis to strike these requests for relief.  The motion is denied for these items.

Defendants move to strike Paragraphs 71 and 72, which allege Plaintiff’s entitlement to “a monetary judgment against Defendants equal to all of the rent and other monies paid to Defendants by Plaintiff during the term of his tenancy.”  According to Defendants, “[r]estitution is not available to recover money paid pursuant to a valid contractual obligation, nor may a plaintiff obtain disgorgement of profits in which the plaintiff has no ownership interest.”  (Motion at p. 9.)  Restitution is an available remedy under the UCL for property that “may have been acquired by means of such unfair competition.”  (See Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17203.)  Here, Plaintiff alleges that Defendants continued to collect rent on a property that Defendants know to be uninhabitable, in violation of law.  (Complaint ¶¶ 68-69.)  The motion is denied for these items.

CONCLUSION

The demurrer to the second and fourth causes of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.  The demurrer is otherwise OVERRULED.

The motion to strike is DENIED.

Defendants are ordered to file an answer within 10 days.
