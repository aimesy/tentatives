PLAINTIFF’S REQUEST FOR DEFAULT JUDGMENT

 

           

Plaintiff Audrey Best (“Plaintiff”) requests entry of default judgment against Defendant Gemma R Capispisan (“Defendant”). Plaintiff seeks judgment in the total amount of $27,785.00, comprising $9,185.00 in special damages, $18,000.00 in general damages, and $600.00 in costs.

Most recently, the Court denied Plaintiff’s request for default judgment on March 16, 2026 due to three errors. Plaintiff corrected two of them. However, Plaintiff did not file evidence that she served a statement of damages on Defendant.

“[A] trial court’s jurisdiction to grant relief on a default judgment [is limited] to the amount stated in the complaint.¿¿” (¿¿Dhawan ¿v. Biring (2015) 241 Cal.App.4th 963, 968¿¿¿.) “The amount of the default judgment ‘cannot exceed that demanded in the complaint, in the statement required by ¿¿Section 425.11¿¿, or in the statement provided for by ¿¿Section 425.115.’” (Ibid., citing Code Civ. Proc., § 580, subd. (a)¿¿.) “The statement required by ¿¿section 425.11¿¿ is a statement of damages served separately on a defendant in ‘an action to recover damages for personal injury or wrongful death.’” (¿¿Id. at pp. 968-969, citing Code Civ. Proc., § 425.11, subd. (b)¿¿.)

Because Plaintiff’s injuries arise from a car accident, Code of Civil Procedure section 425.11, subdivision (b), states that “[w]hen a complaint is filed in an action to recover damages for personal injury or wrongful death, the defendant may at any time request a statement setting forth the nature and amount of damages being sought.” “If no request is made for the statement referred to in subdivision (b), the plaintiff shall serve the statement on the defendant before a default may be taken.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.11, subd. (c).) Plaintiff has not proffered evidence to the Court that she served a statement of damages on Defendant. Plaintiff is required to do so, in compliance with Code of Civil Procedure section 425.11, subdivision (d)(1). (See Code Civ. Proc., § 425.11, subd. (d)(1) [“If a party has not appeared in the action, the statement shall be served in the same manner as a summons.”].)

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff’s request for default judgment is DENIED without prejudice. The Court notes that the statute requires that the statement of damages be served before default is taken. Discussion

A.    Financial Discovery

Code of Civil Procedure section 3295 requires that “[n]o pretrial discovery by the plaintiff shall be permitted with respect to” (1) the profits the defendant has gained by virtue of the wrongful course of conduct of the nature and (2) type shown by the evidence and the financial condition of the defendant, “unless the court enters an order permitting such discovery pursuant to this subdivision.” (Id. at subds. (a)(1)-(2), (c).)

“[W]hen the agreement itself is procured by fraud, none of its provisions have any legal or binding effect. This provision of the contract -- even if we assume that, if valid, it could redound to the benefit of the Association -- was not a waiver of plaintiffs’ right to maintain the action. The fraud which was the inducing cause of the execution of the contract renders the whole instrument vulnerable -- the clause in question as well as all the other provisions.” (Vai v. Bank of America Nat’l Trust & Sav. Asso. (1961) 56 Cal.2d 329, 344 [internal quotations omitted].) Punitive damages “may be awarded where a defendant fraudulently induces the plaintiff to enter into a contract. [Citations.] The words ‘oppression, fraud, or malice’ in Civil Code section 3294 being in the disjunctive, fraud alone is an adequate basis for awarding punitive damages.” ((Glendale Fed. Sav. & Loan Assn. v. Marina View Heights Dev. Co. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 101, 135.)

As a preliminary matter, Plaintiff points out that the Court previously made a determination regarding fraud in its September 8, 2025 Order. After considering the evidence provided by the parties, the Court chose not to enforce a forum-selection clause in the parties’ Sales/Purchase Agreement because Plaintiff presented evidence showing Rosenberg procured the agreement by fraud. (Sharp Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 2.) The Court explicitly found, inter alia, that “[i]n light of the foregoing, the Court finds that Plaintiff has provided evidence to support her argument that the subject agreement was ‘procured by fraud’ such that ‘none of its provisions have any legal or binding effect.’ (Vai v. Bank of America Nat’l Trust & Sav. Asso., supra, 56 Cal.2d at p. 344.) Accordingly, the Court denies the Airtime Defendants’ apparent motion to enforce the subject forum selection clause.” (Order 9/8/25, 7:13-17.)

Not only did the Court previously decide that Plaintiff established fraud sufficient to void the contract, but Plaintiff also presents additional evidence in the instant motion to support fraud.

First, in the FAC, Plaintiff alleges, inter alia, that her “consent to the May 8, 2024, Sales / Purchase Agreement with Airtime LLC was induced by fraud and/or undue influence within the meaning of California Civil Code § 1689, entitling Plaintiff Sefton to rescission of the contract.” (FAC, ¶ 56.) Next, in Plaintiff’s supporting declaration, Plaintiff attests, inter alia, that “Rosenberg called me and told me he had found an owner of a 1999 Eurocopter EC120 Colibri five-seat, single-engine, light utility helicopter, FAA registration N42GH (the ‘EC120’). Rosenberg told me that the owner of the EC120 was a wealthy individual who was a ‘weekend flyer’ and ‘looking to offload some of his toys’ after being diagnosed with cancer. I later learned that these representations were false.” (Sefton Decl., ¶ 9.)

Plaintiff states that “Rosenberg told me that I needed to act quickly if I wanted to buy the EC120, because the seller had not yet put the aircraft on the market, and was about to do so, and that he expected the helicopter to be snapped up if offered for sale to other potential purchasers as the market was very competitive.” (Sefton Decl., ¶ 10.) Plaintiff further attests that “[i]n our phone conversation, among other things, Rosenberg told me that the EC120 was overdue for certain inspections called for by Eurocopter, which he referred to as the ‘12 year’ and ‘15 year’ inspections. Rosenberg downplayed the significance of these inspections, telling me that they would only be required if I wanted to use the helicopter for commercial purposes, and that it was common for private owners to forego these inspections. I later learned that these representations were false.” (Sefton Decl., ¶ 11.)

Plaintiff states that “[b]ased on Rosenberg’s statements about the EC120, and believing that he was acting as my broker, I authorized him to convey an offer of $600,000 to the owner of the EC120.” (Sefton Decl., ¶ 12.) Plaintiff states that “[o]n Thursday, May 2, 2025, I texted Rosenberg that I would accept the seller’s $625,000 counter-offer.” (Sefton Decl., ¶ 14.) Plaintiff further states that “Defendant Rosenberg on or about May 6, 2024, sent me a draft document entitled ‘Sales / Purchase Agreement.’ The document left a blank in the space provided for the name and address of the seller. Based on Rosenberg’s statements to me, I believed the seller was a wealthy individual in Nevada, or an entity owned and controlled by that individual.” (Sefton Decl., ¶ 17.) Plaintiff states that “I spoke to Rosenberg by phone on or about May 6, 2024, concerning my edits to the draft contract. Rosenberg did not tell me that he had already signed a contract with the seller to purchase the EC120 himself on behalf of Airtime LLC, and did not tell me that he or his company would be the seller when I purchased the helicopter.” (Sefton Decl., ¶ 18.)

Plaintiff states that she “learned that Rosenberg had arranged to purchase the EC120 himself from the Nevada owner for approximately $400,000, using funds I had wired into escrow at Rosenberg’s direction, and immediately sell the EC120 to me for the inflated price of $625,000… He did not disclose to me that he was purchasing the EC120 for himself and making a profit at my expense.” (Sefton Decl., ¶ 24.) In addition, Plaintiff states that she “reviewed the Sale/Purchase Agreement and for the first time focused on the listing of Airtime LLC as the ‘Seller.’ I called Rosenberg on the phone and asked him why he had listed Airtime LLC as the Seller, and he told me, ‘I don’t know.’” (Sefton Decl., ¶ 23.)

Plaintiff’s motion asserts that “[t]he most clear and convincing evidence is Rosenberg's own admission that his fraud on Sefton was part of a common scheme or plan that he has engaged in with others.” (Mot., 9:14-16.) Rosenberg’s declaration in support of the forum non conveniens motion states, inter alia, that

5. When I assist a client, on behalf of Airtime, I utilize a transaction structure referred to as a back-to-back transaction.

6. In a back-to-back transaction a middle person like Airtime acts as both an interim buyer and interim seller of an aircraft.

7. The middle person is aware of a potential seller and buyer, buys the aircraft from the seller and then sells it to the buyer.

8. The middle person’s compensation is the difference between the purchase price from the original seller and the sale price to the end buyer.

            (Sharp Decl., ¶ 5; Ex. 9, Rosenberg Decl., ¶¶ 4-9.) Plaintiff asserts that Rosenberg admits he acts as a broker for his clients. Plaintiff cites Horning v. Shilberg (2005) 130 Cal.App.4th 197, 204, where the court expressed that a “person acts as a broker only if he or she is acting (1) for compensation and (2) on behalf of someone else.” Plaintiff contends that “Rosenberg’s declaration does not assert that he discloses to his ‘clients’ that he intends to wear multiple hats (‘both an interim buyer and interim seller’ as well as his client’s broker). He does not assert that his clients agreed to allow Rosenberg to use their money, deposited in escrow, to be an ‘interim buyer’ of an aircraft available for purchase from a third party. Here, Rosenberg purported to act on behalf of Sefton, conveying an offer of $600,000 on her behalf, purporting to negotiate to replace the front windows, securing hangar space at Whiteman Airport, and offering her advice on safety, valuation, and setting up an LLC, among other things.” (Mot., 10:5-11.)

            Plaintiff cites Ward v. Taggart (1959) 51 Cal.2d 736, 740-742, where the Supreme Court held that punitive damages are available when a broker defrauds a client by making a “secret profit” at the client’s expense. The court stated that “section 3294 of the Civil Code … authorizes exemplary damages ‘in an action for the breach of an obligation not arising from contract, where the defendant has been guilty [of] oppression, fraud, or malice.’ The word ‘contract’ is used in this section in its ordinary sense to mean an agreement between the parties, not an obligation imposed by law despite the absence of any such agreement. Taggart’s obligation does not arise from any agreement between him and plaintiffs. It arises from his fraud and violation of statutory duties.” (Id. at pp. 742-743.)

            Airtime Defendants contend that Plaintiff cannot gain access to their financial condition because she has not demonstrated a probability of prevailing, and Civil Code section 3294, the basis for Plaintiff’s punitive damages request, bars Plaintiff’s request. Regarding the probability of prevailing, the Court already expressly determined Plaintiff has a probability of prevailing on her fraud cause of action. Additionally, the foregoing evidence demonstrates Airtime Defendants may have acted fraudulently and in violation of their duty to act on behalf of their clients’ interests. As for Civil Code section 3294, subdivision (a), it explicitly states that “[i]n an action for the breach of an obligation not arising from contract, where it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice, the plaintiff, in addition to the actual damages, may recover damages for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant.” Airtime Defendants contend that “[e]verything about this case arises from contract: a contract to purchase a helicopter. The contract is itself attached as an exhibit to the 1st Amended Complaint. Thus, even if Plaintiff were able to show strong likelihood of prevailing on her fraud claims, those claims are ineligible for punitive damages because they most assuredly arise from the helicopter sales contract.” (Opp., 6:1-5.)

            Plaintiff’s reply refutes this, asserting that her “claims are for fraud and violation of the Consumer Legal Remedies Act (CLRA), and not for breach of contract. (Sharp Dec. Exh. 1 [First Amended Complaint].) Plaintiff’s CLRA claim arises from statute, not contract, and punitive damages are available for a willful violation of the CLRA by statute.” (Reply, 4:9-13; see also Civ. Code, § 1785.31 [“Any consumer who suffers damages as a result of a violation of this title by any person may bring an action in a court of appropriate jurisdiction against that person to recover[,] … [i]n the case of a willful violation[,] … [p]unitive damages.”].) Plaintiff also asserts that her “fraud claim sounds in tort, not contract, and it is well-settled that ‘[a]lthough punitive damages may not ordinarily be given for breach of contract, . . .such damages may be awarded where a defendant fraudulently induces the plaintiff to enter into a contract.’” (Reply, 4:13-16, citing Glendale Fed. Sav. & Loan Assn. v. Marina View Heights Dev. Co., supra, 66 Cal.App.3d at p. 135.) Lastly, Plaintiff contends that “[t]his squarely-on-point authority was cited in Plaintiff’s motion; Defendants simply ignore it.” (Reply, 4:18-19.)

            The Court finds that Plaintiff adequately demonstrated a probability of prevailing on her fraud cause of action, thus warranting financial discovery into Airtime Defendants’ financial condition. Airtime Defendants do not adequately refute Plaintiff’s evidence, nor do they provide the Court with law demonstrating Plaintiff’s punitive damages request is barred. Plaintiff’s causes of action are not based in contract, as Airtime Defendants contend. Accordingly, the Court grants Plaintiff’s request to conduct discovery into Airtime Defendants’ financial condition.

B.     Production of Unredacted Contract Documents

“[A]ny party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or to the determination of any motion made in that action, if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence… Discovery may be obtained of the identity and location of persons having knowledge of any discoverable matter…” ((Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010.)

Plaintiff seeks unredacted contract documents because Rosenberg redacted the names of the previous clients. Plaintiff contends that a claim for punitive damages requires the trier of fact to assess “[t]he reprehensibility of the conduct of the defendant.” (Adams v. Murakami (1991) 54 Cal.3d 105, 126 (dis. opn. of Mosk, M.).) “Prior bad acts evidence is admissible to show a common plan or scheme if the probative value of the evidence outweighs the potential for prejudice.” ((Butler v. LeBouef (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 198, 205.) Plaintiff contends that, “[t]hus, Rosenberg’s conduct with his other ‘clients’ is relevant and admissible on Sefton’s claim for punitive damages.” (Mot., 12:11-12.)

Plaintiff also asserts that Rosenberg waived objections based on privacy. Plaintiff asserts that “Rosenberg’s responses to Sefton’s Interrogatories and Document Requests did not mention privacy, or cite any statute or constitutional provision. (Sharp Dec. Exhs. 12 & 13.) Rosenberg did not move for a protective order authorizing him to redact documents or withhold discovery. (See. Civ. Proc. Code § 2031.060(a).) Had he done so, he would have been required to carry the burden of demonstrating good cause.” (Mot., 12:21-25.) Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.060, subdivision (a), provides that “[w]hen an inspection, copying, testing, or sampling of documents, tangible things, places, or electronically stored information has been demanded, the party to whom the demand has been directed, and any other party or affected person, may promptly move for a protective order.” “The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other person from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.060, subd. (b).)

Plaintiff also contends that in an effort to avoid motion practice, her counsel emailed Airtime Defendant’s counsel stating that “[a] protective order will alleviate whatever confidentiality concern your client may have. If you will not agree to produce unredacted contracts subject to a protective order, please cite whatever authority you are relying on to withhold the requested discovery.” (Sharp Decl., ¶ 8, Ex. 15.) Airtime Defendant’s counsel responded, asserting that “[t]hese contracts are privileged and confidential pursuant to their express terms. No further ‘authority’ is needed to determine that privileged and confidential status. They will not be produced in un-redacted form.” (Ibid.) Plaintiff asserts that “[t]hus, Rosenberg repeatedly failed to cite a specific, legally cognizable objection, and thus waived any such objections.” (Mot., 13:7-8.)

Airtime Defendants’ opposition asserts that “[t]hose contracts contained express confidentiality provisions, which were protected from disclosure by redaction. Plaintiff now seeks to ignore that express confidentiality, requesting disclosure of the identities of those 3rd parties to whom protection was promised by Defendants. Moreover, there is no relevance to the identities of 3rd parties that had similar contracts to the within agreement between Plaintiff and Defendants, but had nothing at all to do with the within transaction. Disclosure ignoring the express confidentiality will also not lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. Naming names doesn't provide anything more than a springboard for harassment of people that have no connection to any of the relevant issues in this case.” (Opp., 6:21-7:2.) Further, Airtime Defendants contend that “[t]he mere fact that there were back-to-back transactions, in and of themselves does not rise to the level of ‘Fraud.’ Nor does the allegations that Defendants have conducted similar past transactions rise to the level of ‘Fraud.’ Further Plaintiff has failed to overcome Defendants’ position that ‘no good cause’ or relevant facts which could lead to admissible evidence has been presented.” (Opp., 7:11-16.)

Airtime Defendants do not present the Court with a legal basis for withholding the identities of the contracted parties. Airtime Defendants assert that the confidentiality provisions control; however, as noted by Plaintiff, a confidentiality provision is insufficient on its own to withhold relevant discovery and, Airtime Defendants sought to maintain the privacy of the parties’ identities, they could move for a protective order. Airtime Defendants did not move for a protective order and have not demonstrated good cause in the instant motion to keep the names redacted. Rather, the Court finds that Plaintiff demonstrated good cause to gain access to the unredacted contracts. Plaintiff demonstrates that this information could lead to admissible evidence that supports her punitive damages claim. Airtime Defendants do not provide the Court with a legal basis for denying Plaintiff’s request. Accordingly, the Court grants Plaintiff’s request for unredacted documents of the contract documents.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff’s motion is GRANTED in its entirety. The Court finds that Plaintiff may conduct discovery into the financial condition of Airtime Defendants. The Court orders Airtime Defendants to produce unredacted copies of the contract documents described as “back-to-back transactions.”

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of this Order.

Legal Standard

            A party may file a special motion to strike to dismiss entire complaints, specific causes of action, or portions of a cause of action when the party believes the lawsuit is a strategic attempt to infringe upon their “valid exercise of the constitutional rights of freedom of speech and petition for the redress of grievances.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (a).) The anti-SLAPP statute (strategic lawsuits against public participation) protects parties from frivolous lawsuits: “you have a right not to be dragged through the courts because you exercised your constitutional rights.” ((People ex rel. Lockyer v. Brar (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1317.) The legislature intended this statute to be construed broadly. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (a).)

Anti-SLAPP actions must be filed within 60 days of service of the operative complaint and stays discovery until after disposition of the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subds. (f), (g).)

Anti-SLAPP actions employ a two-step analysis to determine the merits. (Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal. 4th 82, 88 (Navellier).) In the first prong, the defendant must make a threshold showing that the challenged cause of action arises from protected activity. (Ibid.) “A defendant meets this burden by demonstrating that the act underlying the plaintiff’s cause fits one of the [following] categories:” (Ibid.)

(1) any written or oral statement or writing made before a legislative, executive, or judicial proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law,

(2) any written or oral statement or writing made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a legislative, executive, or judicial body, or any other official proceeding authorized by law,

(3) any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest, or

(4) any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest.

(Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (e).) If the court finds the defendant made the requisite showing, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to demonstrate the probability that their cause(s) of action will succeed. (Code Civ. Proc. § 425.16, subd. (b)(1); Navellier, supra, at p. 88-89.)

The second step employs a “summary-judgment-like procedure,” because the court will not weigh evidence or resolve conflicting factual claims. (Sweetwater Union High School District v. Gilbane Building Co. (2019) 6 Cal.5th 931, 940 (Sweetwater).) The court’s inquiry is limited to whether the plaintiff “stated a legally sufficient claim and made a prima facie factual showing sufficient to sustain a favorable judgment.” (Ibid.) This burden is minimal; however, a plaintiff cannot solely rely on their complaint—even if verified. (Ibid.) Instead, they must provide competent evidence, which the court accepts as true. (Ibid.) The court will only evaluate the defendant’s showing “to determine if it defeats the plaintiff’s claim as a matter of law.” (Ibid.)

“Only a cause of action that satisfies both prongs of the anti-SLAPP statute—i.e., that arises from protected speech or petitioning and lacks even minimal merit—is a SLAPP, subject to being stricken under the statute.” (Navellier, supra, at p. 89.)

If a defendant prevails on their anti-SLAPP motion, they are entitled to attorney fees and costs as a matter of right. (Code Civ. Proc. § 425.16, subd. (c)(1).) However, if the anti-SLAPP motion is deemed frivolous or intended to cause unnecessary delay, the court will award the prevailing plaintiff reasonable fees, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 128.5. (Ibid.)

Discussion

            Attorney Defendants move to strike the sole cause of action alleged in Plaintiff’s complaint, which alleges malicious prosecution.

A.    Prong One: Protected Activity

The Anti-SLAPP Statute protects “¿(1) any written or oral statement or writing made before a legislative, executive, or judicial proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (2) any written or oral statement or writing made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a legislative, executive, or judicial body, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (3) any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest, or (4) any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (e).)

a.      Attorney Defendants’ Motion

Attorney Defendants assert that “Ms. Almadova’s malicious prosecution claims are based solely on WRLG’s activities as counsel for Mr. Hougland in the Underlying Lawsuit. Ms. Almadova does not allege that WRLG acted outside of this context. Regardless of whether the Underlying Lawsuit was maintained maliciously, the act of filing pleadings in a civil action on behalf of a client is per se protected petitioning activity under the Anti-SLAPP statute.” (Mot., 12:10-14.) Plaintiff does not appear to dispute this.

In the complaint, it states, “[i]n February 2024, Defendants commenced retaliatory litigation against Ms. Almadova in the Superior Court of California, County of Orange (Hougland v. Cannon Cochran Management Services, Inc. and Susan Almadova -- later removed to the United States District Court for the Central District of California, Southern Division, USDC Case No. 8:24-00532 JWH ADS), asserting two frivolous claims: violation of California Labor Code section 1102.5 (Whistleblower Retaliation) and Age Harassment in violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act (‘FEHA’) (the ‘Hougland Matter’). The Hougland Matter asserted the same two claims and eight additional claims against CCMSI.” (Compl., ¶ 4.) The complaint alleges that “Defendants, willfully disregarding the fact that Mr. Hougland could not identify a single instance in which Ms. Almadova engaged in any discriminatory or harassing conduct or speech (much less a pattern), drafted and filed a public document falsely accusing Ms. Almadova of ‘a pattern and practice of harassing [Mr. Hougland],’ ‘engaging in offensive conduct,’ and ‘acting in a hostile and abusive manner’ toward him.” (Compl., ¶ 6.)

Because Plaintiff’s complaint solely and directly arises from Attorney Defendants’ litigation of and involvement in the Hougland Matter, Plaintiff’s complaint is predicated on protected petitioning activity. Thus, the cause of action arises out of protected activity, and prong one is satisfied.

b.      Hougland’s Motion

Hougland contends that Plaintiff’s cause of action against him implicates his protected activity of filing the employment lawsuit. Hougland cites Jarrow Formulas, Inc. v. LaMarche (2003) 31 Cal.4th 728, 734-735, where the court stated that “by its terms, section 425.16 potentially may apply to every malicious prosecution action, because every such action arises from an underlying lawsuit, or petition to the judicial branch. By definition, a malicious prosecution suit alleges that the defendant committed a tort by filing a lawsuit.”

Plaintiff’s opposition does not appear to dispute Hougland’s contention. Similar to Attorney Defendants, Plaintiff’s complaint was filed against Hougland because of his involvement and litigation of his case against Plaintiff. Thus, the cause of action arises out of protected activity, and prong one is satisfied.

B.     Prong Two: Probability of Success on the Merits - Attorney Defendants

Attorney Defendants demonstrated that the cause of action arises out of protected activity, so the burden shifts to Plaintiff to show that “each challenged claim based on protected activity is legally sufficient and factually substantiated.” (Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 396.) Affidavits, declarations, and their equivalents may be considered in evaluating an anti-SLAPP motion, so long as the evidence is admissible or reasonably capable of being admitted at trial. (Sweetwater, supra, 6 Cal.5th at p. 949.)

“[I]n order to establish a cause of action for malicious prosecution of either a criminal or civil proceeding, a plaintiff must demonstrate ‘that the prior action (1) was commenced by or at the direction of the defendant and was pursued to a legal termination in his, plaintiff’s, favor [citations]; (2) was brought without probable cause [citations]; and (3) was initiated with malice.’” (Van Audenhove v. Perry (2017) 11 Cal.App.5th 915, 919.)

a.      Favorable Termination

“A favorable termination does not occur merely because a party complained against has prevailed in an underlying action. While the fact he has prevailed is an ingredient of a favorable termination, such termination must further reflect on his innocence of the alleged wrongful conduct. If the termination does not relate to the merits—reflecting on neither innocence of nor responsibility for the alleged misconduct—the termination is not favorable in the sense it would support a subsequent action for malicious prosecution. [W]hen the underlying action is terminated in some manner other than by a judgment on the merits, the court examines the record to see if the disposition reflects the opinion of the court or the prosecuting party that the action would not succeed.” (Sycamore Ridge Apartments, LLC v. Naumann (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1385, 1399 (Sycamore Ridge) [internal quotations and citations omitted].)

“However, actions for malicious prosecution have historically been viewed with disfavor due to the potential chilling effect on persons considering reporting a crime or pursuing legal remedies in court. [Citation.] For this reason, the twin requirements of no probable cause and favorable termination are, and should be, strictly enforced.” (Ludwig v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 8, 29.) “[A] dismissal resulting from negotiation, settlement or agreement is generally not deemed a favorable termination of the proceedings. [Citations.] The purpose of a settlement is to avoid a determination on the merits.” (Villa v. Cole (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 1327, 1335-1136; see also Ludwig v. Superior Court, supra, at p. 29 [“Villa … make[s] clear that if a dismissal results from a negotiated settlement, the plaintiff’s motivation is not to be considered”].) “It is true that where a case is dismissed pursuant to a give-and-take settlement, it ‘will not be viewed as a favorable termination as long as [the dismissal] was a necessary condition to achievement of the overall settlement.’” (Roche v. Hyde (2020) 51 Cal.App.5th 757, 789-790.)

            Attorney Defendants’ motion asserts that Plaintiff “cannot plead malicious prosecution as to the Whistleblower claim because her dismissal from that cause of action was obtained through a stipulation, in which Ms. Almadova agreed to a waiver of fees and costs.” (Mot., 13:17-19.) Attorney Defendants concede that “[u]nlike the Whistleblower claim, WRLG does not dispute that the underlying age harassment claim was terminated in Ms. Almadova’s favor. However, WRLG strongly contends Ms. Almadova still cannot show the Underlying Lawsuit was brought without probable cause or with malice.” (Mot., 14:13-15.)

            Plaintiff asserts that “[w]hile admitting the age harassment claim was terminated on the merits, the Attorney Defendants misleadingly portray their dismissal of Hougland’s whistleblower retaliation claim as a non-merits-based compromise in exchange for Almadova’s waiver of fees and costs and argue that a negotiated dismissal is not a ‘favorable termination’ for purposes of a malicious prosecution claim. What the Attorney Defendants fail to disclose is that the stipulation merely gave effect to their unilateral decision to abandon their whistleblower retaliation claim by filing a Notice of Non-Opposition to Almadova’s Motion to Dismiss – a decision made before any discussion of a stipulation (with or without a fee and cost waiver).” (Opp., 12:9-16.)

            Plaintiff provides supporting evidence for how the motion to dismiss stipulation arose. Plaintiff provides the declaration from her counsel, Jeffrey M. Cohon, who “personally handled the defense of the Hougland Action since CCMSI and Ms. Almadova first made an appearance in the District Court.” (Cohon Decl., ¶ 2.) Cohon attests that he “sent a detailed meet and confer letter to the Attorney Defendants advising them that their claim for whistleblower retaliation against Ms. Almadova pursuant to Labor Code section 1102.5 was improper because the whistleblower statute did not provide for individual liability. [He] also provided legal authority supporting this position, including judicial decisions from the Central District of California specifically holding that supervisors cannot – as a matter of law – be liable for an alleged violation of the whistleblower statute.” (Cohon Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 2.) Cohon explains that during a meeting with Mr. DeClue, counsel of record for the Hougland Matter, “Mr. DeClue could not identify a single case holding that an individual supervisor could be liable under Labor Code section 1102.5. Instead, Mr. DeClue inaccurately claimed that the whistleblower statute had been amended to provide for liability of a supervisor. I asked Mr. DeClue for any authority to support his position that the statute had been amended to provide for individual liability, but he did not provide it to me.” (Cohon Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 3.) The email from Mr. DeClue provides that “I reviewed your position regarding individual liability for Labor Code 1102.5 claims. I appreciate your diligence. However, I sense that we will agree to disagree. Individual liability has been a grey area for the Court for years. However, in 2023, the legislature made it very clear by amending the statute to clarify that individuals can be named in Labor Code 1102.5 claims.” (Cohon Decl., Ex. 3, p. 48.) In response, on March 25, 2024, Cohon stated that “we respectfully disagree with your contention that in 2023 the California Legislature amended Labor Code section 1102.5 to provide for individual liability. For your review, we attach the 2023 amendment to Labor Code section 1102.5. The handful of amendments to section 1102.5 address other issues entirely. Interestingly, this past week, another federal district court adopted the reasoning in Sean Dawson (and other cases) and dismissed the plaintiff’s individual whistleblower claim against his supervisor. Jack Arne Friedman V. Brooke Jenkins 2024 WL 1182878, at *6 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 19, 2024). We know that you said in your last email that the parties will have to agree to disagree, but we are writing to revisit this issue one last time before we file a motion to dismiss this claim.” (Cohon Decl., Ex. 3, p. 46.)

Cohon attests that Mr. DeClue did not respond to his March 25, 2024 communications, so he “had no choice but to file the Motion to Dismiss seeking dismissal of the Labor Code section 1102.5 claim alleged against Ms. Almadova, which I did on April 12, 2024.” (Cohon Decl., ¶ 8, Ex. 4.) Cohon explains that, in response to the motion, Attorney Defendants filed a non-opposition, and “[o]n April 26, 2024, in the interest of cooperation and so that the Court would not have to waste time working up the unopposed motion, and because this dismissal was early in the litigation, Ms. Almadova thereafter stipulated pursuant to Fed. R. Civ. P. 41(a)(2) to allow Mr. Hougland to dismiss his whistleblower claim against her. Importantly, up to that point, the Attorney Defendants failed to provide me with any authority to support a possible opposition to Ms. Almadova’s Motion to Dismiss, despite refusing to dismiss Plaintiff’s whistleblower claim prior to forcing Ms. Almadova to file her dismissal motion.” (Cohon Decl., ¶¶ 9-10, Exs. 5-6.)

However, as Attorney Defendants point out, a negotiated dismissal or compromise “does not constitute a ‘favorable termination.’” (Ludwig v. Superior Court, supra, 37 Cal.App.4th at p. 29.) Which, they contend, “is exactly what occurred here. Ms. Almadova agreed to dismissal of the Whistleblower claim in exchange for a waiver of fees and costs in April 2024. A fee-and-cost waiver constitutes consideration sufficient to show compromise, even where no money is paid, and it defeats favorable termination as a matter of law.” (Reply, 5:1-4.)

Plaintiff asserts that “the Attorney Defendants’ decision to not oppose Almadova’s motion to dismiss that claim on the merits is a concession that their claim ‘would not succeed’ and lacked legal probable cause. The proposal to file a stipulation of dismissal, solely to avoid an unnecessary hearing on an unopposed motion, arose after the non-opposition.” (Opp., 12:26-13:2.) Plaintiff contends that “[b]y electing not to oppose Almadova’s motion, ‘the inference is unavoidable that the decision to abandon [that claim] was made independently’ of whether Almadova would later stipulate to a waiver of fees and costs to formalize the dismissal more expeditiously.” (Opp., 12:16-19, citing Roche v. Hyde, supra, 51 Cal.App.5th at p. 792.)

Plaintiff contends that Attorney Defendants’ decision to file a non-opposition to the motion to dismiss, before the stipulation was discussed or executed, demonstrates Attorney Defendants’ concession and creates an inference supporting favorable termination; however, this is insufficient because favorable termination is to be “strictly enforced.” (Ludwig v. Superior Court, supra, 37 Cal.App.4th at p. 29.) “There is no legal way for a defendant who elects to settle a suit rather than going to trial to demonstrate that the dismissal resulting from the settlement constituted a favorable termination on the merits.” (Ibid.) As noted above, “a dismissal resulting from negotiation, settlement or agreement is generally not deemed a favorable termination of the proceedings.” (Villa v. Cole, supra, 4 Cal.App.4th at p. 1335.)

Regardless of the parties’ underlying motivations for stipulating to dismissal of the whistleblower cause of action, case law demonstrates that an inference is insufficient when there is a memorialized stipulation or settlement between the parties. Accordingly, Plaintiff does not demonstrate a probability of success as to the whistleblower retaliation cause of action for the first element of malicious prosecution. However, Plaintiff demonstrates a probability of success for the age harassment cause of action. Because a malicious prosecution cause of action will still stand if it is based on a single meritless cause of action from the underlying proceeding, the Court looks at whether Attorney Defendants had probable cause and acted with malice. (See Franklin Mint Co. v. Manatt, Phelps & Phillips, LLP (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 313, 333 [“A claim for malicious prosecution need not be addressed to an entire lawsuit; it may, as in this case, be based upon only some of the causes of action alleged in the underlying lawsuit.”].)

b.      Probable Cause

“[A]n attorney may be held liable for malicious prosecution for continuing to prosecute a lawsuit discovered to lack probable cause.” (Zamos v. Stroud (2004) 32 Cal.4th 958, 970.) “‘An action is deemed to have been pursued without probable cause if it was not legally tenable when viewed in an objective manner as of the time the action was initiated or while it was being prosecuted.’ [Citation.] The test is whether, on the basis of facts then known, any reasonable attorney would have believed that instituting or maintaining the prior action was tenable.” (Citizens of Humanity, LLC v. Hass (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 589, 599 (Citizens).) “Probable cause, moreover, must exist for every cause of action advanced in the underlying action. ‘[A]n action for malicious prosecution lies when but one of alternate theories of recovery is maliciously asserted....’” (Soukup v. Law Offices of Herbert Hafif (2006) 39 Cal.4th 260, 292.) Granting an anti-SLAPP is not proper “when there is a dispute as to the state of the defendant’s knowledge and the existence of probable cause turns on resolution of that dispute, [because] there becomes a fact question that must be resolved before the court can determine the legal question of probable cause.” (Citizens, supra, 46 Cal.App.5th at pp. 599-600.)

1.      Age Harassment

For a harassment cause of action, a plaintiff must “show that (1) he was a member of a protected class; (2) he was subjected to unwelcome [] harassment; (3) the harassment was based on [membership in the protected class]; (4) the harassment unreasonably interfered with his work performance by creating an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5) the [employer] is liable for the harassment.” (¿Thompson v. City of Monrovia (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 860, 876¿; see also ¿Dickson v. Burke Williams, Inc. (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 1307, 1313¿ [“¿One of the elements of a harassment claim … is that the harassment be sufficiently severe or pervasive so as to alter the conditions of employment and create an abusive working environment.¿”].) The elements of severe or pervasive harassment unreasonably interfering with work performance are assessed from the perspective of a reasonable person of the protected class of the plaintiff. (Id. at p. 877.)

Under Government Code section 12932 subdivision (a), “harassment creates a hostile, offensive, oppressive, or intimidating work environment and deprives victims of their statutory right to work in a place free of discrimination when the harassing conduct sufficiently offends, humiliates, distresses, or intrudes upon its victim, so as to disrupt the victim’s emotional tranquility in the workplace, affect the victim’s ability to perform the job as usual, or otherwise interfere with and undermine the victim’s personal sense of well-being.” “A single incident of harassing conduct is sufficient to create a triable issue regarding the existence of a hostile work environment if the harassing conduct has unreasonably interfered with the plaintiff’s work performance or created an intimidating, hostile, or offensive working environment.” (Gov. Code, § 12923, subd. (b).) “The existence of a hostile work environment depends upon the totality of the circumstances and a discriminatory remark, even if not made directly in the context of an employment decision or uttered by a nondecisionmaker, may be relevant, circumstantial evidence of discrimination.” (Gov. Code, § 12923, subd. (c).)

Here, Attorney Defendants assert that they “had probable cause to file the underlying age harassment cause of action based on its initial understanding of the facts and the representations made by Mr. Hougland at the outset of the Underlying Lawsuit. (DeClue Decl., ¶¶ 50–53, 56–58.) Mr. DeClue was provided with pertinent information regarding Ms. Almadova’s pattern of hostility and animosity toward Mr. Hougland, and he formed a good-faith belief—under the objective probable cause standard—that Ms. Almadova was the person responsible for the age-related comments made to Mr. Hougland during his termination meeting. (Id. ¶ 56.) In other words, based on the evidence available at the time, WRLG reasonably believed Ms. Almadova was culpable for the age harassment alleged.” (Mot., 16:20-27.)

            DeClue’s declaration specifically states that he “considered the following in naming Almadova for age harassment: Mr. Hougland was 67 years old at the time of his termination; Ms. Almadova treated Ms. Hougland with hostility from the time she began managing him; CCMSI had no problems with Mr. Hougland until Ms. Almadova supervised him; Ms. Almadova took actions that led to Mr. Hougland’s termination; in Mr. Hougland’s termination meeting, he was told that he is not getting any younger and that he should retire.” (DeClue Decl., ¶ 51.) Plaintiff’s opposition points out that “[n]oticeably absent are any facts to satisfy the actual elements of an age harassment claim, namely, that Hougland was subjected to ‘verbal or physical conduct of an age-related nature, that the conduct was unwelcome, and that the conduct was sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of [his] employment and create an abusive work environment.’” (Opp., 14:23-26 [emphasis omitted].) “Being unfriendly, causing someone’s termination, and suggesting retirement during the meeting when they are being terminated is not age harassment – as the District Court found when it granted Almadova’s Motion for Summary Judgment.” (Opp., 14:27-15:1; see also Cohon Decl., ¶ 41, Ex. 14, p. 218 [“The record is devoid of facts that support a harassment claim against Almadova. It is undisputed that Hougland could not recall any discriminatory statements or conduct by Almadova regarding his age, nor any person’s age. Hougland agrees that his age harassment claim against Almadova cannot be substantiated, due to a lack of evidence.”].)

            Plaintiff asserts that “the factors concocted by the Attorney Defendants are directly contradicted by the Hougland Complaint. The Attorney Defendants alleged in the Hougland Complaint that Almadova’s hostility began after Hougland (supposedly) complained about one of her directives in May 2023, (Cohon Decl., Ex. 1 at ¶ 24) not immediately after she was appointed his supervisor in November 2022 (DeClue Decl., ¶ 51).” (Opp., 15:5-9.) As noted by Plaintiff, the Hougland complaint states that Hougland “complained to Defendants, including Defendant Almodova” and “[s]hortly after reporting CCMSI’s violations, and thereafter complaining about and/or opposing Defendant’s illegal business practices, … Plaintiff’s superiors, including Defendant Almodova, began to treat him with hostility.” (Cohon Decl., Ex. 1, ¶¶ 23-24.) Plaintiff avers that “[t]he Attorney Defendants’ attempt to now modify the allegations of the Hougland Complaint to imply ageism rather than retaliation (when there was neither) is itself evidence that the facts as alleged did not support probable cause.” (Opp., 15:9-11.) When an attorney “actively advocated the changed story and abandoned the facts as pleaded in the complaint[,] [i]t is probable a jury could find [the attorney] knowingly prosecuted a false claim.” (Golden State Seafood, Inc. v. Schloss (2020) 53 Cal.App.5th 21, 37.)

Plaintiff points to Attorney Defendants’ lack of evidence about ongoing and pervasive age harassment before prosecuting the Hougland lawsuit to support the lack of probable cause. Plaintiff also presents evidence in Hougland’s complaint that the harassment complained of by Hougland arose after he complained to management, but DeClue’s declaration in support states that “Ms. Almadova treated Ms. Hougland with hostility from the time she began managing him.” (DeClue Decl., ¶ 51.) This directly conflicts with the Hougland complaint. Moreover, as stated above, in the District Court’s order for the motion for summary judgment, the Court stated that the “record is devoid of facts that support a harassment claim against Almadova.” (Cohon Decl., ¶ 41, Ex. 14, p. 218.) This is probative evidence that Attorney Defendants pursued a meritless cause of action.

Accordingly, Plaintiff presents competent evidence that Attorney Defendants pursued the age harassment cause of action despite lacking probable cause.

c.       Malice

“The malice element of the malicious prosecution tort goes to the defendant’s subjective intent in initiating the prior action. For purposes of a malicious prosecution claim, malice is not limited to actual hostility or ill will toward the plaintiff. Rather, malice is present when proceedings are instituted primarily for an improper purpose. Suits with the hallmark of an improper purpose include, but are not necessarily limited to, those in which: ... (1) the person initiating them does not believe that his claim may be held valid; (2) the proceedings are begun primarily because of hostility or ill will; (3) the proceedings are initiated solely for the purpose of depriving the person against whom they are initiated of a beneficial use of his property; (4) the proceedings are initiated for the purpose of forcing a settlement which has no relation to the merits of the claim. (Sycamore Ridge Apartments, supra, 157 Cal.App.4th at p. 1407 [internal quotations and citations omitted].) “[E]vidence that an attorney failed to conduct an adequate investigation before filing a lawsuit supports a finding of malice.” (Citizens, supra, 46 Cal.App.5th at p. 607 [internal citation omitted].)

Plaintiff discusses that “the Attorney Defendants argue that they reasonably (but mistakenly) believed they had probable cause for the age harassment claim until they ‘learned’ – during their own client’s deposition – that Almadova was not the ‘female in management’ who mentioned retirement during Hougland’s termination (Mtn. at 20.) However, they clearly knew this months before Hougland’s March 2025 deposition – evidenced by the facts that in August 2024 they served written discovery but did not ask about age harassment, and they deposed Almadova in January 2025 and did not ask her a single question relating to age harassment.” (Opp., 15:20-26; see Cohon Decl., ¶ 13 [“The first deposition taken in the case was of Ms. Almadova, taken on January 29, 2025. Even though Mr. Hougland’s third cause of action for age harassment alleged that Ms. Almadova displayed ‘a pattern and practice of harassing [Mr. Hougland],’ and ‘engag[ed] in offensive conduct’ and/or ‘act[ed] in a hostile and abusive manner,’ Mr. Hougland’s counsel never asked Ms. Almadova if she made any specific age-based statements to Mr. Hougland or whether she engaged in any conduct that might be perceived as age-related.”].)

In DeClue’s declaration, he attests that “[b]efore the employment lawsuit was filed, I conducted a reasonable inquiry into the facts of this matter, which included interviewing Mr. Hougland, meeting and conferring with Mr. Rogal, and reviewing the limited documents available to me. Many of the documents were not available to me because CCMSI failed to produce Mr. Hougland’s personnel file. Based on my understanding of the facts, I believed that Ms. Almadova was the person responsible for causing Mr. Hougland’s termination. Also, based on the facts presented to me, including the representations made by Mr. Hougland and Mr. Rogal, I had a reasonable belief that Mr. Hougland had viable grounds to bring an age harassment claim against Ms. Almadova.” (DeClue Decl., ¶ 53.) DeClue further declares that “Mr. Hougland relayed to me that during his termination meeting, a female in management was on the call and made age-related comments. At the time, I believed that Mr. Hougland was referring to Ms. Almadova since she was the person who harbored animosity towards Mr. Hougland and caused his termination. I later learned that it was Ms. Rhonda Stuebe that was on the phone at the meeting and made comments about Mr. Hougland retiring, my belief was made in good faith based on this context.” (DeClue Decl., ¶ 56.) Attorney Defendants’ motion asserts that “Mr. DeClue only learned of the identity issue through deposition testimony more than a year after filing and promptly sought to correct it. (DeClue Decl. ¶¶ 53, 54, 56.) A reasonable error corrected once discovered is not evidence of malice.” (Mot., 20:3-5.)

However, Plaintiff’s opposition asserts that “[t]he Attorney Defendants subjected Almadova to a year of frivolous litigation based on an assumption. When Hougland supposedly told the Attorney Defendants that a ‘female in management’ suggested retirement, they had a duty to ask the obvious follow-up question: ‘Do you know who she was?’ Instead, with shocking indifference (i.e., malice), they simply assumed he meant Almadova despite the clear indicator that he did not, i.e., he did not use her name. Maintaining the charade of the Attorney Defendants’ mistake-based defense further requires that they never asked Hougland who participated in his termination meeting, a rather basic investigatory fact to have been overlooked in an employment case hinging on termination.” (Opp., 18:3-10.)

As noted above, Plaintiff contends that the Attorney Defendants knew Plaintiff was not the female in management that suggested retirement by January 29, 2025, the date of Almadova’s deposition, because they did not ask Plaintiff “a single question about alleged age-based statements,” and “[d]espite this, they still refused to dismiss her when faced with evidence in support of her summary judgment motion in March 2025, and ultimately waited until April 4, 2025 to propose dismissal without prejudice and with a waiver of fees and costs. (Id. at ¶¶ 17-35, Ex, 11.) This was not a serious offer.” (Opp., 18:11-16.)

A finding of malice is supported when there is “evidence that an attorney failed to conduct an adequate investigation before filing a lawsuit.” (Citizens, supra, 46 Cal.App.5th at p. 607 [internal citation omitted].) In Citizens, the Court of Appeal found “sufficient evidence of malice,” in part, because the attorneys “conducted little investigation into [plaintiff’s] claims, even when significant questions were raised regarding the validity of her claims.” (Id. at pp. 607-608.) The court noted that “there [wa]s enough evidence here that satisfies [the plaintiff’s] burden with respect to this element,” and that even when “circumstances do not conclusively establish malice, they [may be] sufficient to allow the issue to go to the trier of fact for resolution.” (Id. at p. 608.) From the evidence presented by Plaintiff and DeClue’s own declaration, Attorney Defendants did not conduct an investigation into the comment allegedly made by Plaintiff before initiating the lawsuit. Accordingly, Plaintiff presents competent evidence that Attorney Defendants acted maliciously.

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff made a prima facie factual showing she meets the elements of a malicious prosecution cause of action and that there is a probability she will succeed. (See Sweetwater, supra, 6 Cal.5th at p. 940.)

C.     Prong Two: Probability of Success on the Merits - Hougland

Hougland contends that Plaintiff cannot demonstrate a probability of success on the merits.

a.      Favorable Termination

Hougland’s motion states that “[a]s an initial matter, Mr. Houghland [sic] does not dispute that he initiated the employment lawsuit and that it was terminated in Plaintiff’s favor. However, Mr. Hougland submits that Plaintiff cannot meet show [sic] that the employment lawsuit was brought without probable cause and was brought with malice.” (Mot., 9:25-10:2.) However, Hougland’s reply asserts that “[f]irst and foremost, Plaintiff’s contention that the whistleblower claim was terminated in her favor is incorrect. There is no judgment, nor is there an order indicating as such. The Complaint concedes that the Whistleblower claim was dismissed pursuant to Stipulation. Complaint at ¿ 31. In order to maintain an action for malicious prosecution, the plaintiff must first demonstrate that there was a favorable termination of the underlying litigation.” (Reply, 3:20-25.)

The Court notes that “[p]oints raised for the first time in a reply brief will ordinarily not be considered, because such consideration would deprive the respondent of an opportunity to counter the argument.” (American Drug Stores, Inc. v. Stroh (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1446, 1453.) Hougland waived the ability to challenge the favorable termination element for both causes of action when he conceded in the moving papers that his employment lawsuit “was terminated in Plaintiff’s favor.” (Mot., 9:26.) Accordingly, the Court does not consider Hougland’s argument in his reply, and Hougland’s concession in the moving papers demonstrates that the element of favorable termination is satisfied.

b.      Probable Cause

As noted above, “[p]robable cause, moreover, must exist for every cause of action advanced in the underlying action. ‘[A]n action for malicious prosecution lies when but one of alternate theories of recovery is maliciously asserted....’” (Soukup v. Law Offices of Herbert Hafif, supra, 39 Cal.4th at p. 292.) Granting an anti-SLAPP is not proper “when there is a dispute as to the state of the defendant’s knowledge and the existence of probable cause turns on resolution of that dispute, [because] there becomes a fact question that must be resolved before the court can determine the legal question of probable cause.” (Citizens, supra, 46 Cal.App.5th at pp. 599-600.)

1.      Whistleblower Retaliation

Under Labor Code section 1102.5, employers may not obstruct an employee from disclosing information about “a violation of state or federal statute, or a violation of or noncompliance with a local, state, or federal rule or regulation.” (Lab. Code, § 1102.5, subd. (a).) Also, employers may not retaliate against employees who disclose such information or who “refus[e] to participate in an activity that would result in a violation of federal or state law, rule, or regulation.” (Lab. Code, § 1102.5, at subds. (b)-(c).)

To state a prima facie case for whistleblower retaliation, “a plaintiff must show that she engaged in protected activity, that she was thereafter subjected to adverse employment action by her employer, and there was a causal link between the two.” (Morgan v. Regents of Univ. of California (2000) 88 Cal.App.4th 52, 69.) “The retaliatory motive is proved by showing that plaintiff engaged in protected activities, that his employer was aware of the protected activities, and that the adverse action followed within a relatively short time thereafter. [Citation.] The causal link may be established by an inference derived from circumstantial evidence, such as the employer’s knowledge that the [employee] engaged in protected activities and the proximity in time between the protected action and allegedly retaliatory employment decision.” (Id. at pp. 69-70 [internal quotations omitted].)

Plaintiff contends that “Hougland’s whistleblower retaliation claim against Almadova lacked both legal and factual probable cause from inception. Just two weeks before the filing of the Hougland Complaint, yet another federal district court held that Labor Code section 1102.5 does not permit an alleged whistleblower to recover money damages from an individual who retaliated against him on behalf of an employer… In light of this authority, no reasonable attorney could have believed Hougland had a viable whistleblower retaliation claim against Almadova. Nevertheless, even when provided with this authority, Hougland and the Attorney Defendants forced Almadova to file a motion to dismiss – only to not oppose it. (Annaguey Decl., Ex. 4 and 5.) Although this should end the probable cause inquiry, it is worth noting that, even if individual liability were available under section 1102.5, there was no evidence establishing probable cause for such a claim against Almadova.” (Opp., 15:16-25.)

            Hougland’s complaint in the underlying action alleges that in “April 2023, Plaintiff became aware of several instances of late payments improperly made by Defendant CCMSI for indemnity benefits… Plaintiff immediately informed Defendant Susan Almodova… However, instead of immediately complying with applicable laws and paying the legally-mandated penalties to aggrieved employees, Defendant Almodova instructed Plaintiff to remain silent about the late payments and related penalties owed.” (Annaguey Decl., ¶ 2; Ex. 1, ¶¶ 16-17.) Plaintiff’s declaration in support explains that she “never instructed Mr. Hougland to ‘remain silent’ about or to ‘delete claim notes’ relating to penalties for late payments to claimants. Rather, on May 2, 2023, I instructed him not to put notes in a claim file about a penalty unless and until such penalty was determined by CCMSI management to actually be owed and then paid. Adding a note to a claim file prior to a penalty being approved and paid would have been incorrect. Mr. Hougland not only never told me that he disagreed with this approach; on the contrary, he actually agreed that this sequence was appropriate, stating: ‘I will remove the notes regarding the penalty. I am not sure why the supervisors enter a note prior to the penalty being approved. We can change the process and have the supervisor enter the note after Rod [Golden, CCMSI’s Chief Operating Officer,] approves the penalty.’” (Almadova Decl., ¶ 11, Ex. 15.)

            Plaintiff contends that “[c]ontemporaneous evidence establishes that, rather than objecting, Hougland supported the directive from Almadova concerning the timing of claim file notes about penalties.” (Opp., 15:25-27; see also Almadova Decl., Ex. 15.) Plaintiff also asserts that “Hougland’s only communication with CCMSI management in temporal proximity to his termination was his routine email to Golden on May 8, 2023, requesting approval of 54 penalties incurred under his watch, which did not mention that directive or any other purportedly illegal conduct. Hougland testified that he never reported to anyone that he believed Almadova’s directive about claim file notes was illegal.” (Opp., 15:27-16:3.) Moreover, the Plaintiff’s deposition transcript for Hougland appears to confirm that no one told Hougland not to report illegal or unlawful activity. (Annaguey Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 2, 233:23-235:7.)

            Plaintiff establishes a probability of success for lack of probable cause regarding the whistleblower cause of action because, not only is a whistleblower retaliation cause of action not properly brought against an individual, but Plaintiff presents evidence that the allegations of Hougland’s complaint were not supported.

2.      Age Harassment

As discussed above, for a harassment cause of action, a plaintiff must “show that (1) he was a member of a protected class; (2) he was subjected to unwelcome [] harassment; (3) the harassment was based on [membership in the protected class]; (4) the harassment unreasonably interfered with his work performance by creating an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5) the [employer] is liable for the harassment.” (¿Thompson v. City of Monrovia (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 860, 876¿; see also ¿Dickson v. Burke Williams, Inc. (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 1307, 1313¿ [“¿One of the elements of a harassment claim … is that the harassment be sufficiently severe or pervasive so as to alter the conditions of employment and create an abusive working environment.¿”].) The elements of severe or pervasive harassment unreasonably interfering with work performance are assessed from the perspective of a reasonable person of the protected class of the plaintiff. (Id. at p. 877.)

Under Government Code section 12932 subdivision (a), “harassment creates a hostile, offensive, oppressive, or intimidating work environment and deprives victims of their statutory right to work in a place free of discrimination when the harassing conduct sufficiently offends, humiliates, distresses, or intrudes upon its victim, so as to disrupt the victim’s emotional tranquility in the workplace, affect the victim’s ability to perform the job as usual, or otherwise interfere with and undermine the victim’s personal sense of well-being.” “A single incident of harassing conduct is sufficient to create a triable issue regarding the existence of a hostile work environment if the harassing conduct has unreasonably interfered with the plaintiff’s work performance or created an intimidating, hostile, or offensive working environment.” (Gov. Code, § 12923, subd. (b).) “The existence of a hostile work environment depends upon the totality of the circumstances and a discriminatory remark, even if not made directly in the context of an employment decision or uttered by a nondecisionmaker, may be relevant, circumstantial evidence of discrimination.” (Gov. Code, § 12923, subd. (c).)

Here, Hougland asserts that he consulted three attorneys, who “verified that he had a viable claim against Ms. Almadova for age harassment.” (Mot., 12:3-4.) Hougland further asserts that “[t]hroughout the period when the employment suit was pending, Mr. Hougland relied on the advice of all three attorneys in good faith,” and “before the employment suit was filed, Mr. Hougland did not make any decisions on whom to name as defendants.” (Mot., 12:8-12 [emphasis omitted].) Hougland contends that “[a]s is evident from Mr. DeClue’s declaration, Mr. Hougland provided not only context but factual information in granular detail.” (Mot., 11:21-22.) “Hougland submits that after his truthful disclosures to the three attorneys, his good faith reliance on the advice of not just one but three attorneys negates the probable cause, an essential element on the tort of malicious prosecution.” (Mot., 13:13-15.) Hougland’s declaration in support attests that “[s]hortly after my termination, I truthfully informed Mr. Wolverton about the circumstances around my termination (discussed in more detail below), and he informed me that I had viable retaliation and age-related claims against CCMSI and my immediate supervisor Ms. Almadova, and that I should hire an employment law attorney.” (Hougland Decl., ¶ 16.)

            Plaintiff asserts that “[w]hile Hougland claims to have disclosed all factual information to his attorneys in ‘granular detail,’ the Attorney Defendants aver that they did not know until March 2025 (over a year into the litigation) that Almadova ‘was not the person in the termination meeting who made the comments about Mr. Hougland not getting any younger and that he should retire.’ (DeClue Decl., ¶ 82.) Upon ‘learning’ this information, the Attorney Defendants agreed there was no basis for an age harassment claim against Almadova, the implication being that they would not have asserted that claim in the first place if they had known all the facts – but even if Almadova had been the speaker, probable cause was still lacking.” (Opp., 16:18-19:1.)

Plaintiff cites Albertson v. Raboff (1960) 185 Cal.App.2d 372, 385-386, where the court stated that “[r]eliance on advice of counsel in good faith is a defense to an action for malicious prosecution, inasmuch as such defense shows probable cause. [Citations.] But in order to avail himself of such defense he must have disclosed to his attorney all of the pertinent facts within his knowledge.” “In the absence of a proper disclosure to his attorney, advice of counsel could furnish no protection to the appellant.” (Id. at p. 387.)

            Plaintiff further contends that, even if a full disclosure of facts occurs, “reliance on advice of counsel is not a defense if the defendant knows, as the insurer did in this case, that it does not have probable cause to file suit. [Citation.] Good faith reliance is an obvious prerequisite.” (George F. Hillenbrand, Inc. v. Insurance Co. of North America (2002) 104 Cal.App.4th 784, 814.) Plaintiff asserts that “[g]iven that Hougland knew Almadova had never made any age-related comments to him or engaged in any age-related discriminatory conduct directed at him, and given that he knew he supported and never complained about her directive concerning the timing of claim file notes, ‘a full, fair and complete disclosure of such facts to his attorney obviously could not have resulted’… In other words, even if the Attorney Defendants told Hougland he had viable claims against Almadova, Hougland knew he did not.” (Opp., 17:14-21.)

            Plaintiff finally asserts that “it is no defense that Hougland did not (he claims) review the complaint before it was filed and did not select the causes of action,” because “Hougland testified that he reviewed the complaint in preparation for his deposition and that there were no allegations he believed were inaccurate or that he would want to change.” (Opp., 17:22-18:3; see Annaguey Decl., Ex. 2, 16:22-25 [Q: “In reviewing the Complaint, did you come across any allegations that you thought might be inaccurate or you would otherwise would want to change?” Hougland’s A: “No.”].)

            Plaintiff presents evidence that Hougland did not provide his attorneys with sufficient evidence that Plaintiff harassed Hougland based on age. Hougland’s moving papers and declaration in support similarly fail to assert that Plaintiff made age-related discriminatory comments to Hougland. Rather, Hougland relies on the advice of his attorneys as a scapegoat, while neglecting to attest to what he told his attorneys to make them believe he had a viable cause of action for age harassment. Moreover, Plaintiff presents evidence that Hougland did not provide his counsel with sufficient details for the age harassment cause of action. This is evidenced by Hougland’s counsel learning, over a year into litigation, that Plaintiff is not the individual who made the purported age-harassment comments to Hougland. Plaintiff also presents information that Hougland reviewed the complaint and did not want to change any of the allegations.

Accordingly, Plaintiff presents competent evidence that Hougland pursued the age harassment cause of action despite lacking probable cause.

c.       Malice

As defined above, “[t]he malice element of the malicious prosecution tort goes to the defendant’s subjective intent in initiating the prior action. For purposes of a malicious prosecution claim, malice is not limited to actual hostility or ill will toward the plaintiff. Rather, malice is present when proceedings are instituted primarily for an improper purpose. Suits with the hallmark of an improper purpose include, but are not necessarily limited to, those in which: ... (1) the person initiating them does not believe that his claim may be held valid; (2) the proceedings are begun primarily because of hostility or ill will; (3) the proceedings are initiated solely for the purpose of depriving the person against whom they are initiated of a beneficial use of his property; (4) the proceedings are initiated for the purpose of forcing a settlement which has no relation to the merits of the claim. (Sycamore Ridge Apartments, supra, 157 Cal.App.4th at p. 1407 [internal quotations and citations omitted].) “[A] plaintiff acts with malice when he asserts a claim with knowledge of its falsity, because one who seeks to establish such a claim ‘can only be motivated by an improper purpose.’ [Citation.] A lack of probable cause will therefore support an inference of malice.” (Drummond v. Desmarais (2009) 176 Cal.App.4th 439, 452.)

Plaintiff contends that “[h]ere, not only did Hougland lack evidentiary support for his claims, he actively invented allegations – for example, that Almadova ‘had a pattern and practice of harassing [him] and other employees . . . based on their age’ (Annaguey Decl., Ex. 1 at ¶ 49) – that he knew to be false.” (Opp., 18:21-24.) Plaintiff asserts that “[b]y Hougland’s own allegations, he blames Almadova for his termination. (Annaguey Decl., Ex. 1 at ¶¶ 24-27.) Even before his termination, Hougland did not conceal his resentment of Almadova’s oversight and efforts to modify his office procedures, raising his voice and defying her instructions on multiple occasions. (Almadova Decl., ¶¶ 5-8, 10.) Malice is further evidenced by the gamesmanship of Hougland and the Attorney Defendants throughout the underlying litigation, which included service of the complaint in a public manner; forcing Almadova to file a motion to dismiss that was not opposed; seeking the dismissal of Almadova’s counsel (which was denied); conditioning a stipulation to dismiss the baseless age harassment claim on Almadova’s waiver of costs and attorney’s fees, thereby forcing her to file a summary judgment motion; and then obstructing the timely filing of that motion.” (Opp., 19:9-18.)

Plaintiff’s declaration in support of the instant motion attests to examples of Hougland’s resentment towards her. Specifically, Plaintiff attests that Hougland “dismissed and ignored my instructions and treated me with disdain and hostility. Indeed, the very first time I met him in person, I proposed a change in the way the mail was handled. This proposal was intended to eliminate delays and improve efficiency. Mr. Hougland did not appreciate this and yelled at me (loud enough to be heard by another supervisor two offices away) for daring to make this recommendation.” (Almadova Decl., ¶ 5.) Plaintiff also provides another example, explaining that “in approximately October 2022, before I was promoted to State Director of California, I had been asked to review the caseloads of the adjusters in the Irvine office and make reassignment recommendations, as there were several small accounts that were spread out among multiple adjusters. In furtherance of this request, I spent several hours reviewing each adjuster’s caseload and came up with a plan to reallocate work more efficiently. I emailed this plan to Mr. Hougland and others. Mr. Hougland responded with multiple reasons why he was not going to follow any of the recommendations… During [a] call [to go over the recommendations], Mr. Hougland became extremely defensive, argumentative and combative – to such an extent that the call was ended without any resolution.” (Almadova Decl., ¶ 6.) Further, Plaintiff attests that “[a]lthough Mr. Hougland clearly felt hostility toward me and treated me with extraordinary disrespect, I never reciprocated this treatment to him. I always treated Mr. Hougland with professionalism and respect, and I never made any disparaging personal remarks to him about anything, much less about his age.” (Almadova Decl., ¶ 10.)

Plaintiff’s declaration provides evidence that Hougland felt hostile towards her, and the complaint filed in the underlying action provides further support that Hougland appears to blame Plaintiff for his termination. (See Annaguey Decl., Ex. 1, ¶¶ 24-27.) As discussed in the last section, Plaintiff provides evidence to support that Hougland pursued his employment action against Plaintiff without probable cause. An action for malicious prosecution “does not necessarily infer the presence of anger or vindictiveness. It is sufficient if it appear[s] that the former suit was commenced in bad faith to vex, annoy or wrong the adverse party [citation]. The plaintiff is not required to prove that the [defendant] was inspired by personal hostility, a grudge, or ill will. [Citation.] Like any other fact in issue, malice may be proved by direct evidence or may be inferred from all the circumstances in the case. It may, but need not necessarily, be inferred from want of probable cause.” (Albertson v. Raboff, supra, 185 Cal.App.2d at p. 389.) Accordingly, this creates an inference that Hougland initiated the case with malice.

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff made a prima facie factual showing she meets the elements of a malicious prosecution cause of action and that there is a probability she will succeed. (See Sweetwater, supra, 6 Cal.5th at p. 940.)

Conclusion

Attorney Defendants’ motion to strike the sole cause of action of the complaint is DENIED. Hougland’s motion to strike the sole cause of action of the complaint is DENIED.
