DEPARTMENT M LAW AND MOTION RULINGS

Dept. M issues tentative rulings in many, but not all motion hearings. There is no set time at which tentatives are posted. Please do not call the staff to inquire if a tentative will be posted. 

If parties are satisfied with the ruling, parties may submit on the tentative. However, if an opposing party does not submit, they will be permitted to argue. Please check with the other side before calling the courtroom to submit. The staff does not keep track of which parties submitted and which did not, so please do not ask. 

If a matter is also a scheduling hearing (CMC, TSC, OSC etc) an appearance is still required even if a party submits on the tentative ruling.

            Joyce L. Ezzell v. American Honda Motor Company, Inc., et al.

            24TRCV00218

1.      American Honda Motor Company, Inc.’s Motion to Compel Compliance with Court Order Regarding Deposition of Plaintiff and Request for Sanctions  

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

            American Honda Motor Company, Inc.’s Motion to Compel Compliance with Court Order Regarding Deposition of Plaintiff and Request for Sanctions is denied.

 

            Background

 

Plaintiff filed the Complaint on January 23, 2024. This is an action under the Song-Beverly Act.     

 

Motions to Compel Compliance  

 

The party noticing the deposition may move for an order compelling appearance at the deposition and production of documents, pursuant to the deposition notice, from the party deponent who fails to appear or produce materials requested in the deposition notice, and who has not served a valid objection under § 2025.410(a).  CCP § 2025.450(a). 

 

Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450(h) states:

“If that party or party-affiliated deponent then fails to obey an order compelling attendance, testimony, and production, the court may make those orders that are just, including the imposition of an issue sanction, an evidence sanction, or a terminating sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against that party deponent or against the party with whom the deponent is affiliated. In lieu of, or in addition to, this sanction, the court may impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against that deponent or against the party with whom that party deponent is affiliated, and in favor of any party who, in person or by attorney, attended in the expectation that the deponent's testimony would be taken pursuant to that order.”

 

Defendant moves for an order compelling compliance with the Court order of January 8, 2026 ordering Plaintiff to appear and testify and produce documents at her deposition and for the imposition of sanctions. The motion is made on the grounds that the deponent failed to appear for the re-noticed date of the deposition and the deponent has failed to schedule an alternative date for the deposition.

 

Plaintiff filed an opposition stating that the deposition date has been rescheduled to July 10, 2026. The Court notes that this date was prior to the previous scheduled date of the motion which was July 17, 2026. The motion was continued from that date to September 22, 2026. The Court presumes that the deposition went forward on July 10, 2026. Thus, no additional Court order is required to compel the deposition.

 

As to the request for monetary sanctions, the request is denied. It appears that the deposition has been rescheduled upon mutual agreement of the parties and any additional monetary sanctions are not warranted at this time.

 

Thus, Defendant’s Motion to Compel Compliance with Court Order Regarding Deposition of Plaintiff and Request for Sanctions is denied.

 

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of this ruling.

Shahe S. Kekilian v. Michael C. Pepe, et al.    

25TRCV01288

1.      Southern Glazer’s Wine & Spirits, LLC’s Motion for Discovery of Peace Officer Personnel Records 

 

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

Southern Glazer’s Wine & Spirits, LLC’s Motion for Discovery of Peace Officer Personnel Records is denied without prejudice.

 

Background

 

Plaintiff’s Complaint was filed on April 22, 2025. Plaintiff was involved in a motor vehicle collision with Defendants. At the time, Plaintiff was lawfully operating a marked police motorcycle.

 

Pitchess Motion

 

Pursuant to Pitchess v. Superior Court (1974) 11 Cal.3d 531, a criminal defendant “could ‘compel discovery’ of certain relevant information in the personnel files of police officers by making ‘general allegations which establish some cause for discovery’ of that information and by showing how it would support a defense to the charge against him.  In 1978, the California Legislature codified the holding of Pitchess by enacting Penal Code Sections 832.7 and 832.8, as well as Evidence Code Sections 1043 through 1045.  . . . To initiate discovery, the defendant must file a motion supported by affidavits showing ‘good cause for the discovery,’ first by demonstrating the materiality of the information to the pending litigation, and second by ‘stating upon reasonable belief’ that the police agency has the records or information at issue.  This two-part showing of good cause is a ‘relatively low threshold for discovery.’”  Warrick v. Superior Court (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1011, 1018-19 (internal citations omitted).  A Pitchess motion can also be made in a civil action.  Haggerty v. Superior Court (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 1079, 1085.

 

Thus, peace officer records may be discoverable in civil actions under the mechanisms provided for in Evidence Code Sections 1043 through 1046.

 

Evid. Code § 1043 states:

“(a) In any case in which discovery or disclosure is sought of peace or custodial officer personnel records or records maintained pursuant to Section 832.5 of the Penal Code or information from those records, the party seeking the discovery or disclosure shall file a written motion with the appropriate court or administrative body upon written notice to the governmental agency that has custody and control of the records, as follows:

(1) In a civil action, the written notice shall be given at the times prescribed by subdivision (b) of Section 1005 of the Code of Civil Procedure.

(2) In a criminal action, the written notice shall be served and filed at least 10 court days before the hearing. All papers opposing a motion so noticed shall be filed with the court at least five court days, and all reply papers at least two court days, before the hearing. Proof of service of the notice shall be filed no later than five court days before the hearing.

(b) The motion shall include all of the following:

(1) Identification of the proceeding in which discovery or disclosure is sought, the party seeking discovery or disclosure, the peace or custodial officer whose records are sought, the governmental agency that has custody and control of the records, and the time and place at which the motion for discovery or disclosure shall be heard.

(2) A description of the type of records or information sought.

(3) Affidavits showing good cause for the discovery or disclosure sought, setting forth the materiality thereof to the subject matter involved in the pending litigation and stating upon reasonable belief that the governmental agency identified has the records or information from the records.

(c) Upon receipt of a notice served pursuant to subdivision (a), the governmental agency shall immediately notify the individual whose records are sought.

(d) No hearing upon a motion for discovery or disclosure shall be held without full compliance with the notice provisions of this section except upon a showing by the moving party of good cause for noncompliance, or upon a waiver of the hearing by the governmental agency identified as having the records.”

 

A declaration by counsel, on information and belief, may be sufficient.   People v. Oppel (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1146, 1153, n.6.  Generally, counsel would have to provide specific facts that the officer committed misconduct. Warrick, 35 Cal.4th at 1026; People v. Hill (2005) 131 Cal.App. 4th 1089, 1098 overruled on other grounds in People v. French (2008) 43 Cal.4th 36, 48, fn. 5.  Here, however, in the context of this civil action, counsel would have to provide, generally, the facts of the instant action and why the materials sought would be material to the underlying litigation.  Haggerty v. Superior Court (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 1079, 1087. Evidence of habit, custom and practice may be admissible to prove that the conduct of an officer on a specified occasion conformed with her habit and custom.  People v. Gill (1997) 60 Cal.App.4th 743, 749.  Further, the scope of disclosure is not necessarily limited to mere witness contact information.  Evid. Code § 1045(a); Haggerty v. Superior Court (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 1079, 1087.

 

If the court orders an in camera inspection, the court inspects the records to determine the relevance of the materials to the instant action. People v. Gill (1997) 60 Cal.App.4th 743, 749.  The scope of the in camera review is governed by Sections 1045(b) and (c).

 

Evid. Code Sections 1045(b) and (c) state:

“(b) In determining relevance, the court shall examine the information in chambers in conformity with Section 915, and shall exclude from disclosure:

(1) Information consisting of complaints concerning conduct occurring more than five years before the event or transaction that is the subject of the litigation in aid of which discovery or disclosure is sought.

(2) In any criminal proceeding the conclusions of any officer investigating a complaint filed pursuant to Section 832.5 of the Penal Code.

(3) Facts sought to be disclosed that are so remote as to make disclosure of little or no practical benefit.

(c) In determining relevance where the issue in litigation concerns the policies or pattern of conduct of the employing agency, the court shall consider whether the information sought may be obtained from other records maintained by the employing agency in the regular course of agency business which would not necessitate the disclosure of individual personnel records.”

 

Evid. Code § 1045(a) provides broad discovery rights of information relating to an incident that directly involves the peace officer and is the subject of the litigation.  Haggerty v. Superior Court (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 1079, 1087.  However, Evidence Code § 1045(b)(1) specifically requires the court to exclude from disclosure information consisting of complaints concerning conduct occurring more than five years before the event.

 

Defendant moves “pursuant to Evidence Code section 1043, for discovery or disclosure of the peace or custodial officer personnel records or records maintained pursuant to Section 832.5 of the Penal Code for plaintiff [a] peace officer [...] of the City of Los Angeles, Department of Airports a.k.a. Los Angeles World Airports (“LAWA”), or information from those records.” (Notice of Motion, p. 2, lines 6-10). The motion is made on the grounds that “[t]he records are material to the subject matter of this action as Plaintiff is seeking recovery of monetary damages for bodily injuries and loss of income/earning capacity.” (Id. at p. 2, lines 10-12).

 

Defendant contends that Plaintiff has served a privilege log in connection with Plaintiff’s deposition subpoena for records. Defendant’s counsel states: ‘Based on the information provided in the Privilege Log, specifically relating to prior workers’ compensation claims which is in direct contradiction to Plaintiff’s Initial and Amended Responses (both of which are verified under penalty of perjury), I am informed and believe that such records are directly relevant to Plaintiff’s claims for damages on the basis of physical and psychological injuries, as well as Plaintiff’s credibility.” (Decl., Franklin L. Stoker, ¶ 8).

 

Plaintiff argues that the motion was improperly served as it was not served upon the governmental agency which has access to the records. Plaintiff also argues that the motion fails to adequately describe with specificity the types of records sought and fails to identify the government agency that has custody and control of the records. Evidence Code § 1043(b)(1) and (2). Further, Plaintiff argues that the good cause requirement is not satisfied with competent evidence since the only evidence provided, the declaration of Frederick L. Stoker, III, merely provides a recitation of the facts which led to the filing of this motion.

 

Plaintiff contention that the motion was not properly served in compliance with Evidence Code § 1043(a) upon the government agency that has custody and control of the records is well taken. Upon review of the proof of service of the motion, indeed, the motion was not served upon the governmental agency that has custody and control of the records. Instead, the motion was served only upon the attorneys representing the Plaintiff and Plaintiff in Intervention City of Los Angeles.

 

Defendant argues that it has established good cause for this non-compliance pursuant to Evidence Code § 1043(d). However, the burden to establish good cause rests of Defendant. Simply stating that LAWA received notice because if LAWA had been served it would have simply forwarded the motion to the same attorney who filed the opposition is insufficient. Otherwise, the statute would have set forth a mechanism for service in this manner which would have been typical for how many other motions are typically served. Instead, this particular statute specifically set forth a distinct notice requirement. Stating that opposing party would have been able to oppose the motion regardless of the notice defect does not constitute good cause.

 

In addition, Defendant did not provide competent evidence, even in the form of a declaration from counsel, describing the types of records or information sought in compliance with Section 1043(b)(2). The declaration simply provides a conclusory reference to a privilege log but does not describe in sufficient detail the types of records or information that is sought. (Decl., Frederick L. Stoker, III, ¶ 8). A declaration by counsel, made on information and belief, may be sufficient to establish good cause.  People v. Oppel (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1146, 1153, n.6. However, at a minimum, the types of records or information sought should be described with sufficient particularity.

 

Thus, the Court finds that Defendant has not met the requirements set forth in Evid. Code § 1043(b) to compel disclosure to the Court for an in camera review. Therefore, Defendant Southern Glazer’s Wine & Spirits, LLC’s Motion for Discovery of Peace Officer Personnel Records is denied without prejudice.

 

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of this ruling.

Christopher Ramirez Portillo, et al. v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Companies, et al. 

26TRCP00035

1.      State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Companies’ Motion for Protective Order Limiting the Scope of Deposition and Production of Documents  

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Companies’ Motion for Protective Order Limiting the Scope of Deposition and Production of Documents is granted.

 

Background

 

On January 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed the petition to compel uninsured motorist arbitration.

 

Motion for Protective Order

 

In Miranda v. 21st Century Ins. Co. (2004) 117 Cal. App. 4th 913, the Court of Appeal held that the superior court has exclusive jurisdiction over discovery disputes arising in uninsured motorist arbitrations. 

 

The Court is authorized to limit discovery through a protective order.  The order may be granted on the motion of any party or other person affected by the discovery sought.  CCP §§ 2017.020(a), 2019.020(b), 2019.030(a).  A protective order may be obtained to limit the frequency or extent of use of any discovery method on any of the following grounds:  (1)  “The discovery sought is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative …;  (2)  “(The information) is obtainable from some other source that is more convenient, less burdensome or less expensive;  (3)  “The selected method of discovery is unduly burdensome or expensive, taking into account the needs of the case, the amount in controversy, and the importance of the issues at stake in the litigation.”  CCP § 2019.030(a)(1)-(2).

 

Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.420 states:

“(a) Before, during, or after a deposition, any party, any deponent, or any other affected natural person or organization may promptly move for a protective order. The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.

(b) The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party, deponent, or other natural person or organization from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense. This protective order may include, but is not limited to, one or more of the following directions:

(1) That the deposition not be taken at all.

(2) That the deposition be taken at a different time.

(3) That a video recording of the deposition testimony of a treating or consulting physician or of any expert witness, intended for possible use at trial under subdivision (d) of Section 2025.620, be postponed until the moving party has had an adequate opportunity to prepare, by discovery deposition of the deponent, or other means, for cross-examination.

(4) That the deposition be taken at a place other than that specified in the deposition notice, if it is within a distance permitted by Sections 2025.250 and 2025.260.

(5) That the deposition be taken only on certain specified terms and conditions.

(6) That the deponent's testimony be taken by written, instead of oral, examination.

(7) That the method of discovery be interrogatories to a party instead of an oral deposition.

(8) That the testimony be recorded in a manner different from that specified in the deposition notice.

(9) That certain matters not be inquired into.

(10) That the scope of the examination be limited to certain matters.

(11) That all or certain of the writings or tangible things designated in the deposition notice not be produced, inspected, copied, tested, or sampled, or that conditions be set for the production of electronically stored information designated in the deposition notice.

(12) That designated persons, other than the parties to the action and their officers and counsel, be excluded from attending the deposition.

(13) That a trade secret or other confidential research, development, or commercial information not be disclosed or be disclosed only to specified persons or only in a specified way.

(14) That the parties simultaneously file specified documents enclosed in sealed envelopes to be opened as directed by the court.

(15) That the deposition be sealed and thereafter opened only on order of the court.

(16) That examination of the deponent be terminated. If an order terminates the examination, the deposition shall not thereafter be resumed, except on order of the court.”

 

Meet and Confer

 

Respondent set forth a meet and confer declaration in substantial compliance with CCP § 2025.420(a).  (Declarations, Michelle Z. Maleky; Maria A. Grover, ¶¶ 2-3).

 

Motion for Protective Order

 

Respondent moves “for issuance of a protective order barring or limiting the deposition of State Farm’s “person most knowledgeable” (“PMQ”) and production of documents requested in the notice, because all topics and categories focus on subject matter that is improper and irrelevant in an uninsured motorist arbitration proceeding.” (Notice of Motion, p. 2, lines 5-8). The motion is made on the grounds that “[t]he discovery does not seek information pursuant to Insurance Code section 11580.2, and the applicable State Farm policy, but instead solely and improperly seeks to discover evidence to support their alleged future “bad faith” litigation.” (Id. at p. 2, lines 8-10).

 

Ins. Code, § 11580.2(f) states: “The policy or an endorsement added thereto shall provide that the determination as to whether the insured shall be legally entitled to recover damages, and if so entitled, the amount thereof, shall be made by agreement between the insured and the insurer or, in the event of disagreement, by arbitration.”

 

“Absent language in the insurance agreement expanding the issues to be arbitrated [citation], underinsured motorist arbitrations contemplate only two issues—‘whether the insured shall be legally entitled to recover damages, and if so entitled, the amount thereof’ ([ibid.]). In this context, the term ‘damages’ refers to the amount of damages the insured is entitled to recover from the underinsured motorist; it does not include determination of the extent of coverage and the amount of money the insurance company is obligated to pay the insured.” Glassman v. Safeco Ins. Co. of America (2023) 90 Cal.App.5th 1281, 1311 (internal quotations omitted). “Issues of bad faith breach of the insurance contract are beyond the scope of the UIM arbitration.” Id. at 1311, fn. 15.

 

Respondent states that liability is uncontested. Thus, the only issue for determination is whether petitioners are entitled to damages and the amount of damages. Respondent argues that the deposition notice and the accompanying document requests do not seek information regarding these topics but instead seek information related to respondent’s claims handling practices similar to what would be propound in an insurance bad faith action. In addition, respondent states that the documents to be produced are privileged based on the attorney/client privilege, work product doctrine, and trade secret protection.

 

In the opposition, petitioners stated that they agreed to narrow the categories to 8 categories and 14 requests for production during the meet and confer process. In addition, the Court notes that the opposition provided substantive arguments to only categories 2, 4, 6, and 7. It is unclear whether petitioners have abandoned categories 1, 3, 5, and 8.

 

The burden on a motion for protective order is on the moving party to establish “good cause” for whatever relief is requested: “Generally, a deponent seeking a protective order will be required to show that the burden, expense, or intrusiveness involved in … [the discovery procedure] clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” Emerson Elec. Co. v. Sup.Ct. (Grayson) (1997) 16 C4th 1101, 1110.

 

Category 1 states: “State Farm’s written procedures for complying with California Code of Regulations, Title 10, section 2695.1 et seq. (Fair Claims Settlement Practices Regulations) in uninsured motorist bodily injury claims, including the 40-day deadline to accept or deny a claim (§ 2695.7(b)), the 15 day acknowledgement requirement, and the requirement to provide written status updates every 30 days (§ 2695.7(c)), as applied to the handling of this claim.”

 

Category 2 states: “The specific monetary settlement and reserving authority limits for the adjuster assigned to this claim, the claim team manager/supervisor, and any “Round Table,” Large Loss Committee, Claim Committee, or Home Office committee required to approve evaluations, reserves, offers, or arbitration positions above those limits on this file.”

 

Category 3 states: “State Farm’s policies requiring supervisory, managerial, or committee review and written sign-off on UM bodily injury evaluations, reserve changes, initial offers, final offers, authorization to proceed to arbitration, and authorization of arbitration positions, as applied to this file.”

 

Category 4 states: “The policies and procedures for setting, reviewing, and adjusting loss reserves on UM bodily injury claims, and the complete reserve history on this claim, including all increases, decreases, rationales, and approvals.”

 

Category 5 states: “The terms, scope, and interpretation of the UM coverage on the subject State Farm Policy No. 829 4863-A28-75, including compliance with California Insurance Code section 11580.2, stacking and anti-stacking provisions, offset and credit provisions, the identity of insured persons, and the per-person and per-accident UM limits applicable to this claim.”

 

Category 6 states: “The complete evaluation methodology, range assignment, and basis for each and every offer and pre-arbitration position taken by State Farm on this claim, including injury classifications, severity codes, impairment inputs, jury verdict research, comparable file data, Colossus/ClaimIQ outputs, round table notes, and supervisor/committee approvals, and the factual basis for why State Farm’s evaluation is materially lower than the insureds’ demand.”

 

Category 7 states: “State Farm’s training, guidelines, and policies regarding California medical damages law in UM claims, including the treatment of billed versus paid medical charges under Howell v. Hamilton Meats & Provisions, Inc. (2011) 52 Cal.4th 541, Corenbaum v. Lampkin (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 1308, balance billing, Medi-Cal/Medicare liens, and ERISA/health plan subrogation, as applied to the evaluation of this claim

 

Category 8 states: “State Farm’s policies and procedures governing UM arbitration under California Insurance Code section 11580.2(f), including pre-arbitration discovery, mandatory exchange of witness and document lists, selection of the neutral arbitrator, evaluation of demands and offers within policy limits, and the internal authority and sign-offs required to extend or reject pre-arbitration settlement offers on this file.”

 

As can be demonstrated above, each of the categories relate to respondent’s evaluation of petitioners’ damages and internal methodology as to how respondent evaluated the amount. These categories are beyond the scope of an uninsured or underinsured motorist bodily injury arbitration action. Similarly, as to document requests 1 to 5 and 9 to 14, the requests for documents also seek documents related to respondent’s internal evaluation and investigation of the claim and do not seek information as to petitioners’ damages. The requests for these documents attempt to understand respondent’s potential offers made to petitioners. However, the requests do not contemplate obtaining information as to the element of damages, but, instead, are designed to understand the basis for respondent’s settlement offers. In addition, such requests for documents related to the claims handling practices may also implicate matters of attorney/client privilege, trade secret protection, and the attorney work product doctrine.

 

As to request 6, coverage is not at issue and respondent states that it has already produced a copy of the insurance policy. Requests 7 and 8 appear to seek documents that may be related to damages. However, since the motion for protective order is being granted as to all the categories at issue here, any requests that are tied to the deposition notice must also be encompassed within the protective order.

 

Respondent has established good cause for the issuance of a protective order. Any deposition notice that is re-noticed shall be limited to the scope of the issues involved in a UM arbitration action namely liability and damages. As it appears that the notice that was issued in this case did not limit the scope of inquiry into these areas, respondent is not required to appear and produce documents in conformity with the subject notice. Petitioners may re-notice another deposition and the parties are free to meet and confer to discuss the scope of the deposition and documents to be produced. While respondent argues that a deposition of a claims adjuster is not authorized, no binding California authority has been produced which prevents such a deposition. Instead, respondent only cited to out of state authority.

 

For the foregoing reasons, State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Companies’ Motion for Protective Order Limiting the Scope of Deposition and Production of Documents is granted.

 

Sanctions

 

Petitioners’ request for monetary sanctions is denied.

 

Respondent is ordered to give notice of this ruling.
