MANSUR INVESTMENTS, LLC vs LINDA MARIE NORA

July 16, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: MOTION OF SARAH SHAPERO ESQ, SHAPERO LAW FIRM, TO BE RELIEVED AS COUNSEL-CIVIL.

 

RULING:

The Court grants the Motion.

The Court will complete, sign and file the Form Order received-stamped on June 15, 2026.

Movant Sarah Shapero, Esq., Shapero Law Firm to give notice.

 

ANALYSIS:

By the Motion, filed June 15, 2026, counsel moves to be relieved as attorney of record for Defendant by declaring, in the form Declaration, at paragraph 2: “ There has been a breakdown of the relationship with client and a material term or terms of the underlying agreement between client and Shapero Law Firm, P.C. such that SLF cannot continue its representation.”

Here, the form notice, declaration, proposed order, and proof of service sufficiently comply with the requirements for a motion to be relieved as counsel. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362.)

Further, moving counsel’s declaration shows cognizable grounds for withdrawal:

o          A breakdown in the attorney-client relationship (or personality clash). (Estate of Falco v. Decker (1987) 188 Cal.App.3d 1004, 1014 (Falco).)

o          The client by conduct renders it unreasonably difficult for the member to carry out the employment effectively (e.g., noncooperation or noncommunication). (Rules Prof. Conduct, rule 1.16(b)(4). Cf. Falco, supra, 188 Cal App.3d at p. 1020 [“We find no abuse of discretion in the trial court's implicit finding rejecting the contention that respondents' lack of cooperation justified appellants' withdrawal.”].)

o          The client breaches an agreement as to the member as to expenses or fees. (Rules Prof. Conduct, rule 1.16; Lempert v. Sup. Ct. (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 1161, 1173.)

Finally, no opposing document is filed in order to show any prejudice caused by attorney withdrawal. (See Rules Prof. Conduct, rule 1.16(d); Vann v. Shilleh (1975) 54 Cal.App.3d 192, 197.)

Therefore, the Court grants the Motion and will sign the proposed Order counsel submitted.

 


Case Number: 24STCV16664    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: 311

24STCV16664

BENITO ROJAS, AN INDIVIDUAL vs POLYMATH DEVELOPMENT GROUP INC, et al.

July 16, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: DEFENDANT ANDREW MOLLINEDO’S MOTION TO VACATE ENTRY OF DEFAULT AND DEFAULT JUDGMENT.

 

RULING:

The Court grants the Motion.

The Court quashes service of the Summons and Complaint as to Defendant ANDREW MOLLINEDO.

Plaintiff is granted another 60 days within which to serve the Defendant.

Defendant to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

The Court incorporates by reference the Background section of the Minutes entered on October 23, 2025.

On May 13, 2026, ANDREW MOLLINEDO (Defendant), filed the Motion to Vacate entry of Default and Default Judgment, asserting Plaintiff’s failure to serve the Summons and Complaint and substantive lack of merit of the case as against him.

Plaintiff opposes, counterarguing that the Registered Process Server’s Declaration shows valid substituted service, and Defendant delayed approximately half a year after learning of the judgment before making the Motion.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) provides: “The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect....” A motion pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473 must be made “‘within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken….’” (Zamora v. Clayborn Contracting Group, Inc. (2002) 28 Cal.4th 249, 258 [quoting Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b)].) It may be an abuse of discretion to grant relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473 where there is unexplained delay of over three months before moving for relief. (Huh v. Wang (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1406, 1421, fn.4.)

“Section 473.5, subdivision (c) allows the court to set aside the default judgment if it finds the defendant's lack of actual notice in time to defend was not caused by the defendant's avoidance of service or inexcusable neglect.” (Ellard v. Conway (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 540, 547.) A motion pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473.5 must be made in a reasonable time, not beyond the earlier of two years after default judgment entry, or 180 days after service on the party of written notice of default or judgment entry, must have evidence that lack of notice was not the result of moving party’s inexcusable neglect or avoidance of service, and must include a proposed pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5.)

“When a defendant argues that service of summons did not bring him or her within the trial court's jurisdiction, the plaintiff has ‘the burden of proving the facts that did give the court jurisdiction, that is the facts requisite to an effective service.’” (American Exp. Centurion Bank v. Zara (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 383, 387.) (Underscoring added.) Complainants have the initial burden to evidence valid statutory service of a summons and complaint. (Dill v. Berquist Const. Co., Inc. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1439-1440; Floveyor Internat. v. Superior Court (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 789, 794.)

 

Court findings as to conflicting proof regarding whether a summons and complaint were properly served are upheld if based upon substantial evidence. (Stafford v. Mach (1998) 64 Cal.App.4th 1174, 1182.) A trial court has discretion as to whether to accept a process server declaration, or contradictory evidence, in deciding whether service of a summons and complaint was validly accomplished. (Am. Express Centurion Bank v. Zara (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 383, 390; Khourie, Crew & Jaeger v. Sabek, Inc. (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 1009, 1014 [“Sabek points out that it introduced evidence, in the form of declarations, that tend to contradict the declaration of Murray Woods…. The trial court, however, was entitled to believe Woods [the process server].”].)

A filed proof of service creates a rebuttable presumption that service was proper if it complies with applicable statutory requirements. (Floveyor Internat. v. Superior Court (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 789, 795.) Where service was by a registered process server, Evidence Code section 647 applies to accord a presumption as to the facts stated in the proof of service. (Palm Property Investments, LLC v. Yadegar (2011) 194 Cal.App.4th 1419, 1425-1427.)

As to defendants invalidly served with summonses and complaints, court lack personal jurisdiction, and default judgments are void and subject to being set aside. (Hearn v. Howard (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1200; Lee v. An (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 558, 564.) Default judgments entered against defendants who were not served with a summons and complaint in the manner prescribed by statutes, are void, notwithstanding the defendants’ knowledge of the action. (County of San Diego v. Gorham (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1226.) “‘[C]ompliance with the statutory procedures for service of process is essential to establish personal jurisdiction.’” (Ellard v. Conway (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 540, 544. Accord, Renoir v. Redstar Corp. (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 1145, 1152.) “[A] section 473(d) motion to vacate a judgment that is void for lack of proper service is not subject to the judicially imposed two-year limitation.” (California Capital Ins. Co. v. Hoehn (2024) 17 Cal.5th 207, 225.)

However, a Court would err in ruling on the merits of a complaint in deciding a motion to quash a summons made on the ground of defective service. (Nelson v. Horvath (1970) 4 Cal.App.3d 1, 5.)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

Defendant requests an Order vacate Default entry and Judgment, essentially based on a Declaration about Defendant never having been served with the Summons and Complaint. Also, Defendant also argues extensively that the case lacks merit as against him.

Plaintiff responds that the Proof of Service (Opposition, Exhibit 1) shows valid substituted service, which Defendant contests by his Declaration with no corroborating evidence, and Defendant unreasonably delayed. Plaintiff adds that the merits are irrelevant for this matter.

Defendant’s Reply asserts that Plaintiff:

1.      Fails to justify service at a mail receiving entity, under Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20(c), as to which the Process Server Declaration is silent.

2.      Plaintiff reports service at an address, but it is associated with the entity Defendant, rather than the individual Defendant.

3.      The Motion was filed timely pursuant to Section 473.5(a) that provides for the earlier of two years after entry of the default judgment, or 180 days after service of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.

 

Code of Civil Procedure “Section 415.20, subdivision (b), provides in part: ‘If a copy of the summons and complaint cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to the person to be served ... a summons may be served by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the person's dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge of his or her office, place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, at least 18 years of age, who shall be informed of the contents thereof, and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and of the complaint by first-class mail, postage prepaid to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left.’” (Trackman v. Kenney (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 175, 182-83. Accord American Exp. Centurion Bank v. Zara (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 383, 389.) Service of summons at a defendant’s usual mailing address includes a commercial post office box. (E.g., Hearn v. Howard (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1203.)

“Notwithstanding subdivision (b), if the only address reasonably known for the person to be served is a private mailbox obtained through a commercial mail receiving agency, service of process may be effected on the first delivery attempt by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint with the commercial mail receiving agency in the manner described in subdivision (d) of Section 17538.5 of the Business and Professions Code.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.20(c).) “[S]ection 415.20, subdivision (c) provides that substitute service may be effectuated on an individual by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint with a CMRA (commercial mail receiving agency) ‘if the only address reasonably known for the person ... is a private mailbox obtained through a commercial mail receiving agency.’ ” (Kremerman v. White (2021) 71 Cal.App.5th 358, 373 [“no evidence in the judgment roll that indicates whether the referenced statutory requirements were met.”].)

Here, Plaintiff offers an unregistered Process Server’s Declaration (Opposition, Exhibit 1) that includes nothing about (1) efforts to serve any address reasonably known other than the commercial mail receiving entity or (2) whether that location was Plaintiff’s address. Additionally, Plaintiff supports a finding that the address served is not his individual address, as he received no mail about summons service. (See, e.g., Motion, p.11, ¶ 5; Reply 5:11-27.)

Therefore, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s service of the Summons and Complaint is void, and need not reach issues about Defendant’s alternative grounds for relief.

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

“‘The court may dismiss without prejudice the complaint in whole, or as to that defendant, when dismissal is made pursuant to Section 418.10.’” (PNEC Corp. v. Meyer (2010) 190 Cal.App.4th 66, 71 [quoting Code Civ. Proc., § 581, subd. (h)], disapproved on other grounds by DisputeSuite.com, LLC v. Scoreinc.com (2017) 2 Cal.5th 968, 979. See also California Rules of Court, rules 3.110(b) [“The complaint must be served on all named defendants and proofs of service on those defendants must be filed with the court within 60 days after the filing of the complaint.”]; 3.110(h) [“When a default is entered, the party who requested the entry . . . must obtain a default judgment . . . within 45 days….”]; and 3.110(f) [“If a party fails to serve and file pleadings as required under this rule, and has not obtained an order extending time to serve its pleadings, the court may issue an order to show cause why sanctions shall not be imposed.”].)

The Court grants the Motion, but without prejudice to serve Defendant validly within an extended time of 60 days from entry of this Order.

 


Case Number: 24STCV23670    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: 311

24STCV23670

MAYA KOGAN BY AND THROUGH HER SUCCESSOR-IN-INTEREST, SAMAIL KOGAN, et al. vs GRANADA HILLS CONVALESCENT HOSPITAL, INC.

July 16, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT, OR IN THE ALTERNATIVE, SUMMARY ADJUDICATION OF ISSUES BY DEFENDANT GRANADA HILLS CONVALESCENT HOSPITAL, INC. dba GRANADA HILLS CARE CENTER.

 

RULING:

The Court denies the Motion for Summary Judgment and Alternative Motion for Summary Adjudication of Issues.

The Court overrules Defendant’s Evidentiary Objections filed July 2, 2026.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On September 13, 2024, MAYA KOGAN, SAMAIL KOGAN, IRINA BRANDALIS, and MARINA KOGAN AKOPNIK, (Plaintiffs) filed a Complaint against GRANADA HILLS CONVALESCENT HOSPITAL, INC. (Defendant), listing Causes of Action for:

1.      ELDER ABUSE/NEGLECT  (Pursuant to Welfare and Institutions Code §§15657 & 15610.57);

2.      NEGLIGENCE;

3.      VIOLATION OF RESIDENT’S RIGHTS (Pursuant to Health and Safety Code § 1430); and 

4.      WRONGFUL DEATH. 

Plaintiffs allege that MAYA KOGAN (Deceased) was a dependent elder who suffered preventable injuries, including pulmonary embolism, cerebral edema, anoxic brain encephalopathy, multiple infections and death, and who Defendant convalescent hospital had neglected and denied.

On April 23, 2026, Defendant filed the Motion for Summary Judgment or Summary Adjudication as to each Cause of Action, along with a uniquely heavy emphasis upon evidentiary objections to medical expert testimony.

Plaintiffs oppose, based upon arguments such as that experts Dr. Rearick and Nurse Elbe support triable issues of material fact about Defendant’s staff failing to recognize and respond to the decedent’s markedly reduced mobility after orthopedic surgery and polytrauma, which contributed to her fatal thromboembolic event.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) “[T]he role of the court in summary judgment proceedings is not to weigh the evidence, but to determine whether there exists a triable issue of material fact.” (Habitat Trust for Wildlife, Inc. v. City of Rancho Cucamonga (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1306, 1342.)

“A trial court enjoys broad discretion in ruling on foundational matters on which expert testimony is to be based.” (Maatuk v. Guttman (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 1191, 1197.) “The court must not weigh an opinion's probative value or substitute its own opinion for the expert's opinion. Rather, the court must simply determine whether the matter relied on can provide a reasonable basis for the opinion or whether that opinion is based on a leap of logic or conjecture.” (Sargon Ent., Inc. v. Univ. of So. Cal. (2012) 55 Cal.4th 747, 772.) “ ‘The goal of trial court gatekeeping is simply to exclude “clearly invalid and unreliable” expert opinion.’ ” (Cooper v. Takeda Pharms. Am., Inc. (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 555, 592 [“methodologies were explored in detail through cross-examination and with the defense expert witnesses, and constituted evidence that went to the weight and not the admissibility….”].) There is a “fundamental distinction between the admissibility of evidence and its weight.” Union Pac. R.R. Co. v. Santa Fe Pac. Pipelines, Inc. (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 134, 187.) “[I]ssues about clarity and certitude go to credibility and weight, not admissibility.” (Collins v. Navistar, Inc. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 1486, 1514.) “‘It is for the trial court to determine, in the exercise of a sound discretion, the competency and qualification of an expert witness to give his opinion in evidence [citation], and its ruling will not be disturbed on appeal unless a manifest abuse of that discretion is shown.’” (Osborn v. Irwin Memorial Blood Bank (1992) 5 Cal.App.4th 234, 274.) “ ‘[Q]uestions regarding the degree of an expert's knowledge go more to the weight of the evidence presented than to its admissibility.’ ” (Richard v. Union Pac. R.R. Co. (2024) 105 Cal.App.5th 1263, 1277.)

Regarding the Elder Abuse Act, if a health care facility knows it must provide a certain type of care on a daily basis but provides it sporadically, or is supposed to provide multiple types of care but only provides some, then the withholding of care may constitute more than negligence, and reckless conduct, in support of heightened remedies, where a trier of fact finds a significant pattern of withholding medical care. (Sababin v. Superior Court (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 81, 90. See also Winn v. Pioneer Medical Group, Inc. (2016) 63 Cal.4th 148, 163-165 [Elder Abuse Act applies to defendants having had a caretaking or custodial relationship with an elder or dependent adult, including health care providers, administrators or employees of health facilities, persons providing care or services, members of support and maintenance staff, nursing homes, and clinics]; Stewart v. Superior Court (2017) 16 Cal.App.5th 87, 108 [triable issues regarding whether medical entity’s decision-making structure, and doctor’s decision about treatment, were beyond professional negligence and instead reckless]; Intrieri v. Superior Court (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 72, 85 [determining triable issue existed regarding reckless neglect in not providing medical care]; Benun v. Superior Court (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 113, 126 [“the intent of the Elder Abuse Act is to subject health care providers to its ‘heightened remedies’ when their acts or omissions are reckless or willful and, thus, more culpable than professional negligence.”]; Worsham v. O'Connor Hospital (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 331, 338 [alleged failures to provide a doctor-recommended sitter to prevent fall, understaffing and undertraining, amounted to professional negligence lacking in specific facts indicating any recklessness, defined as a conscious choice of action done with knowledge of serious danger to others involved]; Nevarrez v. San Marino Skilled Nursing and Wellness Centre (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 102, 128 [“To recover enhanced remedies under the act, ‘a plaintiff must prove more than simple or even gross negligence in the provider's care or custody of the elder.’”].)

The elements of a Cause of Action for Medical Negligence are these:

1.      Duty of the professional to use skill, prudence, and diligence as other members of the profession possess and exercise commonly;

2.      breach of that duty;

3.      proximate cause; and

4.      actual loss or damage.


(Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 468, fn.2; Hanson v. Grode (1999) 76 Cal.App.4th 601, 606.)

 

California Health & Safety Code “section 1430(b) provides that a current or former patient of a skilled nursing facility ‘may bring a civil action against the licensee of a facility who violates any rights of the resident or patient as set forth in the Patients Bill of Rights in Section 72527 of Title 22 of the California Code of Regulations, or any other right provided for by federal or state law or regulation. ... The licensee shall be liable for up to five hundred dollars ($500), and for costs and attorney fees, and may be enjoined from permitting the violation to continue ....’” (Jarman v. HCR ManorCare, Inc (2020) 10 Cal.5th 375, 381. Accord Nevarrez v. San Marino Skilled Nursing & Wellness Ctr., LLC (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 102, 123.)

And the elements of cause of action for wrongful death are a tort, resulting death, and damages of the heirs. (Quiroz v. Seventh Ave. Center (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1256, 1263.)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

Defendant contends that Plaintiff’s expert declarations incompetently lack certain details, including any overlooked symptoms, possibility of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism even without negligence, and any treatable or earlier intervention that would have improved the outcome. Defendant contrasts that with defense expert William Klein, M.D. who reportedly explained that Defendant’s staff performed appropriately and while the decedent’s vital signs were normal.

Plaintiffs counterargue that Defendant’s staff violated regulatory rules, knowingly withheld care, failed to assess DVT after polytrauma and recent surgery, and failed to implement a DVT prophylaxis like sequential compression devices. As evidence, the Opposition contains references to Plaintiffs’ declarations (Exhibit E) about staff lack of care provided to their mother, and two expert declarations contradicting Defendant’s expert.

“When the moving party produces a competent expert declaration showing there is no triable issue of fact on an essential element of the opposing party's claims, the opposing party's burden is to produce a competent expert declaration to the contrary.” (Bozzi v. Nordstrom, Inc. (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 755, 761-762.) Summary judgment must be denied if an opposing expert declaration raises a triable issue as to whether a physician deviated from the standard of care, thereby causing an injury and actual loss or damage. (Tortorella v. Castro (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1, 12-13.)

Here, in response to Defendant’s expert declarations, Plaintiff filed declarations of a doctor and registered nurse, providing reasonable bases for their opinions about Defendant’s neglect, withheld care, and causation, all based upon well explained medical records. And Defendants detailed arguments about asserted insufficiencies of the expert declarations go to their weight and not their admissibility.

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

Therefore, Court denies the Motion, entirely.

25STCV22236

CENTURY CITY MALL, LLC, A DELAWARE LIMITED LIABILITY COMPANY vs DIGITAL BRANDS GROUP, INC., A DELAWARE CORPORATION, et al.

July 16, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL COMPLIANCE WITH DEFENDANT’S AGREEMENT TO PRODUCE DOCUMENTS TO FIRST SET OF REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS FROM DEFENDANT AND WITH CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE SECTION 2031.280.  PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO FIRST SET OF REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS FROM DEFENDANT.

 

RULING:

First, the Court grants the Motion to Compel Compliance with Agreement to Produce.

On or before July 21, 2026, Defendant DIGITAL BRANDS GROUP, INC. shall produce documents, without objections, and in full compliance with Defendant’s agreement to produce documents to First Set of Requests for Production of Documents numbers specified in Plaintiff’s “Supplemental Brief in Support of Plaintiff’s Unopposed Motion to Compel Compliance with Defendant’s Agreement to Produce Documents…”, pursuant to the California Discovery Act (Code Civ. Proc. § 2016.010 et seq.).

Second, the Court grants the Motion to Compel Further Document Responses as to the request for Privilege Logs but denies it as to the request for Tax Returns.

On or before July 21, 2026, Defendant DIGITAL BRANDS GROUP, INC. shall serve the Privilege Log.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On July 29, 2025, CENTURY CITY MALL, LLC (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against DIGITAL BRANDS GROUP, INC (Defendant), “FOR ALTER EGO ACTION ON JUDGMENT.”

Plaintiff alleges that it seeks to add Defendant as an alter ego to a Judgment against Plaintiff’s former tenant Bailey 44, LLC, for over $1.4 million.

On June 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Motion to compel Defendant to produce all documents as agreed, and Plaintiff reports that Defendant has not produced all documents it agreed to.

Defendant has filed no Opposition to that Motion.

On June 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Motion to Compel Further Responses to First Set of Requests for Production of Documents from Defendant.

In an Opposition filed July 2, 2026, Defendant briefly argues that the requested tax returns do not exist, and the privilege logs would be too burdensome.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

A motion to compel compliance as agreed with a request for production of documents is based upon a showing that the respondents failed “to permit an inspection ‘in accordance with that party's statement of compliance.’” (Standon Co. v. Superior Court (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 898, 903. See also Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.320, subd. (a).) A separate motion to compel production of documents as agreed in responses, cannot be substituted for a motion to compel further responses. (Ibid.)

Where respondents object or respond inadequately to discovery requests, a motion lies to compel further responses, as to which respondent has the burden to justify the objections. (Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (2000) 22 Cal.4th 245, 255; Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a) [motion to compel further responses lies “[o]n receipt of a response to an inspection demand….”].) A propounding party's remedy when it deems objections in discovery responses to be without merit is to move for an order compelling further responses. (Catalina Island Yacht Club v. Superior Court (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1127.)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

 

A.    Document Production as Agreed

Plaintiff states that Defendant has produced additional documents after the Motion’s filing thereby resolving some issues, but nevertheless remaining documents have not been produced, as specified in the “Supplemental Brief in Support of Plaintiff’s Unopposed Motion to Compel Compliance with Defendant’s Agreement to Produce Documents ….”

“If the party fails to make a production in accordance with its statement of compliance, the opposing party may file another motion to compel seeking compliance.” (Bd. of Registered Nursing v. Superior Court (2021) 59 Cal.App.5th 1011, 1033 [citing Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.320].)

“Any documents or category of documents produced in response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling shall be identified with the specific request number to which the documents respond.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.280, subd. (a).)

Notwithstanding supplemental productions, the Motion is granted, given Defendant’s reported failures to produce some remaining documents as was agreed in discovery responses.

 

B.     Further Document Responses

Plaintiff requests (1) Defendant’s federal and state tax returns for its subsidiary BAILEY 44, LLC (BAILEY) as to which Plaintiff has a judgment in another case and (2) a privilege log concerning attorney-client communications since 2020 with the attorneys that represented Bailey’s interests in that lawsuit.

Defendant responds that (1) Baily has no tax return because there has been a consolidated tax return of entities and Defendant’s other affiliated companies can assert privacy rights and the tax return privilege, and (2) “[w]hatever relevance such a privilege log might have is vastly outweighed by the substantial burden of preparing it.”

The Reply Declaration of Meegan Maczek filed July 9, 2026, updates about additional supplemental responses and ongoing meeting and conferring.

“A party is entitled to take the testimony of his opponent before trial for the purpose of discovery. [Citation.] The party who is examined is required to answer fairly all proper questions which are put to him, but he is under no obligation to volunteer information or to disclose relevant and material matters which are not asked for.” (Meyer v. Cooper (1965) 233 Cal.App.2d 750, 754 [addressing depositions]. Cf. Great Am. Ins. Cos. v. Gordon Trucking, Inc. (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 445, 453 [“The interrogatories cited do not ask generally what caused the fire; they ask specifically for information about any malfunction….”].) Whether a discovery request is stated with reasonable particularity, enabling a reasonably adequate response, or it needs to be rephrased, is for court determination. (Flora Crane Serv., Inc. v. Superior Ct. In & For City & Cnty. of San Francisco (1965) 234 Cal.App.2d 767, 778 [addressing interrogatories].) “If it finds some requests too ambiguous to allow intelligent reply, it may sustain objection to them or, more consistently with justice, it may order such questions to be rephrased.” (Cembrook v. Superior Ct. (1961) 56 Cal.2d 423, 430.)

“A party may not deliberately misconstrue a question for the purpose of supplying an evasive answer. [Citation.] Indeed, where the question is somewhat ambiguous, but the nature of the information sought is apparent, the proper solution is to provide an appropriate response.” (Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 783. Accord, Field v. U.S. Bank Nat'l Assn. (2022) 79 Cal.App.5th 703, 708.)

“[A] party seeking to compel production of records from a nonparty must articulate specific facts justifying the discovery sought; it may not rely on mere generalities.” (Bd. of Registered Nursing v. Superior Court (2021) 59 Cal.App. 5th 1011, 1039.) (Underscoring added.) “The permissible scope of discovery in general is not as broad with respect to nonparties as it is with respect to parties.” (Catholic Mut. Relief Soc'y v. Superior Court (2007) 42 Cal.4th 358, 366, fn. 6.)

“The corporate right to privacy is a lesser right than that held by human beings and is not considered a fundamental right.” (SCC Acquisitions, Inc. v. Superior Court (2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 741, 756 [evaluating whether the corporate information appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence, balanced against the corporate right, with doubts about relevance generally resolved in favor of allowing the discovery].)

There is a qualified privilege to withhold disclosure of tax returns, which is subject to implied waiver. (E.g., Fortunato v. Superior Court (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 475, 482 [potential relevance alone does not render the tax-return privilege inapplicable]; Weingarten v. Superior Court (2002) 102 Cal.App.4th 268, 274; Deary v. Superior Court (2001) 87 Cal.App.4th 1072, 1079 [tax return privilege is derived from Revenue and Taxation Code and is inaccurately referred to as a constitutional right of privacy]; Schnabel v. Superior Court (1993) 5 Cal.4th 704, 721; Wilson v. Superior Court (1976) 63 Cal.App.3d 825, 831 [“Having initiated this action over her tax returns, plaintiff cannot interdict legitimate defenses thereto by asserting a privilege as to the very matters she has placed at issue in the litigation.”].) Information that is an integral part of tax returns, or contained in tax returns, qualifies for the tax privilege, including documents that are required to be attached to income tax returns. (Brown v. Superior Court (1977) 71 Cal.App.3d 141, 143-144.) “[T]he [tax return] privilege is waived or does not apply where (1) there is an intentional relinquishment …, (2) the ‘gravamen of (the) lawsuit is so inconsistent with the continued assertion of the taxpayer's privilege as to compel the conclusion that the privilege has in fact been waived …, or (3) a public policy greater than that of confidentiality of tax returns is involved ….” (Sammut v. Sammut (1980) 103 Cal.App.3d 557, 560.)

Here, regarding a federal tax return, Defendant has no obligation to serve a response to a question never asked—i.e. for a consolidated tax return. But whether there is any unconsolidated state tax return, remains unclear from the filings. Plaintiff’s Reply, at pages 1 and, 3 through 4, argues that, under federal law, consolidated returns contain the return of subsidiaries. However, “California law governs discovery disputes that arise in our courts.” (Unzipped Apparel, LLC v. Bader (2007) 156 Cal.App.4th 123, 130.)

Additionally, objections of nonparties that did not file a lawsuit, would need to be addressed, including their own corporate privacy rights and the tax return privilege.

As for the privilege log, Defendant’s burden objection is unsupported by law. There simply is no burdensome exception to the statutory requirement of producing a privilege log. (Riddell, Inc. v. Superior Court (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 755, 772.)

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

The Court grants the Motion to Produce as Agreed, and grants in part the Motion to Compel Further Responses as to a Privilege Log, as further specified herein.

NA YOUNG LEE vs UNIVERSITY OF SOUTHERN CALIFORNIA, et al.

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: DEFENDANT UNIVERSITY OF SOUTHERN CALIFORNIA’S DEMURRER TO PLAINTIFF’S FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT.

 

RULING:

The Court sustains the Demurrer without leave to amend as to the Seventh Cause of Action (Labor Code §§ 1050, 1052) and otherwise overrules it.

Twenty days to answer.

Defendant to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On May 4, 2026, (Plaintiff) filed the First Amended Complaint (FAC) against (Defendant), listing Causes of Action for:

(1)  SEX/RACE/NATIONAL ORIGIN HARASSMENT - Govt. Code §12940(j);

(2)  SEX/RACE/NATIONAL ORIGIN DISCRIMINATION - Govt. Code §12940(a);

(3)  RETALIATION - Govt. Code §12940(h);

(4)  FAILURE TO PREVENT HARASSMENT, DISCRIMINATION, and RETALIATION - Govt. Code §12940(k);

(5)  VIOLATION OF UNRUH ACT CIVIL RIGHTS ACT – Civil Code §51;

(6)  DISCRIMINATION AND HARASSMENT IN THE EDUCATIONAL SETTING – Ed. Code §66270;

(7)  VIOLATION OF LABOR CODE §§1050, 1052;

(8) INTENTIONAL INFLICTION OF EMOTIONAL DISTRESS; and

(9) NEGLIGENT SUPERVISION.

 

Plaintiff alleges that a professor and serial sexual harasser of USC’s students and employees discriminated, harassed, and retaliated against Plaintiff, as both her academic advisor and supervisor, based upon sex, race and national origin, forcing her to quit her job.

On June 2, 2026, Defendant filed the Demurrer to the FAC, requesting that it be sustained as to Causes of Action One through Four (FEHA); Five (Unruh Act); Seven (Labor Code §§ 1050, 1052); Eight (Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress (IIED)) ; and Nine (Negligence).

Plaintiff opposes, based upon counterarguments such as, “each of the challenged Causes of Action states facts sufficient to proceed, or, at minimum, is capable of being cured by amendment,…” (Opposition, 5:19-21.)

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

Demurrers are to be sustained where a pleading fails to plead adequately any essential element of the cause of action. (Cantu v. Resolution Trust Corp. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 857, 879-880.) “[T]he complaint must include the prima facie elements of employment discrimination….” (Caldwell v. Paramount Unif. Sch. Dist. (1995) 41 Cal.App.4th 189, 202, fn. 7.) A complaint does not necessarily need to be a model of pleading but needs sufficient factual allegations. (E.g., State v. The Superior Court (2001) 87 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1411.)

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally, reasonably and in context. (MKB Management, Inc. v. Melikian (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 796, 802; McKenney v. Purepac Pharmaceutical Co. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 72, 77; Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved of on other grounds by Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1173.) In ruling upon demurrers, courts treat as being true “not only the complaint's material factual allegations, but also facts that may be implied or inferred from those expressly alleged.” (Poseidon Development, Inc. v. Woodland Lane Estates, LLC (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 1106, 1111-1112. Accord, Schauer v. Mandarin Gems of Cal., Inc. (2005) 125 Cal.App.4th 949, 953.)

“In considering a demurrer, the court is not required to ignore controlling law merely because the plaintiff does not rely on it.” (Dey v. Continental Central Credit (2008) 170 Cal.App.4th 721, 730.)

III.              ANALYSIS

Defendant seeks an Order sustaining the Demurrer to certain claims, addressed specifically below.

In opposition, Plaintiff primarily asserts the following positions:

·         Read in its full context, the FAC sufficiently alleges that harassment and discrimination were by a supervisor and work-related, within a university context.

·         Employers can be liable for supervisors’ sexual harassment.

·         Alleged retaliation occurred both before and after employment ended, which are both actionable.

·         The underlying claims do not fail and so neither does Failure to Prevent FEHA violations.

·         The Unruh Act claim is not confined to employment but includes denying equal privileges, which applies to universities (Kao v. Univ. of San Francisco (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 437).  

·         Plaintiff could better allege Labor Code violations, including that misrepresentations serve as “a direct conduit to prospective academic employers” and were “calculated to poison the small professional pool in Plaintiff's field,…” (Opposition, pp. 14-15.)

·         Defendant’s Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress is alleged, including its decision to leave Kang in place as Plaintiff's academic advisor and supervisor, during the protracted investigation.

·         A negligence claim is viable based upon foreseeability and physical contact (Walton vs. Victor Valley Community College Disc., 119 Cal.App.5th at 1172-1175).

 

A.    First-through-Fourth Claims (FEHA).

Defendant contends that Plaintiff inadequately alleges harassment during an academic relationship but not in employment (citing Fitzsimons v. California Emergency Physicians Med. Grp. (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 1423).

Where sufficient factual allegations are intermingled with conclusions, the complaint is not subject to a demurrer just as to the conclusions. (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.)

Modernly, in pleading FEHA causes of action, plaintiffs are only required to set forth the essential facts sufficiently to acquaint defendants with the cause of action. (See Alch v. Superior Court (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 339, 382-383 [“They further allege the employers have hired statistically significant lower numbers of older writers than would be expected given the relevant qualified applicant pool, and these disparities increase in direct relationship to age. They also describe anecdotal evidence of intentional discrimination. We discern no missing statutory element in these allegations”]; Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1236 [“[e]ven though” plaintiff “did not specifically plead ...” the court determined that plaintiff “sufficiently pleaded the causal link between the protected activity and the defendants’ adverse action.”], disapproved of on other grounds by Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1173; Thomas v. Regents of Univ. of California (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th 587, 614 [“allegations of pervasive ongoing harassment, combined with the general allegation that gender was ‘[a] substantial motivating reason for … conduct’ are sufficient.”].) However, it is insufficient to allege just conclusions concerning alleged discrimination, harassment or retaliation. (Bagatti v. Dep't of Rehab. (2002) 97 Cal.App.4th 344, 366, fn. 8 [“These conclusionary allegations, which allege no specific acts, are insufficient to survive demurrer.”]; Fisher v. San Pedro Peninsula Hosp. (1989) 214 Cal.App.3d 590, 604, 608, 613 [as to  claims for harassment and retaliation, “facts in support of each of the requirements of a statute upon which a cause of action is based must be specifically pled”].)

“[I]n some cases the hostile message that constitutes … harassment is conveyed through official employment actions, and therefore evidence that would otherwise be associated with a discrimination claim can form the basis of a harassment claim.” (Roby v. McKesson Corp. (2009) 47 Cal.4th 686, 708.) Even official employment actions can evidence a harassment claim where the action communicates a hostile or offensive message to the employee. (Pollock v. Tri-Modal Distribution Services, Inc. (2021) 11 Cal.5th 918, 932-933.)

An employer may be strictly liable for harassment of an employee by an agent or supervisor. (State Dept. of Health Services v. Superior Court (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1026, 1034; Fiol v. Doellstedt (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 1318, 1331; John Doe v. Capital Cities (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 1038, 1052. Accord Patterson v. Domino's Pizza, LLC (2014) 60 Cal.4th 474, 491 [“‘under the FEHA, an employer is strictly liable for all acts of sexual harassment by a supervisor’”].) “An employer continues to be strictly liable for hostile environment sexual harassment by a supervisor. An employee's failure to report harassment to the employer is not a defense on the merits to the employee's action under the FEHA, but at most it serves to reduce the damages recoverable.” (State Dept. of Health Services v. Superior Court (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1026, 1049.) “[W]here the conduct … occurred offsite and/or during nonworking hours, the question of whether the employer is strictly liable for a supervisor’s conduct tends to overlap with whether the conduct is sufficiently work related.” (Kruitbosch v. Bakersfield Recovery Services, Inc. (2025) 114 Cal.App.5th 200, 217.)

The FAC, read in its full context, contains references to employment-related discrimination, harassment and retaliation, done contemporaneously while Plaintiff was still being supervised and Defendant was being officially investigated without any action. The FAC combines specific enough facts, mixed in with some conclusions. (See, e.g., FAC, ¶¶ 5 (“ As her direct supervisor, Kang targeted Plaintiff and first required her to perform domestic duties for him at his home,… Kang aggressively propositioned Plaintiff romantically.  When Plaintiff rejected him, Kang physically assaulted Plaintiff and harassed Plaintiff and her partner … in person, on the phone, and via text message and email.”), 6 (“harassment and retaliation against Plaintiff was so severe and pervasive that it forced her to quit….”), 7 (“she complained to USC about Kang’s conduct and participated in an investigation of his misconduct.  In response, Kang directly retaliated against Plaintiff by threatening to remove her name from an academic paper they co-authored and which represented years of Plaintiff’s academic work towards her PhD.”), 46 (“communications and requests spanned from at least April 2020 to October 23, 2021 and occurred on a daily basis, sometimes multiple times a day, and are far too numerous to itemize….”), 89 (“harassment and retaliation for Plaintiff rebuffing his romantic advances when she was employed by USC and he was her supervisor,…”), 94 (“USC investigators interviewed Plaintiff extensively about Kang’s retaliatory emails, threats and theft of her intellectual work but did nothing to address or prevent it.”), 105 (“During her employment, Kang subjected Plaintiff to severe and pervasive sexual harassment based on her sex and her race/national origin.”), 108 (“harassment thus spanned from 2020 until at least August 2024,….”), and 110 (“USC knew as early as 2015 and no later than December 2, 2022 that other female USC employees had complained that Kang bullied and harassed women at USC.”).)

An opinion that Defendant cited (Reply, 5:25), distinguishably had analyzed evidence, and not allegation sufficiency. (See Capitol City Foods, Inc. v. Superior Court (1992) 5 Cal.App.4th 1042, 1050 [“defendant conclusively refuted the allegation Johnson forced plaintiff to accompany him or coerced her in any way prior to entering his bedroom.”].) With regard to ruling upon demurrers, courts have found unsupportive counsel’s citations to authorities that did not address pleading standards. (E.g., Alch v. Superior Court (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 339, 382, fn. 37 ["Both ... were appeals from a grant of summary judgment ..., and did not address pleading requirements.”].)

Furthermore, the rule about specific allegations controlling over general, is inapposite, because Plaintiff’s allegations of FEHA violations, occurring in the two contexts of employment and education, are not necessarily mutually exclusive or impossible to occur at different times. If there is any inconsistency between the specific allegations upon which a conclusion must be based and the conclusion, the specific allegations control. (E.g., Careau & Co. v. Sec. Pac. Bus. Credit (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1371, 1390; B & P Dev. Corp. v. City of Saratoga (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 949, 953.) “‘[I]t is possible that specific allegations will render a complaint defective when the general allegations, standing alone, might have been sufficient.’” (Ferrick v. Santa Clara Univ. (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 1337, 1352.) (Underscoring added.)

These Demurrer points are overruled.

 

B.     Fifth Claim (Unruh Act).

Defendant contends that the Unruh Act is inapposite to employment discrimination allegations based on sexual harassment (citing Brown v. Smith (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 767, 787).

That proposition is to argue a distinction without a difference, because Civil Code section 51 et seq. in its broader scope beyond the Unruh Act, can apply. This has been an issue of some confusion in the past. Provisions against discriminatory violence and intimidation, and denial of civil rights by means of threats and intimidation (Civ. Code, §§ 51.7 and 52.1) may be asserted in cases alleging wrongful termination and employment discrimination, but they are not part of the Unruh Civil Rights Act. (Stamps v. Superior Court (2006) 136 Cal.App.4th 1441, 1456.)

Here, the FAC alleges, at paragraph 174, “the requirements of the Unruh Act, codified in Civil Code section 51, et seq.” (Underscoring added.) Thus, the Fifth Cause of Action, incorporating-by-reference all previous allegations, expressly encompasses sections 51.7 and 52.1. Moreover, the claim label need not be accurate. In ruling upon demurrers, courts consider whether any cause of action has been alleged sufficiently, notwithstanding that a cause of action is labeled otherwise. (Arce ex rel. Arce v. Kaiser Found. Health Plan, Inc. (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 471, 482; Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525; McBride v. Boughton (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 379, 387.) “[T]he allegations in the body of the complaint, not the caption, constitute the cause of action against the defendant.” (Davaloo v. State Farm Ins. Co. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 409, 418.)

Further, the FAC is not confined to employment as that constitutes one part of broader allegations. (See, e.g., FAC, ¶ 181 (“Plaintiff had the right to be free from gender and racial discrimination, sexual harassment and retaliation under California’s Unruh Civil Rights Act while she was a student and employee….”).) Demurrers do not lie as to only parts of causes of action, where some valid claim is alleged. (E.g., Poizner v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119 [“A demurrer must dispose of an entire cause of action to be sustained.”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redev. Agency (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1046.)

The Demurrer position is overruled.

 

C.     Seventh Claim (Labor Code §§ 1050, 1052).

Defendant maintains that the Labor Code claim fails to be pled specifically, including as to the identity of prospective employers.

Labor  Code section 1050 states: “Any person, or agent or officer thereof, who, after having discharged an employee from the service of such person or after an employee has voluntarily left such service, by any misrepresentation prevents or attempts to prevent the former employee from obtaining employment, is guilty of a misdemeanor.” Section 1050 applies to misrepresentations made to prospective employers. (Kelly v. General Telephone Co. (1982) 136 Cal.App.3d 278, 288.) Employers violating Labor Code section 1050 may be liable civilly for treble damages to former employees. (Lab. Code, § 1054.) Labor Code section 1052 provides any person who causes, permits, or fails to take reasonable steps to prevent, a violation of section 1050, also may be in violation. Labor Code section 1053 states, in pertinent part: “Nothing in this chapter shall prevent an employer or an agent, employee, superintendent or manager thereof from furnishing, upon special request therefor, a truthful statement concerning the reason for the discharge of an employee or why an employee voluntarily left the service of the employer.”

Plaintiff’s broad interpretation of those sections as including misrepresentations made during employment having the potential to reach prospective employers post-employment, is unsupported by any applicable authority. On the contrary, there is very little citable California law on point as to those statutory provisions that plainly reference employees who already left work followed by employers misrepresentations trying to prevent the employees’ successive employment. Judges should not disregard express statutory language or add language that is not there. (Yao v. Superior Court (2002) 104 Cal.App.4th 327, 333.)

Here, Plaintiff’s allegations completely lack any reference to the elements of a misrepresentation about her as a former employee to any prospective employer.

Additionally, from the Opposition and allegations, the Court finds that any amendment of this claim would be futile. “ ‘[W]here the nature of the plaintiff's claim is clear, and under substantive law no liability exists, a court should deny leave to amend because no amendment could change the result.’ ” (San Mateo Union High Sch. Dist. v. County of San Mateo (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 418, 441.) “ ‘[L]eave to amend should not be granted where … [an] amendment would be futile.’ ” (Long v. Century Indemnity Co. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1460, 1468.)

Therefore, the Demurrer is sustained, without leave to amend, as to the Seventh Cause of Action.

 

D.    Eighth Claim (IIED).

Defendant reasons that the IIED claim cannot be based upon vicarious liability based upon an individual’s conduct, and outrageous conduct is not alleged as to Defendant.

“[W]hether conduct is ‘outrageous’ is usually a question of fact.” (So v. Shin (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 652, 672. See also Cross v. Bonded Adjustment Bureau (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 266, 283 [outrageous conduct is a fact question where reasonable minds may differ]; Kovr-Tv, Inc. v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 1023, 1028 [outrageous conduct is not a bright-line determination, but depends upon an intuitive appraisal of “values, sensitivity threshold, and standards of civility....”]; Pulver v. Avco Fin. Servs. (1986) 182 Cal.App.3d 622, 637 [demurrer should have been overruled, given sufficient allegations that defendant’s conduct was, “‘outrageous, was engaged [in] with the intent to cause and with conscious disregard of the probability of causing emotional distress. As a legal result of that conduct, plaintiff did in fact suffer severe emotional distress and incurred the damages’….” ].)

Employment actions can involve outrageous conduct including by employers. (E.g., Rulon-Miller v. I.B.M. Corp. (1984) 162 Cal.App.3d 241, 255 [employer stated to employee that she could not see her romantic friend or she would face employment termination], overruled on other grounds by Guz v. Bechtel Nat’l, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 351; Ankeny v. Lockheed Missiles and Space Co. (1979) 88 Cal.App.3d 531, 537 [employer’s outrageous conduct not alleged due to dearth of facts].)

Here, Plaintiff adequately alleges that Defendant knowingly allowed the same supervisor to sexually harass employees for years, and during Plaintiff’s employment. (E.g., FAC, ¶¶ 7 (“When Plaintiff learned that Kang had been harassing her Asian female colleagues, she complained to USC….”), 41 (“USC did not provide Plaintiff with training regarding discrimination and/or sexual harassment…. USC did not provide Kang with this training either,…”), 69 (“At the end of 2021, Plaintiff was so distressed at the thought of seeing Kang on the USC campus that she was forced to quit her position as Kang’s Research Assistant.”), 100 (“USC did not terminate Kang and continued to feature him prominently on its website until approximately March 2026.”, 8 (“USC was fully aware of these facts but did nothing to prevent or remedy this outrageous outcome.”), 9 (“despite USC’s ultimate conclusion that Kang had harassed Plaintiff and others, and despite knowing that Kang had severely penalized Plaintiff for her complaint against him, Kang remained a tenured professor….”), and 225 (“Defendants have thus created and enabled a culture of toxic abuse of its employees and students, and has let the abusers, like Kang, walk freely among their victims.”).)

The Demurrer is overruled as to these bases.

 

E.     Ninth Claim (Negligence). 

Defendant argues that the employer cannot be liable for a claim of negligence related to sexual harassment or conduct occurring apart from the employment.

Defendant’s cited opinion contains a disclaimer about it being case-specific. (See Thomas v. Regents of Univ. of California (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th 587, 627–628 [“This is not to say sexual harassment in the form alleged in this case does not cause significant harm, nor that a university may never be held to have a duty to protect its students from nonphysical injuries. We simply are not prepared to take the step Thomas suggests on the basis of the briefing she has provided.”].) That opinion also mentions cases basing employers’ duties upon involvement of “sexual assault”, “foreseeable assault” and “negligent hiring/supervision”, which are aspects alleged in the FAC. (E.g., ¶¶ 106 (“he physically assaulted her….”), 28-29 (“sexual assault”), 28 (“USC’s culture of hiring, employing and enabling men in positions of authority who prey on Asian female international students.”), 182 (“Defendants did not reasonably investigate, supervise or monitor Kang to ensure the safety of its students and student/employees, including Plaintiff.”).)  Indeed, colleges may have a duty to protect students from physical harassment, or to refrain from negligent hiring, supervision and retention. (Walton v. Victor Valley Cmty. Coll. Dist. (2026) 119 Cal.App.5th 1164, 1175.)

And, generally speaking, factors for determining whether there is a duty owed to a plaintiff include “ ‘forseeability of harm to the plaintiff, the degree of certainty that plaintiff suffered injury, the closeness of the connection between the defendant's conduct and the injury suffered, the moral blame attached to defendant's conduct, the policy of preventing future harm, the extent of the burden to the defendant and the consequences to the community of imposing a duty to exercise care with resulting liability for breach, and the availability, cost, and prevalence of insurance for the risk involved.’ " (Gu v. BMW of North America, LLC (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 195, 208.)

In summary, given the particular allegations of the FAC, the Court cannot soundly rule as a matter of law that the allegations fail to support a duty in negligence.

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

Accordingly, the Court sustains the Demurrer only as to the Seventh Cause of Action, and otherwise overrules it, for reasons stated herein.

The Court grants the Motion.

ANALYSIS:

By the Motion, filed June 15, 2026, counsel moves to be relieved as attorney of record for Defendant by declaring, in the form Declaration, at paragraph 2: “ There has been a breakdown of the relationship with client and a material term or terms of the underlying agreement between client and Shapero Law Firm, P.C. such that SLF cannot continue its representation.”

Here, the form notice, declaration, proposed order, and proof of service sufficiently comply with the requirements for a motion to be relieved as counsel. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362.)

Further, moving counsel’s declaration shows cognizable grounds for withdrawal:

o          A breakdown in the attorney-client relationship (or personality clash). (Estate of Falco v. Decker (1987) 188 Cal.App.3d 1004, 1014 (Falco).)

o          The client by conduct renders it unreasonably difficult for the member to carry out the employment effectively (e.g., noncooperation or noncommunication). (Rules Prof. Conduct, rule 1.16(b)(4). Cf. Falco, supra, 188 Cal App.3d at p. 1020 [“We find no abuse of discretion in the trial court's implicit finding rejecting the contention that respondents' lack of cooperation justified appellants' withdrawal.”].)

o          The client breaches an agreement as to the member as to expenses or fees. (Rules Prof. Conduct, rule 1.16; Lempert v. Sup. Ct. (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 1161, 1173.)

Finally, no opposing document is filed in order to show any prejudice caused by attorney withdrawal. (See Rules Prof. Conduct, rule 1.16(d); Vann v. Shilleh (1975) 54 Cal.App.3d 192, 197.)

Therefore, the Court grants the Motion and will sign the proposed Order counsel submitted.
