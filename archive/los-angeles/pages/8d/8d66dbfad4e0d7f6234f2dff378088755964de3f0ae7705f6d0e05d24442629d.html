Case Number: 23SMCV03134    Hearing Date: June 24, 2026    Dept: I

This is a motion by defendant to declare plaintiff a vexatious litigant.  The court is inclined to DENY the motion.  The motion is governed by CCP section 391.  There are three bases upon which a party can be declared vexatious.  One is that within the last 7 years the party has filed a lawsuit that failed and did so as a self-represented litigant or has unjustifiably allowed a case to remain pending for two years without a trial.  The second way is where a litigant has attempted to relitigate an issue that has been finally determined, again as a self-represented party.  The third is where a self-represented litigant has repeatedly filed unmeritorious motions or papers or engages in tactics that are frivolous. 

 

The court does not believe that the first option works.  Most of the five actions defendant claims count have not been finally determined against plaintiff in the prior seven year period.  And in some of them plaintiff was represented by counsel.  (Plaintiff was represented by counsel in the instant case all the way through judgment.)  The second option is also not the one upon which the court relies.  Again, finality is required and plaintiff must be unrepresented.  But again, in many of the cases cited plaintiff was represented by counsel.  That leaves the third option.  And the court thinks that defendant has made the point there.  The case before the court involves a “Kashet.”  Plaintiff sued to force defendant to open it, presumably because plaintiff believed he was entitled to some of the jewels he thinks are inside.  The court ultimately ruled for defendant on the claim, finding that the issue had been fully and finally adjudicated in other fora.  On appeal, the Court of Appeal agreed, finding that the matter had been adjudicated—and more than once.  After the Supreme Court denied review, a remittitur was issued affirming the court’s judgment.  That ought to be the end of this case.  But plaintiff now seeks to set that judgment aside.  Defendant claims that this is precisely the kind of motion that the statute seeks to prevent.  Defendant also points to motions in other cases, including motions to grant relief from deadlines to file discovery motions, motions to add the Kashet issue to other cases, and motions for reconsideration of motions previously denied.  The fact is that this is somewhat of a close call.  The court has some concern that unless the motion is granted, plaintiff will just keep suing over this issue even though the issue has been adjudicated.  The court notes that this prong of the test does not require a specific time frame or a specific number of motions or actions.  Rather, the resolution of that is in the court’s discretion.  (Morton v. Wagner (2007) 156 Cal.App.4th 963.)  There is no bright line rule on this ground, but the court here looks to the totality of the circumstances.  (Id.)  And to find a litigant vexatious, the court should conclude that the litigant’s actions are unreasonably hampering the objects of the other party’s actions and the courts as contemplated by the statute.  (Goodrich v. Sierra Vista Regional Medical Center (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1260.)  The court believes that plaintiff believes that his cause is just.  But a finding of vexatiousness does not require bad faith or ill motive.  It only requires that a party will not stop re-litigating matters that have already been decided or filing motions or actions that lack merit.  On the other hand, the court is also aware that defendant has kicked the proverbial hornet’s nest and has brought his own action against plaintiff.  The court is not saying defendant’s action is improper or bad faith; it is just that when one kicks a hornet’s nest, one cannot complain too loudly about being stung.  On balance, the court believes that it is better to err on the side of denying the motion than of granting it, and, accordingly, the motion is DENIED.

 

The court had also continued the motion to vacate the judgment to this date.  While the court’s original intention was to continue that motion further, little purpose would be served in doing so.  Nor would there be any real advantage to anyone to require the pre-filing requirement to kick in with regard to this already-filed motion.  Therefore, the court will exercise its discretion to consider the motion on the merits unless defendant objects on the ground that defendant has been denied the opportunity to file an opposition.

 

To the extent that the motion is predicated on the judgment being void, the court sees no extrinsic fraud or mistake.  Extrinsic fraud occurs where the other side is deprived of an opportunity to present the claim in court, such as being given the wrong hearing date such that the party did not appear at the hearing, the concealment of a community property asset so that it could not be divided during a dissolution, or convincing the other side that there is no need to retain counsel because the case will be dismissed but then not dismissing it.  The point is that one party has to have prevented the losing party of having a day in court.  (Navarro v. IHOP Properties, Inc. (2005) 134 Cal.App.4th 834.)  Intrinsic fraud, on the other hand, occurs where there has been an opportunity to present the case, but the case just went the other way.  That can happen because the court gets it wrong, there are issues regarding discovery such that one party does not honestly comply with discovery requests, or even when a party presents an untrue declaration to the court upon which the court relies.  The point is that when it goes to the merits of the case as opposed to the ability of a party even to participate, the fraud is almost always intrinsic.  Extrinsic mistake usually appears in the case of a default—that is, the defendant just does not appear for some excusable reason and a default judgment is entered so there is never a merits ruling at all, such as a situation where the defaulted party was in the hospital and thus was never aware of the action even though service was proper.  (Mechling v. Asbestos Defendants (2018) 29 Cal.App.5th 1241.) 

 

The instant motion goes to the merits.  Defendant’s prior motions were properly noticed; plaintiff was properly served with them.  Plaintiff had a full and fair opportunity to explain why it was that the case was not barred by prior proceedings and plaintiff was well represented by able counsel; it is just that the court did not agree.  And plaintiff was not defaulted; plaintiff just lost.  The court is sympathetic to plaintiff’s health issues, but the court does not believe that the 2021 issue is sufficient to undo a judgment entered in 2024. 

 

It is possible that the courts got the merits wrong.  Courts are not infallible.  Judges—and appellate justices—are drawn from the human race.  Sometimes, there are mistakes.  But the notion of justice is not that it will be perfect; it is that it will be fair and unbiased.  The court is confident that the proceedings here were fair and unbiased.  Accordingly, the motion to vacate the judgment is DENIED.

 

One last thing.  Although the court denied the vexatious litigant motion, that is on the assumption that plaintiff now gets the message.  The Kashet issue is over and done.  Another attempt to relitigate that question will, at least in this court’s mind, likely suggest that the court erred in denying the instant vexatious litigant motion and the court is unlikely to make the same mistake again.

Case Number: 23SMCV05294    Hearing Date: June 24, 2026    Dept: I

This is an application to continue the trial.  It is DENIED.

 

This case is over 2.5 years old.  The Case Management Conference was held on December 24, 2024.  At the CMC, the court set the instant trial date, but only after confirming that there were no conflicts with the date and that all parties were available and that it was sufficient time for the parties to be ready for trial.  The court verbally stated that there would be no continuances absent truly unforeseen and unforeseeable circumstances, and that was reflected in the Minute Order, which states that there will be no continuances absent extraordinary good cause.  The court also set a mediation completion date of March 16, 2026—a date that was suggested by the parties.

 

The parties state that they have been diligent, but that discovery is still ongoing.  Further, they would like to mediate at the end of July.  That makes no sense to the court.  The trial date was set about 20 months before the trial.  There is no explanation as to why the necessary discovery could not be done in a timely way.  Other than a statement that the parties have been diligent, the court has no evidence of diligence.  As to mediation, there is no explanation as to why the parties ignored the court’s order (made with the parties’ express approval) that the mediation be completed by March 16, 2026—a date they suggested.  The reason for the mediation completion date was precisely to allow the parties to do the necessary discovery (or at least the critical discovery) and then mediate well in advance of the trial.

 

The court is not trying to be difficult.  When this court took over the department, the court had about 800 cases on the docket.  The court routinely gave first continuances of a few months if the request was joint because the court had plenty of spots to put the trial.  The court now has over 1300 cases on the docket, and at the CMC, the court had over 1100 cases on the docket.  Because this is an I/C court, trials take longer.  It takes 3 weeks to try most cases (start to finish), so if one does the math it will take about 100 years to try the current docket.  Now, we all know that is not a real number because the vast majority of cases settle or otherwise resolve without a trial or a continuance.  But even assuming that 95% of the cases are in that category, it will take 5 years to try the cases that remain.  Worse, the docket is growing each month.  (This is a problem County-wide, not just in Department I or the West District.)  The reality is that the court has nowhere to put this case if the trial date is vacated.  And that is why the court gave the warning that it did at the CMC; so that the parties would know that the deadlines were real and act accordingly.

 

Because the court’s docket is what it is, the request must be DENIED.


Case Number: 24SMCV02759    Hearing Date: June 24, 2026    Dept: I

This is an ex parte application to strike a lien filed by the City of Culver City.  The underlying case involves the injury of a City employee due to a defective piece of equipment.  The manufacturer, Toter, was sued for the injury.  Allegedly, during discovery, the parties learned that the City was also potentially partially responsible.  Plaintiff says that this changed the nature of its case because Toter now had another potentially responsible party, thereby reducing its liability.  Plaintiff says that the settlement reflects that realty.  Plaintiff and Toter have settled, and Toter has issued a check.  However, the City has filed a lien.  Plaintiff was injured while on the job, and accordingly sought workers’ compensation.  The City seeks a lien on the settlement proceeds on the theory that to the extent it pays out on the workers’ compensation, it is entitled to all or some of the money Toter has paid for the same injury.  Recall that there is a lack of symmetry here.  Toter might be able to reduce its liability to the extent plaintiff’s injury is caused by the City, but the City cannot do the same because workers’ compensation is strict liability.  Thus, the City’s remedy if (hypothetically) it paid out 100% of plaintiff’s injury to plaintiff in the workers’ compensation action, is to seek a recovery from Toter.  Here, it is apparently seeking to do that through the lien.

 

Plaintiff complains that the City has not specified an amount for the lien, nor has it attempted to describe how the unspecified amount was derived.  Accordingly, the lien is for an indeterminate amount and stops the parties from negotiating Toter’s check.  That, plaintiff claims, is a problem and is unfair. 

 

The court does not believe that this is appropriate for ex parte resolution.  The court will, however, construe the application as an ex parte application to shorten time to hear the motion to strike or modify the lien, and will GRANT the OST.  The court will take the original papers as plaintiff’s moving papers and discuss with the City and plaintiff a schedule for an opposition, a reply, and a hearing date.  The court’s initial thoughts are that the City does need to specify an amount—it is not proper to have a lien in a secret amount.  And it ought to be able or willing to set forth how the amount was derived.  It might be that the amount of the lien is held by plaintiff’s counsel by way of an agreement between plaintiff and the City pending resolution of the merits of the lien, but the remainder can be released to plaintiff.  

 


Case Number: 24SMCV02786    Hearing Date: June 24, 2026    Dept: I

There are two relatively similar matters before the court.  One is a demurrer by defendant Invasix and the other is a motion for judgment on the pleadings by Inmode.  Because they motions are similar, the court discusses them together.

 

Plaintiff brought this action following a septoplasty and turbinate reduction procedure to help with his sleep apnea.  Dr. Alessi recommended an additional Facetite procedure in the neck and chin area for the apnea and cosmetic reasons.  The machine used for the procedure was designed and sold by defendants InMode and Invasix.  Dr. Alessi allegedly told plaintiff that the machine and probe were specifically designed to be safe and would not burn the skin, and also that the procedure was minimally invasive.  Plaintiff agreed to the procedure, and it took place on March 2, 2023.  Plaintiff alleges that he was told to pick up prescriptions from the pharmacy but was not told that the prescriptions should have included antibiotics or that there were none in the medication that was dispensed.  As a result, plaintiff claims, the recovery was unpleasant and plaintiff developed an infection that did not resolve until June.  He also claims that he was burned by the laser during the Facetite procedure and will need another surgery to correct those problems.  InMode and Invasix are alleged to have designed, manufactured, assembled, sold, supplied, or distributed the subject machine.

 

One problem is that the third cause of action is for strict products liability, negligence, and breach of warranty.  Those really should be pled separately.  The court views the election to lump them together as artful pleading, and the court will not use that as a basis to deny the motion on the theory that a demurrer or MJOP must eliminate an entire cause of action.  (The better procedure, plaintiff states, would be a motion to strike.  The problem with forcing that issue is that we are actually getting close to trial and doing this all over again serves no purpose.)

 

Turning, then, to the merits, defendants seem to contend in part that one problem is that the causes of action for strict liability, negligence, and breach of warranty overlap.  That may be so, but plaintiff has not had the benefit of discovery.  The court will allow plaintiff to plead in the alternative even if the damages may wind up overlapping.

 

The first major challenge is that plaintiff cannot (the defense contends) sue for strict liability.  The court agrees with regard to the design defect theory.  California follows the Restatement on this point.  And under the law in California, a manufacturer is not strictly liable for injuries caused by a medical product that has been properly made and was distributed with proper information regarding the risks and dangers the manufacturer knew or should have known at the time.  In other words, for policy reasons, a defect in the design of a product will not be awarded for purposes of strict liability in the context of a medical device.  (Hufft v. Horowitz (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 8.)  The court is aware that Hufft extended a case involving prescription medicine to a case involving an implanted medical device, and this case is neither.  But the logic of Hufft supports application of that principle here.  In Hufft, the Court of Appeal discussed an earlier case, Brown v. Superior Court (1988) 44 Cal.3d 1049.  In Brown, our Supreme Court noted the fear expressed by legislators that the imposition of strict liability in the field of medical drugs may not be in the interest of public policy, a concern set forth in Section 402A of the Restatement (Second) of Torts, comment k, at least to the extent of design.  The point is that there are some products that just cannot be capable of being made fully safe, such as certain drugs.  The Brown Court observed that this exception still imposes liability on a manufacturer if it failed to warn of a danger about which it should have known, of if the product was not made properly.  But the risk of stifling new medications was too great to allow strict liability on a design theory.  The Hufft court extended that principle to medical implant devices.  The court there was concerned that such products serve a salutary purpose, and that the purpose could be undone if strict liability for design could be had.  The court went on to differentiate, as had the court in Brown, devices like wheelchairs.  Specifically, the Hufft court noted that the device there at issue—a surgical implant—itself poses the potential of harm just to implant it or in its regular and intended use, whereas something like a wheelchair or walker does not.  This court believes that similar logic applies here, even though the product is neither a drug nor an implant.  There are presumably therapeutic benefits to the Facetite procedure and the device at issue here.  The public interest is furthered, not thwarted, but relieving the manufacturer from strict liability for a design defect.  Both Brown and Hufft suggested avoiding a case-by-case discussion whether to apply comment k to a particular medical device, and this court agrees.  The court believes that the strong public policies animating the Restatement, Brown, and Hufft, support defendants’ arguments here.  In particular, strict product liability for design requires that a fact finder weigh the design to determine if the risks inherent in the product are outweighed by the potential benefits and whether it could have been done a different way.  The problem is that this is done one fact-finder at a time, and always with the benefit of hindsight and in the context of a specific person.  Courts are ill-suited to make that determination in the medical field, and so are juries.  The court notes that strict liability remains a viable cause of action for a manufacturing defect or for failure to warn, as discussed below.  The court agrees with the defense and therefore the demurrer is SUSTAINED and the MJOP is GRANTED, as to the strict liability design defect claim.

 

The next question is strict liability for a manufacturing issue.  Such a defect occurs where the product differs from the manufacturer’s intended result or from other identical units from the same product line.  That means that where the product is not manufactured as designed, and if that defect was a substantial factor causing injury, then the cause of action will stand.  (Garrett v. Howmedica Osteonics Corp. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 173.)  Defendants argue that plaintiff has not identified the specific defect in the product that caused the injury.  But the court is not so sure.  Plaintiff at least inferentially does so.  Plaintiff alleges that during the procedure, plaintiff sustained a third-degree burn leaving a deep scar hole despite the advertisement that the product was safe and would not burn the skin.  Inferentially, it is the part of the product that caused the burn that contains the defect.  That is, to be sure, a (very) liberal reading of the complaint.  But the court also notes that complaints are to be read in that fashion in the context of a pleading motion, and also that plaintiff has not had a chance to do discovery yet.  Recall that the product in question is not in plaintiff’s possession, like an allegedly defective dishwasher, where plaintiff can examine the product (or have an expert do so) to identify the specific part that is not manufactured properly.  The demurrer on that theory is OVERRULED and the MJOP is DENIED.

 

The failure to warn argument also fails.  A failure to warn cause of action exists where there is a known danger, or one that should reasonably be known, but there is no warning about the danger.  (DeLeon v. Commercial Manufacturing & Supply Co. (1983) 148 Cal.App.3d 336.)  Defendants state that the purportedly required warning is unclear, but the court can reasonably infer that the warning that plaintiff alleges should have been given was a warning concerning the injuries that came to pass.  The demurrer is OVERRULED in this respect and the MJOP is DENIED.

 

The court also disagrees with the defense on the negligence cause of action. While the damages may overlap, they are different torts with somewhat different elements.  While potentially duplicative, the court believes there is enough to survive a pleading challenge.  And pleading negligence does not require great specificity.  The demurrer in this regard is OVERRULED and the MJOP is DENIED.

 

There is a problem, though, with the implied warranty cause of action.  Defendants contend that the implied warranty exists only when there is privity of contract between the parties.  The court agrees.  Privity of contract is required in California in order to recover on a theory of breach of the implied warranties for fitness and merchantability.  (Blanco v. Baxter Healthcare Corp. (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1039.)  There is no privity here.  The owner of the machine is not plaintiff.  In other words, the moving parties did not have a contract with plaintiff, so there is no privity of contract.  On this theory, the demurrer is SUSTAINED and the MJOP is GRANTED.  The court is not persuaded by the third party beneficiary theory.  First, it is not pled in the SAC.  But beyond that, plaintiff would need to allege that plaintiff was an intended beneficiary (meaning that the contracting parties intended to provide a benefit to plaintiff) and that permitting the cause of action is consistent with the objectives of the contract and the contracting parties.  The court does not believe this is adequately alleged.

 

So the demurrer is SUSTAINED IN PART AND OVERRULED IN PART, and the MJOP is GRANTED IN PART AND DENIED IN PART.  Given that this is the second amended complaint, LEAVE TO AMEND IS DENIED.

Case Number: 24SMCV03557    Hearing Date: June 24, 2026    Dept: I

This is an application for a TRO, although it is not captioned that way.  Plaintiff contends that defendant is planning to sell his unit in a nonjudicial foreclosure sale tomorrow.  He asserts that the sale is based on an illegal lien that was placed on his unit because the assessment methodology is improper.  More specifically, he contends that the lien and sale were not formally approved by the Board in an open meeting (as he states is required) and that his requests for Informal Dispute Resolution and Alternative Dispute Resolution have been rejected.  He asserts that under the law and under the CC&Rs, an IDR is required before a lien is placed on the property and an ADR is required before a foreclosure sale.

 

The court is not excited about issuing a TRO or advancing what is really a motion on the merits of the case.  On the other hand, this issue popped up this morning and the sale is purportedly set for tomorrow.  What the court would like to do is to consider this a motion for a preliminary injunction and set a briefing schedule to have it heard sooner rather than later (although the court notes that it is dark between July 20 and August 14) and have defendant agree not to sell the property in the interim.  That would obviate the need for a TRO, or at least if the court ordered the sale not to occur pending the PI hearing, it would not be a huge imposition.  The court will inquire.


Case Number: 24SMCV03904    Hearing Date: June 24, 2026    Dept: I

This is an unopposed motion for terminating sanctions brought by plaintiff.  Plaintiff served defendant Avanesyan with written discovery a year ago.  Objection-only responses were served.  Plaintiff moved to compel further and the motion was heard and granted.  Defense counsel withdrew, and so plaintiff served the court’s order on defendant directly.  There are still no responses.  Plaintiff states that this is key discovery and defendant is just ignoring it.  The court agrees that plaintiff has made out the case.

 

The court is aware that sanctions are generally done on a graduating scale.  Here, plaintiff leaps directly from monetary sanctions to terminating sanctions.  But the court also notes that defendant is just ignoring the case and the court’s order.  There is not even an opposition to this motion.  Accordingly, any proper sanction to deal with defendant’s decision just to ignore the case either must be a terminating sanction or the equivalent.  Therefore, the motion is GRANTED.  The answer will be STRICKEN and the court will enter a default.  However, the effect of this order will be STAYED for 30 days.  If, during that time, defendant brings a motion for reconsideration explaining why the case and the court’s orders are being ignored, the court will hear the motion before allowing this order to go into effect.  The court also awards sanctions in the amount of $2310 against defendant, but not former counsel, payable within 30 days.  The reason for the reduction is that no reply was needed and there is no justification for counsel’s extremely high hourly rate to draft a relatively straightforward motion.
