      I.          Background

 

This is a nuisance abatement and receivership case arising from the hazardous and dangerous condition of a property owned by Defendants Uttam Mistry and Kanta Mistry. Court-Appointed Receiver Richardson Griswold moves the court for an order distributing funds, discharging the receive, and exonerating the surety.

 

On March 25, 2026, Plaintiff City of Palmdale (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendants Uttam Mistry, Kanta Mistry (the Mistrys), and JPMorgan Chase Bank, National Association (Chase) as successor in interest to Coast Federal Bank, Federal Savings Bank, asserting a cause of action for violations of state housing law and receivership.

 

Specifically, Plaintiff alleges that real property, located at 3039 Conestoga Canyon Road, Palmdale, California 93550, Assessor’s Parcel Number 3052-053-046 (Property), owned by the Mistrys contains conditions endangering the life, limb, health, property, safety, or welfare of the public or any occupants of the property, and therefore constitutes a substandard building in violation of state law. (Compl., ¶ 40.) Plaintiff states it has been ordering the unlawful conditions to be abated for approximately 13 years prior to filing its complaint and provided the Mistrys ample opportunity to remedy the conditions. (Compl., ¶ 43.) Plaintiff brought suit against the Mistrys and Chase, which holds a recorded beneficial interest in the property, and requested that the Court appoint a receiver to take full and complete possession and control of the property to rehabilitate and bring it into full compliance with all applicable laws. (Compl., ¶¶ 5, 46.)

On December 5, 2024, Plaintiff filed an ex parte application for appointment of a receiver.

On March 25, 2026, Griswold filed the present motion for distribution of funds, discharge of receiver, and exoneration of surety.

 

No opposition has been filed by any party.

   II.          Legal Standard

Standard for Discharge of Receiver – On termination of a receivership, a receiver shall present a final account to the trial court for approval, a request for discharge, and a request for exoneration of its surety. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1184.) “No memorandum needs to be submitted in support of the motion or stipulation served and filed under (a) unless the court so orders.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1183, subd. (b).) “Notice of the motion or of the stipulation must be given to every person or entity known to the receiver to have a substantial, unsatisfied claim that will be affected by the order or stipulation, whether or not the person or entity is a party to the action or has appeared in it.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1183, subd. (c).) Finally, “[i]f any allowance of compensation for the receiver or for an attorney employed by the receiver is claimed in an account, it must state in detail what services have been performed by the receiver or the attorney and whether previous allowances have been made to the receiver or attorney and the amounts.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1183, subd. (d).)

“A surety on a bond given in an action or proceeding may at any time apply to the court for an order that the surety be released from liability on the bond.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 996.110.)

      I.          Background

 

This case arises from Defendant General Motors’ alleged violation of its statutory obligations under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act.

 

On October 19, 2023, Plaintiff Marlon Hamilton (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendant General Motors, LLC (Defendant), asserting causes of action for (1) violation of subdivision (d) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (2) violation of subdivision (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (3) violation of subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability, and (5) violation of the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act.

 

On November 23, 2023, Defendant filed its answer.

 

On February 25, 2025, Plaintiff filed a notice of conditional settlement of the entire case.

 

On July 18, 2025, pursuant to a stipulation, the case was dismissed with prejudice and the Cout retained jurisdiction pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 to enforce the terms of the settlement, including Plaintiff’s attorney’s fees, costs, expenses, and the funding of such. (Stip. And Order, p. 2:1-11, 3:8-10.)

 

On March 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed the motion for attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses.

 

On June 26, 2026, Defendant filed its opposition.

 

On July 7, 2026, Plaintiff filed his reply.

 IV.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Motion for Attorney’s Fees, Costs, and Expenses – Under¿Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), the prevailing buyer under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act is entitled to fees that were reasonably incurred: “If the buyer prevails under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the Court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).) The trial court has an obligation to award only those attorneys’ fees that are reasonable. (See PLCM Group v. Drexler¿(2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095-96; see also¿Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.) A prevailing party includes the party with a net monetary recovery in settlement of the case. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (a)(4).) 

 

The lodestar method is the primary method for¿determining¿a reasonable attorney fee award under section 1794, subdivision (d). (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc.¿(2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 818-19.) “A trial court assessing attorney fees begins with a touchstone or lodestar figure, based on the careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney involved in the presentation of the case.” (Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1321 [internal quotations omitted].)  “The reasonableness of attorney fees is within the discretion of the trial court, to be determined from a consideration of such factors as the nature of the litigation, the complexity of the issues, the experience and expertise of counsel and the amount of time involved. [Citation.] The court may also consider whether the amount requested is based upon unnecessary or duplicative work.”  (Wilkerson v. Sullivan (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 443, 448 (citations omitted).) “The basis for the trial court’s calculation must be the actual hours counsel has devoted to the case, less those that result from inefficient or duplicative use of time.” (Horsford v. Board of Trustees Of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 395.)  “The law is clear, however, that an award of attorney fees may be based on counsel’s declarations, without production of detailed time records.” (Raining Data Corp. v. Barrenechea (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1363, 1375.)

 

In setting the hourly rate for an attorney fees award, courts are entitled to consider the rate of “fees customarily charged by that attorney and others in the community for similar work.” (Bihun v. AT&T Information Systems, Inc. (1993) 13 Cal. App. 4th 976, 997 [affirming rate of $450 per hour], overruled on other grounds by Lakin v. Watkins Associated Indus. (1993) 6 Cal. 4th 644, 664; see also Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1009 [“[R]ate determinations in other cases, particularly those setting a rate for the plaintiffs’ attorney, are satisfactory evidence of the prevailing market rate.”].)

    V.          Discussion

 

Application – Plaintiff requests an order for attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses amounting to $22,597.18 pursuant to a signed California Code of Civil Procedure section 998 Offer of Compromise (998 Offer), the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act, and California Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d). (Motion, p. 9:16; Notice of Motion, p. 2:5-7.) This total is comprised of (1) $13,742.50 for attorney’s fees, (2) $4,809.88 for an enhancement of attorney’s fees by a multiplier of 1.35, (3) $2,044.80 for costs and expenses, and (4) $2,000 for anticipated attorney’s fees for review of Defendant’s opposition, preparation of a reply, and appearing at the hearing on this motion. (Motion, p. 9:12-16.)

 

a.     Entitlement to Attorney’s Fees, Costs, and Expenses

 

Here, there is no dispute that Plaintiff is the prevailing party to this litigation, nor that he was entitled to recover reasonable attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses incurred in litigation. The 998 Offer establishes that Plaintiff accepted the offer, in relevant part, pursuant to paragraph 3, which states that as an alternative to Defendant’s offer to pay Plaintiff’s attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses in the amount of $20,000, “at Plaintiff’s election, Plaintiff’s attorney’s fees, expenses and costs that have been reasonably incurred pursuant to California Civil Code § 1794(d) may be determined by the Court via noticed motion. For purposes of any such motion, GM will agree that Plaintiff is the prevailing party.” (Stip. and Order dated July 18, 2025, Ex. 1.) The signature of Plaintiff is dated February 24, 2025. (Ibid.) Plaintiff admits to accepting this offer on February 25, 2025. (Motion, p. 3:17-18.)

 

In any event, it is undisputed that this 998 Offer was accepted by Plaintiff, and it is undisputed that Plaintiff elected for his attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses to be determined by the Court on noticed motion pursuant to Section 1794, subdivision (d). Where an accepted 998 Offer designates the plaintiff as the prevailing party and specifically delegated determination of fees to the Court, it thereby entitles the plaintiff to bring the subject motion. (Engle v. Copenbarger & Copenbarger, LLP (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 165, 168-69.)

 

b.     Timeliness of Plaintiff’s Motion

 

Before the Court considers the motion on the merits, the Court must address the timeliness of the present motion. A motion for attorneys' fees must be served and filed within the time for the filing of a notice of appeal in a civil case. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1702(b)(1).) The time for the filing of a notice of appeal in an unlimited action is 60 days if a "Notice of Entry" of Judgment was served by the court clerk or a party to the action, or within 180 days of entry of judgment or dismissal. (Id., rule 8.104.)

 

The attorney fee motion deadline is triggered by the "conclusion of the litigation" which is marked by entry of judgment or dismissal in the trial court. (Catlin Ins. Co., Inc. v. Danko Meredith Law Firm, Inc. (2022) 73 Cal.App.5th 764, 781; see also Cal. Rules of Court, rules 3.1702(b)(1), 8.104(a)(1)(A)-(C).) The clock starts to run from “either the service of notice of entry of judgment or dismissal (starting a 60-day clock), or if no such notice is given, the entry of judgment or dismissal (starting a 180-day clock)." (Catlin Ins. Co., supra, 73 Cal.App.5th at 781.) Accordingly, when an order of dismissal is entered and no notice of dismissal is served, a fee motion, like an appeal, must be filed within 180 days after the date of dismissal. (See Sanabria v. Embrey (2024) 92 Cal.App.4th 422, 426-429.)

 

Defendant argues that because the Court dismissed this case on July 18, 2025, this is the latest date on which the clock could begin to run. Plaintiff had 180 days from this date to file his motion for attorney’s fees, with a deadline of January 14, 2026. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion is untimely. Defendant also cites in support Hatlevig v. General Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644.

 

The Court is not persuaded that the motion is untimely under either Rule of Court 3.1702(b)(1). The parties here expressly agred that the Court “shall retain jurisdiction pursuant to California Civil Procedure 664.6 to enforce settlement including Plaintiff's attorneys' fees, costs, and expenses and funding of such.” (Joint Stipulation to Dismiss with Prejudice and Retain Jurisdiction Pursuant to CCP 664.6, and Order thereon, of July 17, 2025). The parties’ agreement included that “GM will also pay Plaintiff’s attorney fees, expenses, and costs in the amount of $20,000” or, in the alternative, that Plaintiff could bring a noticed motion for his fees and costs and Plaintiff would be deemed the prevailing party for the purposes of such motion. (Id., Ex. 1 thereto.) These promises by GM, agreed to by Plaintiff, were without qualification as to when a motion for attorney’s fees should be filed. The Court finds that the parties’ unqualified agreement that Plaintiff was entitled to fees, could bring a fees motion, would be deemed the prevailing party for the purposes of the fee motion, and that the Court would retain jurisdiction to enforce these promises, are binding promises to be enforced.

 

In any event, California Rule of Court rule 3.1702(d) provides that a trial judge may extend the time for filing the motion for good cause, even in the absence of a stipulation or for a longer period than allowed by stipulation. Rule 3.1702(d) permits the court to extend the deadline liberally and retroactively. (See Robinson v. U-Haul Co. of California (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 304, 326 [“A court may grant a request for extension of time to file a motion for attorney's fees even if the motion is not filed until after the deadline for filing an attorney's fees motion under rule 3.1702. Even a claim of inadvertence, if it is not prejudicial, may constitute good cause for a late filing.”].)

 

Here, the Court finds good cause to extend the time for filing the motion for two reasons. First, the award of attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses in an amount to be determined by the Court was expressly contemplated and agreed to by both parties, and there is no prejudice to Defendant, who offered Plaintiff this route, in upholding the clear terms of the agreement. Second, while Plaintiff filed the present motion after the ruling in Hatlevig v. General Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644, the Court notes that the motion was filed within one month of the Court’s decision, which is new authority on the issue.

 

Thus, the Court retroactively extends the deadline for filing the present motion and will hear the motion on the merits.

 

c.      Reasonableness of Attorney’s Fees Amount

 

As discussed, the standard method for calculating reasonable attorney’s fees is by multiplying the reasonable hourly rate of the attorneys by the reasonable number of hours billed for the work performed.

 

                                                  i.     Reasonableness of Attorneys’ Hourly Rates

 

Plaintiff’s counsel argues that their rates are reasonable and consistent with rates regularly approved by courts throughout California for similar lemon law litigation, citing to a plethora of superior and district court cases on the issue. (Motion, p. 6:7-8:2.) The Declaration of Dhara Chandy sets forth the autobiographies and hourly rates of ten timekeepers who worked on the present case. Their roles and hourly rates are as follows:

 

1.     Angel Barker, attorney, admitted to California State Bar in 2003, $595/hr.

2.     James Carroll, attorney, admitted to California State Bar in 2006, $595/hr.

3.     Tionna Carvalho, attorney, admitted to California State Bar in 2014, $570/hr. in 2023, $595/hr. in 2024, $650/hr. as partner in 2025.

4.     Dhara Chandy, attorney, admitted to California State Bar in November 2025, $375/hr.

5.     Shelby Clark, attorney, admitted to California State Bar in 1999, $535/hr.

6.     Ian McCallister, attorney, admitted to California State Bar in 1998, $595/hr. in 2024, $610 in 2025.

7.     Maro Orte, attorney, admitted to California State Bar in 2015, $525/hr.

8.     Michael Shwartz, attorney, admitted to California State Bar in 1998, $700/hr.

9.     Brian Tan, attorney, admitted to California State Bar in 2018, $450/hr. in 2023, $475/hr. in 2025.

10.  Sanam Vaziri, attorney, admitted to California State Bar in 1995, $610/hr.

 

(Chandy Decl., p. 5-11, ¶¶ A-J.)

 

Defendant does not argue that any of these rates are unreasonable or should be reduced. As the issue of rate reasonableness is uncontested, the Court accepts the rates for the purposes of this motion. This ruling should not be construed by any other court that this Court has found, after a disputed adjudication, that Plaintiff’s counsel’s rates are reasonable.

 

Having reviewed the rates, the Court is not persuaded that Mr. Schwartz’s $700/hr. rate is reasonable in light of the facts that he is not a named partner with SLP and that he, like Mr. McCallister, has 28 years of experience. No reasoning is offered for why Mr. Schwartz’s rate is beyond that of Ms. Carvalho, a named partner, Mr. McCallister, an attorney with the same years of experience, or Ms. Vaziri, who has more experience.

 

Accordingly, Mr. Schwartz’s rate is reduced to $610/hr. The rates requested for the nine other billing attorneys are acepted as requested.

 

                                                ii.     Reasonableness of Hours Billed

 

Plaintiff’s counsel argues they reasonably spent 29.4 hours in prosecuting this action from inception through settlement, with the work allocated efficiently among attorneys and staff, with a majority of the hours concentrated among a core group of attorneys who collectively billed a substantial percentage of the total hours worked. (Motion, p. 7:6-9.) Plaintiff further contends all work performed on the case until settlement was necessary, as Defendant actively proceeded with defending the case and refused to offer settlement until the eve of trial, forcing Plaintiff to continue with litigation. (Motion, p. 7:23-25.)

 

In opposition, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s request should be denied or reduced due to excessive staffing of ten billing attorneys on a standard lemon law action, resulting in excessive time on routine tasks and avoidable duplication, and to prevent Plaintiff’s counsel from benefitting from the resulting inefficiencies. (Opp., p. 2:1-4.) Defendant further notes that it provided Plaintiff an opportunity to avoid motion practice by offering to cover fees, costs, and expenses in the amount of $20,000, but Plaintiff instead elected to file the instant motion, seeking fees in excess of Defendant’s $20,000 offer. (Opp., p. 5-9.) Defendant further contends the fees should be significantly reduced due to Plaintiff’s counsel’s reliance on standard templates and recycled trial documents, which Defendant argues do not materially vary from case to case, and some of which were ultimately never filed in this action, providing examples of a complaint, discovery responses and requests, an ESI letter, an NOD, motions in limine, trial documents, and a fee motion from other cases Plaintiff has filed against Defendant for comparison. (Opp., p. 2:21-22, 2:27-28, 3:4-5, 3:8-9, 3:26-4:2; Quezada Decl., ¶¶ 3, 5-6, 8, 15-16, 17, 19.) Defendant argues Plaintiff’s counsel’s bill padding and requests for preparing the same templates repeatedly in each case constitute unjust enrichment. (Opp., p. 6:26-7:5.) Finally, Defendant notes significant periods of inactivity in this case, reflected in the lack of billing entries. (Quezada Decl., ¶¶ 4, 7, 12, 17.)

 

Defendant specifically objects to the following charges and requests the specified reduction:

 

1.     Drafting templated complaint – 1.0 hour ($590) billed on October 18, 2023 – should be reduced by 0.75 hours ($513.75) (Opp., p. 7:12-15);

2.     Templated discovery responses – 1.0 hour ($450) and 1.5 hours ($892.50) billed on December 14, 2023 – should be reduced by 2.0 hours ($1,117.50) (Opp., p. 7:16-20);

3.     Templated discovery requests, ESI Letter, and Notice of Deposition – 4.4 hours ($2,234) billed on March 11, 2024 and January 17, 2025) – should be reduced by 3.4 hours ($1,699.00) (Opp., p. 7:21-25);

4.     Templated motions in limine – 2.4 hours ($1,464) billed on February 3, 2025 – should be reduced by 1.9 hours ($1,159.00) (Opp., p. 7:26-8:2);

5.     Templated trial documents – 6.1 hours ($3,202.50) billed on February 18, 2025 – should be reduced by 5.1 hours ($2,677.50) (Opp., p. 8:3-6);

6.     Templated fee motion, reply, and attending hearing – 4.0 hours ($2,332) billed on March 6, 2026, anticipated fees ($2,000) – should be reduced by 1.0 hour ($427) and the anticipated fees should be stricken (Opp., p. 8:7-14);

7.     Excessive review and revision – 3.3 hours ($1,733.50) billed throughout the case – some entries are block-billed and Defendant requests a reduction of 1.65 hours ($866.75) (Opp., p. 8:19-23.)

 

On reply, Plaintiff argues that Defendant failed to identify a single duplicative entry or excessive billing resulting from alleged overstaffing of the case. (Reply, p. 2:15-15, 4:22-5:1.) Plaintiff also contends that Defendant’s template and bill-padding arguments have been argued and rejected against Defendant in other courts. (Reply, 3:18-22.)

 

The prevailing party has the burden of showing that the requested attorney fees were "reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation, and were reasonable in amount." (Robertson, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at 817.) The party seeking attorney fees "'is not necessarily entitled to compensation for the value of attorney services according to [his] own notion or to the full extent claimed by [him].'" (Levy v. Toyota Motor Sales, USA, Inc. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 807, 816.) Therefore, if the "time expended or the monetary charge being made for the time expended are not reasonable under all the circumstances, then the court must take this into account and award attorney fees in a lesser amount." (Nightingale v. Hyundai Motor America (1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 99, 104.)

 

A court may "reduce a fee award based on its reasonable determination that a routine, noncomplex case was overstaffed to a degree that significant inefficiencies and inflated fees resulted."(Morris v. Hyundai Motor America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 39.) It is also appropriate to reduce an award based on inefficient or duplicative efforts. (Id. at p. 38.) However, the analysis must be "reasonably specific" and cannot rely on general notions of fairness or the Court’s opinion of the attorneys or importance of a case. (Kerkeles. City of San Jose (2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 88, 102.) Further, in conducting the analysis, courts are not permitted to tie any reductions in the fee award to some proportion of the buyer's damages recovery. (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc.(2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 28, 39.) The detailed billing records Plaintiffs have submitted to validate the billed hours Plaintiffs request "are entitled to credence in the absence of a clear indication the records are erroneous." (Horsford, supra, 132 Cal.App.4th at p. 396.) On presentation of a detailed fee bill, accompanied by a sworn affidavit as to the accuracy of the fee bill, the bill is presumed credible, and the court must use these records as a starting point for its lodestar determination. (Id at 396-97.)

 

Here, the Court finds most of Plaintiff’s counsel’s billing reasonable. From October 18, 2023 through March 6, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel billed a total of 24.9 hours of work, including drafting the complaint and filing forms, drafting discovery responses and requests, drafting demands for expert witness designation and declaration, meeting and conferring with Defense counsel, drafting objections to deposition notice and notice to appear at trial, drafting eight motions in limine, reviewing correspondence and offers of settlement from Defendant and communicating with Plaintiff, and reviewing and finalizing the final settlement, notice of settlement, and drafting the present fee motion. (Chandy Decl., Ex. 1, p. 1.)

 

As Plaintiff argues, while reliance on templated drafts is common and can significantly reduce an attorney’s work, counsel is still expected to review these templates and modify them accordingly, considering the specific legal issues raised, the facts of the case, and conducting further research where necessary. For example, Defendant objects to 2.5 hours billed by two attorneys, Mr. Tan and Mr. Carroll, for drafting, finalizing, reviewing, and revising Plaintiff’s discovery responses. However, while the Court acknowledges these responses were drafted off templates, regularly used in such actions as conceded by both parties, the Court finds 2.5 hours reasonable for drafting 98 pages of discovery responses for at least four requests propounded by Defendant, even if said responses were built off a substantially similar template. (See Quezada Decl., Ex. C.)

 

Furthermore, while staffing a routine, simple Song-Beverly action with over ten attorneys may result in inefficiency, unreasonable billing, duplicative work, and excessive review, the detailed billing records provided by Plaintiff do not identify excessive time for the tasks completed, nor does the Court find that these entries are duplicative. Defendant similarly fails to identify duplication within the records provided. Defendant’s offer to pay $20,000 to Plaintiff for fees, costs, and expenses is an unconvincing argument. Had Defendant wished to avoid the present motion, Defendant could have easily not offered this route to Plaintiff. However, Plaintiff was given the express option by Defendant in the 998 Offer to elect to have his fees, costs, and expenses determined by the Court. Moreover, the Court fails to see merit in the argument that periods of inactivity in this case bear weight on the reasonableness of the fees billed, considering no billing entries were submitted for these inactive periods during litigation.

 

Finally, the inconsistencies Defendant notes do not warrant a denial of the reasonable fees. For example, Defendant notes that the Chandy Declaration “identifies a January 11, 2023 task for drafting a Case Management Statement and Notice of Posting Jury Fees, even though this action was not filed until October 19, 2023. (Chandy Decl., ¶¶ 2, 7, 10.) Plaintiff’s moving memorandum also states that Plaintiff accepted GM’s second 998 offer on February 25, 2025, while other materials identify February 24, 2025. These discrepancies reinforce that Counsel’s motion was assembled from templates and was not carefully reviewed before filing.” (Opp., p. 4:17-22.) On the contrary, these discrepancies are clearly clerical, typographical errors. The billing records provided and the record of the case establishes that the drafting of the CMS and notice of posting jury fees was billed on January 11, 2024, and filed with the Court the same day. The one day discrepancy related to the acceptance of the 998 Offer similarly appears to be a typographical error. In any event, as discussed above, the Court finds the time expended in drafting the present fee motion and conforming it to the facts and specific legal issues in this case reasonable.

 

However, the Court finds that Maro Orte’s billing entry on February 18, 2025 for “[c]ollect[ing] and arrang[ing] trial documents including MILs to create trial binders” unreasonable. Maro billed for two hours of time at $525, for a total of $1,050. The Court notes that this is not substantive legal work. While preparation of trial binders is necessary for an upcoming trial, this task is secretarial in nature and does not warrant attorney, or even paralegal, billing. As the United States Supreme Court has held: “Of course, purely clerical or secretarial tasks should not be billed at a paralegal rate, regardless of who performs them. What the court in Johnson v. Georgia Highway Express, Inc., 488 F. 2d 714, 717 (1974), said in regard to the work of attorneys is applicable by analogy to paralegals: ‘It is appropriate to distinguish between legal work, in the strict sense, and investigation, clerical work, compilation of facts and statistics and other work which can often be accomplished by non-lawyers but which a lawyer may do because he has no other help available. Such non-legal work may command a lesser rate. Its dollar value is not enhanced just because a lawyer does it.’” (Missouri v. Jenkins (1989) 491 U.S. 274, 288 n.10.) Thus, while the Court finds the time spent on this task to be reasonable absent evidence to the contrary, Plaintiff’s counsel has made clear they have ample non-attorney staff to whom they are able to delegate such work, and billing this task as an attorney fee is unwarranted. Accordingly, the Court strikes this entry as an unreasonably billed task.

 

                                              iii.     Lodestar Calculation

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court calculates the amount of reasonable attorney’s fees Plaintiff is entitled to recover by multiplying the number of reasonably billed hours for work on this case by the reasonable hourly rate of the billing attorneys as discussed above.

 

From Plaintiff’s $13,742.50 attorney’s fee request, the Court strikes $1,050 for Orte’s billing entry for collection and arrangement of trial documents for preparation of trial binders. The Court further reduces the request by $36.00 to account for the reduced rate for Mr. Schwartz from $700/hour to $610/hour for the February 19, 2025 billing entry for 0.4 hour for preparing for and attending the final status conference and drafting the hearing outcome memorandum submitted.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff is entitled to recover $12,656.50 in reasonably incurred attorney’s fees.

 

d.     Anticipated Fees

 

Plaintiff’s counsel requests $2,000 in anticipated fees in reading and responding to Defendant’s opposition and appearing at the hearing on this matter. (Motion, p. 9:15-16.)

 

Defendant argues in opposition that it should take Ms. Baker no more than half an hour to review the opposition, no more than half an hour to prepare a templated reply brief, and argues the hearing should take no more than one hour. (Opp., p. 8:10-13.) Defendant asks that the court strike the $2,000 for anticipated fees entirely. (Opp., p. 9:1-2.)

 

In reply, Plaintiff submits the Declaration of Angel M. Baker, which states that Ms. Baker spent 0.5 hours reviewing Defendant’s opposition and Plaintiff’s reply and is not seeking reimbursement for time reviewing, but anticipates spending at least 2.0 hours to prepare for (1.0 hour) and attend (1.0 hour) the hearing. (Baker Decl., ¶ 4.) Ms. Baker states she bills at the hourly rate of $650/hr. and was admitted to the California State Bar in 2003. The Court finds this rate excessive in light of the billing rates discussed above for attorneys with the same or more experience without any justification. Thus, the Court reduces Ms. Baker’s hourly rate to $595/hr. for work performed on this case.

 

Ms. Baker states that Aarushi Khanna, a law clerk who is not a member of the California State Bar, spent 2.0 hours drafting the reply brief and has an hourly rate of $360/hr. (Baker Decl., ¶ 3.) To support this rate, Ms. Baker cites numerous court orders approving Ms. Khanna’s hourly rate in similar cases. (Baker Decl., ¶ 3.) Paralegal fees are recoverable as attorney’s fees. (Guinn v. Dotson (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 262, 269.) However, the Court is unpersuaded by other courts’ approvals of Ms. Khanna’s rates in this matter as evidence of market rates for other law clerks or paralegals in the community. While another court may have determined Ms. Khanna’s rates are reasonable, the proper standard is reasonable compared to the community, i.e., legal professionals in the community other than those appearing in this case. (See Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132 [“the reasonable value of attorney services is variously defined as the ‘hourly amount to which attorneys of like skill in the area would typically be entitled.’].) The Court finds that this rate is not reasonable for a non-attorney who is not licensed to practice. The Court thus reduces Ms. Khanna’s rate to $200/hr.

 

The Court finds the anticipated time in preparing for and attending the hearing reasonable at 2.0 hours. The Court also finds 2.0 hours in preparing the reply reasonable, given the length and substantive content directly addressing Defendant’s arguments raised in opposition.

 

Accordingly, the Court grants Plaintiff reasonable anticipated fees in the amount of $400 for preparation of the reply (2.0 hours at $200/hr. for Ms. Khanna) plus $1,190 for preparation for and attendance of the hearing on this motion (2.0 hours at $595/hr. for Ms. Baker), for a total of $1,590.

 

e.      Fee Multiplier

 

Plaintiff requests a fee multiplier of 1.35 to the lodestar amount given the result obtained, the substantial risks undertaken, and the delay in Defendant’s payment of fees. (Motion, p. 4:1-3.)

 

Once the Court has determined¿an appropriate lodestar¿figure, the court may then determine whether that figure should be adjusted with a positive or negative multiplier. (Graham v. DaimlerChrysler Corp.¿(2005) 34 Cal.4th 553, 582.) Whether a multiplier or negative multiple is appropriate depends on several factors, including (1) the risks presented by the litigation; (2) the novelty and difficulty of the legal and factual issues involved; (3) the results obtained on behalf of the plaintiff; (4) the skill exhibited by counsel; (5) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys; and (6) the contingent nature of the fee award based on the uncertainty of prevailing on the merits and of establishing eligibility for the award. (Consumer Privacy Cases¿(2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 545, 556; Robertson, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at 819.) “The ‘results obtained’ factor can properly be used to enhance a lodestar calculation where [1] an exceptional effort produced [2] an exceptional benefit.”¿(Graham supra, 34 Cal.4th at 582.)¿ 

 

Plaintiff argues the fee multiplier is warranted for the three grounds stated above. First, Plaintiff argues his counsel obtained an excellent result, providing Plaintiff with full compensation for the defective vehicle and vindicating his rights under the Song-Beverly Act, achieved only after his counsel invested substantial time and resources preparing for trial as a result of Defendant’s failure to offer reasonable settlement until the eve of trial. (Motion, p. 8:8-16.) Second, Plaintiff’s counsel undertook representation on a contingency basis in this case, assuming the risk of no recovery if the case was unsuccessful, and that this risk was substantial as Defendant vigorously defended its case and refused to make a settlement offer until the eve of trial. (Motion, p. 8:21-24.) Third, Plaintiff’s counsel has not been compensated for the work performed in this case, which spanned from October 2023 through February 2025, and this delay in payment presents a significant opportunity cost to counsel, who otherwise could have invested their time in matters providing immediate compensation. (Motion, p. 9:4-8.)

 

Defendant opposes, arguing that this case does not warrant a fee multiplier given the 28 months of inactivity, the lack of novelty or difficulty in the legal and factual questions raised by the case, particularly because Song-Beverly cases tend to raise virtually identical issues, and that no argument has been or could be made that counsel was precluded from taking on other cases, particularly so given that Plaintiff’s counsel has over 1,300 active Song-Beverly cases pending against Defendant alone. (Opp., p. 9:4-6, 17-20, 21-26.) Defendant also argues that Plaintiff’s counsel’s hourly rates already reflect the contingent nature of their work, and that it is impermissible to seek a multiplier for contingency risk and factoring in the contingency risk to the hourly fees of the attorneys who billed on the case, as it results in an unfair double counting and unreasonable fee award. (Opp., p. 9:27-10:13; see Robertson, supra 144 Cal.App.4th at 822; see also Horsford, supra, 132 Cal.App.4th at 395.) Defendant also argues that the contingency risk in this case was minimal because Code of Civil Procedure section 1794 mandates fee-shifting of attorney’s fees and costs to a prevailing party. (Opp., p. 10:14-15.) Finally, Defendant argues that in the absence of any other factor leaning in favor of a fee multiplier, Plaintiff’s position, in essence, is that every case taken on a contingency warrants a multiplier which is in clear opposition of the law, which provides that fee enhancements are entirely discretionary. (Opp., p. 10:26-11:2.)

 

The Court finds that a multiplier is not warranted in this case. While Plaintiff received an excellent result in settlement, and while Plaintiff’s counsel took this case on contingency, the Court does not find that this case warrants a multiplier. This is a standard Lemon Law action, and based on the record, the moving and opposition papers filed for the present motion, and the relatively minimal time spent by Plaintiff’s counsel on this case (24.9 hours for a case filed in October, 2023 and settled in February, 2025 on the eve of trial), the Court does not find the novelty or difficulty of the legal and factual issues involved, nor the skill exhibited by Plaintiff’s counsel, rising to the level of warranting a multiplier. Furthermore, there is no evidence presented that this case precluded Plaintiff’s counsel from taking on other employment.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s request for a fee multiplier is DENIED.

 

f.      Costs and Expenses

 

Plaintiff requests costs and expenses totaling $2,044.80 in costs and expenses incurred in this matter.

 

"Except as otherwise expressly provided by statute, a prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs in any action or proceeding." (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (b).) "Allowable costs shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation." (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(2).) "If the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary." (Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal. App. 4th 761, 774.) "On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs." (Ibid.)

 

"A prevailing party who claims costs must serve and file a memorandum of costs within 15 days after the date of service of the notice of entry of judgment or dismissal by the clerk under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5 or the date of service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, or within 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is first. The memorandum of costs must be verified by a statement of the party, attorney, or agent that to the best of his or her knowledge the items of cost are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case." (Cal. Rules Ct., rule 3.1700, subd. (a)(1).)

 

In opposition to Plaintiff’s request, Defendant argues this request should be denied outright as Plaintiff failed to file a Memorandum of Costs and has not provided any evidence to support the claimed costs beyond a table included with counsel’s billing records. (Opp., p. 12:10-12.) Defendant further argues that Plaintiff is undoubtably seeking costs neither allowable nor reasonably necessary to litigation, and without a more detailed description of the costs and documentation to establish the costs were billed to the correct case, it is impossible to determine whether these costs were actually incurred in this case and whether they are reasonable or necessary. (Opp., p. 12:14-19.)

 

The Court is not convinced by Defendant’s arguments. While Plaintiff failed to file a Memorandum of Costs as required by Rules of Court, rule 3.1700, subdivision (a)(1), for the reasons stated above, namely that Plaintiff’s recovery of these costs were expressly contemplated for recovery in Defendant’s 998 Offer, the Court will consider the costs as offered in Exhibit 1 of the Chandy Declaration, which is signed by Dhara Chandy under the penalty of perjury as being true and correct. (Chandy Decl., ¶ 35, p. 22:6-10.) Furthermore, the costs and expenses table provided by Plaintiff state the date the cost was incurred, who it was paid to, the total amount, and a detailed description of the purpose of the cost. Defendant is incorrect that further supporting documentation is needed, and Defendant fails to point to a single entry which is neither allowable nor reasonably necessary. In reviewing these costs and expenses, they all seem to be related to the service, delivery, or filing of a specified document, which upon review of the record, appears to have been actually served or filed on or about the same date the cost was incurred, or otherwise directly relate to the case. Furthermore, the costs and expenses sought coincide with the active work being done as delineated on the time entries provided by Plaintiff. For example, on February 18, 2025, Maro Orte was preparing exhibit binders for trial. On March 3, 2025, Plaintiff’s counsel incurred a charge for $108.02 for “Print B&W, tabs cardstock, and binder.” (Chandy Decl., Ex. 1, p. 2-3.) The Court sees no inconsistencies and no inappropriate, unreasonable, or unrelated charges which are facially improper.

 

In the absence of evidence or argument to the contrary, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s request of $2,044.80 in costs is reasonable and recoverable.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s request for attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses incurred in prosecuting this litigation is GRANTED in the total amount of $16,291.30, consisting of $12,656.50 in attorney’s fees, $1,590 in fees incurred in drafting the reply ($400) and anticipated fees related to this motion ($1,190). and $2,044.80 in costs and expenses.

 

-----

 

 VI.          Conclusion

 

Plaintiff Marlon Hamilton’s Motion for Attorneys’ Fees, Costs, and Expenses is GRANTED in the total amount of $16,291.30.

On September 9, 2025, Defendant specially appeared and moved to quash the service of summons.

On December 9, 2025, the Order granting the motion to quash was filed.

   II.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Motion to Quash Service  – A defendant who contends service of summons is defective may, on or before the last day its time to plead or within any further time that the court may allow for good cause, may move to quash service of summons on the ground of lack of personal jurisdiction. (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a)(1).)

 

“[C]ompliance with the statutory procedures for service of process is essential to establish personal jurisdiction. [Citation.]” (Dill v. Berquist Construction Co.  (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1444.) “When a defendant challenges the court’s personal jurisdiction on the ground of improper service of process, ‘the burden is on the plaintiff to prove the existence of jurisdiction by proving the facts requisite to an effective service.’” (Summers v. McClanahan (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 403, 413 (internal citations omitted).) “The filing of a proof of service creates a rebuttable presumption that the service was proper. However, the presumption arises only if the proof of service complies with the applicable statutory requirements.” (Floveyor Internat., Ltd. v. Superior Court (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 789, 795.)

 

“‘On a motion to quash service of summons, the plaintiff bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that all jurisdictional criteria are met. [Citations.] The burden must be met by competent evidence in affidavits and authenticated documents; an unverified complaint may not be considered as supplying the necessary facts.’ [Citation.]” (Brown v. Garcia (2017) 17 Cal.App.5th 1198, 1203; see also Sheard v. Superior Court (1974) 40 Cal.App.3d 207, 211 [“[W]here a defendant properly moves to quash service of summons the burden is on the plaintiff to prove facts requisite to the effective service.”].)

 

-----

 

 III.          Discussion

 

Application – SISC moves to quash service of summons on the grounds that the proof of service file does not establish that service was proper, that service was not proper under California Code of Civil Procedure section 416.50, and that Plaintiff is improperly serving SISC in an attempt to serve Vista San Gabriel Elementary School (Vista).

 

For service to be valid, it must (1) be made by a non-party to the case who is over the age of 18 (Code Civ. Proc., § 414.40), (2) be made through a permitted method of service as delineated in California Code of Civil Procedure §§ 415.10 – 415.95, and (3) must be made on a person eligible to receive service of process for the served defendant (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 416.10-416.90).

 

The proof of service filed March 12, 2026 (POS) states that the pleadings were served on SISC on February 23, 2026 via personal service on “Lucy Perez, HR Tech – Person Authorized to Accept Service of Process” at 18020 E Ave O, Palmdale, CA 93591. (POS, ¶¶ 2-5.) The POS also states that the service was made pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 416.50, which governs service of summons on a public entity. Section 416.50 states, in relevant part, that a summons may be served on a public entity by delivering a copy of the summons and complaint to the clerk, secretary, president, presiding officer, or other head of its governing body.

 

Here, SISC argues that service was improper. (Motion, p. 6:5-8.) SISC notes that Plaintiff is aware that Dr. Ostash of SISC is the only individual eligible to receive service of process on behalf of SISC, but that the POS reflects service on a Lucy Perez, who is not even an employee with SISC. (Motion, p. 6:5-7.) Furthermore, the address where service occurred is apparently not an office of SISC. (Motion, p. 6:7-8.) Additionally, Defendant states that SISC is a third party administrator for Joint Powers Association, handling an insurance pool for school districts, and that SISC is not a school district, is not a school, and does not own, operate, or manage Wilsona School District or Vista. (Motion, p.6:26-7:1; Bourget Decl., ¶ 3.) Dr. Ostash states he is the Chief Executive Officer of SISC, that Lucy Perez is not and never has been an employee or agent of SISC, that 18020 E. Ave. O is not an address of SISC, and he has not and never has been served process for this case, appointed or authorized any person to receive process for SISC on his behalf, and that SISC has not appointed or authorized any other individuals to receive process for SISC. (Ostash Decl., ¶¶ 1, 3-4.)

 

SISC also provides the Declarations of Katerina J. Valencia and Louis R. Dumont, detailing the efforts SISC has taken to contact Plaintiff’s counsel and correct the improper service and to inform Plaintiffs that they were not serving the proper defendant in this case. Dumont’s Declaration discusses three separate improper service attempts and Dumont’s inability to rectify the issues addressed in the present motion with Plaintiff’s counsel directly. (Dumont Decl., ¶¶ 2-10.)

 

As stated above, Plaintiff bears the burden of proof to establish that service was proper. Plaintiff has not filed any opposition to this motion and has failed to establish that the proper individual was served with process.

 

Accordingly, SISC’s motion to quash service of summons is GRANTED.

 IV.          Conclusion

 

Defendant Self-Insured Schools of California’s Motion to Quash Service of Summons is GRANTED.

      I.          Background

 

This is a breach of the implied warranty of habitability case arising from Plaintiff Casey Michell-Collier’s leasing of a bedroom on the property owned by Defendant Dillon Sandidge.

 

On August 18, 2025, Plaintiff Casey Mitchell-Collier (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendant Dillon Sandidge (Defendant).

Specifically, Plaintiff alleges that from April 1, 2021 through July 31, 2025, he leased a master bedroom and private bathroom at a property owned and leased by Defendant, located at 43141 Hampton Street, Lancaster, California (the Property). (FAC, ¶¶ 1-2, 5.) Upon moving in, Plaintiff claims he had no knowledge of any conditions of habitability, and alleges that Defendant concealed the existence of a long-standing plumbing defect in the bathroom and failed to disclose any prior leaks, repairs, or damage. (FAC, ¶ 5.) On April 3, 2025, Plaintiff was allegedly informed by a contractor that there was black mold in the bathroom as a result from the plumbing leak which had existed for years and was known to Defendant, who failed to take steps to repair until 2025. (FAC, ¶¶ 9-10. 12.)

 

On October 17, 2025, Defendant filed a demurrer to the first amended complaint and a motion to strike portions of the first amended complaint and as to all four individual causes of action.

 

The hearing on Defendant’s demurrer is set for hearing on August 4, 2026.

 

On February 26, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion to deem specified matters admitted and for monetary sanctions.

 

On April 23, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition.

 

On June 3, 2026, Defendant filed a notice of errata re the opposition to correct the hearing date.

 

No reply has been filed.

 

-----

 

   II.          Legal Standard

Standard for Motion to Deem Requests for Admission Admitted – A party must respond to requests for admission within 30 days after service of such requests. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.250, subd. (a).)When a party fails to timely respond, the propounding party may move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the request be deemed admitted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (b).) An untimely responding party waives all objections, including privilege, unless they subsequently serve responses in substantial compliance with the Civil Discovery Act, and they demonstrate that their failure is the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a); § 2033.280, subd. (a).)

For untimely responses to requests for admission, the Court shall deem the requests for admission admitted unless the responding party serves a proposed code compliant response prior to the hearing. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).)

 

-----

 

 III.          Discussion

 

Application – Plaintiff moves to deem the truth of the matters contained in Plaintiff’s Requests for Admissions numbers 7, 9, 14-18, 23, 29-30, 32, and 36 admitted. Plaintiff states that on November 24, 2025, he served his Requests for Admissions, Set One, on Defendant by electronic mail. (Motion, p. 2:8-10; Mitchell-Collier Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. 1.)

 

On December 24, 2025, Defendant’s counsel served only objections due to a power outage which prevented access to the finalized responses, representing that complete responses would be served on or before December 30, 2025. (Motion, p. 2:11-13; Mitchell-Collier Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 2.) On December 30, 2025, Defendant served the amended responses, which omitted the Requests for Admissions numbers 7, 9, 14-18, 23, 29-30, 32, 34, and 36. (Motion, p. 2:15-18; Mitchell-Collier Decl., ¶¶ 5-6, Ex. 3.) Plaintiff requests that these Requests for Admissions be deemed admitted for Defendant’s failure to provide a response.

 

In opposition, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s motion is baseless because (1) Defendant served timely o on December 24, 2025, (2) Defendant served a written verified response which substantially complied with the procedural requirements of California Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.010 on December 30, 2025, (3) Plaintiff failed to meet and confer regarding Defendant’s two responses served in December, and (4) Plaintiff’s discovery request does not include a proof of service. (Opp., p. 2:19-28.)

 

Preliminarily, the Court notes that in neither Defendant’s December 24, 2025 objections nor in his December 30, 2025 amended response did he include any objection, denial, admission, or statement of inability to admit or deny for requests 7, 9, 14-18, 23, 29-30, 32, or 36. No reason for this omission is offered. The Court also notes that a proof of service of Plaintiff’s Requests for Admissions was filed on November 25, 2025 and Defendant’s response on the deadline, December 24, 2025, indicates that Defendant was, in fact, served. Finally, Defendant is incorrect that Plaintiff’s motion is predicated on California Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.290, as Plaintiff’s motion is made pursuant to only sections 2033.280 and 2033.030, which do not require a meet and confer effort. (Opp., p. 2:19.)

 

A party who propounds requests for admissions may generally initiate three types of motions: “(1) motions to deem RFAs admitted based upon the responding party's failure to serve any responses at all in a timely fashion (§ 2033.280, subd. (b)); (2) motions to compel further responses to RFAs where the responses are claimed to be inadequate or the objections improper (§ 2033.290, subd. (a)); and (3) motions to deem responses admitted and/or for sanctions based upon the responding party's disobedience of an order compelling further responses (id., subd. (e)).” (St. Mary v. Superior Court (2014) 223 Cal. App. 4th 762, 777.)

 

Here, Plaintiff’s motion falls within the first category. However, Defendant did not fail to serve any responses at all in a timely fashion. Defendant served unverified objections to 24 of the 36 requests, admitting, denying, or stating insufficient information or knowledge to admit or deny on December 24, 2025, excluding requests 7, 9, 14-18, 23, 29, 30, 32, and 36. However, Defendant’s responses were unequivocally not code compliant. (See Allen-Pacific, Ltd. v. Superior Court (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1546, 1551 [unverified response to requests for admissions is not in substantial compliance], disapproved on other grounds in Wilcox v. Birtwhistle (1999) 21 Cal.4th 973, 983; Appleton v. Superior Court (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 632, 636 [an unverified response to requests for admissions is “tantamount to no response[] at all”].)

 

Though untimely, Defendant then served verified amended responses as to 23 of the 36 requests, excluding requests 7, 9, 14-18, 23, 29, 30, 32, 34, 36, which either admitted, denied, or stated insufficient knowledge or information to admit or deny the requests on December 30, 2025. “[A] responding party's service, prior to the hearing on the ‘deemed admitted’ motion, of substantially compliant responses, will defeat a propounding party's attempt under section 2033.280 to have the RFAs deemed admitted.” (St. Mary, supra, 223 Cal.App.4th at 776.) Furthermore, even where a responding party has failed to timely serve code-compliant responses, has not demonstrated that the failure to so serve was a result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect, and has thus waived any objection to the requests for admission, the presence of waived objections in a response governed by section 2033.280(c) does not necessarily prevent substantial compliance with Section 2033.220. (Katayama v. Continental Investment Group (2024) 105 Cal.App.5th 898, 901.)

 

The Court must evaluate the response to the request as a whole, and is not permitted to “segregate each individual RFA response for the purpose of finding that portions of the document are code compliant (and will therefore be accepted), while concluding that other portions are noncompliant (and will thus be rejected).” (St. Mary, supra, 223 Cal. App. 4th at 780.) The ability of the Court to do so is further negated by the fact that “there is an effective statutory vehicle by which a propounding party may seek a court order compelling a responding party to cure individual RFA responses deemed not to be in compliance with section 2033.220—namely, a motion to compel further responses under section 2033.290.” (Ibid.)

 

The Court finds that although Defendant’s verified amended response to Plaintiff’s Requests for Admissions was untimely and omitted any response to 13 of the 36 requests, as a whole, the response is in substantial compliance with Section 2033.220. Therefore, because Defendant served a substantially compliant verified amended response before the instant motion was filed and before the hearing date, the Court finds that this motion is not the proper avenue through which to address the deficiencies and omissions in Defendant’s response.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion to deem the omitted Requests for Admissions admitted is DENIED.

Proc., § 2023.030, subd.¿(a).)¿Failing to respond¿or to¿submit¿to an authorized method of discovery constitutes a misuse of the discovery process. (Code Civ. Proc.,¿§¿2023.010,¿subd. (d).) The Court must impose a monetary sanction on the party or attorney whose failure to serve timely¿requests for admission responses¿necessitated¿the motion.¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd.¿(c).)¿ “It is mandatory that the court impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) on the party or attorney, or both, whose failure to serve a timely response to requests for admission necessitated this motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(c).)

 

Plaintiff requests monetary sanctions against Defendant and his counsel in the amount of $110.00, comprised of the $60.00 filing fee for the motion and a $50.00 service fee. (Motion, p. 3:17-18.) In opposition, Defendant requests monetary sanctions in the amount of $375 against Plaintiff, payable within 20 days of the hearing on this motion. (Opp., p. 2:2-3; Travis Decl., ¶ 6.)

 

Because Plaintiff’s motion is denied, Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions is DENIED.

 

The Court also finds that Plaintiff’s motion is not a misuse of the discovery process warranting monetary sanctions, particularly in light of Defendant’s deficient responses. Thus, in exercising its discretion, the Court DENIES Defendant’s request for monetary sanctions.

 IV.          Conclusion

   II.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Motion to Vacate Dismissal – A court may, upon just terms, relieve a party or attorney from a dismissal taken against the party through the party’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).) The motion for relief shall be made within a reasonable time, but in no case exceeding six months after the dismissal was taken. (Ibid.)

 

No affidavit or declaration of merits is required of the moving party. (Ibid.) However, where a timely and proper motion is accompanied by an attorney’s sworn affidavit attesting to the attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, the court shall vacate any resulting dismissal against the attorney’s client, unless the court finds that the dismissal was not caused by the attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect. (Ibid.)

 

-----

 

 III.          Discussion

 

Application – Plaintiffs move to vacate the dismissal and restore this matter to the Court’s calendar pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b), and the Court’s inherent authority to correct orders entered as a result of mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.

 

Plaintiffs state that the dismissal of this action occurred as a result of their counsel’s misunderstanding during the CMC regarding the procedural posture of the parties’ prior unlawful detainer action. (Id at p. 5:19-20.) (Motion, p. 6:1-4.) Thus, Plaintiffs claim the dismissal of the present action was not the result of inattention by Plaintiffs, but rather, occurred because of a procedural misunderstanding of counsel regarding the viability of filing a cross-complaint in a case that has been dismissed and sealed. (Motion, p. 6:5-9.)

 

The Court also acknowledges that Plaintiff’s counsel, Michael Joel Kelly, has concurrently filed a sworn affidavit, attesting to his good faith statement in dismissing the present action being made upon his misunderstanding of the procedural posture of the unlawful detainer case. (Kelly Decl., ¶¶ 9-10, 13.)

 

The Court finds good causes exists to grant Plaintiff’s motion to vacate dismissal, to the extent the matter even was dismissed. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion to vacate dismissal and restore this matter to the Court’s calendar is GRANTED.

On February 25, 2026, Defendant filed its amended answer to the complaint, asserting thirteen affirmative defenses for (1) failure to state a cause of action, (2) laches, (3) mitigation of damages, (4) arbitration agreement, (5) statute of limitation, (6) state-of-the-art, (7) failure to provide notice of breach, (8) duration of implied warranty, (9) unreasonable or unauthorized use of vehicle, (10) negligence of others, (11) lack of maintenance and other exclusions, (12) modification of property, and (13) no timely revocation of acceptance.

-----

 

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Timeliness of Plaintiff’s Demurrer – A party has 10 days after service of the answer to demur. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.40, subd. (b).) However, “[i]f the parties are not able to meet and confer at least 5 days before the date the responsive pleading is due, the demurring party shall be granted an automatic 30-day extension of time within which to file a responsive pleading, by filing and serving, on or before the date on which a demurrer would be due, a declaration stating under penalty of perjury that a good faith attempt to meet and confer was made and explaining the reasons why the parties could not meet and confer. The 30-day extension shall commence from the date the responsive pleading was previously due, and the demurring party shall not be subject to default during the period of the extension.” (Code Civ. Proc., §430.41, subd. (a)(2).)

 

Plaintiff’s counsel asserts that upon receipt of Defendant’s first answer on January 15, 2026, he attempted to meet and confer at least five days before filing a demurrer. (Souto Decl., ¶¶ 2-3, Ex. A, B.) On January 23, 2026, the parties met and conferred, and Defense counsel agreed to Plaintiff’s filing a declaration of demurring party in support of an automatic 30-day extension of time to file the demurer to the answer to provide Defendant time to file an amended answer. (Souto Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. C, D.) A demurer to the original answer filed by Defendant was due on January 27, 2026. The extension made this demurrer due on February 26, 2026.

 

Defendant filed and served its amended answer on February 25, 2026. Plaintiff’s demurer to the amended answer was due on or before Monday, March 9, 2026. Plaintiff demurred to the amended answer on March 10, 2026. The extension for filing the demurer as to the first answer filed by Defendant does not grant Plaintiff an additional day to file the present demurer to the amended complaint. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s demurrer is untimely.

 

However, this demurrer is unopposed. In the absence of any prejudice to Defendant, and in the interests of adjudicating the issue, the Court exercises its discretion to rule on the merits.

 

Meet and Confer – Before filing a demurrer, the demurring party is required to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due, in person, by telephone, or by video conference, with the party who filed the pleading demurred to for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) The demurring party shall file and serve with the demurrer a declaration stating the means by which the parties met and conferred and that the parties did not reach an agreement to resolve the issues raised, or that the party who filed the pleading demurred to failed to respond or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith. (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff’s counsel Astrid Souto declares that on February 27, 2026, she sent an email to Defense counsel detailing the grounds for Plaintiff’s demurrer, and on March 6, 2026, the parties telephonically met and conferred regarding Plaintiff’s demurrer to the amended answer. (Souto Decl., ¶¶ 7-8.) The Court finds that the meet and confer was procedurally untimely as it was not held at least five days before the filing of the present demurrer.

 

However, a determination that the meet and confer process was not sufficient is not grounds to overrule a demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(4).) Accordingly, the Court will consider the Demurrer on the merits.

 

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Demurrer to Answer – Pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 430.20, the party against whom an answer has been filed may object to the answer by demurrer.   

When considering a demurrer to an answer, “the defect in question need not appear on the face of the answer. The determination of the sufficiency of the answer requires an examination of the complaint because its adequacy is with reference to the complaint it purports to answer… [T]he demurrer to the answer admits all issuable facts pleaded therin and eliminates all allegations of the complaint denied by the answer… [E]ach so-called defense must be considered separately without regard to any other defense.” (South Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 733-34.)

A demurrer to an answer may be brought on one of three grounds: (1) the answer does not include facts sufficient to constitute a defense; (2) uncertainty; and (3) the facts, as pleaded, are insufficient to determine whether an alleged contract in the answer is written or oral.¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 430.20.) 

“The answer to a complaint shall contain: (1) [t]he general or specific denial of the material allegations of the complaint controverted by the defendant … [and] (2) [a] statement of any new matter constituting a defense.” (Code Civ. Proc., 431.30, subd. (b).) 

Defenses should be relevant to a plaintiff’s legal claims and averred carefully, and with as much detail as the facts constituting the corresponding causes of action in the complaint.¿ (FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 384.) Defenses should not be proffered in the form of “terse legal conclusions.”¿(Ibid.) “The defenses shall be separately stated, and the several defenses shall refer to the causes of action which they are intended to answer, in a manner by which they may be intelligibly distinguished.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.30, subd. (g).) 

“The phrase ‘new matter’ refers to something relied on by a defendant which is not put in issue by the plaintiff.” (State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 721, 725.) “A plea controverting the original cause of action and tendering no new issue is a mere traverse and cannot be properly described as a plea setting up new matter.” (Rancho Santa Margarita v. Vail (1938) 11 Cal.2d 501, 543.) 

“However, [n]o error or defect in a pleading is to be regarded unless it affects substantial rights …. The primary function of a pleading is to give the other party notice so that it may prepare its case …, and a defect in a pleading that otherwise properly notifies a party cannot be said to affect substantial rights. (Harris v. City of Santa Monica (2013) 56 Cal.4th 203, 240 [internal citations omitted].) 

"[A]bsent a showing of prejudice to the adverse party, the rule of great liberality in allowing amendment of pleadings will prevail." (Board of Trustees v. Superior Court (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1154, 1163.) Thus, where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)

-----

 

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Plaintiff demurs to Defendant’s amended answer on as to the following affirmative defenses and on the following grounds: (1) the second, third, and sixth through thirteenth affirmative defenses because they fail to state facts sufficient to constitute a defense under California Code of Civil Procedure sections 430.20, subdivision (a) and California Code of Civil Procedure section 431.30, subdivision (b), or are uncertain under California Code of Civil Procedure section 430.20, subdivision (b); (2) the fifth affirmative defense for being in violation of California Code of Civil Procedure section 458 or is otherwise barred; and (3) the fourth affirmative defense as it is not a legally cognizable affirmative defense. (Demurrer, p. 2, ¶¶ 1-3.)

 

a.     Failure to State Facts Sufficient to Constitute a Defense as to Affirmative Defenses Nos. Two, Three, and Six Through Thirteen

 

Plaintiff demurs to Defendant’s the second, third, and sixth through thirteenth affirmative defenses because they fail to state facts sufficient to constitute a defense under California Code of Civil Procedure sections 430.20, subdivision (a) and California Code of Civil Procedure section 431.30, subdivision (b), or are uncertain under California Code of Civil Procedure section 430.20, subdivision (b). (Demurrer, p. 2, ¶ 1.)

 

Affirmative defenses are a “new matter” where they introduce new issues not raised in the complaint. (Quantification Settlement Agreement Cases (2011) 201 Cal.App.4th 758, 812.) Defenses or objections based on “new matter” “must be specifically pleaded.” (Advantec Group, Inc. v. Edwin's Plumbing Co., Inc. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 621, 628.) “The phrase ‘new matter’ refers to something relied on by a defendant which is not put in issue by the plaintiff.”¿(Walsh v. West Valley Mission Community College District (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1532, 1546.) Where the answer sets forth facts showing some essential allegation of the complaint is not true, such facts are not new matters but are denials. (Ibid.)¿ “[A]ffirmative defenses cannot be pled as mere legal conclusions but must instead be alleged with as much factual detail as the allegations of a complaint.” (Quantification, supra, 201 Cal.App.4th at p. 813.)

 

Defendant fails to cite to the Complaint in its denials or provide requisite facts to support its ‘new matter’ affirmative defenses. Without specific factual allegations to support Defendant’s affirmative defenses, Plaintiff is not on notice as to what specific act or omission gave rise to the affirmative defense. Moreover, without specific factual allegations, Defendant’s defenses consist solely of legal conclusions. (See Bagatti v. Department of Rehabilitation (2002) 97 Cal.App.4th 344, 366, fn. 8 [“These conclusionary allegations, which allege no specific acts, are insufficient to survive demurrer.”].) 

 

As Defendant failed to plead factual allegations to support its second, third, and sixth through thirteenth affirmative defenses, Plaintiff’s demurrer to these affirmative defenses is sustained with leave to amend.

 

b.     Violation of California Code of Civil Procedure Section 458 or Otherwise Barred as to Affirmative Defense No. Five

 

Defendant demurs to the fifth affirmative defense for being in violation of California Code of Civil Procedure section 458 or is otherwise barred. (Demurrer, p. 2, ¶ 2.)

 

The fifth affirmative defense states Plaintiff’s claims are barred by the applicable statutes of limitation, specifically the limitations periods outlined in California Code of Civil Procedure sections 335.1, 337, 338, 338.1, and 340, Civil Code sections 1783 and 1791.1, and Commercial Code section 2725. (Amended Answer, p. 3, ¶ 5.)

 

California Code of Civil Procedure section 458 provides that a state of limitation affirmative defense may be pled without any accompanying facts. Specifically, it states: “In pleading the Statute of Limitations it is not necessary to state the facts showing the defense, but it may be stated generally that the cause of action is barred by the provisions of Section ____ (giving the number of the section and subdivision thereof, if it is so divided, relied upon) of the Code of Civil Procedure.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 458.)

 

When alleging a statute of limitations defense, a defendant must specify, where applicable, each of the statutes’ applicable subdivisions. (See Davenport v. Stratton (1944) 24 Cal.2d 232, 246-247 [“answer did not refer to the proper subdivision. […] the provisions of section 458 of the Code of Civil Procedure must be strictly applied, and that the attempted plea of the statute of limitations by defendant was insufficient to raise that issue in the trial court”].)

 

Here, the subdivisions for California Code of Civil Procedure sections 337, 338, and 340, Civil Code section 1791.1, and Commercial Code section 2725 are not identified as required. Thus, Defendant’s identification of said statutes without subdivisions in its amended answer to the complaint is insufficient to raise the statutes as a defense.  (See Davenport, supra, 24 Cal.2d at pp. 246-247.)

 

Accordingly, the demurrer to the fifth affirmative defense is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.  

 

c.      Not a Legally Cognizable Affirmative Defense as to Affirmative Defense No. Four

 

Plaintiff demurs to the fourth affirmative defense on the grounds that it is not a legally cognizable affirmative defense. (Demurrer, p. 2, ¶ 3.)

 

The fourth affirmative defense states that Defendant is informed and believes, and on that basis alleges, that this dispute is subject to an arbitration agreement with Plaintiff such that this matter is not properly brought before the Court. (Amended Answer, p. 3, ¶ 5.)

 

An agreement to arbitrate is an affirmative defense. (Ross v. Blanchard (1967) 251 Cal.App.2d 739, 742-43.) The Court acknowledges that this affirmative defense is similarly lacking in factual specificity. However, Plaintiff does not demur on the grounds that the fourth affirmative defense fails to state sufficient facts, that it is uncertain, or that the facts, as pleaded, are insufficient to determine whether an alleged contract in the answer is written or oral. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.20.) 

 

Accordingly, the demurrer to the fourth affirmative defense is OVERRULED.

Plaintiff Jorge Barajas’s Demurrer to Defendant FCA US, LLC’s Amended Answer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to the second, third, fifth, and sixth through thirteenth affirmative defenses.

 

Plaintiff Jorge Barajas’s Demurrer to Defendant FCA US, LLC’s Amended Answer is OVERRULED as to the fourth affirmative defense.

(Levya Decl., ¶ 6.1-6.2.)

 

The Court finds that Insurance Code section 10139.5(a)(1) satisfied.¿ 

 

b.     Whether the Payee Has Been Advised in Writing by the Transferee to Seek Independent Professional Advice Regarding Transfer and Has Either Received That Advice or Knowingly Waived, in Writing, the Opportunity to Receive That Advice

 

Petitioner attaches an IPA Waiver signed by Levya, indicating that Petitioner advised Levya to obtain independent legal or financial advice regarding the transaction, that Petitioner would cover up to $1,500 of the cost of such representation, that Petitioner’s accountant, counsel, or actuary may not provide Levya with advice regarding the transaction, and that Petitioner has advised Levya to obtain independent professional advice from a lawyer experienced in tax matters regarding income tax consequences he may face from selling his structured settlement payment rights, but that Petitioner could not provide him with the name of any such professional. (Petition, Ex. C.) The waiver states “I hereby knowingly waive in writing the opportunity to seek and receive such independent professional advice,” followed by Levya’s signature, dated April 27, 2026.

 

Further, Levya asserts that he has been advised by Petitioner of his right to seek independent counsel and financial advice in connection with this petition for approval and has been advised that upon his retention of an attorney, licensed actuary, or licensed certified public accountant, Petitioner would pay the fees regardless of the work done and regardless of whether the petition is approved, in an amount not to exceed $1,500. (Levya Decl., ¶ 13.) Levya states he waives that advice and does not wish to retain an independent advisor. (Levya Decl., ¶ 14.)

 

Insurance Code section 10139.5(a)(2) is satisfied.¿ 

 

c.      Whether Transferee Has Complied With Notification, Disclosure Form, and Transfer Agreement Requirements

 

Petitioner has complied with the notification requirements and has attached a California Transfer Disclosure form that complies with Insurance Code section 10136 and the transfer agreement complies in full with Insurance Code sections 10136 and 10138. (Petition, Ex. D.) Levya states he received these disclosures at least 10 days before he signed the transfer agreement. (Levya Decl., ¶ 12.)

 

Insurance Code section 10139.5(a)(3) is satisfied.¿ 

 

d.     Whether Transfer Contravenes Any Applicable Statute or Order of Any Court or Government Authority

 

The transfer  complies with statutory requirements, and there is no evidence that it contravenes the order of any court or other government authority.¿ 

 

Insurance Code section 10139.5(a)(4) is satisfied. 

 

e.      Whether Payee Understands Terms of Transfer Agreement

 

Levya declares he understands the terms of the transaction and is entering into the transaction with Petitioner of his own free will and volition, without reservation, duress, or undue influence. (Leyva Decl., ¶ 15.)¿ 

 

Insurance Code section 10139.5(a)(5) is satisfied.¿ 

 

f.      Whether Payee Understands and Does Not Wish to Exercise His Right to Cancel the Transfer Agreement

 

Levya signed the transfer agreement directly under a box containing bolded text stating, in relevant part: “You have the right to cancel this agreement without any cost or obligation until the date the court approves this agreement” and setting forth the manner in which he can cancel. (Petition, Ex. B.)

 

Levya declares he understands he has a right to cancel the agreement until it is approved by the court and that he does not wish to exercise his right to cancel the agreement. (Leyva Decl., ¶ 16.)

 

Insurance Code section 10139.5(a)(6) is satisfied.  

 

Insurance Code section 10139.5 subd. (b) and (c) provide further factors for the court’s consideration, under the totality of the circumstances, in determining whether the proposed transfer should be approved. The Court finds the transfer to be fair and reasonable after considering Levya’s declaration regarding his financial situation, age, stated purpose, the terms of the transaction, that the settlement funds were not intended, nor does Levya rely on them, to pay for future medical care and treatment or for necessary living expenses, Levya’s income from other sources, including his employment, the discount rate applied, Levya’s previous transactions and that no such transactions were ever denied or withdrawn, and all other circumstances delineated in the Petition and Levya’s declaration. (Levya Decl., ¶¶ 4-9, 11.)

 

Accordingly, the petition is granted.
