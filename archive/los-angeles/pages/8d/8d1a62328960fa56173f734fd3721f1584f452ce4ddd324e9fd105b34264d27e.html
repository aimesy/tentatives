Case Number: 20SMCV01925    Hearing Date: July 22, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Dry, et al., v. Dell, et al.

CASE NO.:                20SMCV01925

MOTION:                  Motion for Attorney’s Fees

HEARING DATE:   7/22/2026

 

Legal Standard

 

With respect to attorney fees and costs, unless they are specifically provided for by statute (e.g., CCP §§ 1032, et seq.), the measure and mode of compensation of attorneys and counselors at law is left to the agreement, express or implied, of the parties.¿(CCP § 1021.) The prevailing party on a contract, which specifically provides for attorney fees and costs incurred to enforce the agreement, is entitled to reasonable attorney fees in addition to other costs.¿(Civ. Code § 1717(a); CCP §§ 1032, 1033.5(a)(10)(A).)¿The court, upon notice and motion by a party, shall determine the prevailing party and shall fix, as an element of the costs of suit, the reasonable attorney fees.¿(Civ. Code § 1717(a), (b).)¿Any notice of motion to claim attorney fees as an element of costs under shall be served and filed before or at the same time the memorandum of costs is served and filed; if only attorney fees are claimed as costs, the notice of motion shall be served and filed within the time specified in CRC 3.1700 for filing a memorandum of costs.¿(CRC 3.1702; Gunlock Corp. v. Walk on Water, Inc. (1993) 15 Cal.App.4th 1301, 1303, fn. 1.)

 

“It is well established that the determination of what constitutes reasonable attorney fees is committed to the discretion of the trial court, whose decision cannot be reversed in the absence of an abuse of discretion. [Citation.]” (Melnyk v. Robledo (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 618, 623 624.) The fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily “begins with the ‘lodestar’ [method], i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.” (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.) “[A] computation of time spent on a case and the reasonable value of that time is fundamental to a determination of an appropriate attorneys’ fee award.” (Margolin v. Reg’l Planning Comm’n (1982) 134 Cal.App.3d 999, 1004.) The lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided. (See Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49 [discussing factors relevant to proper attorneys’ fees award].) Such an approach anchors the trial court’s analysis to an objective determination of the value of the attorney’s services, ensuring that the amount awarded is not arbitrary. (Id. at 48, fn. 23.) The factors considered in determining the modification of the lodestar include “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Mountjoy v. Bank of Am. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 266, 271.)

 

In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence.¿(Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc. v. California Ins. Guaranty Assoc. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 564.)¿General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice. (Ibid.) 

 

Analysis

 

Defendants Jack Dell, 1025 Ocean Avenue, Ltd., L.P. and American Alliance Capital Group, LLC move for an award of attorney’s fees against Plaintiffs Richman Bry, Jr. and Riviera Capital Group, LLC in the amount of $174,487.00, and for costs of $7,240.87 as set forth in the Memorandum of Costs.

 

Defendants note that on February 26, 2026, the Court awarded $12,980.00 in sanctions against Plaintiffs, which are not sought in the instant motion for attorneys’ fees. Defendants request that the ultimate award here also reflect that amount previously awarded.

 

Evidentiary Issues

 

Defendants’ objections to the Speiser Declaration are SUSTAINED as to no. 1-4 (relevance, hearsay). Defendants’ objection to the Chodos declaration is SUSTAINED (relevance).

 

Discussion

 

There is no reasonable dispute that Defendants are the prevailing parties in this action, since they received a full dismissal on the contract claims. Defendants are entitled to reasonable attorneys’ fees incurred in connection with this action, including on proceedings where it did not prevail as to the relief requested. (Civ. Code § 1717; see Frog Creek Partners, LLC v. Vance Brown, Inc. (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 515, 546 [“a party who ultimately prevails on a contract action is entitled to all of its fees, including fees incurred during the lawsuit in proceedings where it did not prevail.”].)

 

Defendants demonstrate an initial lodestar of $174,487.00 which was incurred to obtain Defendants’ litigation objective of a dismissal of Plaintiffs’ claims. (Gold Decl., ¶¶ 19, 26, Ex. N.) The lodestar is supported by verified, contemporaneous fee records. (Id.) Counsel spent over 303.1 hours litigating this action. Defendants demonstrate that their counsel’s fee rates are reasonable when compared with similarly situated attorneys with similar experience. (Id., ¶¶ 22-25, Exs. O [Laffey Matrix], P [2025 Judicial Salary Plan], Q [2024 Judicial Salary Plan].) Counsel set forth their training, education and experience. (Id., ¶¶ 18-20, 25.) The hourly rates charged by ECJ attorneys for the work conducted in connection with this action ranges from $435 per hour (for Elliot Z. Chen, an attorney with four years’ experience in 2021) to $800 per hour (for Gold, a partner with over 35 years’ experience). (Id.) The rates increased progressively over the six years of litigation. (Id.) Counsel shows that their rates are below those comparable hourly rates for attorneys’ with similar experience and in comparable markets. (Id., ¶¶ 25-26, Ex. N.) This evidence meets Defendants’ prima facie showing that under the lodestar method. Plaintiffs therefore have the burden to make specific objections that the sought fees are excessive, duplicative, or otherwise unreasonable.

 

Plaintiff challenges the fees incurred in connection with Defendants’ successful March 18, 2026, motion to dismiss, arguing that the motion was not overly complex. The court does not find that the requested fees in connection with that motion were unreasonably incurred. Indeed, this was no trivial motion, as it terminated this suit which had been pending more than five years. Defendants were required to put forth evidence to establish that Plaintiffs’ delay did not fall within any exception of Section 583.340, which reasonably required extensive analysis of the record, conducting legal research, drafting the memorandum (including detailing the history of the action), and preparing the supporting declaration and exhibits. (See Gold Decl., Ex. N [entries of 3/3/2026–3/18/2026].) The motion was opposed, so Defendants also had to spend time reviewing the opposition to the motion, researching Plaintiffs’ authorities, and preparing the reply and evidentiary objections to the declarations filed in support of the opposition. (Id. [4/22/2026–4/24/2026].) Spending roughly twenty hours to research, brief, reply to, and win a dispositive motion ending a five-and-a-half-year case is reasonable.

 

            Plaintiff also challenges the fees incurred between the first fee motion and the instant fee motion, asserting that the fees are duplicative or excessive. The Court concurs that the time spent on the initial fee motion was excessive. The time records show that counsel spent 19.2 hours on the original fee motion (not including the 8.7 hours billed by the paralegal (“KJD”) for preparation of the spreadsheet of time billed and fees incurred). (Gold Decl., Ex. N (6/13/2025-8/5/2025).) The court will accordingly reduce the fees by $3,150.00 (which is a reduction of 3 hours @ 415/hr. and 3 hours @ 635.00/hr.).

 

The court also concurs that the $7,200 incurred to update the fee motion was excessive or duplicative. The updated motion simply added to the billing entry spreadsheet to include all work conducted from July 2025 through June 2026, and changes to the memorandum to reflect that work. (Id. (5/11/2026-6/2/2026).) These records show over 11.1 hours spent on these edits. The court will accordingly reduce the fees by $2,580.00 (a 2 hour reduction @ 695/hr. plus 2.1 hour reduction @ 595/hr.).

 

Plaintiff raises that time spent on intra-office discussions are unfairly duplicative. Plaintiff does not specifically cite any particular entries. However, upon independent review, the Court concurs that some of the intra-office discussions are excessive, duplicative and/or unreasonable. The Court will reduce the fee award by $1,289.50.

 

Defendants support additional reasonable fees incurred in reply for this motion. Defendants incurred and will incur at least $5,125 in legal fees in connection with the work conducted by their counsel to review Plaintiffs’ opposition to the fee motion and prepare the instant reply brief, as well as for their counsel to prepare for and appear at the hearing on the fee motion. (Supp. Gold Decl., ¶¶ 2-4.) The Court finds this requested amount to be reasonable. 

 

Plaintiffs do not object to the request for costs. Therefore, Defendants are entitled to the costs as requested.

 

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED. Fees are awarded in the amount of $172,592.50, not including the sanctions previously awarded in the amount of $12,980.00. Costs are awarded in the noticed amount of $7,240.87.

Case Number: 23SMCV04254    Hearing Date: July 22, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           WLA Dental Center v. Taylor Electrical Corp., et al.

CASE NO.:                23SMCV04254

MOTION:                  Motion to Compel Deposition

HEARING DATE:   7/22/2026

 

 

Legal Standard

 

Service of a proper deposition notice obligates a party or “party-affiliated” witness (officer, director, managing agent or employee of party) to attend and testify, as well as produce any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing for inspection and copying. (CCP § 2025.280(a).) If, after service of a deposition notice, a party deponent fails to appear, testify, or produce documents or tangible things for inspection without having served a valid objection under CCP § 2025.410, the deposing party may move for an order compelling attendance, testimony, and production. (CCP § 2025.450(a).) The motion must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration, or, when a party deponent fails to attend the deposition, the motion must also be accompanied by a declaration stating that the moving party has contacted the party deponent to inquire about the nonappearance. (CCP § 2025.450(b)(2).) If the deposition notice included a request for production of documents, the motion to compel attendance must also show good cause to justify the production. (CCP § 2025.450(b)(1).) 

  

A motion to compel production of documents described in a deposition notice must be accompanied by a showing of good cause. (CCP § 2025.450(b)(1).) In other words, the moving party must provide declarations containing specific facts justifying inspection of the documents described in the notice. Courts liberally construe good cause in favor of discovery where facts show the documents are necessary for trial preparation. 

 

Analysis

 

Plaintiff WLA Dental Center moves to compel Defendant Zachary J. Howes to attend his deposition and provide documents and testimony. Plaintiff demonstrates proper service of a deposition notice. On April 23, 2026, Plaintiff served, via mail, a Notice of Taking Deposition of Defendant, setting Defendant’s deposition for May 13, 2026. (Kalantari Decl., ¶ 2.) Despite this notice, Defendant failed to appear for deposition. (Id.) Defendant did not serve any objections or response. (Id.)

 

The Court also finds good cause for production. Independently reviewing the document requests, each request pertains to the claims and defenses at issue, especially concerning the subject Project. Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED. Defendant is ordered to appear at his deposition as noticed, on a mutually agreeable date within 30 days, at Afifi Law Group located at 525 Colorado Ave, Santa Monica, CA, 90401, beginning at 10:00 a.m. Sanctions are imposed in the total amount of $1,560.00 against Defendant. Sanctions are to be paid by Defendant to Plaintiff’s counsel of record within 30 days.


Case Number: 25SMCV03990    Hearing Date: July 22, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Amic v. CAI Acquisitions Venice LLC, et al.

CASE NO.:                25SMCV03990

MOTION:                  Motion for Trial Preference

HEARING DATE:   7/22/2026

 

 

Legal Standard

 

A party who is 70 years of age or older, or who reach that age during pendency of the action, may be entitled to preference if they establish to the court's satisfaction that: 1) she has a “substantial interest in the action as a whole”; and 2) her health is “such that preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing the party's interest in the litigation.” (CCP § 36(a), (c)(2).) In contrast to minor plaintiffs, trial¿priority is not mandatory and absolute merely because one of the parties is age 70. The court has discretion to determine the¿extent¿of that party's interest and find as a matter of fact the risk posed of that party's death or incapacity if¿trial¿is delayed. (CCP § 36(a).) However, if the factors are shown, the Court “shall” grant preference. 

 

A¿trial¿must be set within 120 days even if opposing parties have not completed discovery or pretrial preparations. (Swaithes v. Superior Court¿(1989) 212 Cal.App.3d 1082, 1086.) Mere inconvenience to the court or to other litigants is irrelevant. (Rice¿v.¿Superior Court (1982) 136 Cal.App.3d 81, 89-94; but see Polibrid Coatings, Inc. v. Superior Court (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 920, 923-924 [when brought into case after over half the time to litigate has passed, party should be given “at least enough time” to “reasonably complete discovery and bring a summary judgment motion.”].) Thus, the Court¿generally focuses on the moving parties’ burden, rather than striking a balance between the conflicting interests of opposing litigants.

 

A declaration supporting a motion for CCP section 36(a)¿preference¿“may be signed by the attorney for the party seeking¿preference¿based upon information and belief¿as to the¿medical diagnosis and prognosis¿of¿any party.” (CCP § 36.5.) Accordingly, an attorney's declaration can consist entirely of hearsay and conclusions. (Fox v. Superior Court (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 529, 534.)

 

Moreover, the court has the general discretionary power to grant priority to any case upon a showing of good cause, i.e. “that the interests of justice will be served by granting this¿preference.” (CCP § 36(e).)

 

Analysis

 

Plaintiff Penny Amic moves for an order setting trial pursuant to CCP section 36(a). Plaintiff explains that she was born on August 9, 1948, and as of the date of this hearing will be two weeks short of 78-years-old. (Amic Decl., ¶2.) There is no dispute Plaintiff has a substantial interest in this action as the sole plaintiff. Plaintiff has been living with Multiple Sclerosis (“MS”) for 40-years. (Id. ¶3.) The known effects of her MS include frequent and extreme fatigue, muscle weakness, constant numbness and tingling on the right lower extremity. The weakness and fatigue have slowly progressed over the years and the numbness and tingling has appeared over the last handful of years. (Id. ¶ 4.) Plaintiff notes that, if the trial is set without preference, then trial in this action may take place in 2028, when Plaintiff will be 80-years-old and the escalation of her condition will be unknown.


The Court requires a more specific statement that Plaintiff’s MS poses a risk that Plaintiff will die or become incapacitated if¿trial¿is delayed, or that preference is otherwise required to prevent prejudice to Plaintiff’s interest in this litigation. Plaintiff only states that she has MS, which she has apparently managed for 40 years. She does not explain whether her symptoms have been worsening such that any delay in trial will prejudice her interests.

 

Accordingly, the motion is CONTINUED to August 11, 2026, at 8:30 a.m. Plaintiff may submit a supplemental declaration supporting the motion on or before 9 court days prior to the continued hearing date.


Case Number: 25SMCV04054    Hearing Date: July 22, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Alexander v. Netradyne Inc.

CASE NO.:                25SMCV04054

MOTION:                  Demurrer and Motion to Strike the First Amended Complaint  

HEARING DATE:   7/22/2026

 

LEGAL STANDARD 

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (CCP §§ 430.30, 430.70.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732, internal citations omitted.) 

 

A special demurrer for uncertainty is disfavored and will only be sustained where the pleading is so bad that defendant cannot reasonably respond—i.e., cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him/her. (CCP § 430.10(f); Khoury v. Maly’s of Calif., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Moreover, even if the pleading is somewhat vague, “ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Ibid.)  

 

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof. (CCP § 435(b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1322(b).) The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (CCP §§ 436(a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].) 

 

“Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given.” (Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227.) It is an abuse of discretion for the court to deny leave to amend where there is any reasonable possibility that plaintiff can state a good cause of action. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) The burden is on plaintiff to show¿in what manner¿plaintiff can amend the complaint, and¿how¿that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading.¿(Id.) 

 

ANALYSIS 

 

Defendant Netradyne, Inc., demurs to the second, sixth, and seventh causes of action stated in Plaintiff Ethan Alexander’s (“Plaintiff”) First Amended Complaint (“FAC”). Defendant also moves to strike certain allegations in the FAC, including allegations regarding the Palisades Fire, Pre-Offer Representations, Attorneys’ Fees, and Punitive Damages.

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

Defendant’s unopposed request is GRANTED.

 

Fraudulent Inducement

 

Defendant asserts that the fraud cause of action is barred by the integration clause in the subject agreement and the parol evidence rule. Even if the alleged misrepresentations made prior to entry of the agreement contradict the terms of the written agreement, the parol evidence rule does not apply to fraud claims. CCP section 1856(a), (g) and (f) states in relevant part:

 

Terms set forth in a writing intended by the parties as a final expression of their agreement with respect to the terms included therein may not be contradicted by evidence of a prior agreement or of a contemporaneous oral agreement. […] Where the validity of the agreement is the fact in dispute, this section does not exclude evidence relevant to that issue. […] This section does not exclude other evidence of the circumstances under which the agreement was made or to which it relates, as defined in Section 1860, or to explain an extrinsic ambiguity or otherwise interpret the terms of the agreement, or to establish illegality or fraud.

 

This section “establishes a broad exception to the operation of the parol evidence rule: ‘Where the validity of the agreement is the fact in dispute, this section does not exclude evidence relevant to that issue.’ This provision rests on the principle that the parol evidence rule, intended to protect the terms of a valid written contract, should not bar evidence challenging the validity of the agreement itself. ‘Evidence to prove that the instrument is void or voidable for mistake, fraud, duress, undue influence, illegality, alteration, lack of consideration, or another invalidating cause is admissible. This evidence does not contradict the terms of an effective integration, because it shows that the purported instrument has no legal effect.’ [Citations.]” (Riverisland Cold Storage, Inc. v. Fresno-Madera Production Credit Assn. (2013) 55 Cal.4th 1169, 1174-75.) Defendant also contends that the exception only applies where the plaintiff challenges the validity of the agreement itself. Plaintiff does so here. Plaintiff alleges that the parties “entered into an oral and implied-in-fact agreement for employment on specific compensation terms, including commissions at a rate of 10% of total contract value.” (Compl., ¶ 84.) With this allegation, Plaintiff effectively requests enforcement of the oral/implied contract terms, with commissions at 10% of the total contract value, rather than any potential limitations found in the terms of the written agreement.

 

Second, Defendant challenges the reasonableness of Plaintiff’s reliance on the alleged representations considering the written terms of the agreement. The elements of fraud are: “(a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Charnay v. Cobert (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 170, 184.) In California, fraud must be pled with specificity. (Small v. Fritz Companies, Inc. (2003) 30 Cal.4th 167, 184.) “The particularity demands that a plaintiff plead facts which show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.” (Cansino v. Bank of America (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1462, 1469.)¿ 

 

“The reasonableness of the reliance is ordinarily a question of fact. However, whether a party's reliance was justified may be decided as a matter of law if reasonable minds can come to only one conclusion based on the facts.” (Guido v. Koopman (1991) 1 Cal.App.4th 837, 843, internal citations omitted.) Reasonableness of the plaintiff's reliance is judged by reference to the plaintiff's knowledge and experience, and whether circumstances were such to make it reasonable for plaintiff to accept defendant's statements “without an independent inquiry or investigation.” (OCM Principal Opportunities Fund, L.P. v. CIBC World Markets Corp. (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 835, 864.)

 

Reliance may be unreasonable as a matter of law when oral representations are at odds with the provisions of the written contract. (See Hadland v. NN Invs. Life Ins. Co., (1994) 24 Cal. App. 4th 1578 [plaintiffs' reliance on an oral representation that their new insurance policy provided coverage as good or better than their prior policy was unjustified as a matter of law, because the written policy's schedule of benefits expressly and unambiguously provided maximum benefit caps that were inconsistent with the oral representation].) Where the written terms are clear and unambiguous, and directly contradict the oral promises, a reasonable person would have read the contract and discovered the contradiction, making reliance on the oral promises unreasonable as a matter of law. (Hadland, supra, 24 Cal. App. 4th at 1586; but see Orozco v. WPV San Jose, LLC, (2019) 36 Cal. App. 5th 375, 392 [failure to read the contract does not automatically defeat the reliance element where, even if the plaintiff had read the contract, plaintiff would not necessarily have been alerted to the defendant's false representations].) Defendant cites several cases affirming this basic principle in the employment context, generally regarding continuing versus at-will employment. (See Slivinsky v. Watkins-Johnson Co. (1990) 221 Cal.App.3d 799, 807 [“alleged reliance on Watkins–Johnson’s oral promises of continuing employment is simply not justifiable because the representations contradict the parties’ integrated employment agreement which provided that the employment was at will”]; Shapiro v. Wells Fargo Realty Advisors (1984) 152 Cal.App.3d 467, 482 [the allegations were conclusory and plaintiff “could not have reasonably relied on any implied promise by Wells Fargo which contradicted the express provisions of the written stock option agreement which he signed.”]; Agosta v. Astor (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 596, 606 [no justifiable reliance on “a collateral promise of long-term employment because the ‘at-will’ provision superseded any prior or contemporaneous agreement at variance with that term. But he could justifiably rely on the written promises regarding compensation terms”]; Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 393–394 [“the letter did not contain promises of long-term employment or that termination would be only for cause; AWI expressly and unambiguously stated that Dore’s employment was at will”].)

 

The alleged oral promises do not entirely contradict the May 2023 letter even though there are some potential inconsistencies between the oral pre-offer employment discussions alleged in paragraphs 1, 2, and 16-21 of the FAC and the May 2023 offer letter attached as Exhibit A to Plaintiff’s original complaint. The offer letter provided some compensation terms, including that Plaintiff would be “eligible for the company standard commission plan for sales reps with a target commission of up to $175,000.00 payable annually based on quota.” (RJN Ex. A, § 2.) However, the offer letter does not provide commissions at any particular rate or state whether the rate would be based on the total contract value as allegedly represented. The letter only makes a vague reference to a standard commission plan, but the record does not reveal any standard commission plan effective in 2023. Plaintiff also argues that the offer letter’s reference to a “target commission of up to $175,000.00 payable annually based on quota” is ambiguous, and does not establish a cap as a matter of law. For pleading purposes, the court agrees that the written contract is reasonably susceptible to Plaintiff’s interpretation. (See George v. Automobile Club of So. Cal. (2011) 201 Cal.App.4th 1112, 1128 [on demurrer, the court must consider the sufficiency of the allegations, including any parol evidence allegations, to determine whether the contract is reasonably susceptible to the plaintiff’s alleged interpretation]; Performance Plastering v. Richmond American Homes of Cal., Inc.¿(2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 659, 672 [courts will defer to plaintiffs’ reasonable interpretations].) The contract states that he would be “eligible for the company standard commission plan for sales reps with a target commission of up to $175,000.00 payable annually based on quota.” (RJN Ex. A, emphasis added.) The term “target” could mean that the “up to $175,000.00” is not a cap on the total commissions earned, but a cap on the target expected by Defendants of the commissions earned. Thus, this term may establish the upward boundary of a quota, rather than a hard cap to all annual commissions Plaintiff could earn. Moreover, even if this effectively caps Plaintiff’s annual commissions, the terms do not otherwise contradict the oral promises of specific rates and measures of commissions. Thus, the proper interpretation of the contract could depend on the oral representations concerning the commission earnings.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is OVERRULED as to this cause of action.

 

Wrongful Termination

 

“The elements of a claim for wrongful discharge in violation of public policy are (1) an employer-employee relationship, (2) the employer terminated the plaintiff’s employment, (3) the termination was substantially motivated by a violation of public policy, and (4) the discharge caused the plaintiff harm.” (Nosal-Tabor v. Sharp Chula Vista Medical Center (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 1224, 1234-35, quotations omitted.)  “A discharge is actionable as against public policy if it violates a policy that is: ‘(1) delineated in either constitutional or statutory provisions; (2) ‘public’ in the sense that it ‘inures to the benefit of the public’ rather than serving merely the interests of the individual; (3) well established at the time of discharge; and (4) ‘substantial’ and ‘fundamental.’’” (Id. at 1238-39, quotations omitted.)

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s admission that he was “unable to return to work,” and was then terminated for “abandonment of his job responsibilities,” does not meet the standard for pleading this Tameny claim. (FAC ¶¶ 64, 65, 116, 132.) Defendant reasons that Plaintiff’s inability and/or refusal to return to work was “caused by the voluntary action of [Plaintiff as the employee] or by conditions or matters beyond [Defendant Netradyne’s as the employer] reasonable control.” (Turner v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1238, 1248.) However, Defendant completely controlled Plaintiff’s leave. Moreover, Defendant’s reasoning for declining leave and requiring Plaintiff to return to work was allegedly pretextual. (FAC ¶ 9.) In reality, Defendant was allegedly punishing Plaintiff for seeking clarification of his commissions, in violation of specific sections of the Labor Code. (FAC ¶¶ 95-138.)

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is OVERRULED.

 

Constructive Discharge

 

            Defendant argues that the constructive discharge claim fails because the claim requires Plaintiff to have quit rather than be fired. (Vasquez v. Franklin Management Real Estate Fund, Inc. (2013) 222 Cal.App.4th 819, 827; see Turner, supra, 7 Cal.4th at 1246 [“an employee cannot simply ‘quit and sue,’ claiming he or she was constructively discharged”].) Indeed, Plaintiff does not allege that he was forced to resign. (FAC ¶¶ 149-152.) Instead, Plaintiff’s employment was terminated by Defendant. (FAC ¶ 64.)

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave as to this cause of action.

 

Motion to Strike – Re: Palisades Fire

 

Defendant argues that the FAC contains many, many “irrelevant” and/or “improper” paragraphs and/or portions thereof “not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state,” including those relating to the so-called “Palisades fire,” the loss of Plaintiff’s home in that fire, and its asserted impact on Plaintiff’s family. These allegations provide context/background for his employment-based allegations, including the wrongful termination claims. Additionally, the allegations provide additional support for the alleged emotional distress.

 

Accordingly, the motion to strike is DENIED as to these allegations.

 

Motion to Strike – Re: Pre-Offer Representations

 

Defendant moves to strike certain allegations that Plaintiff relied on pre-offer verbal representations about his compensation. Defendant argues that the allegations are irrelevant since they are barred by the integration clause and the parol evidence rule. As discussed above, the parol evidence may be admissible to support a fraud cause of action. (CCP § 1856(f), (g); Riverisland, supra, 55 Cal.4th 1169, 1174-75.)

 

Accordingly, the motion to strike is DENIED as to these allegations.

 

Motion to Strike – Attorneys’ Fees under § 17200

 

Defendant moves to strike the request for attorneys fees in connection with the Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200 claim. The request for fees under Bus. & Prof. Code section 17200 is unsupported. Plaintiff cites no authority that he may recover fees in connection with such a claim.

 

Accordingly, the motion to strike is GRANTED as to this request.

 

Motion to Strike - Punitive Damages

 

            Civil Code section 3294 permits punitive damages where there are allegations of “Fraud”, meaning “an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.” (Civ. Code § 3294(c)(3).) As discussed, the FAC states a claim for fraud.

 

Accordingly, the motion to strike is DENIED as to the request for punitive damages.

LEGAL STANDARD 

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (CCP §§ 430.30, 430.70.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732, internal citations omitted.) 

  

“Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given.” (Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227.) It is an abuse of discretion for the court to deny leave to amend where there is any reasonable possibility that plaintiff can state a good cause of action. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) The burden is on plaintiff to show¿in what manner¿plaintiff can amend the complaint, and¿how¿that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading.¿(Id.) 

 

ANALYSIS 

 

Defendants George Molayem and Adeliya Gibadullina demur to Plaintiff Ruby Siddiqui’s complaint.         

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

Defendants’ request for judicial notice is GRANTED. (Evid. Code § 452(c), (d).)

 

UTVA Transfer

 

Defendants challenge the UTVA cause of action on the grounds that there is no predicate transfer.  Generally, a “fraudulent conveyance is a transfer by the debtor of property to a third person undertaken with the intent to prevent a creditor from reaching that interest to satisfy its claim.”¿(Kirkeby v. Superior Court of Orange County (2004) 33 Cal.4th 642, 648.) To plead a fraudulent transfer claim, a plaintiff must establish: 1) Transfer made or obligation incurred by a debtor; 2) with actual intent to hinder, delay, or defraud any creditor of debtor; 3) without receiving a reasonably equivalent value in exchange for the transfer or obligation: a) debtor was engaged or was about to engage in a business or a transaction for which remaining assets were unreasonably small in relation to the business or transaction; or b) intended to incur, or believed or reasonably should have believed that debtor would incur, debts beyond ability to pay as they became due; and 4) injury to the creditor. (Civil Code §§3439.04(a), 3439.05; Kirkeby, supra, (2004) 33 Cal.4th 642, 651 [fraudulent transfer sufficiently alleged based on defendant’s transfer of property with the intent to defraud, hinder or delay creditors in collection]; Annod Corp. v. Hamilton & Samuels (2002) 100 Cal.App.4th 1286, 1298 [proof of fraudulent intent in conveyances often is inferences from the surrounding circumstances].) 

 

The Complaint alleges that on May 16, 2019, while Plaintiff’s underlying action against Molayem was pending, Molayem transferred title to the real property located at 2124 South Atlantic Boulevard, Commerce, California 90040, to his wife, Gibadullina, by grant deed recorded in the Official Records of Los Angeles County as Instrument No. 20190463855. (Compl., ¶ 7, Ex. A.) At the time of the transfer, Molayem was the owner of the Property and a defendant in Plaintiff’s pending lawsuit, in which Plaintiff sought substantial monetary relief for Molayem’s fraudulent conduct. (Id., ¶ 8.) The interspousal fraudulent transfer was made without adequate consideration as Gibadullina paid no fair market value for the Property. (Id., ¶ 9.) Following the transfer, Molayem continued to control and benefit from his de facto ownership

of the Property. (Id., ¶ 10.)

 

Defendants argue that the judicially noticeable record establishes that Molayem did not transfer title to the subject property to Gibadullina as alleged. Defendants contend that Exhibit A to the Complaint shows an Interspousal Transfer Grant Deed, which is “not a change of ownership.” (Compl., Ex A.) They further contend that Gibadullina paid valuable consideration to the original owner, Jeff Weisfeld, that Weisfeld acknowledged receipt of consideration, and Gibadullina paid the full transfer tax for the property.

 

The record does not affirmatively establish that Molayem never owned the subject property and never transferred title to Gibadullina as alleged. RJN Exhibit 1 establishes that on April 30, 2019, a third party granted Gibadullina, as her sole and separate property, the subject real property. However, this recorded instrument does not establish all facts necessary to conclude that Molayem never acquired any interest in the property between April 30, 2019, and May 16, 2019. To prevail on this argument, Defendants would have to rely on extrinsic evidence, including evidence that Molayem never acquired the subject property (despite the transfer shown in Exhibit A). Of course, this argument would logically lead to the question of why Molayem would grant his interest in the subject property to his wife if he never had interest in the property in the first place.

 

Further, as noted above, Exhibit A to the Complaint shows an interspousal transfer grant deed where Molayem, as spouse of grantee, granted to Gibadullina, as her sole and separate property, the subject real property. Per the text of the exhibit, an interspousal grant deed is not a change in ownership under § 63 of the Revenue and Taxation Code. Under Tax. & Rev. Code section 60-63, a “change in ownership” means a transfer of a present interest in real property, including the beneficial use thereof, the value of which is substantially equal to the value of the fee interest, but excludes interspousal grants under certain circumstances, including the transfer solely between spouses of any co-owner’s interest. A transfer of real property which results in a “change in ownership” has certain tax implications, e.g., reassessment/reevaluation of a property. (Cal. Const., Art. XIIIA.) While this was not a “change in ownership” for tax purposes, Defendants cite no authority that an interspousal grant deed could not be considered a transfer of property under the UTVA.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer on these grounds is OVERRULED.

 

Abatement

 

Defendants assert that another action is pending. A plea in abatement may be made by demurrer when there is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action. (Plant Insulation Co. v. Fibreboard Corp. (1990) 224 Cal.App.3d 781, 789.)  The later-filed suit is “abated” (stayed) pending resolution of the earlier action which, if concluded on the merits will be res judicata and if not, the later action will proceed. (Ibid.) The court can take judicial notice of the court files in determining the basis for this ground. (Bistawros v. Greenberg (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 189, 191-192.) “In order to sustain the plea of another action pending it is essential that it shall appear: (1) That both suits are predicated upon the same cause of action; (2) that both suits are pending in the same jurisdiction; and (3) that both suits are contested by the same parties. [Citation.] Where the plea is sustained the order should be merely an abatement or continuance of the second action, and it is error to give judgment for the defendant on the merits.” (Colvig v. RKO General, Inc. (1965) 232 Cal.App.2d 56, 70-71.)  Here, this UTVA action is not the same cause of action as those addressed in the underlying fraud action. Thus, the Court does not find grounds for abatement.

 

CCP § 916

 

Defendant fails to adequately address the applicability of CCP section 916 to an UTVA cause of action. At best, the demurrer on this basis would only support a stay of this action, rather than dismissal.

 

The perfecting of an appeal stays proceedings in the trial court upon the judgment or order appealed from or upon the matters embraced therein or affected thereby, including enforcement of the judgment or order, but the trial court may proceed upon any other matter embraced in the action and not affected by the judgment or order. (CCP § 916(a).) The purpose of the appeal stay is to protect the appellate court's jurisdiction by preserving the status quo until the appeal is decided. (Varian Medical Systems, Inc. v. Delfino (2005) 35 Cal.4th 180, 198.) The automatic stay prevents the trial court from rendering an appeal futile by altering the appealed judgment or order or by conducting other proceedings that may affect it. (Ibid.) In determining whether a proceeding is embraced in or affected by the appeal, the Court must consider the appeal and its possible outcomes in relation to the proceeding and its possible results. (Ibid.) “[W]hether a matter is ‘embraced’ in or ‘affected’ by a judgment [or order] within the meaning of [section 916] depends on whether postjudgment [or postorder] proceedings on the matter would have any effect on the ‘effectiveness' of the appeal.” (In re Marriage of Horowitz (1984) 159 Cal.App.3d 377, 381.) “If so, the proceedings are stayed; if not, the proceedings are permitted.” (Betz v. Pankow (1993) 16 Cal.App.4th 931, 938.)

 

Here, the UTVA action is a proceeding "embraced therein or affected" by the stayed action, since it specifically seeks enforcement of the underlying judgment, and not some other claim(s). The effectiveness of the appeal would be affected if judgment is entered in the UTVA action and enforced against Defendants. Notably, reversal of the underlying judgment will eliminate the predicate claim for the UVTA action. (See Cortez v. Vogt, (1997) 52 Cal. App. 4th 917, 931 [where there is a reversal of the underlying judgment on which the plaintiff relies to bring his action as a creditor to set aside the fraudulent transfer, the creditor is no longer entitled to the latter remedy].) Thus, this UTVA action would not be merely a collateral matter which could occur regardless of the appeal’s outcome. (Varian, supra, 35 Cal.4th at 190.) Additionally, to the extent that the claim existing at the time of the transfer was for the potential fraud claim, that judgment has already been fully satisfied. Section 3439.07(a)(1) only allows avoidance of a transfer to the extent necessary to satisfy the creditor's claim.

 

Therefore, the Court would OVERRULE the demurrer but stay this action until the underlying appeal is resolved.
