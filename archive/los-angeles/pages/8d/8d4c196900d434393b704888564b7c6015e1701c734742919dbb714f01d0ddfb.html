Tentative Ruling

Jose Escobar Guzman v. City of Los Angeles, Case no. 25SMCV00583

Defendant’s Motion to Compel Compliance with Deposition Subpoena

Hearing Date: September 15, 2026

Background

Plaintiffs Jose Escobar Guzman and David Orozco sue Defendants City of Los Angeles and State of California for negligence and premises liability arising from a motorcycle accident. Plaintiffs allege that Defendants’ failure to maintain the pacific coast highway caused them to crash after hitting a large pothole on February 24, 2024. (Complaint, p. 4.)

On February 5, 2025, Plaintiffs filed the complaint.

On April 17, 2025, The City filed a cross-complaint against the California Department of Transportation for indemnity.

On April 29, 2025, Plaintiffs dismissed the City.

On March 26, 2026, the Department of Transportation moved to compel Crenshaw Chiropractic’s custodian of records to produce documents in compliance with a subpoena served on or about February 9, 2026. As of September 11, 2026, this motion is unopposed.

Motion to Compel Compliance with Subpoena

Code of Civil Procedure § 1987.1, subdivision (a) states, “[i]f a subpoena requires . . . the production of books, documents, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b), or upon the court’s own motion after giving counsel notice and an opportunity to be heard, may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders. In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a), italics added.)  Subdivision (b) of Section 1987.1 states that "a party" may bring a motion to compel compliance with a subpoena pursuant to subdivision (a).  (Id., § 1987.1, subd. (b).) 

“[I]n making an order pursuant to motion made under . . . Section 1987.1, the court may in its discretion award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonably attorney’s fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.2, subd. (a).) 

 Discussion

The Department of Transportation seeks an order from the Court compelling Crenshaw Chiropractic’s Custodian of Records to comply with a subpoena for the production of business records.   

On February 9, 2026 the Department of Transportation served Crenshaw Chiropractic with a subpoena for the production of business records.  (Rhoads Decl., ¶ 3.)  The deposition subpoena seeks Plaintiff's medical records “ reflecting or relating to treatment or diagnosis of medical conditions to patient's head, neck, left arm, left shoulder, left leg, chest, and back, including medical and office records and reports, all doctors' and nurses' notes, findings, medical histories, physical examination files, consultation reports, billing documents, x-ray reports, which pertain to the care and treatment prescribed, recommended and/or given, physical therapy treatment, trauma treatment and emergency room.”  (Id., Ex. A.)  Neither Plaintiffs nor Crenshaw Chiropractic has not objected to the deposition subpoena, nor have they otherwise provided the records requested by Defendant.   

Here, the Court find good cause to compel Crenshaw Chiropractic’s compliance with the deposition subpoena.  First, Defendant has submitted evidence demonstrating that both Crenshaw Chiropractic and Plaintiffs were served with copies of the deposition subpoena on February 9, 2026.  (Rhoads Decl., ¶ 3, Exhibit 1.)  Second, Defendant attests that neither Crenshaw Chiropractic nor Plaintiff have objected to the deposition subpoena or provided responsive documents.  (Id. at ¶ 5.)  Third, in compliance with California Rules of Court, rule 3.1346, Defendant has properly personally served Crenshaw Chiropractic with a copy of the present Motion.  (See Declaration of Service; Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1346.)  Lastly, the Court finds that the documents sought by the deposition subpoena are relevant to the litigation because Plaintiffs request compensation for physical injuries. Based on the foregoing, the Court finds good cause to compel Crenshaw Chiropractic’s compliance with the deposition subpoena. 

Further, the Court finds merit to Defendant’s request for monetary sanctions against Chrenshaw Chiropractic.  The Court awards monetary sanctions against Fallas Paredes in the amount of $2,175.  (Rhoads Decl., ¶ 11-13.) 

Conclusion

The Department of Transportation’s Motion to Compel Compliance with Subpoena is GRANTED. The Department of Transportation is granted $2,175 in sanctions.


Case Number: 26SMCV01433    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Sharon Crystal, Inc. v. Sharon Crystal, Case No. 26SMCV01433

Motions:

(1)   Defendants’ Motion for Reconsideration

(2)   Plaintiff’s Motion to Bifurcate

Hearing Date: September 15, 2026

Background

On March 17, 2026, Plaintiffs Sharon Crystal, Inc. (“Crystal”) and Shannon Bankosh (“Bankosh”) sued Defendants Sharon Crystal (“Sharon”), Holly Crystal (“Holly”), Darla Smith, and Chad Miller for twenty claims, including conversion, theft, intentional interference with contractual relations, breach of fiduciary duty, defamation, fraud, and breach of lease. Plaintiffs allege that Sharon is Bankosh and Holly’s mother. (Complaint, ¶ 12.) Plaintiffs allege that Sharon and her husband Terry started Crystal, a company selling goods at Renaissance Faires throughout the country, in the 1970s. (Id. at ¶ 9.) Plaintiffs allege that Sharon and Terry gave control of the company to Bankosh when they decided to retire in 2022, though Sharon remained an active employee. (Id. at ¶ 19.) Plaintiffs allege that Holly was not involved in Crystal until January 2026, when she, Darla Smith, and Chad Miller took over the company and locked Bankosh out of Crystal’s Culver City offices. (Complaint, ¶ 34.)

On April 24, 2026, Sharon and Holly filed a cross-complaint against Bankosh alleging financial elder abuse, fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, cancellation of instruments, and conversion.

On April 30, 2026, Sharon and Holly filed a motion for trial preference. On June 3, 2026, the Court denied Sharon and Holly’s motion for trial preference. Sharon and Holly appealed. On June 16, 2026, the Court received the Court of Appeals’ tentative order directing the court to either vacate the order denying preference and enter a new order granting preference or show cause why a peremptory writ should not issue. On June 17, 2026, the Court issued a minute order setting an OSC re: Appellate Order for July 1, 2026, indicating that it was considering vacating the June 3 order. On June 24, 2026, both parties submitted briefs in anticipation of the July 1 hearing. On July 1, 2026, the Court granted Defendants’ motion for trial preference and set trial for September 28, 2026. The Court also advanced the parties’ pending demurrers and motions to strike to August 5, 2026.

On July 23, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the motion to bifurcate. On September 1, 2026, Defendants filed opposition to the Motion to bifurcate.

On August 10, 2026, following the August 5, 2026, hearing, the Court sustained Defendants’ demurrer to two of Plaintiffs’ claims with leave to amend and four without leave to amend; and sustained Plaintiffs’ demurrer to Defendants’ cross-complaint without leave to amend.

On August 19, 2026, Defendants filed this motion for reconsideration. On September 1, 2026, Plaintiffs filed opposition to the motion for reconsideration. On September 8, 2026, Defendants replied.

Defendants’ opposition to Plaintiff’s motion to bifurcate depends on the Court’s decision on the Motion for Reconsideration, so the Court will decide that motion first:

Motion for Reconsideration

Defendants move for reconsideration of the Court’s August 10, 2026, decision denying Defendants’ leave to amend the cross-complaint. Defendants argue that James Diamond’s deposition on August 10, 2026, after the demurrer hearing, produced evidence that the stock certificate bearing Sharon’s signature and dated January 24, 2022, was actually signed significantly later. (Motion for reconsideration, p. 5.) Defendants also cite emails from Mr. Diamond in December 2022 indicating that the certificate had not yet been signed. (Id. at p. 6.) Defendants argue that Diamond’s testimony shows that though the certificate was dated January 24, 2022, it could not have been signed until December 2022, within the statute of limitations on Defendants’ cross-complaint. (Id. at p. 8.)

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that Defendants have not shown the required diligence and that the facts gleaned from Diamond’s deposition are not new because they were known or readily available before the demurrer hearing as Diamond was Sharon’s attorney and Defendants had access to Sharon’s client file. (Opposition, p. 5-10.) Next, Plaintiffs argue that Sharon’s verified response to Plaintiff’s requests for admission conclusively establishes that Sharon signed the certificate on January 24, 2022, and that the proposed amendment would be futile as a sham pleading. (Id. at p. 11.) On the merits, Plaintiffs argue that the new evidence is insufficient to show that the certificate was actually signed later because Diamond’s testimony was equivocal. (Id. at p. 14.) Next, Plaintiffs argue that granting this motion and permitting Defendants leave to amend would prejudice them because they would have no time to prepare for trial. (Id. at p. 15.) Finally, Plaintiffs argue that the Court’s decision on this motion has no bearing on Defendants’ breach of fiduciary duty claim because the Court’s decision on that claim was not based on the statute of limitations. (Id. at p. 16.)

In reply, Defendants argue that Diamond’s August 10 testimony is new evidence that supports this motion for reconsideration. Defendants argue that Film Packages, Inc. v. Brandywine Film Productions, Ltd. (1987) 193 Cal.App.3d 824, 829 supports a finding that new evidence can cast existing knowledge in a different light and form the basis for a motion for reconsideration. (Reply, p. 3.) Defendants argue that Plaintiffs’ authorities are procedurally and factually distinct from this case. (Id. at p. 4.) Defendants argues that Sharon’s response to Plaintiff’s requests for admission does not eliminate the need for leave to amend and should be addressed through a motion to modify or withdraw the response. (Id. at p. 6.) Plaintiffs argue that the proposed amendment adequately addresses the discovery rule, and the claimed prejudice does not justify denying a meritorious request for leave to amend.

Motion for Reconsideration

“When an application for an order has been made to a judge, or to a court, and refused in whole or in part, or granted, or granted conditionally, or on terms, any party affected by the order may, within 10 days after service upon the party of written notice of entry of the order and based upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, make application to the same judge or court that made the order, to reconsider the matter and modify, amend, or revoke the prior order. The party making the application shall state by affidavit what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd., (a).)  

As it relates to new or different facts, circumstances, or law under Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a), “the moving party must provide a satisfactory explanation for the failure to produce that evidence at an earlier time.” (Shiffer v. CBS Corp. (2015) 240 Cal.App.4th 246, 255.) Furthermore, “facts of which the party seeking reconsideration was aware at the time of the original ruling are not “new or different.” (In re Marriage of Herr (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1463, 1468.)¿ 

Additionally, “[e]very court of record has the inherent power to correct its records so that they shall conform to the facts and speak the truth, and likewise to correct any error or defect occurring in a record through acts of omission or commission of the clerk in entering of record the judgments or orders of the court, and such correction may be made at any time by the court on its own motion.” (Nunn v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 346, 361, quoting Siegal v. Superior Court of Los Angeles County (1968) 68 Cal.2d 97, 101.)

Discussion

Procedural Requirements

Defendants filed the motion for reconsideration less than ten days after service of the Court’s ruling on the demurrer and have attached the appropriate affidavits.

Motion for Reconsideration

The Court agrees with Plaintiffs on all counts.

First, the evidence on which Defendants base their motion is not new because it is based on emails in Defendants’ possession long before the deposition testimony. While the quoted passages Defendants rely on in their motion come from a deposition taken after the demurrer hearing, the emails which provide the basis for Diamond’s deposition testimony were produced before the hearing. Diamond’s statements in his deposition are equivocal. For example, he states that the certificate “would have been signed sometime after all of the other documents would have been signed or completed, I guess.” (Montgomery Decl., Exhibit B, 186:13-16.) Diamond’s subsequent, more concrete statements heavily reference the emails—when he was asked if it was his recollection that he had not sent the certificate to Sharon and Terry to sign as of December 2022, he responded “That’s what the email says.” (Id. at 194:15.) Defendants do not contest that the emails referenced had long been in their possession.

Second, the evidence on which Defendants rely is not new because the evidence was readily available to Defendants before the demurrer hearing. Indeed, at all relevant times, Diamond was Defendants’ attorney. Defendants could have spoken with Diamond at any time and had full access to their own client file, including the emails that support Diamond’s deposition testimony. Defendants also could have communicated with Diamond at any point before the demurrer hearing – including before filing the cross-complaint. There is no evidence Diamond refused to answer questions before his deposition. Importantly, Defendants offer no explanation for their failure to discover this evidence earlier. Instead, they simply insist that Diamond’s schedule required the deposition to take place on August 10. That does not explain why Defendants did not speak with Diamond informally about the key documents and/or timeline as part of their standard due diligence.

Not only could defendants have discovered this information sooner, counsel likely had an affirmative obligation to discover this information prior to asserting these claims. When Defendants decided to pursue a fraud claim based on the 2022 transaction, their counsel had an obligation to conduct a reasonable investigation. “[A]n attorney's duty to exercise the skill and care that a reasonably careful attorney would use in similar circumstances extends to prelitigation investigation and evaluation of a client's potential claims.” (Mireskandari v. Edwards Wildman Palmer LLP (2022) 77 Cal.App.5th 247, 260.) “When one suspects that another has caused harm, a preliminary investigation is usually necessary in order to know whether one has a potential legal claim, evaluate the likelihood of success, and decide whether or not to assert it. Consequently, the investigation of a potential claim is normally and reasonably part of effective litigation, if not an essential part of it.” (Id. (citations omitted).) Any reasonable investigation would have revealed the evidence that they claim is new.

Accordingly, the Court finds that Defendants have not shown new facts or reasonable diligence in discovering those facts.

Even if the Court were to consider Diamond’s deposition new evidence, amendment would be futile because there is a separate reason that Defendants’ claims are time-barred. Sharon was represented by Diamond in the allegedly-fraudulent transaction. Sharon’s attorney (Diamond) prepared the stock certificate in January 2022 transferring the company to Bankosh as an individual. Diamond’s knowledge is imputed to Sharon because he was her agent in the transaction. (Chapman College v. Wagener (1955) 45 Cal.2d 796, 802 [“The general rule of agency is that notice to or knowledge possessed by an agent is imputable to the principal.”]; Roche v. Hyde (2020) 51 Cal.App.5th 757, 797 [“an attorneys’ knowledge is imputed to the principal even where … the agent does not actually communicate with the principal, who thus lacks actual knowledge of the imputed fact.”].) Since Diamond knew the transaction was to Banksoh as an individual in January 2022 when he prepared and dated the unsigned certificate, so did Sharon.

Additionally, Sharon’s verified responses to the requests for admission contain an admission that she signed the certificate in January 2022, directly contradicting the “new” facts Defendants seek to allege. Defendants’ assertion that he will seek to modify or withdraw the response is meaningless because they have not done so and cannot now do so because minimum notice on a motion to modify RFAs is more time than remains until trial. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2033.300.)

Finally, Defendants cannot use their trial preference as both a sword and a shield. At every juncture, Defendants have refused to continue trial to afford the parties more time to litigate the pleadings and complete discovery. Trial is currently set for September 28, 2026 – less than two weeks from this hearing on the motion for reconsideration. Defendants chose to move for trial preference before the pleadings were set. Defendants cannot now argue that the Court should provide the parties with multiple attempts to plead a viable cause of action while at the same time holding steadfast to their right to trial within seventy days.

Motion to Bifurcate

Since the Court denies Defendants’ Motion for Reconsideration, Plaintiff’s Motion to Bifurcate is unopposed. The Court agrees with Plaintiffs’ analysis. The Motion to Bifurcate is GRANTED.

Conclusion

Defendants’ Motion for Reconsideration is DENIED.

Plaintiffs’ Motion to Bifurcate is GRANTED.

Tentative Ruling

Shirley Shelly Bradley v. The Regents of the University of California, Case No. 26SMCV01997

Defendant’s Demurrer with Motion to Strike

Hearing Date: September 15, 2026

Demurrer

On April 9, 2026, Plaintiff Shirley Shelly Bradley sued Defendants The Regents of the University of California and UCLA Health Auxiliary for (1) Dependent and Elder Abuse/Neglect; (2) willful misconduct; and (3) medical negligence. Bradley alleges that she developed pressure ulcers while she was receiving medical treatment at the UCLA Santa Monica Medical Center between April 28, 2025 and July 3, 2025.

On May 28, 2026, Bradley dismissed Defendant UCLA Health Auxiliary.

On June 11, 2026, The Regents filed this demurrer and motion to strike. The regents argue that Plaintiffs do not plead compliance with the claim presentation requirement of the government tort claims act.

The Government Tort Claims act requires claims “relating to a cause of action for death or for injury to a person or to personal property” to be presented to the public entity “not later than six months after the accrual of the cause of action.” (Gov. Code § 911.2(a).) A failure to allege facts demonstrating or excusing compliance with the claim presentation requirements subjects a claim to a demurrer for failure to state a claim. (State of California v. Superior Court (2004) 32 Cal.4th 1234, 1239 (“Bodde”).)

Here, Bradley only alleges that “Plaintiff has complied with applicable claim statutes.” (Complaint, ¶ 9.) This allegation is conclusory and does not satisfy the requirements of Bodde, supra.

Because the Court sustains The Regents’ demurrer based on a lack of detail, leave to amend is appropriate. The Regent’s demurrer is SUSTAINED with twenty (20 days leave to amend.

Because the demurrer is sustained with leave to amend, the motion to strike is moot.
