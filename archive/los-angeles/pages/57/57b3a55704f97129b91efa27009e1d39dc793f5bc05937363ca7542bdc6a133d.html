DEPARTMENT 6 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 20STCV11677    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: 6

CASE NAME:  Nobia Joyce v. Shawn M. Millner, et al. 

Defendants/Cross-Complainants’ Motion to Strike or Tax Trial Costs [MC-010] 

TENTATIVE RULING 

The Court GRANTS in part Defendants/Cross-Complainants’ Motion to Strike or Tax Trial Costs. The Court STRIKES the $2,800.00 charge for the conference room and the $1,301.68 for the deposition costs. The Court DENIES the Motion in all other respects. 

             Defendants are is ordered to give notice of the Court’s ruling within five calendar days of this order. 

BACKGROUND 

This is a quiet title action. On March 20, 2020, plaintiff Nobia Joyce (Plaintiff) filed this action against defendants Shawn M. Millner, the Shawn Marie Millner Revocable Trust (collectively, Millner), Laurence Todd (Todd) (Millner and Todd may be collectively referred to as Defendants), and Does 1 through 20, alleging causes of action for quiet title, partition, declaratory relief, and ejectment and damages. 

On November 29, 2022, Defendants filed a cross-complaint against Plaintiff, Stephen R. Golden, Stephen R. Golden & Associates, P.C., and Does 1 through 20. On February 2, 2024, Defendants filed the Second Amended Cross-Complaint (SACC) against the same parties, alleging causes of action for breach of the July 5, 2018 Settlement Agreement, breach of the covenant of good faith of the July 5, 2018 Settlement Agreement, fraud (No. 1), breach of the August 2018 Co-signor agreement, breach of the covenant of good faith breach [sic] of the August 2018 Co-tenant [sic] agreement, fraud (No. 2), assault, battery, false imprisonment, negligent infliction of emotional distress, intentional infliction of emotional distress, slander of title, fraud (No. 3), invasion of privacy, unjust enrichment, comparative indemnity, apportionment of fault, breach of the May 2023 Settlement Agreement, and breach of the covenant of good faith of the May 2023 Settlement Agreement. 

On May 29, 2024, Defendants filed the operative Third Amended Cross-Complaint (TACC) against Plaintiff, Stephen R. Golden, Stephen R. Golden & Associates, P.C., and Does 1 through 20, alleging causes of action for battery, intentional infliction of emotional distress, slander of title, breach of contract, invasion of privacy, and unjust enrichment. On August 27, 2024, the Court dismissed cross-defendants Stephen R. Golden and Stephen R. Golden & Associates, P.C., from the TACC without prejudice. 

On August 28, 2024, Plaintiff dismissed the First and Second Causes of Action for quiet title and partition as to Defendant Todd without prejudice. 

On March 5, 2026, Plaintiff dismissed Todd from the complaint. 

From March 17, 2026, through March 26, 2026, a jury trial was held. The jury issued a verdict in favor of Plaintiff on her ejectment claim and against Defendant Millner. The jury also issued a verdict in favor of Plaintiff on all claims in the TACC and against Defendants. 

On April 22, 2026, the Court entered judgment according to the jury’s verdict. 

On May 4, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Memorandum of Costs. On May 14, 2026, Defendants moved to strike or tax trial costs. On June 8, 2026, Plaintiff opposed the Motion. On June 17, 2026, Defendants replied to the Opposition. 

LEGAL STANDARD 

(a) Claiming costs 

(1) Trial costs 

A prevailing party who claims costs must serve and file a memorandum of costs within 15 days after the date of service of the notice of entry of judgment or dismissal by the clerk under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5 or the date of service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, or within 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is first. The memorandum of costs must be verified by a statement of the party, attorney, or agent that to the best of his or her knowledge the items of cost are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case. 

… 

(b) Contesting costs 

(1) Striking and taxing costs 

Any notice of motion to strike or to tax costs must be served and filed 15 days after service of the cost memorandum. If the cost memorandum was served by mail, the period is extended as provided in Code of Civil Procedure section 1013. If the cost memorandum was served electronically, the period is extended as provided in Code of Civil Procedure section 1010.6(a)(3). 

(2) Form of motion 

Unless objection is made to the entire cost memorandum, the motion to strike or tax costs must refer to each item objected to by the same number and appear in the same order as the corresponding cost item claimed on the memorandum of costs and must state why the item is objectionable. 

(3) Extensions of time 

The party claiming costs and the party contesting costs may agree to extend the time for serving and filing the cost memorandum and a motion to strike or tax costs. This agreement must be confirmed in writing, specify the extended date for service, and be filed with the clerk. In the absence of an agreement, the court may extend the times for serving and filing the cost memorandum or the notice of motion to strike or tax costs for a period not to exceed 30 days. 

(4) Entry of costs 

After the time has passed for a motion to strike or tax costs or for determination of that motion, the clerk must immediately enter the costs on the judgment. 

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700, bold in original.) 

DISCUSSION 

Summary of Arguments 

Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs must be struck because the underlying March 2026 jury trial was void due to the trial court’s lack of subject matter jurisdiction and violations of the mandatory stay under Code of Civil Procedure section 916. Defendants assert that the Court of Appeal retained exclusive jurisdiction over both the interlocutory partition judgment and the related referee-appointment order until the remittiturs issued on March 25 and 26, 2026, making all trial proceedings, including the $70,120 verdict, legally void. Defendants contend that the trial court compounded these errors by relying on a January 29, 2026 sua sponte order that attempted to fix fatal defects in the interlocutory judgment, despite lacking jurisdiction and never issuing corrected judgments. Defendants argue that the interlocutory judgment remains permanently void under the law of the case because it awarded a collective 50% interest to a non-entity, i.e., “the Trust,” in violation of Portico Mgmt. Grp., LLC v. Harrison (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 464 (Portico). Based on these defects, Defendants maintain that no valid judgment exists to support a cost award, requiring the cost memorandum to be struck in its entirety. 

Defendants also argue that Plaintiff is judicially estopped from recovering any trial costs because she previously declared under penalty of perjury in Defendant’s 2025 bankruptcy case that her claim was limited to a 50% property interest and that she had no ejectment or loss of use claims. Defendants assert that Plaintiff used those statements to obtain an advantage in federal court, then reversed positions to pursue inconsistent claims at the state court trial. Defendants contend Plaintiff commingled time-barred 2024 bench trial expenses with 2026 jury trial expenses, failed to itemize any costs, and sought reimbursement for categories, including a $2,800 conference room rental, that are not allowable or reasonable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5. Defendants argue that Plaintiff sought a double recovery by including deposition and preparation expenses already awarded to Todd on May 13, 2026. Defendants further contend that the cost memorandum misidentifies the courthouse branch, provides no itemization, conflates separate legal capacities, and seeks costs already judicially allocated elsewhere. Defendants finally argue that the entire filing is procedurally void and must be struck or taxed to zero. 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendants’ core claim – that the trials and related nunc pro tunc orders are void – is meritless because both the trial court and the Court of Appeal have already rejected those jurisdictional challenges. Plaintiff contends the conference room rental cost should be allowed because it was necessary for coordinating a multi-attorney pro bono trial team, particularly as two attorneys traveled from distant cities and the team required consistent workspace to prepare effectively. Plaintiff maintains that clerical errors such as listing the wrong courthouse on the cost memorandum are insignificant because the correct case number, judge, and department were provided, and Defendants suffered no prejudice. Plaintiff further argues that worksheets detailing costs are not required unless specific cost items are challenged, and other than the conference room, Defendants raised only generalized objections. Plaintiff also asserts that costs from the 2024 bench trial are not time barred because the final judgment was not entered until 2026, and that no “double subsidy” exists, since each party’s deposition costs relate to different litigation needs. 

In reply, Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s opposition ignores the central, fatal defects that render the entire cost request void, namely, that the second-phase jury trial was conducted while the Court of Appeal still retained exclusive jurisdiction, making all proceedings before the remittiturs legally void. Defendants maintain that the trial court’s January 29, 2026 sua sponte order improperly attempted to modify an already-appealed judgment, further violating the mandatory stay under Code of Civil Procedure section 916 and tainting all subsequent trial activity. Defendants also contend that Plaintiff cannot recover costs because the underlying judgment is unenforceable, as Plaintiff failed to name the indispensable trustee in any operative complaint, meaning the quiet title and partition judgment is void and cannot support a prevailing party cost award. Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s lump sum cost memorandum is defective because it fails to itemize expenses between the valid and void trial phases or between separate parties and claims, preventing the Court from determining which costs are legally recoverable. Defendants further assert that Plaintiff’s cost demands are premature in a partition action, where all litigation expenses must be allocated through the partition referee’s final accounting after the property sale. Finally, Defendants argue that the statute of limitations now bars any attempt to bind the trustee, cementing the judgment’s void status and requiring that Plaintiff’s cost bill be reduced to zero. 

Analysis           

            The Court finds most of Defendants’ motion unpersuasive and unavailing. Plaintiff only needed to provide a verified memorandum of costs and was not required to provide an itemization worksheet. (See Jones v. Dumrichob (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1267 (Jones) [party claiming costs does not need to provide documentary evidence of claimed costs unless those costs have been put in issue via a motion to tax costs].) Plaintiff provided a verified Memorandum of Costs and therefore satisfied her preliminary burden. (Memorandum of Costs (5/4/26).) Defendants then bore the burden of identifying the costs to be challenged and providing evidence to show that Plaintiff’s claimed costs were not reasonable. (See Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 774 (Ladas) [party seeking to tax costs generally bears burden of showing that the costs were not reasonably necessary].) 

Defendants did not provide sufficient evidence to support striking the entire cost memorandum. First, Defendants’ basis for attacking the entire cost memorandum is that the jury trial verdict is void due to the Court’s purported lack of jurisdiction and violation of stay under Code of Civil Procedure section 916. Defendants’ arguments are incorrect. Code of Civil Procedure section 916, subdivision (a), expressly states that, “the trial court may proceed upon any other matter embraced in the action and not affected by the judgment or order.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 916, subd. (a).) The interlocutory judgment only affected Plaintiff’s quiet title and partition claims. (Corrected Interlocutory Judgment (11/5/24).) The jury trial addressed only Plaintiff’s ejectment claim and Defendants’ cross-claims. (Special Verdict (3/26/26).) The Court’s clerical correction to the interlocutory judgment had no bearing on the jury trial. 

Second, courts may make clerical corrections to judgments at any time. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (d).) “’[a] court of general jurisdiction has the power, after final judgment, and regardless of lapse of time, to correct clerical errors or misprisions in its records, whether made by the clerk, counsel, or the court itself, so that the records will conform to and speak the truth.’ [Citation.] ‘The correction may be made even though an appeal has been taken and is pending. [Citations.] And it may also be made after affirmance of the judgment on appeal.’ [Citation.]) (In re Marriage of Boblitt (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 1004, 1028, italics in original.) The Court’s January 29, 2026 correction to the interlocutory judgment merely provided the correct name for Millner as trustee. (Minute Order (1/29/26.) Also, contrary to Defendants’ contention, the interlocutory judgment on file reflects the clerical correction. (Corrected Interlocutory Judgment (11/5/24).) 

Third, Defendants’ contentions regarding Portico are unavailing. The Court of Appeal in Galdjie v. Darwish (2003) 113 Cal.App.4th 1331 affirmed the trial court judgment even though the complaint failed to name the trust or specify that the defendants were being sued in their capacities as trustees. (See id. at pp. 1342, 1350.) Here, the Complaint names both Millner and her trust. (See generally, Compl.) Moreover, Millner answered the Complaint both individually and in her capacity as trustee and otherwise participated throughout this action in both capacities. (See Answer (11/28/22).) 

Finally, the Court of Appeal affirmed the interlocutory judgment in favor of Plaintiff and against Defendants. (Appeal – Opinion Receiver (10/28/25); Remittitur (3/25/26).) Defendants’ arguments that the judgment is void are therefore without merit. 

Notwithstanding the foregoing, the Court finds that the conference room charge was not reasonably incurred. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5. subd. (c)(4).) Plaintiff presented evidence showing that it facilitated the handling of the trial in this matter given the involvement of pro-bono attorneys from different locales throughout California who assisted Plaintiff in this matter. (Gaskins Decl., ¶¶ 2-3; Pallack Decl., ¶ 2.) However, it is unclear to the Court why so many attorneys, i.e., three from Morrison and Foerster and at least one from Neighborhood Legal Services, needed to be involved with the trial of this case. The Court finds that the rental of a conference room to provide working space for attorneys was not reasonably incurred for the trial of this case and that Plaintiff cannot pass on the overhead expense to Defendants as a reasonable cost of litigation. The Court will therefore STRIKE the $2,800.00 charge for the conference room. 

The Court also finds Plaintiff failed to provide evidence showing the deposition costs were reasonably incurred. Defendants specifically challenged those costs, so Plaintiff needed to provide evidence showing they were reasonably incurred. (Jones, supra, 63 Cal.App.4th at p. 1265 [burden shifts to person claiming costs to provide documentary evidence substantiating the claimed costs once they are put in issue via a motion to tax costs].) Plaintiff did not provide any such evidence. It seems Plaintiff is arguing that those costs were incurred taking Defendants’ depositions, but Plaintiff provided no evidence showing that those were the deposition costs claimed. (See Opp., 3:15-21.) The Court will therefore STRIKE the $1,301.68 for deposition costs. 

Defendants did not challenge any other specific items, and the Court otherwise finds Defendants’ remaining arguments unpersuasive. Nothing in the bankruptcy related filing from Plaintiff says anything about Plaintiff not having evidence, not having claims for ejectment or loss of use, or otherwise waiving those claims. (Bowman Decl., Ex. C.) Defendants’ argument regarding the caption page on the cost memorandum is also unavailing. The clerk’s office corrected the mistake after Plaintiff filed it. (Memorandum of Costs (5/4/26).) There is no prejudice to Defendants because the cost memorandum otherwise provided adequate notice of Plaintiff’s claimed costs, which Defendants addressed by filing this Motion. As for the purported failure to apportion liabilities, that is unavailing because Plaintiff prevailed against both Defendants at trial by defeating Defendants’ cross-claims, in addition to prevailing on her ejectment claim against Millner. 

Furthermore, “until a final judgment is entered in the trial itself neither party is entitled to costs. [Citation.]” (Muller v. Reagh (1960) 179 Cal.App.2d 814, 815.) Final judgment in this case was not entered until April 22, 2026. (Judgment (4/22/26).) Plaintiff therefore could not have obtained costs before the final judgment was entered. Also, Plaintiff correctly notes that Todd was awarded costs for his taking of Plaintiff’s deposition before the September 2024 trial. (Order re Court Ruling (5/13/26).) 

Finally, Defendants’ statute of limitations arguments in their reply papers were improper since these were new arguments not raised in their moving papers. New arguments are generally not permitted on reply; otherwise, a further hearing would be required to permit the opposing party to respond. (Jay v. Mahaffey (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 1522, 1537-1538; see also Alfaro v. Cmty. Hous. Improvement Sys. & Plan. Assn., Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1393, fn. 23 [“’The general rule is that points raised for the first time in a reply brief will not be considered unless good cause is shown for the failure to present them before.’ [Citation.]”].) Even then, the arguments fail for the same reasons set forth above, namely that the Court of Appeal in Galdjie v. Darwish, supra, 113 Cal.App.4th 1331 already rejected the argument regarding a complaint that fails to name the defendants in their capacities as trustees. (Id. at pp. 1342, 1350.) 

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS in part the Motion. The Court STRIKES the $2,800.00 charge for the conference room and the $1,301.68 for the deposition costs. The Court DENIES the Motion in all other respects. 

CONCLUSION 

The Court GRANTS in part Defendants/Cross-Complainants’ Motion to Strike or Tax Trial Costs. The Court STRIKES the $2,800.00 charge for the conference room and the $1,301.68 for the deposition costs. The Court DENIES the Motion in all other respects. 

            Defendants are is ordered to give notice of the Court’s ruling within five calendar days of this order.

Case Number: 24PSCV02015    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: 6

CASE NAME:  Daniel Romero Jr. v. Vilma G. Romero, et al. 

Plaintiff Daniel Romero Jr.’s Motion to Confirm Sale and to Approve Referee’s Report 

TENTATIVE RULING 

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff Daniel Romero Jr.’s Motion to Confirm Sale and to Approve Referee’s Report. The Court approves the referee’s report and confirms that the property located at 1710 McKinley Avenue, Pomona, California 91767, bearing Los Angeles County Assessor Parcel Number 8322-025-22 should be sold by private sale to Juan Carlos Obregon Zamudio. The Court directs the referee to deposit the net sale proceeds with the Court within 20 calendar days’ completion of the sale. The Court will set a further hearing for the distribution of the sale proceeds at the hearing on this motion. 

            Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of the Court’s ruling within five calendar days of this order. 

BACKGROUND 

This is an action for partition of real property. On June 24, 2024, plaintiff Daniel G. Romero Jr. (Plaintiff) filed this action against defendants Vilma G. Romero, individually, and as trustee of the Vilma Gladiz Romero Trust dated May 11, 2020 (Vilma), Mortgage Electronic Registration Systems, Inc. (MERS), and Does 1 through 10, alleging the sole cause of action for partition. On August 20, 2024, Vilma answered the complaint. On September 25, 2024, Vilma filed a First Amended Answer (the “Amended Answer”). 

Following a non-jury trial on January 12, 13, and 14, 2026, the Court found Plaintiff owns a 1/3 interest in the subject property and that Defendant Vilma owns a 2/3 interest in the subject property. The Court further found Plaintiff was entitled to a partition by sale and ordered the property sold. On January 29, 2026, the Court entered an interlocutory judgment. On February 25, 2026, the Court entered an amended interlocutory judgment.    

On February 13, 2026, Plaintiff moved to confirm the sale and to approve referee’s report. On April 16, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s ex parte application to shorten the time for hearing on this motion to April 28, 2026. The Court ordered that any opposition was due by April 22, 2026, and that any reply was due by noon on April 24, 2026. On April 28, 2026, the Court continued the hearing to June 11, 2026, and ordered Plaintiff to serve a notice of sale fully compliant with Code of Civil Procedure sections 701.540 and 873.640. The Court further ordered Plaintiff to provide a supplemental declaration setting forth the costs of partition to be deducted from the sale price, if any. Plaintiff did not provide the supplemental declaration. 

Following the initial hearing on June 11, 2026, the Court continued the hearing on the motion and directed Plaintiff to submit a supplemental declaration by June 18, 2026. On June 18, 2026, Plaintiff submitted the supplemental declaration and a supplemental memorandum in support of the motion. No opposition has been filed. 

LEGAL STANDARD 

(a) A purchaser, the referee, or any party may move the court to confirm or set aside the sale. 

(b) The moving party shall give not less than 10 days' notice of motion to: 

(1) The purchaser if the purchaser is not the moving party; and 

(2) All other parties who have appeared in the action. 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 873.720.) 

(a) At the hearing, the court shall examine the report and witnesses in relation to the report. 

(b) The court may confirm the sale notwithstanding a variance from the prescribed terms of sale if to do so will be beneficial to the parties and will not result in substantial prejudice to persons interested in the sale. 

(c) The court may vacate the sale and direct that a new sale be made if it determines any of the following: 

(1) The proceedings were unfair or notice of sale was not properly given. If there is no finding at the hearing of unfairness or improper notice, the sale may thereafter not be attacked on such grounds. 

(2) The sale price is disproportionate to the value of the property. 

(3) It appears that a new sale will yield a sum that exceeds the sale price by at least 10 percent on the first ten thousand dollars ($10,000) and 5 percent on the amount in excess thereof, determined after a reasonable allowance for the expenses of a new sale.” 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 873.730.) 

PRELIMINARY ISSUES 

            Plaintiff’s notice of motion, amended notice of motion, and notice of continuation contain inaccurate information regarding the Los Angeles County Superior Court’s tentative ruling system, including citation to a non-existent local rule, among other inaccuracies. The Court again admonishes Plaintiff’s counsel to not provide this information in its motion notices going forward. If this continues, the Court may set an order to show cause re sanctions and other disciplinary measures. 

DISCUSSION 

            Plaintiff moves the Court to approve the sale of the subject property for $630,000.00. Plaintiff indicates that the property has two liens of record in favor of Mortgage Electronic Registration Systems, Inc. (MERS) and Plaintiff’s counsel’s firm, but only the lien in favor of MERS will be paid through the escrow proceeds. Plaintiff indicates that the court-appointed referee, Laura Vazquez (Vazquez), recommends the property be sold by private sale through standard commercially available methods rather than public auction because it is likely to result in the highest possible price, which will be more beneficial to the parties. Plaintiff indicates Vazquez also recommends selling the property to the highest cumulative bidder and to sell the property as is. 

Vazquez’s declaration indicates having marketed the property on MLS on April 1, 2026, with a listing price of $599,000.00, and that on April 7, 2026, Juan Carlos Obregon Zamudio submitted an offer to purchase the property for $630,000.00. Vazquez indicates that the sale will be conducted in a fair manner and in accordance with the terms of the sale, and that a confirmation of the sale will be beneficial to the parties and will not result in substantial prejudice to persons interested in the sale. Vazquez indicates that $630,000.00 is a fair and adequate price for the property, it is proportionate to the value of the property, and that Vazquez has no reason to believe that a new sale will yield a sum that exceeds the sale price by ten percent on the first $10,000.00 and five percent on any amount in excess thereof. Vazquez also indicates that the opening and closing of public and private ways, roads, streets, and easements is unnecessary. (Vazquez Decl., ¶¶ 8-13.) 

In Plaintiff’s supplemental memorandum in support of the motion, Plaintiff argues that no prejudice will result from the sale because the parties now have agreed to sell the subject property to this buyer. Plaintiff contends prejudice may result from the denial of the sale because neither party has paid the mortgage since Vilma vacated the subject property. Plaintiff then contends the sale substantially complied with the law and cites the case of Cummings v. Dessel (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 589 (Cummings) for the proposition that the notice of sale defects in this case were harmless. Plaintiff contends Vazquez posted a “FOR SALE” sign on the physical property with her contact information, which gave notice to the public of the sale. Plaintiff also contends Vilma was given proper notice of all court proceedings. Plaintiff contends a buyer has been identified, the price has been agreed upon, and all parties consent to the sale. 

The Court still finds the proposed sale price of $630,000.00 to be fair and reasonable, and that the report is largely adequate. The Court also finds that Vazquez’s supplemental declaration provides a breakdown of the partition costs to be deducted from the sale proceeds. (See Vazquez Suppl. Decl., ¶¶ 8-18; Code Civ. Proc., § 874.010.) 

Plaintiff still has not served a notice of sale by mail and posting, or otherwise complied with Code of Civil Procedure section 701.540. Posting a “FOR SALE” sign on the subject property does not satisfy the posting requirement. (See Vazquez Suppl. Decl., ¶¶ 4-5.) Notice of sale by posting requires the notice to be posted “in the following places: (1) One public place in the city in which the interest in the real property is to be sold if it is to be sold in a city or, if not to be sold in a city, one public place in the county in which the interest in the real property is to be sold. (2) A conspicuous place on the real property.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 701.540, subd. (d).) It also appears that Plaintiff is required to provide notice by publication 20 days before the sale and mail each lienholder the notice of sale 20 days before the date of sale. (Id., § 701.540, subds. (g)-(h).) 

Nevertheless, the Court of Appeal in Cummings affirmed the trial court’s judgment notwithstanding the lack of proper notice of sale by publication or by posting per Code of Civil Procedure section 701.540. (Cummings, supra, 13 Cal.App.5th at pp. 601-602.) The Court here similarly finds that the failure to provide notice of sale fully in compliance with Code of Civil Procedure section 701.540 is harmless, as there is no evidence of prejudice to the parties. Vazquez did at least post a “FOR SALE” sign on the property and provided an MLS listing. (Vazquez Decl., ¶¶ 4-5, Exs. 1-2.) Moreover, the parties otherwise appear to agree on the sale price. Throughout the proceedings in connection with this motion, Vilma has not once opposed the motion or otherwise indicated that the sale price is inadequate. (See D.I. Chadbourne, Inc. v. Superior Court (1964) 60 Cal.2d 723, 728, fn. 4 [where nonmoving party fails to oppose a ground for a motion, “it is assumed that [nonmoving party] concedes” that ground].)  

Furthermore, Code of Civil Procedure section 701.560, subdivision (a), provides that, “[f]ailure to give notice of sale as required by this article does not invalidate the sale.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 701.560, subd. (a).) Code of Civil Procedure section 873.730, subdivision (b), also provides that, “[t]he court may confirm the sale notwithstanding a variance from the prescribed terms of sale if to do so will be beneficial to the parties and will not result in substantial prejudice to persons interested in the sale.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 873.730, subd. (b).) 

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS the motion. 

CONCLUSION 

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff Daniel Romero Jr.’s Motion to Confirm Sale and to Approve Referee’s Report. The Court approves the referee’s report and confirms that the property located at 1710 McKinley Avenue, Pomona, California 91767, bearing Los Angeles County Assessor Parcel Number 8322-025-22 should be sold by private sale to Juan Carlos Obregon Zamudio. The Court directs the referee to deposit the net sale proceeds with the Court within 20 calendar days’ completion of the sale. The Court will set a further hearing for the distribution of the sale proceeds at the hearing on this motion. 

            Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of the Court’s ruling within five calendar days of this order.
