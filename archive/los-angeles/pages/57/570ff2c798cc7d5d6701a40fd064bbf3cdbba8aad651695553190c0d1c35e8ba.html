BACKGROUND

Factual Background

On August 9, 2023, plaintiff Kevin Symons (“Plaintiff”) filed a Complaint against defendant General Motors (“Defendant”) and DOES 1 through 20.  On November 20, 2023, Plaintiff submitted a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”).  On March 22, 2024, Plaintiff filed a Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) that included the following causes of action: (1) Fraud – Concealment; (2) Business and Professions Code § 17200; (3) Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act – Breach of Express Warranty; (4) Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act – Breach of Implied Warranty; and (5) Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act – Civil Code § 1793.2(b).  On August 20, 2024, the Court sustained Defendant’s demurrer to the first and second causes of action but granted Plaintiff leave to amend.  However, Plaintiff did not file a Third Amended Complaint.

On March 3, 2026, the parties executed a Code of Civil Procedure section 998 Offer of Compromise.  Plaintiff accepted Defendant’s offer on March 3, 2026, with Defendant agreeing to pay the sum of $9,000.00, in addition to Plaintiff’s “attorney’s fees, expenses, and costs that have been reasonably incurred pursuant to California Civil code § 1794(d)[.]”  (Declaration of Guy Mizrahi (“Mizrahi Decl.”), ¶ 8, Exh. D.)  Furthermore, Defendant acknowledged Plaintiff as the prevailing party for the purposes of this motion.  (Mizrahi Decl., ¶ 8, Exh. D.)

On March 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Settlement of Entire Case.

LEGAL STANDARD

Attorneys’ fees are allowed as costs when authorized by contract, statute, or law.  (Code Civ. Proc, § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10)(B).)  In a lemon law action, costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees, may be recovered by a prevailing buyer under the Song-Beverly Act.  (See Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)

Section 1794 provides: “If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.”  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)

Thus, the statute includes a “reasonable attorney’s fees” standard.  The attorney bears the burden of proof as to the “reasonableness” of any fee claim.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(5).)  This burden requires competent evidence as to the nature and value of the services rendered.  (Martino v. Denevi (1986) 182 Cal.App.3d 553, 559.)  “Testimony of an attorney as to the number of hours worked on a particular case is sufficient evidence to support an award of attorney fees, even in the absence of detailed time records.”  (Ibid.)

A plaintiff’s verified billing invoices are prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses, and services listed were necessarily incurred.  (See Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682.)  “In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence.  General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.”  (Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 459, 488, quoting Premier Med. Mgmt. Sys., Inc. v. California Ins. Guarantee Ass’n (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 564.)  The Court has discretion to reduce fees that result from inefficient or duplicative use of time.  (Horsford v. Bd. of Trustees of California State Univ. (2005) 132 Cal. App. 4th 359, 395.)

In determining a reasonable attorney fee, the trial court begins with the lodestar, i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.  (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 36.)  The lodestar may then be adjusted based on factors specific to the case in order to fix the fee at the fair market value of the legal services provided.  (Ibid.)  The factors that courts look at to determine if a multiplier is reasonable are: “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, and the skill displayed in presenting them; (2) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys; (3) the contingent nature of the fee award” and (4) the success achieved.  (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49 (Serrano).)

No specific findings reflecting the court’s calculations are required.  The record only needs to show that the attorney’s fees were awarded according to the “lodestar” or “touchstone” approach.  The court’s focus in evaluating the facts should be to provide a fee award reasonably designed to completely compensate attorneys for the services provided.¿ The starting point for this determination is the attorney’s time records.  (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of Calif. State Univ. (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 395-397 [verified time records entitled to credence absent clear indication they are erroneous].)  However, California case law permits fee awards in the absence of detailed time sheets.  (Sommers v. Erb (1992) 2 Cal.App.4th 1644, 1651; Dunk v. Ford Motor Co. (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 1794, 1810; Nightingale v. Hyundai Motor America (1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 99, 103.)  An experienced trial judge is in a position to assess the value of the professional services rendered in his or her court.  (Serrano, supra, 20 Cal.3d 25 at p. 49.)

Reasonableness of the Hours Incurred

Fraud

Defendant argues that the Court should apportion and decline to award $5,430.00 in fees related to Plaintiff’s fraud causes of action after the Court repeatedly sustained Defendant’s demurrer to these claims.  (Opp., pp. 1:26-2:5, 24-27, 3:26-4:10.)  

“When a cause of action for which attorney fees are provided by statute is joined with other causes of action for which attorney fees are not permitted, the prevailing party may recover only on the statutory cause of action.”  (Akins v. Enterprise Rent-A-Car Co. of San Francisco (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 1127, 1133.)  “Such fees need not be apportioned when incurred for representation on an issue common to both causes of action in which fees are proper and those in which they are not.  [Citation.]  Apportionment is not required when the claims for relief are so intertwined that it would be impracticable, if not impossible, to separate the attorney’s time into compensable and noncompensable units.”  (Bell v. Vista Unified School District (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 672, 687.)

According to Defendant, Plaintiff’s counsel billed a total of $5,430.00 from August 18, 2023, to August 27, 2024, related to their fraud causes of action.  (Opp., pp. 4:11-5:15; Mizrahi Decl., Exh. B.)  This amount includes the following entries: (1) $57.50 for 0.1 hours spent by Mizrahi on reviewing Defendant’s meet and confer letter regarding Plaintiff’s fraud claims on August 18, 2023; (2) $690.00 for 1.2 hours spent by Mizrahi on preparing opposition briefs to Defendant’s demurrer and motion to strike on October 18, 2023; (3) $1,050.00 for 2.8 hours spent by Le on preparing for and attending the hearing on Defendant’s demurrer and motion to strike on October 30 and 31, 2023; (4) $345.00 for 0.6 hours spent by Mizrahi on preparing the FAC on November 20, 2023; (5) $115.00 for 0.2 hours spent by Mizrahi on a telephonic conference with Defendant’s counsel regarding the FAC, which reasserted the fraud claims, on December 8, 2023; (6) $765.00 for 1.4 hours spent by Mizrahi on preparing opposition briefs to Defendant’s demurrer and motion to strike and reviewing Defendant’s reply brief on February 22 and 29, 2024; (7) $487.50 for 1.3 hour spent by Le on preparing for and attending the hearing on the demurrer, motion to strike, and case management conference on March 6, 2024; (8) $517.50 for 0.9 hours spent by Mizrahi on preparing the SAC, which again reasserted the fraud claims, on March 6, 2024; (9) $37.50 for 0.1 hours spent by Le on receiving and reviewing Defendant’s Notice of Ruling regarding the demurrer and motion to strike hearing on March 20, 2024; (10) $37.50 for 0.1 hours spent by Le on receiving and reviewing Defendant’s demurrer and motion to strike to the SAC on April 19, 2024; (11) $862.50 for 2.3 hours spent by Le on preparing opposition briefs to Defendant’s demurrer and motion to strike, and reviewing Defendant’s reply brief on August 7, 8, and 13, 2024; (12) $525.00 for 1.4 hours spent by Le on preparing for and attending the hearing on the demurrer and motion to strike on August 20, 2024; and (13) $37.50 for 0.1 hours spent by Le on receiving and reviewing Defendant’s Notice of Ruling regarding the demurrer and motion to strike hearing on August 27, 2024.  (Opp., pp. 4:11-5:15; Mizrahi Decl., Exh. B.)

The Court clarifies that the total amount of these invoices is $5,527.50, as opposed to $5,430.00.  Furthermore, the Court observes that the invoice dated March 6, 2024, is associated with a case management conference and a hearing regarding the demurrer and motion to strike concerning Plaintiff’s fraud claims.  Consequently, the Court allocates 0.3 hours for the case management conference and 1.0 hours for the hearing on the demurrer and motion to strike.  These adjustments result in a contested amount of $5,415.00.

In contending that apportionment is unnecessary, Plaintiff references Santana v. FCA US, LLC (2020) 56 Cal.Rptr.3d 334.  (Reply, pp. 9:13-23, 10:6-11.)  Plaintiff asserts that the Court need not apportion fees between the claims arising under the Song-Beverly Act and those based on fraud, since “the fraud and Song-Beverly claims share the same essential facts, [rendering] apportionment [] neither required nor practicable.”  (Reply, p. 10:12-13.)

Although the Court concurs that, in numerous instances, it may be impractical or impossible to apportion fees between claims that share significant commonalities, this particular case does not exemplify such a scenario.  The fees under consideration pertain specifically to the meet-and-confer process, demurrers, motions to strike, and notices of rulings that only directly involved Plaintiff’s fraud claims.  Defendant has submitted a list of apportioned fees for the Court’s consideration.  As a result, the Court declines to award $5,415.00 in fees related to Plaintiff’s fraud claims.

Pre-Client Engagement & Representation Work

Defendant asserts that the Court should not award the sum of $920.00 for 1.6 hours of work dedicated to telephonic conferences with Plaintiff, reviewing documents in preparation for representation, and for receiving and reviewing the representation agreement.  (Opp., p. 6:24-27.)  Defendant argues that “initial consultation and due diligence prior to engagement are business expenses routinely treated as part of overhead[,]” and insists that a formal client relationship was not established until June 29, 2023.  (Opp., pp. 6:27-7:4.) 

The work undertaken was reasonably incurred by Plaintiff in connection with the commencement and prosecution of this action.  Moreover, Defendant fails to provide any case law that supports the assertion that Plaintiff cannot recover attorney’s fees for pre-litigation activities or that such expenses are not recoverable.

Consequently, the request to reduce attorneys’ fees on these grounds is DENIED.

Duplicative, Block-Billed Case Analysis Work & Case Review/Updates to Client

Defendant contends that the Court should reduce the requested sum of $2,472.50 to $1,311.25 for 4.3 hours of work performed on specific dates, notably August 23, 2023, October 25 and 26, 2023, November 10 and 11, 2023, December 9, 2024, January 7, 2025, and February 20, 2026.  (Opp., p. 7:5-15.)  This request is based on the assertion that certain billing entries are block-billed, excessive, duplicative, or administrative in nature.  (Opp., p. 7:15-19.) 

Upon review, the Court finds that the tasks in question are neither excessive nor duplicative, and they do not qualify as bill-blocked entries.  Moreover, Defendant fails to provide a sufficient explanation regarding how these entries are deemed bill-blocked, excessive, or duplicative.  Additionally, while Defendant argues that some tasks are administrative, they have failed to cite relevant case law that establishes that such tasks are non-compensable, nor have they clarified which specific tasks they classify as administrative.

Consequently, the request to reduce attorneys’ fees on these grounds is DENIED.

Preparing Templated Discovery Requests

Defendant contends that the Court should reduce the requested sum of $690.00 to $287.50 for the 1.2 hours spent by Mizrahi on preparing Plaintiff’s requests for form interrogatories, special interrogatories, requests for admissions, and requests for production of documents on January 29, 2024.  (Opp., p. 7:20-21.)  Defendant argues that this time is excessive, given that Plaintiff utilized templates.  (Opp., p. 7:22-27.) 

Although Plaintiff acknowledges the use of templates, the Court understands that Plaintiff’s counsel must still devote time to tailoring the four discovery requests to the specifics of the case.  (Reply, p. 5:14-17.)  Thus, the Court finds that the 1.2 hours claimed are reasonable and declines to reduce the requested attorney’s fees on this ground.

Review of Defendant’s Discovery Responses & Preparation of Templated Meet and Confer Correspondence

Defendant contends that the Court should reduce the requested sum of $1,200.00 to $375.00 for the 3.2 hours spent by Le on reviewing Defendant’s discovery responses and documents, preparing meet-and-confer letters related to these responses, and analyzing Defendant’s standard response letters on February 29, March 1 and 19, and April 5, 11, and 23, 2024.  (Opp., p. 8:1-5.)  Defendant argues that Plaintiff utilized templates and that Defendant’s responses do not vary significantly from case to case.  (Opp., p. 8:5-10.) 

Although Defendant employed similar responses and letters, the Court recognizes that Plaintiff’s counsel must still allocate time to review these responses and draft meet-and-confer letters that specifically address the current discovery responses under consideration.  Thus, the Court finds that the 3.2 hours claimed are reasonable.  The request to reduce the attorney’s fees on this ground is DENIED.

Templated Discovery Responses

Defendant contends that the Court should reduce the requested sum of $1,500.00 to $262.50 for the 4.0 hours spent by Le on drafting Plaintiff’s responses to Defendant’s requests for form interrogatories, special interrogatories, requests for admissions, and requests for production of documents on March 26, 2024.  (Opp., p. 8:11-12.)  Defendant argues that Plaintiff utilized templates and that the responses were not novel to this case.  (Opp., p. 8:13-17.) 

Although Plaintiff provided similar responses, the Court recognizes that Plaintiff’s counsel must still dedicate time to ensure that these responses specifically address the current discovery requests.  Thus, the Court finds that the 4.0 hours claimed are reasonable.  The request to reduce attorney’s fees on this ground is DENIED.

Motions to Compel Further Discovery Responses

Defendant contends that the Court should reduce the requested sum of $5,850.00 to $1,875.00 for the 14.6 hours spent by Le on tasks related to Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses to Plaintiff’s special interrogatories and requests for production of documents.  (Opp., p. 8:18-21.)  The defendant argues that the time claimed is excessive, as Plaintiff utilized templates and dedicated 2.3 hours to drafting an amended motion to compel further responses to a request for production of documents, which was ultimately denied.  (Opp., pp. 8:21-9:3.)

However, the Plaintiff must spend time revising motions to ensure they are applicable to this specific case.  Moreover, the Court notes that although the amended motion was denied, the “dispute was [ultimately] resolved through further meet-and-confer and voluntary supplemental production[,]” a development that likely occurred as a direct result of Plaintiff’s motion.  (Reply, p. 6:26-28.)  Thus, the Court finds that the 14.6 hours claimed are reasonable.  The request to reduce attorney’s fees on these grounds is DENIED.

Motion to Compel Person Most Knowledgeable (“PMK”) Deposition

The defendant argues that the Court should decline to award $1,912.50 in attorneys’ fees associated with the motion to compel the deposition of Defendant’s PMK, because the Court ultimately denied the motion due to Plaintiff’s failure to engage in the meet and confer process in accordance with the applicable statutes.  (Opp., p. 9:4-11.) 

While it is accurate that the Court denied the motion on the basis of Plaintiff’s failure to adhere to meet and confer requirements, this procedural denial does not imply that “the underlying dispute lacked merit[,]” as Plaintiff aptly points out.  (Reply, p. 7:12.)  The Court acknowledges that, in general, testimony from a PMK concerning a manufacturer’s knowledge of a defect is standard and pertinent to civil penalty exposure.  (Reply, p. 7:14-16.)  Consequently, the Court concludes that the motion was reasonably incurred in connection with the commencement and prosecution of this action.  Thus, the request to reduce the attorney’s fees on this ground is DENIED.

Templated Motions in Limine

Defendant contends that the Court should reduce the requested sum of $1,012.50 to $187.50 for the 2.7 hours spent by Le on drafting nine motions in limine.  (Opp., p. 9:12-19.)  Defendant argues that the claimed time is excessive, as Plaintiff utilized templates, and it is questionable that Plaintiff dedicated precisely 0.3 hours to each motion.  (Opp., pp. 8:21-9:3.) 

First, the Court finds that 0.3 hours, equivalent to 20 minutes, constitutes a reasonable amount of time for each motion to ensure that they accurately represent the specifics of this case.  Moreover, although it may appear unusual for each motion to take exactly 20 minutes, the Court deems this not improbable, given the considerable similarity among the templated motions, which may allow for efficient time management.  Thus, the Court finds that the 2.7 hours claimed are reasonable. The request to reduce the attorney’s fees on these grounds is DENIED.

OSC Hearing re: Dismissal

Defendant argues that the Court should decline to award $487.50 in attorneys’ fees related to the Order to Show Cause re: Dismissal on May 18, 2026, stating that “[t]he OSC hearing was only necessary because Counsel had yet to file a fee motion.”  (Opp., p. 9:23-24.) 

The hearing was necessitated by Plaintiff’s filing of a notice of settlement for entire case and would have proceeded regardless of whether Plaintiff filed the noticed motion.  Thus, the Court finds that the hours claimed are reasonable. The request to reduce the attorneys’ fees on this ground is DENIED.

Fee Motion

Defendant contends that the Court should reduce the requested sum of $3,622.50 to $1,725.00 for the 6.3 hours spent by Mizrahi on this motion.  (Opp., pp. 9:27-10:11.)  This amount includes the following entries: (1) $402.50 for 0.7 hours spent by Mizrahi on auditing Plaintiff’s timesheets, calculating costs, and attempting to resolve this motion informally; (2) $1,207.50 for 2.1 hours spent by Mizrahi on drafting this motion; and (3) $2,012.50 for 3.5 projected hours allocated to reviewing the opposition brief, composing a reply brief, and attending the hearing.  (Opp., pp. 9:27-10:3; Mizrahi Decl., Exh. B.)  Defendant contends that the claimed time is excessive, asserting that Plaintiff utilized templates and arguing that “auditing billing records is a ministerial and business task not properly charged to one’s client and should not be imputed to” Defendant.  (Opp., p. 9:8-9.) 

The Court first observes that Defendant has not provided any statute or case law to substantiate the claim that auditing constitutes a non-compensable task.  Additionally, the Court finds the requested time to be reasonable.  While the Court acknowledges that the motion is based on a template, it deems 2.1 hours to be a reasonable duration for editing the template and providing the requisite supporting documentation.  Likewise, the Court considers 3.5 hours to be appropriate for reviewing Defendant’s opposition, drafting a reply, and attending the hearing, given the numerous arguments presented by Defendant in its opposition, along with the Court’s expectation that the hearing should last approximately one hour.

Consequently, the request to reduce the attorneys’ fees on these grounds is DENIED.

Clerical/Administrative/General Case Management Tasks

The defendant argues that the Court should decline to award $1,867.00 in attorneys’ fees associated with various clerical and administrative tasks, arguing that such work constitutes overhead and should not be charged to opposing counsel.  (Opp., p. 10:12-24.) 

First, Defendant cites Morris v. Hyundai Motor America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 37 to support the assertion that time spent on these tasks is non-compensable due to its classification as overhead.  (Opp., p. 10:19-24.)  However, the Court observes that the cited section makes no reference to the exclusion of overhead or clerical, administrative, and general case management tasks from a request for attorneys’ fees.

Second, Defendant provides insufficient detail regarding the specific clerical, administrative, and general case management tasks in question.  The absence of dates in the entries prevents the Court from reviewing them to determine their nature.

Thus, the Court finds that the hours claimed are reasonable. The request to reduce the attorneys’ fees on this ground is DENIED.

Costs and Expenses

The plaintiff seeks reimbursement in the amount of $1,499.05 for incurred costs and expenses.  (Motion, pp. 7:17-19; 9:1-15.)  In support of this request, Plaintiff has attached a memorandum of costs.  (Mizrahi Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. C.)  However, as Defendant points out, Plaintiff has not provided any documentation, specifically invoices or receipts, to substantiate the claimed costs.  (Opp., p. 11:4-6.)  In the absence of such evidence, the Court is unable to ascertain whether these costs were reasonably incurred, or indeed, incurred at all.  Given that Defendant limits its objections to $402.05 of the requested costs, the Court will not approve any costs without the requisite supporting documentation.  (Opp., pp. 11:3-12:10.)

As such, Plaintiff’s motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $30,169.50, calculated as follows: $35,986.55 - $5,415.00 - $402.05.

On February 26, 2025, Plaintiff filed an amendment to the Complaint, naming Hamlet Karabekyam as a defendant.

On August 20, 2025, Plaintiff filed another amendment to the Complaint, notifying the Court and other parties that the correct name of Hamlet Karabekyam is, in fact, Hamlet Karabekyan (“Defendant”).

On October 22, 2025, Plaintiff served Defendant with Special Interrogatories, Set One.  (Declaration of Rosibeth Cuevas (“Cuevas Decl.”), ¶ 2, Exh. 1.)  Subsequently, on November 24, 2025, Defendant submitted discovery responses accompanied by a privilege log.  (Cuevas Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 2.)

On December 31, 2025, Plaintiff’s counsel emailed a meet-and-confer letter to Defendant’s counsel regarding Special Interrogatories Nos. 25, 27, 28, and 29.  (Cuevas Decl., ¶ 4, Exhs. 3-4.)

LEGAL STANDARDS

Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300 provides that “[o]n receipt of a response to interrogatories, the propounding party may move for an order compelling a further response if the propounding party deems that” the responses contain: (1) answers that are evasive or incomplete; (2)¿an unwarranted or insufficiently specific exercise of an option to produce documents in lieu of a substantive response; or (3) unmerited or overly generalized objections.  (Code Civ. Proc., §¿2030.300, subd. (a).)  “[N]otice of this motion is given within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the propounding party and the responding party have agreed in writing, the propounding party waives any right to compel a further response to the interrogatories.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §¿2030.300, subd. (c).)  Moreover, “[a] motion under subdivision (a) shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §¿2030.300, subd. (b)(1).)

Additionally, “Unless otherwise limited by order of the court in accordance with this title, any party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or to the determination of any motion made in that action, if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.”  (Code of Civ. Proc., § 2017.010.)  For discovery purposes, information is regarded as relevant “if it might reasonably assist a party in evaluating the case, preparing for trial, or facilitating settlement thereof.”  (City of Los Angeles v. Superior Court (2017) 9 Cal.App.5th 272, 288.)

“The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a further response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §¿2030.300, subd. (d).)

A motion to compel further responses to a demand for inspection or production of documents may be brought based on: (1) incomplete statements of compliance; (2) inadequate, evasive, or incomplete claims of inability to comply; or (3) unmeritorious or overly generalized objections.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).)  Moreover, “[t]he motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §¿2031.310, subd. (b)(2).)

A motion to compel further production must set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the inspection demand.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).)  “To establish good cause, a discovery proponent must identify a disputed fact that is of consequence in the action and explain how the discovery sought will tend in reason to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact.”  (Digital Music News LLC v Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 216, 224 (“Digital Music News LLC”).)  If the moving party has shown good cause for the requests for production, the burden is on the objecting party to justify the objections.  (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal. App.4th 92, 98.)

“[T]he court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further response to a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (h).)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030, subdivision (a), “The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.  The court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney who advised that assertion, or on both.  If a monetary sanction is authorized by any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (a).)  “Misuses of the discovery process include … (d) Failing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subd. (d).)  “The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though no opposition to the motion was filed, or opposition to the motion was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1348(a).)

MEET AND CONFER REQUIREMENT

The plaintiff was required to meet and confer with Defendant before filing these motions.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, subd. (b)(1), 2031.310, subd. (b)(2).)  Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040 states that a meet and confer declaration “in support of a motion [to compel] shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.)  “ ‘The parties must present to each other the merits of their respective positions with the same candor, specificity and support during informal negotiations as during the briefing of discovery motions.  Only after all the cards have been laid on the table, and a party has meaningfully assessed the relative strengths and weaknesses of its position in light of all available information, can there be a ‘sincere’ effort to resolve the matter.’ ”  (In re Marriage of Moore (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 1275, 1293-1294, quoting Townsend v. Superior Court (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 1431, 1435.)

LEGAL STANDARDS

A party must respond to interrogatories, requests for production, or requests for admission within thirty (30) days after service.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.260, subd. (a), 2031.260, subd. (a), 2033.250, subd. (a).)

If a party to whom interrogatories are directed does not provide timely responses, the requesting party may move for an order compelling responses to the discovery.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (b).)  The party also waives the right to make any objections, including one based on privilege or work-product protection.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).)  There is no time limit for a motion to compel responses to interrogatories other than the cut-off on hearing discovery motions, fifteen (15) days before trial.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2024.020, subd. (a), 2030.290.)  No meet and confer efforts are required before filing a motion to compel responses to the discovery.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290; Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 411 (Sinaiko).)

“The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (c).)

Further, when there has been no timely response to a Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.010 demand, the demanding party may seek an order compelling a response.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (b).)  Failure to respond in a timely manner waives all objections, including those based on privilege or work-product protection.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).)  Thus, unless the party to whom the demand was directed obtains relief from a waiver, they cannot raise objections to the documents demanded.  There is no time limit for a motion to compel responses to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling other than the cut-off on hearing discovery motions, fifteen (15) days before trial.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2024.020, subd. (a), 2031.300.)  Likewise, for failure to respond, the moving party need not attempt to resolve the matter outside court before filing the motion.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300.)  Where the motion seeks only a response to the inspection demand, no showing of “good cause” is required.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300.)

“[T]he court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (c).)

Additionally, if a party to whom requests for admission are directed fails to serve a timely response, the unresponsive party waives any objection to the requests, including one based on privilege or the protection for work product.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (a).)  The court, on motion, may relieve that party from this waiver on its determination that the party subsequently served a response that is in substantial compliance and if the party’s failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (b)(1)-(2).)

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.280, “[i]f a party to whom requests for admission are directed fails to serve a timely response,” the court shall make the order deeming the truth of the matters admitted unless the responding party serves before the hearing a proposed response to the requests for admission that is in substantial compliance with Code Civil Procedure section 2033.220.  Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.220 requires each answer to either admit, deny, or specify that the responding party lacks sufficient information or knowledge.  As stated in Demyer v. Costa Mesa Mobile Home Estates, the moving party is not required to meet and confer before bringing this action.  (Demyer v. Costa Mesa Mobile Home Estates, 36 Cal.App.4th 393, 395.)

“It is mandatory that the court impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) on the party or attorney, or both, whose failure to serve a timely response to requests for admission necessitated this motion.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030, subdivision (a), “The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.  The court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney who advised that assertion, or on both.  If a monetary sanction is authorized by any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (a).)  “Misuses of the discovery process include … (d) Failing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subd. (d).)

“The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though no opposition to the motion was filed, or opposition to the motion was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1348(a).)

DISCUSSION

Omnibus Motion

Volkswagen is seeking court orders to compel Plaintiffs’ initial responses to three sets of discovery requests; additionally, it seeks to have Plaintiffs’ Requests for Admission, Set One, deemed admitted.  (MTC re: FROG, p. 3:3-22; MTC re: SROG, p. 3:3-22; MTC re: RFP, p. 3:3-22; MTC re: RFA, p. 3:3-18.)  Volkswagen has erroneously combined eight distinct motions into four separate filings.

Multiple motions should not be combined into a single filing.  (See¿Gov. Code,¿§ 70617, subd. (a)(4) [setting forth the required filing fee for each motion, application, or any other paper or request requiring a hearing].)  Combining multiple motions under the guise of one motion with one hearing reservation manipulates the Court Reservation System and unfairly jumps ahead of other litigants.  Moreover, combining motions to avoid payment of separate filing fees deprives the Court of the filing fees it is otherwise entitled to collect.  Filing fees are jurisdictional, and it is mandatory for court clerks to demand and receive them.  (See Duran v. St. Luke’s Hospital (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 457, 460.)

The Court reminds Volkswagen that all future motions must be filed individually, pursuant to Government Code section 70617.  On June 24, 2026, the Court noted that Volkswagen had submitted an omnibus motion and stated, “Any omnibus filings submitted in the future shall be denied without prejudice.”  (June 24, 2026 Minute Order, p. 6.)  As Volkswagen filed these omnibus motions on May 1, 2026, the Court will not deny them without prejudice.  However, any omnibus motions filed after the June 24, 2026 hearing will be denied without prejudice.  Furthermore, the following orders are contingent upon Volkswagen paying four additional filing fees within ten (10) days of the date of this order.

Untimely Opposition Brief

Volkswagen requests that the Court not consider any untimely opposition briefs filed by Plaintiffs.  (Reply, p. RPD, p. 2:12-20; Reply re: RFA, p. 2:19-27.)  The Court DENIES Volkswagen’s request.

“All papers opposing a motion so noticed shall be filed with the court and a copy served on each party at least nine court days, and all reply papers at least five court days before the hearing.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1005, subd. (b).)

Given that the motions related to the inspection demand and the requests for admission were scheduled to be heard on July 14, 2026, Plaintiffs were required to file and serve their opposition briefs by June 30, 2026.  However, Plaintiffs submitted their opposition brief related to the requests for admission on July 2, 2026, which is two court days past the deadline.

The Court typically exercises its discretion to consider an opposition brief that is filed a couple of days late, particularly when the moving party submits a reply brief.  (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1300(d).)  In this case, Plaintiffs submitted their opposition brief two court days after the established deadline, while Volkswagen submitted its reply brief within the required time frame.  Consequently, the Court chooses to exercise its discretion to consider the untimely opposition brief.

LEGAL STANDARD

“Any party may obtain discovery ... by taking in California the oral deposition of any person, including any party to the action.  The person deposed may be a natural person, an organization such as a public or private corporation, a partnership, an association, or a governmental agency.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.010.)

Protective orders are authorized by several code sections.  A protective order may be issued under Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.420 to prevent or otherwise limit the scope and means of taking a deposition.  The court may issue a protective order upon a showing of good cause, such as to prevent unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden and expense.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.420, subd. (b).)  To establish good cause, the party seeking the protective order must show that the burden involved in the deposition proceeding outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.  (Emerson v. Electric Co. v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 1101, 1110.)  Finally, prior to making such a motion, the moving party must attempt to informally resolve the issue and attach a declaration pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.420, subd. (a).)

Additionally, “[t]he court shall restrict the frequency or extent of use of a discovery method provided in Section 2019.010 if it determines either of the following:  ¶  (1) The discovery sought is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative, or is obtainable from some other source that is more convenient, less burdensome, or less expensive.  ¶  (2) The selected method of discovery is unduly burdensome or expensive, taking into account the needs of the case, the amount in controversy, and the importance of the issues at stake in the litigation.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2019.030, subd. (a).)  “The court may make these determinations pursuant to a motion for a protective order by a party or other affected person.  This motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2019.030, subd. (b).)

Sanctions are mandatory against the party or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion for a protective order unless the party acted with substantial justification or the circumstances make imposition of sanctions unjust.  (Code of Civ. Proc. §§ 2019.030, subd. (c), 2025.420 subd. (h).)  The unsuccessful party bears the burden of demonstrating substantial justification.  (Doe v. United States Swimming, Inc. (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1424, 1435.)  A motion or opposition is substantially justified where it is grounded in both law and fact.  (Padron v. Watchtower Bible & Tract Society of New York, Inc. (2017) 16 Cal. App. 5th 1246, 1269.)

Furthermore, under Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030, subdivision (a), “The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.  The court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney who advised that assertion, or on both.  If a monetary sanction is authorized by any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (a).)  “Misuses of the discovery process include …  ¶  (d) Failing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery.  …  ¶  (h) Making or opposing, unsuccessfully and without substantial justification, a motion to compel or to limit discovery.  ¶  (i) Failing to confer or to attempt to confer, in person, by telephone, or by videoconference with an opposing party or attorney in a reasonable and good faith attempt to resolve informally any dispute concerning discovery, if the section governing a particular discovery motion requires the filing of a declaration stating facts showing that an attempt at informal resolution has been made.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subds. (d), (h)-(i).)

Superior Court (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 1431, 1435.)

MEET AND CONFER REQUIREMENT

Before filing a demurrer or motion to strike, the moving party is required to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or via video conference with the party that filed the pleading that is subject to the motion to determine whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer or motion to strike.  (Code Civ. DEMURRER

Legal Standard

A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable.  (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)  “To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.”  (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)  For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded.  (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.)  A demurrer “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.”  (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

A pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous or unintelligible.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).)  A demurrer for uncertainty may lie if the failure to label the parties and claims renders the complaint so confusing that the defendant cannot tell what he or she is supposed to respond to.  (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2.)  However, “[a] demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.”  (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)

“Notwithstanding any other law, in any action under this chapter in which the defendant demurs or moves to strike the complaint or any portion thereof, the hearing on the motion shall be not less than five court days nor more than seven court days after the filing of the notice of motion.”  (Code Civil Proc., § 1170, subd. (b)(1).)

Discussion

Third Cause of Action

Breach of Warranty of Habitability (Violation of Civil Code § 1942.4)

Defendant demurs to Plaintiffs’ third cause of action for breach of warranty of habitability (Violation of Civil Code § 1942.4) on the grounds that (1) “Plaintiffs have pled no facts indicating any inspection by any public officer or employee responsible for enforcement of any housing law[,]” and (2) “Plaintiffs also have not pled any notice, let alone written notice conveying the specific information necessary to maintain a cause of action under section 1942.4.”  (Dem., p. 3:3-6.) 

“A landlord of a dwelling may not demand rent, collect rent, issue a notice of a rent increase, or issue a three-day notice to pay rent or quit pursuant to subdivision (2) of Section 1161 of the Code of Civil Procedure, if all of the following conditions exist prior to the landlord’s demand or notice:  ¶  (1) The dwelling substantially lacks any of the affirmative standard characteristics listed in Section 1941.1 or violates Section 17920.10 of the Health and Safety Code, or is deemed and declared substandard as set forth in Section 17920.3 of the Health and Safety Code because conditions listed in that section exist to an extent that endangers the life, limb, health, property, safety, or welfare of the public or the occupants of the dwelling.  ¶  (2) A public officer or employee who is responsible for the enforcement of any housing law, after inspecting the premises, has notified the landlord or the landlord’s agent in writing of his or her obligations to abate the nuisance or repair the substandard conditions.  ¶  (3) The conditions have existed and have not been abated 35 days beyond the date of service of the notice specified in paragraph (2) and the delay is without good cause.  For purposes of this subdivision, service shall be complete at the time of deposit in the United States mail.  ¶  (4) The conditions were not caused by an act or omission of the tenant or lessee in violation of Section 1929 or 1941.2.”  (Civ. Code, § 1942.4, subd. (a).)

The Court observes that Plaintiffs have not alleged that a public officer or employee who is responsible for the enforcement of any housing law, after inspecting the premises, has notified the landlord or the landlord’s agent in writing of their obligations to abate the nuisance or repair the substandard conditions.  The absence of allegations that Plaintiffs reported the alleged uninhabitable conditions to a public official or employee further underscores this lack of compliance.  While Plaintiffs claim to have reported the conditions to “Ernesto” and “Leticia,” these individuals are identified as management or managing agents of the property, rather than as public officers or employees.  (Compl., ¶¶ 28, 92.)

Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer with respect to the third cause of action for breach of warranty of habitability (Violation of Civil Code § 1942.4) is SUSTAINED.

Sixth Cause of Action

 Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

Defendant demurs to Plaintiffs’ sixth cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress on the grounds that (1) Plaintiffs fail to allege conduct so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community, and (2) Plaintiffs fail to include facts that would demonstrate Defendant acted with the intent of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress.   (Dem., pp. 3:17-4:22.)  The Court disagrees.

“A cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress exists when there is (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct.  [Citations.]  A defendant’s conduct is outrageous when it is so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.  [Citation.]  And the defendant’s conduct must be intended to inflict injury or engaged in with the realization that injury will result.  [Citation.]”  (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1050-1051, internal citations and quotations omitted.)

“Behavior may be considered outrageous if a defendant (1) abuses a relation or position which gives him power to damage the plaintiff’s interest; (2) knows the plaintiff is susceptible to injuries through mental distress; or (3) acts intentionally or unreasonably with the recognition that the acts are likely to result in illness through mental distress. ”  [Citation.]”  (McDaniel v. Gile (1991) 230 Cal.App.3d 363, 372, internal citation omitted.)  “A person acts with conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others when [he] [she] is aware of the probable dangerous consequences of [his] [her] conduct and willfully and deliberately fails to avoid those consequences.  [Citation.]”  (Poetter v. Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 965, 998, internal citation and quotations omitted.)

Plaintiffs assert that the following actions constitute extreme and outrageous conduct: “Sometime on or around February 2021 and continued to December 2025, Plaintiffs noticed bedbugs in their home and immediately notified management and/or their managing agents, specifically ‘Ernesto’ and ‘Leticia’ of the bed bug infestation in Plaintiffs’ Unit.  Defendants and their agents and managers failed to remediate the uninhabitable conditions (specifically, bed bug infestations), at the Subject Property and Plaintiffs’ Unit.”  (Compl., ¶ 142.)  “Defendants were aware of the dangerous bed bug infestations, which existed at the Subject Property and Subject Unit, and knowingly, intentionally and willfully fail to abate the bed bug infestations so that the problems have persisted for an extended period.  In fact, when Plaintiffs notified Defendants of the bed bug infestations, Defendants knowingly, intentionally and willfully failed to abate the uninhabitable conditions (bed bug infestations), maintaining a company policy of apathy and/or denial.  As such Plaintiffs were forced to live in uninhabitable conditions (bed bug infestations) for an extended period as a result of Defendants’ incessant failure to abide by their statutory duties to abate known uninhabitable conditions.”  (Compl., ¶ 145.)

The Court finds that the allegations presented are adequate to establish both outrageous conduct and a reckless disregard of the probability of causing emotional distress.  Plaintiffs assert that Defendant was aware for more than five years, prior to the initiation of this Complaint, that they were experiencing a bed bug infestation.  Furthermore, Plaintiffs claim that Defendant took no action to remedy the situation during this extended period.  A jury may reasonably conclude that such conduct is exceptionally egregious and exceeds the standards typically accepted within a civilized community, particularly given Defendant’s failure to act despite knowledge that a prolonged infestation could result in extreme emotional distress for Plaintiffs.

Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer with respect to the sixth cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress is OVERRULED.

Ninth Cause of Action

Fraudulent Concealment

Defendant demurs to Plaintiffs’ ninth cause of action for fraudulent concealment on the grounds that (1) Plaintiffs have not clearly identified any alleged misrepresentation or concealment of facts; (2) Plaintiffs fail to meet the heightened specificity requirement; and (3) Plaintiffs’ timeline is contradictory.  (Dem, pp. 4:23-6:1.) 

“The elements of fraud, which give rise to the tort action for deceit, are (a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.”  (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638 (Lazar), quoting 5 Witkin, Summary of Cal. Law (9th ed. 1988) Torts, § 676, p. 778.)

“To maintain a cause of action for fraud through nondisclosure or concealment of facts, there must be allegations demonstrating that the defendant was under a legal duty to disclose those facts.  [Citation.]  There are four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts. [Citation.]  [Citation.]  Where ... there is no fiduciary relationship, the duty to disclose generally presupposes a relationship grounded in some sort of transaction between the parties.  [Citations.]  Thus, a duty to disclose may arise from the relationship between seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement.  [Citation.]  [Citation.]  [Citation.]”  (Los Angeles Memorial Coliseum Com. v. Insomniac, Inc. (2015) 233 Cal.App.4th 803, 831, internal citations and quotations omitted.)

The facts constituting the alleged fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked.  (Lazar, supra, 12 Cal.4th at p. 645.)  In alleging a cause of action for fraud, “[t]he specificity requirement means a plaintiff must allege facts showing how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were made, and, in the case of a corporate defendant, the plaintiff must allege the names of the persons who made the representations, their authority to speak on behalf of the corporation, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when the representation was made.”  (West v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 780, 793.)  However, the Court notes that “the rule is relaxed when it is apparent from the allegations that the defendant necessarily possesses knowledge of the facts.”  (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 29.)

Plaintiffs allege the following: “Sometime on or around July 2020, Plaintiffs entered into a standard Rental Agreement (hereinafter ‘Rental Agreement’) for the Subject Property.”  (Compl., ¶ 27.)  “Prior to sometimes on or around February 2021 and continued to December 2025, Defendants knew of widespread bed bug infestations at the Subject Property and Plaintiffs’ Unit.  However, Defendants intentionally withheld this information from Plaintiffs.”  (Compl., ¶ 169.)  “Plaintiffs justifiably relied upon Defendants’ misrepresentations and/or fraudulent representations that Plaintiffs’ Unit and Subject Property were habitable and free of bedbugs.”  (Compl., ¶ 171.)  “Defendants’ managing agents, specifically ‘Ernesto’ and ‘Leticia’ authorized and/or ratified all offensive acts outlined in this cause of action, as well as all of the other causes of action included throughout the Complaint.”  (Compl., ¶ 172.)

Defendant contends that “the timeline of Plaintiffs’ tenancy renders this allegation nonsensical.  Plaintiffs allege their tenancy commenced in July 2020.  Somehow, six months later, Defendant concealed the existence of bedbugs in a unit Plaintiffs had already occupied for half a year.  The facts pled indicate very strongly that there was no bedbug issue to conceal prior to Plaintiffs’ moving into the unit and that, if anything, Defendant became aware of the issue after Plaintiffs’ notified Defendant.  ”  (Dem., p. 5:20-25.)  The Court does not concur with this interpretation.

Although Plaintiffs assert that they moved into the subject unit in July 2020 and allege that Defendant concealed the infestation six months later, it is possible that a bedbug infestation occurred in other units on the property prior to Plaintiffs’ occupancy or during the initial six months of their tenancy, subsequently spreading to Plaintiffs’ unit.  Thus, Plaintiffs’ timeline is plausible.

Nevertheless, such allegations do not satisfy the heightened pleading standard.  Plaintiffs’ own assertions indicate that Defendant made affirmative misrepresentations regarding the habitability of Plaintiffs’ unit and the condition of the property with respect to a bedbug infestation.  Furthermore, they claim that Defendant was aware that these statements were false but did not disclose knowledge of the infestation.  Plaintiffs failed to include any information about how, when, where, and by what means these affirmative representations were made, and, in the case of a corporate defendant, the names of the persons who made the representations, their authority to speak on behalf of the corporation, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when the representation was made.  Given the current state of the Complaint, the Court is unable to waive the heightened pleading standard because it is not apparent from the allegations that Defendant necessarily possesses knowledge of the facts.  Plaintiffs provide minimal information for the Court and Defendant to assess how, when, where, and by what means these affirmative representations were made.

Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer with respect to the ninth cause of action for fraudulent concealment is SUSTAINED.

 

 

 

 

MOTION TO STRIKE

Legal Standard

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1).)  The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].)  The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with California law, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b), emphasis added.)  An immaterial or irrelevant allegation is one that is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense; is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense; or a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subd. (b).)  The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.)

Discussion

Defendant seeks to strike the following: (1) references to punitive damages contained in paragraphs 1, 40, 41, 80, 81, 83, 87, 115, 130, 131, 141, 143, 144, 146, 148, 155, 171, 173, and 180; (2) claims for statutory damages arising from Plaintiffs’ third cause of action for breach of warranty of habitability (Violation of Civil Code § 1942.4); (3) requests for restitution; and (4) requests for attorneys’ fees.  (Notice of MTS, pp. 2:1-4:17; MTS, pp. 5:25-6:6.)  However, the Court notes that Defendant’s request to strike statutory damages is rendered MOOT due to the sustainment of the demurrer pertaining to the third cause of action.  Moreover, the Court notes that Defendant has not provided a legal analysis concerning restitution or attorneys’ fees.  Consequently, the Court will focus solely on the argument related to punitive damages.

Punitive Damages

Defendant contends that Plaintiffs have not presented sufficient factual allegations to demonstrate malice, oppression, or fraud.  (MTS, pp. 3:21-5:24.)  However, the Court finds this position unconvincing and concludes that the allegations provided are adequate to support claims of malice or oppression.

Punitive damages may be imposed where it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).)  A motion to strike punitive damages is properly granted when a plaintiff does not state a prima facie claim for punitive damages.  (Turman v. Turning Point of Cent. California, Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.)

“Malice” is conduct intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1).)  “‘Punitive damages are proper only when the tortious conduct rises to levels of extreme indifference to the plaintiff’s rights, a level which decent citizens should not have to tolerate.’  [Citation.]”  (Lackner v. North (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 1188, 1210.)

“As amended to include [despicable], the [Civil Code section 3294] plainly indicates that absent an intent to injure the plaintiff, ‘malice’ requires more than a ‘willful and conscious’ disregard of the plaintiffs’ interests.  The additional component of ‘despicable conduct’ must be found.”  (College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 725.)  The statute’s reference to despicable conduct represents a “new substantive limitation on punitive damage awards.”  (Ibid.)  Despicable conduct is “conduct which is so vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or loathsome that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people.  Such conduct has been described as ‘having the character of outrage frequently associated with crime.’ ”  (Tomaselli v. Transamerica Ins. Co. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 1269, 1287.)  Further, “[t]here must be evidence that defendant acted with knowledge of the probable dangerous consequences to plaintiff’s interests and deliberately failed to avoid these consequences.”  (Flyer’s Body Shop Profit Sharing Plan v. Ticor Title Ins. Co. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 1149, 1155.)

“Oppression” means “despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.”  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(2).)

“Fraud” means “an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.”  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(3).)

As discussed, Plaintiffs contend the following: “Sometime on or around February 2021 and continued to December 2025, Plaintiffs noticed bedbugs in their home and immediately notified management and/or their managing agents, specifically “Ernesto” and “Leticia” of the bed bug infestation in Plaintiffs’ Unit.  Defendants and their agents and managers failed to remediate the uninhabitable conditions (specifically, bed bug infestations), at the Subject Property and Plaintiffs’ Unit.”  (Compl., ¶ 142.)  “Defendants were aware of the dangerous bed bug infestations, which existed at the Subject Property and Subject Unit, and knowingly, intentionally and willfully fail to abate the bed bug infestations so that the problems have persisted for an extended period.  In fact, when Plaintiffs notified Defendants of the bed bug infestations, Defendants knowingly, intentionally and willfully failed to abate the uninhabitable conditions (bed bug infestations), maintaining a company policy of apathy and/or denial.  As such Plaintiffs were forced to live in uninhabitable conditions (bed bug infestations) for an extended period as a result of Defendants’ incessant failure to abide by their statutory duties to abate known uninhabitable conditions.”  (Compl., ¶ 145.)

The Court determines that these allegations are sufficient to substantiate claims of malice or oppression.  The long-standing inaction by Defendant, following its awareness of the infestation, may be perceived as conduct that is despicable and executed with a willful or conscious disregard for Plaintiffs’ right to reside in a habitable unit, thereby subjecting them to cruel and unjust hardship.  Accordingly, the Defendant’s motion to strike is DENIED.
