MOTION FOR ATTORNEy’S FEES, costs, and expenses

 

 

MOVING PARTY:            Plaintiff Nikki Lynn Chase

 

RESPONDING PARTY:  No opposition

Motion for Attorney’s Fees, Costs, and Expenses

The court considered the moving papers filed in connection with this motion. No opposition or reply was filed. 

DISCUSSION

Plaintiff Nikki Lynn Chase (“Plaintiff”) moves the court for an order awarding attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses pursuant to the settlement agreement between Plaintiff and defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. in the total amount of $69,017.35 in fees and $ 4,991.06 in costs and expenses.

First, the court finds that Plaintiff is entitled to recover attorney’s fees and costs from Defendant pursuant to the Song-Beverly Act and the parties’ settlement.  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d) [“If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action”].) ¿

Second, the court finds that Plaintiff has established, as to the attorney’s fees incurred to commence and prosecute this action and to prepare the pending fee motion, a lodestar of $47,402.50

“[T]he fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily begins with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate. . . . .¿ The reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work.¿ The lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided.”¿ (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095 (internal citations omitted); Reck v. FCA US LLC (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 682, 691 [“To determine a reasonable attorney fee award, the trial court applies the lodestar method”].)¿ “[T]he verified time statements of the attorneys, as officers of the court, are entitled to credence in the absence of a clear indication the records are erroneous.”¿ (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State Univ. (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 396.)¿¿¿¿¿¿

The court finds that the reasonable hourly rates for the legal staff that performed work in this action are as follows: (1) $595 for attorneys Avelino, Baker, Littles, and McCallister, (2) $520 for attorney Besrchauer; (3) $350 for attorneys Brown, Chandy and Ringstad; (4) $325 for law clerk Canton; (5) $450 for attorney Carvalho; (6) $525 for attorney Liou; (7) $195 for law clerks Luppino and Nguyen; (8) $435 for attorneys Neubauer and Stoliker; (9) $385 for attorneys Ryu and Sanaia; (10) $875 for attorney Shahian; (11) $360 for attorneys Theophil and Tirmizi, and (12) $475 for attorney Whang. (Shahian Decl. ¶ 15.)

The court has considered the billing records of Plaintiff’s legal staff.  (Shahian Decl., Ex. A.)  The court finds that Plaintiff’s legal staff reasonably expended a total number of 98.5 hours (10.6 hours for Avelino, 5.7 hours for Baker, 8.1 hours for Berschauer, 14.4 hours for Brown, 4.7 hours for Canton, 3.6 hours for Carvalho, 0.5 hours for Chandy, 5.6 hours for Liou, 13.6 hours for Littles, 0.1 hours for Luppino, 3.6 hours for McCallister, 10.1 hours for Neubauer, 0.1 hours for Nguyen, 1.6 hours for Ringstad, 1.8 hours for Ryu, 2.2 hours for Sanaia, 1.2 hours for Shahian, 0.6 hours for Stoliker, 5.2 hours for Theophil, 2 hours for Tirmizi, and 3.2 hours for Whang) to commence and prosecute this action and to prepare the pending motion. 

The court therefore finds that Plaintiffs have established a lodestar figure of $47,402.50 ( (33.5 hours x Avelino, Baker, Littles, and McCallister’s $595 hourly rate = $19,932.50) + (8.1 hours x Berschauer’s $520 hourly rate = $4,212.00) + (16.5 hours x Brown, Chandy, and Ringstad’s $350 hourly rate = $5,775.00) + (4.7 hours x Canton’s $325 hourly rate = $1,527.50) + (3.6 hours x Carvalho’s $450 hourly rate = $1,620.00) + (5.6 hours x Liou’s $525 hourly rate = $2,940.00) + (0.2 hours x Luppino and Nguyen’s $195 hourly rate = $39.00) + (10.7 hours x Neubauer and Stoliker’s $435 hourly rate = $4,654.50) + (4.0 hours x Ryu and Sanaia’s $385 hourly rate = $1,540.00) + (1.2 hours x Shahian’s $875 hourly rate = $1,050) + (7.2 hours x Theophil and Tirmizi’s $360 hourly rate $2,592.00) + (3.2 hours x Whang’s $475 hourly rate = $1,520.00.)

Third, after considering the factors the court may consider in determining whether to adjust the lodestar, the court finds that the lodestar figure should not be adjusted by applying a multiplier.  (Shahian Decl. ¶ 20-24; Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 248 [setting forth factors delineated by the Supreme Court that the trial court may consider in adjusting the lodestar].)

Fourth, because no opposition to this motion has been filed and thus no reply is required, court finds that a reasonable award of fees for attending the hearing on this motion is $875, reduced from the request for $4,000 in anticipated fees. (Shahian Decl. ¶¶ 26-27.)

Fifth, the court finds that Plaintiff is entitled to recover costs and Plaintiff’s counsel has shown that they incurred costs to commence and prosecute this action in the total amount of $4,991.06.  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d); Shahian Decl., Ex. 1 PDF page 40.)  

ORDER

The court grants plaintiff Nikki Lynn Chase’s motion for attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses in part as follows:

Pursuant to Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), the court orders that plaintiff Nikki Lynn Chase shall recover from defendant American Honda Motor Co, Inc. attorney’s fees in the total amount of $48,277.50 and costs in the amount of $4,991.06.

The court orders plaintiff Nikki Lynn Chase to give notice of this ruling.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Motion to Tax Costs

The court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers filed in connection with this motion.

DISCUSSION

Plaintiffs Nelly Alejandra Correa Flores and Elvia Flores Pena (“Plaintiffs”) move the court for an order striking or taxing defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.’s (“Defendant”) Memorandum of Costs.

“A ‘verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of the propriety’ of the items listed on it, and the burden is on the party challenging these costs to demonstrate that they were not reasonable or necessary.”  (Adams v. Ford Motor Co. (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 1475, 1486 [internal citation omitted].) 

The Court finds that Plaintiffs have not met their burden to show that the costs claimed by Defendant are not reasonable or necessary. (Adams, supra, 199 Cal.App.4th at p. 1486.) Plaintiffs have not shown that (1) the claimed filing fees were not reasonably incurred or necessary; (2) the claimed deposition costs were excessive; (3) the service of process costs were not reasonably incurred or necessary, and (4) the various “other” costs identified in the memorandum, including arbitration costs, private investigator expenses, and Carfax reports were not reasonably necessary in a Song-Beverly action decided by arbitration. (November 3, 2025 Memorandum of Costs, MC-010 pp.1 [signed by counsel for Defendant under penalty of perjury], Worksheet pp.1, 2, 4].)

ORDER

The court denies plaintiffs Nelly Alejandra Correa Flores and Elvia Flores Pena’s motion to strike or tax fees and costs.

The court orders defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. to give notice of this ruling.

MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

 

MOVING PARTY:            Defendant Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority

 

RESPONDING PARTY:  Plaintiff Tamara Jones

Motion for Summary Adjudication

The court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers filed in connection with this motion.

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS 

The court declines to rule on the evidentiary objections raised by the parties pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 437c subdivision (q).

DISCUSSION

Defendant Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority (“Defendant”) moves for summary adjudication of issues as to whether the decedent, Onna Sophoa Cooper (“Decedent”), violated Vehicle Code sections 21657, 23152, and 21229.

First, Defendant’s motion seeks summary adjudication of an issue which does not “completely dispose[] of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c subd. (f)(1).) Defendant’s notice of motion also does not comply with the requirements of California Rules of Court, rule 3.1350(b), which states, in relevant part: “[i]f summary adjudication is sought, . . . the specific cause of action, affirmative defense, claims for damages, or issues of duty must be stated specifically in the notice of motion . . . .”       

Second, even if the court were to construe this motion as seeking summary adjudication of one or more affirmative defenses that Decedent violated these statutes, those affirmative defenses were not pled in the Answer that was operative on December 15, 2025, when this motion was filed. It would be a violation of Plaintiff’s right to due process to grant summary adjudication on affirmative defenses that are not pled in the Answer. (California Concrete Co. v. Beverly Hills Savings & Loan Association (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 260, 273-74.)

Third, Defendant filed its First Amended Answer on March 23, 2026. Amendment of the pleading on which a motion for summary adjudication is based moots that motion. (See State Compensation Ins. Fund v. Superior Court (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 1124, 1130 [“an amended complaint supersedes all prior complaints”], 1131 [“Because there is but one complaint in a civil action, the filing of an amended complaint moots a motion directed to a prior complaint. Thus, once an amended complaint is filed, it is error to grant summary adjudication on a cause of action contained in a previous complaint”] [internal citations omitted].) 

For the reasons stated above, the court denies Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication because it is procedurally improper.

 

 

ORDER

The court denies defendant Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority’s motion for summary adjudication of issues.  

The court orders plaintiff Tamara Jones to give notice of this ruling.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Motion for Summary Judgment or, in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication

The court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers filed in connection with this motion.

LEGAL STANDARD

The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.)  “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.”  (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.)  A defendant or cross-defendant moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)  “Once the defendant or cross-defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff or cross-complainant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.”  (Code of Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)  “If the plaintiff cannot do so, summary judgment should be granted.”  (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467.)  “When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.”  (Id. at p. 467; Code of Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).)

 

 

 

DISCUSSION

1.     First Cause of Action for Elder Abuse

a.      Abuse or Neglect

Under the Elder Abuse and Dependent Adult Civil Protection Act, “[a]buse of an elder or a dependent adult means any of the following:  (1) Physical abuse, neglect, abandonment, isolation, abduction, or other treatment with resulting physical harm or pain or mental suffering. (2) The deprivation by a care custodian of goods or services that are necessary to avoid physical harm or mental suffering.”  (Welf. & Inst. Code,  §15610.07, subd. (a)(1)-(3).) 

“Neglect” is defined as “(1) The negligent failure of any person having the care or custody of an elder or a dependent adult to exercise that degree of care that a reasonable person in a like position would exercise.  (2) The negligent failure of an elder or dependent adult to exercise that degree of self care that a reasonable person in a like position would exercise.”  (Welf. & Inst. Code, §15610.57, subd. (a).)

“‘Neglect’ includes, but is not limited to, all of the following:  (1) Failure to assist in personal hygiene, or in the provision of food, clothing, or shelter. (2) Failure to provide medical care for physical and mental health needs…(3) Failure to protect from health and safety hazards. (4) Failure to prevent malnutrition or dehydration…”  (Welf. & Inst. Code, §15610.57, subd. (b)(1)-(4).) 

“Thus, neglect within the meaning of former section 15610.57 appears to cover an area of misconduct distinct from ‘professional negligence’ in section 15657.2: ‘neglect’ as defined in former section 15610.57 and used in section 15657 does not refer to the performance of medical services in a manner inferior to the knowledge, skill and care ordinarily possessed and employed by members of the profession in good standing, but rather to the failure of those responsible for attending to the basic needs and comforts of elderly or dependent adults, regardless of their professional standing, to carry out their custodial obligations.”  (Delaney v. Baker (1999) 20 Cal.4th 23, 34.) 

“As used in the Act, neglect refers not to the substandard performance of medical services but, rather, to the failure of those responsible for attending to the basic needs and comforts of elderly or dependent adults, regardless of their professional standing, to carry out their custodial obligations.  Thus, the statutory definition of ‘neglect’ speaks not of the undertaking of medical services, but of the failure to provide medical care.”  (Covenant Care, Inc. v. Supr. Ct. (2004) 32 Cal.4th 771, 783 [“statutory elder abuse may include the egregious withholding of medical care for physical and mental health needs”].)

“The difficulty in distinguishing between ‘neglect’ and ‘professional negligence’ lies in the fact that some health care institutions, such as nursing homes, perform custodial functions and provide professional medical care.”  (Delaney, supra, 20 Cal.4th at p. 34.)  “Section 15657 provides the way out of this ambiguity: if the neglect is ‘reckless,’ or done with ‘oppression, fraud or malice,’ then the action falls within the scope of section 15657 and as such cannot be considered simply based on professional negligence within the meaning of section 15657.2.”  (Id. at p. 35; Carter v. Prime Healthcare Paradise Valley LLC (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 396, 405 [recovery of enhanced remedies under Elder Abuse Act requires plaintiff to prove “by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of recklessness, oppression, fraud, or malice in the commission of the neglect”].) 

            “‘Recklessness’ refers to a subjective state of culpability greater than simple negligence, which has been described as a ‘deliberate disregard’ of the ‘high degree of probability’ that an injury will occur. Recklessness, unlike negligence, involves more than ‘inadvertence, incompetence, unskillfulness, or a failure to take precautions’ but rather rises to the level of a ‘conscious choice of a course of action ... with knowledge of the serious danger to others involved in it.’”  (Delaney, supra, 20 Cal.4th at pp. 31-32.)

In Carter v. Prime Healthcare Paradise Valley LLC (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 396, 406-407, the Court of Appeal set forth several factors that must be present for conduct to constitute neglect under Welfare and Institution Code section 15610.57 subdivision (a):

 

“The plaintiff must allege facts establishing that the defendant (1) had responsibility for meeting the basic needs of the elder or dependent adult, such as nutrition, hydration, hygiene or medical care; (2) knew of conditions that made the elder or dependent adult unable to provide for his or her own basic needs; and (3) denied or withheld goods or services necessary to meet the elder or dependent adult’s basic needs, either with knowledge that injury was substantially certain to befall the elder or dependent adult (if the plaintiff alleges oppression, fraud or malice) or with conscious disregard of the high probability of such injury (if the plaintiff alleges recklessness).”  (Carter, supra, 198 Cal.App.4th at pp. 406-407.) 

 

Defendants argue there is no evidence of elder abuse, neglect, recklessness or willful misconduct.  Defendants submit evidence that Leon was admitted for short term stay to the Facility, which ultimately lasted nine days from December 4, 2020 through December 11, 2020.  (Defendants’ SSUMF Nos. 2 to 24, 31; Defendants’ Evidence ISO MSJ (“Defendants’ Evidence”), Exs. B, D, N and O.)  Defendants submit evidence that Leon was admitted to the Facility with a diagnosis of aspiration pneumonia and urinary tract infection.  (Defendants’ SSUMF No. 3; Defendants’ Evidence, Ex. C.)  Defendants submit evidence that Leon was discharged from Providence St. John’s with a medication order for ampicillin-sulbacram to be given intravenously every six hours for nine days for community-acquired pneumonia.  (Defendants’ SSUMF No. 3; Defendants’ Evidence, Ex. C.)

Defendants submit evidence of the care provided to Leon at the Facility.  Defendants’ claim the evidence establishes that (1) Leon was fed and hydrated; (2) a care plan was created for her to address her desire to return to the group home, her cognitive deficits, communication problems, risk for dehydration, tube feedings, risks for falls, depression, risk for potential nutritional problems, pain and bowel/bladder incontinence; (3) she was regularly examined by physicians and subject to lab tests to determine her health status; (4) she received treatment for her pneumonia through administration of amoxicillin; (5) her condition was evaluated multiple times a day, including for breaks in the skin and pressure wounds.  (Defendants’ SSUMF Nos. 4-27; Defendants’ Evidence, Exs. D, H (care plans) I and N.) 

Defendants submit evidence that they transferred Leon to an acute care facility once her condition began to decline.  (Defendants’ SSUMF Nos. 24-29, Exs. D, N.)  Defendants submit evidence Leon was received by the acute care facility within one hour and eight minutes of her condition becoming acute.  (Defendants’ SSUMF Nos. 27-28.)

Defendants argue Leon’s pressure injuries arose during her 30-hour stay at Good Samaritan Hospital, not during her stay at the Facility.  Defendants submit evidence that, at the time of her admission to the Facility, there were no pressure wounds.  (Defendants’ SSUMF No. 4; Ex. D.)  Defendants maintain Leon suffered from no pressure wounds at the time she was transferred to Good Samaritan Hospital.  (Defendants’ SSUMF No. 28; Defendants’ Evidence, Ex. N, p. 7 of 11; Ex. S; Ex. U, pp. 10-11; Ex. V, p. 63.)

Defense experts reviewed Leon’s medical records, and they opine that the care and treatment provided at the Facility complied with the standard of care.  (Rosen Dec., ¶48; Hibbs Dec., ¶48.)  Defense expert Hibbs opines that the Facility administered proper wound care, as Leon did not develop any wounds while under its care and pressure wounds were first documented on December 13, 2020 by staff at Good Samaritan Hospital.  (Hibbs Dec., ¶51; Defendants’ Evidence, Ex. V.)  Defense expert Hibbs opines the transfer of Leon to Good Samaritan Hospital was within the standard of care, if not beyond it due to the rapidity with which she was transferred.  (Id. at ¶52.)  Defense expert Hibbs opines Leon was properly hydrated and fed in accordance with the standard of care, as evidence by the fact that she gained 5 pounds during her stay at the Facility.  (Id. at ¶53.)  Defense expert Hibbs also points out that Leon remained dehydrated despite Good Samaritan’s admission of aggressive intravenous fluids for severe dehydration.  (Id. at ¶54.)  Defense experts opine that Leon died due to her co-morbidities and not the failure to provide care.  (Hibbs Dec., ¶59; Rosen, ¶60.)

However, Defendants failed to provide pin cites to pages of Leon’s medical records to support their claim that (1) a care plan was created and implemented during her stay at the Facility to address her desire to return to the group home, her cognitive deficits, communication problems, risk for dehydration, tube feedings, risks for falls, depression, risk for potential nutritional problems, pain and bowel/bladder incontinence and (2) the physician’s order regarding her feeding and hydration was implemented and adhered to during her stay.  A review of the hundreds of pages of medical records does not establish either material fact. 

Defendants attach the care plans as Exhibit H.  The care plans attached as Exhibit H have initiation dates ranging from 12/7/20 to 12/21/20.  (Defendants’ Evidence, Ex. H.)  In particular, the care plan intended to address Leon’s “risk for dehydration or potential fluid deficit r/t tube feeding” indicates it was initiated on 12/21/2020, after Leon had left the Facility and after she passed away on 12/18/20.  (Id. at p. 4 of 10.)  The dehydration care plan also lists as an intervention “administer medications as ordered.  Monitor/document for side effects and effectiveness.”  (Id.)  The intervention does not appear to address dehydration.

Defendants attach the physician’s order regarding Leon’s feeding and hydration schedule as Exhibit G.  The order summary indicates “Enteral Feed Order every 24 hours Enteral Nutrition via Pump Glucerna 1.6 at 65 ml per hour for 20 hours a day via pump per G-tube. Start Infusion at 7pm and continue until 3p AND every 24 hours every 24 hours Enteral Nutrition via Pump- Glucerna 1.5 at 55 ml per hour for 20 hours a day via pump per G-tube. Start iInfuelon at 7pm and continue until 3p.”  (Id. at Ex. G.)  The physician also ordered “Enteral Feed Order every 4 hours Give 90 ml sterile water via G-tube every 4 hrs” and “Enteral Feed Order every shift Flush tube with 20-30ml(cc) of water before and after administration of medication pass.”  (Id.)  Defendants claim they complied with these orders, citing to the expert declarations and Defendants’ Exhibits D and G.

The expert declarations are not admissible evidence of the underlying, percipient facts contained in the medical records.  “Expert testimony has its own rules given its issues of reliability because the expert is not testifying to percipient facts, but instead relies on a multitude of materials, including hearsay, in forming his or her opinion. Further, the expert is not bound by actual facts in rendering an opinion, but may opine on hypothetical situations.”  (Cooper v. Takeda Pharmaceuticals America, Inc. (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 555, 583-584.)

The nurses notes attached as Exhibit D also do not establish compliance with the physician’s order, nor do they support SSUMF No. 5.  The nurses’ notes are from 12/3/20 through 12/11/20.  (Defendants’ Evidence, Ex. D.)  The notes are general notes regarding the patient’s presentation and, at most, notations that the “G tube is in place” and “running well.”  There are no specific notes regarding administration of Glucerna or water, e.g. when and how much.  Exhibit G is the physician’s orders, not evidence of Defendants’ compliance with the orders.  (Id. at Ex. G.)

Defendants’ evidence does not support two key material facts (SSUMF Nos. 5, 6) pertaining to Defendants’ provision for Leon’s basic nutritional and hydration needs.  Defendants have therefore not met their burden of showing that the first cause of action for elder abuse has no merit.

In opposition, Plaintiffs also argue there was no dehydration care plan, even though the Facility’s director of nursing identified dehydration as a risk.  (Defendants’ Evidence, Ex. H, p. 4 of 10.)  Defendants submit Leon’s care plans as Exhibit H.  The dehydration care plan indicates it was initiated on 12/21/20, after Leon was transferred to Good Samaritan Hospital.  (Defendants’ Evidence, Ex. H, p. 4 of 10.)  The only intervention listed is “administer medications as ordered.  Monitor/document for side effects and effectiveness.”  (Ibid.)  Given the plan as written, there was no dehydration care plan while Leon was under the Facility’s care.  The dehydration care plan only recognizes Leon’s risk of dehydration but fails to implement any measures addressing the dehydration.  (Ibid.) 

The initial physician order dated December 3, 2020 also required (1) 90 mL of sterile water via G-tube every four hours and (2)  a 20-30 mL G-tube flush before and after each medicine administration every shift.  (Plaintiffs’ COE, Ex. G.)  Plaintiffs submit the nursing flowsheets as evidence that this protocol was not followed on at least five occasions—December 6, 7, 10 and 11.  (Plaintiffs’ COE, Ex. O.) 

Plaintiffs also point out there is no evidence of tube feedings, including water flushes, on December 7 and 11.  (Plaintiffs’ Compendium of Evidence, Exs. B, ¶8(p); Ex. C, ¶10(p); Ex. E; Ex. O.)  The nursing tube-feeding flowsheets do not reflect consistent compliance with the physician’s feeding order.  The physician order required Glucerna 1.5 at 55 mL per hour for 20 hours a day via enteral nutrition pump, with the infusion to start at 7:00 p.m. until 3:00 p.m.  (Plaintiffs’ Compendium of Exhibits, Ex. G.)  The nursing flowsheets only document that the Glucerna pump was turned off at 3:00 p.m. on December 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 and 10, but no corresponding documentation that was turned on at 7:00 p.m.  (Plaintiffs’ Compendium of Exhibits, Ex. B, ¶¶26-29; Ex. C, ¶23; Ex. O, p. 7; Ex. G.)

Plaintiffs’ experts also opine it was precisely Defendants’ failure to implement a dehydration care plan and to adhere to the physician’s orders regarding feeding and hydration that led to Leon’s acute decline in health, including severe dehydration.  (Plaintiffs’ COE, Ex. B, Snodgress Dec., ¶¶28-29; Ex. C, Nasar Dec., ¶¶28-29.)  Plaintiffs’ experts also opine that Leon’s December 7, 2020 lab results were consistent with “active dehydration,” and her blood work on December 11, 2020 were consistent with “severe, advanced dehydration and acute kidney injury.”  (Plaintiffs’ COE, Ex. B, ¶¶27, 30-36; Ex. C, ¶24.)  There is no indication in the records that the lab results were communicated to the physician, nor is there any evidence that Leon’s care was adjusted in response to the lab results.  (Plaintiffs’ COE, Ex. B, ¶32; Ex. C, ¶25; Ex. E; Ex. J.)  Plaintiffs’ experts opine that the failure to communicate the results to a physician or adjust Leon’s care was below the applicable standard of care and qualifies as neglect.  (Plaintiffs’ COE, Ex. B, ¶32; Ex. C, ¶25.)

Plaintiffs also submit evidence that Leon developed pressure wounds before December 13, 2020.  Plaintiffs rely on the admission records for Leon completed at 1700 on December 12, 2020, which indicate multiple pressure wounds.  (Plaintiffs’ COE, Ex. P, P000241-P000245.)  Plaintiffs’ expert opines to a reasonable degree of medical probability that Leon did not develop the pressure wounds developed less than 8 hours after being admitted to the Good Samaritan Hospital.  (Plaintiffs’ COE, Ex. C, ¶¶49-50.)  There is therefore a triable issue of material fact as to whether the pressure wounds developed while Leon was in Defendants’ care and custody.

Plaintiffs also establish that there was no care plan established for Leon to prevent pressure wounds despite being an identified concern for Leon.  Defendants do not claim that such a care plan was ever created or implemented.  Plaintiffs submit evidence that Leon was deemed to be at risk for pressure injuries, but there was no plan for a “turning/repositioning program” created or implemented for Leon.  (Plaintiffs’ COE, Ex. B, ¶8(m); Ex. C, ¶10(m); Ex. L.)

“‘Neglect’ includes, but is not limited to, all of the following:  (1) Failure to assist in personal hygiene, or in the provision of food, clothing, or shelter. (2) Failure to provide medical care for physical and mental health needs…(3) Failure to protect from health and safety hazards. (4) Failure to prevent malnutrition or dehydration….”  (Welf. & Inst. Code, §15610.57, subd. (b)(1)-(4).)  Plaintiffs’ evidence establishes Defendants’ neglect based on their failure to attend to Leon’s basic needs, including feeding and hydrating her in accordance with the physician order issued on December 3, 2020, preventing dehydration and preventing the development of pressure wounds due to lack of movement.  Defendants argue on reply that these failures are failures to provide adequate medical care, not the failure to provide for Leon’s basic needs.  The court disagrees.  Reasonable minds could differ on whether such failures qualify as “the failure of those responsible for attending to the basic needs and comforts of elderly or dependent adults, regardless of their professional standing, to carry out their custodial obligations.”  (Covenant Care, Inc., supra, 32 Cal.4th at 783.) 

The court finds that Defendants have not met their burden of showing that the first cause of action for elder abuse has no merit, because Defendant has not shown that the element of neglect cannot be established.  The court therefore denies Defendants’ (1) motion for summary judgment and (2) motion for summary adjudication of the first cause of action for elder abuse. 

b.     Ratification

“[T]he standards set forth is subdivision (b) of Section 3294 of the Civil Code regarding the imposition of punitive damages on an employer based upon the acts of an employee shall be satisfied before any damages or attorney’s fees permitted under this section may be imposed against an employer.” (Welf. & Inst. Code, § 15657 (c).)  As Defendants acknowledge on reply, ratification is therefore a necessary element to obtain punitive damages under Welfare and Institutions Code section 15657 subdivision (c).  Ratification is therefore not an essential element of Plaintiffs’ elder abuse claim and not grounds for summary adjudication of the elder abuse claim.

Moreover, Defendants fail to satisfy its burden as moving party even if ratification were an element of elder abuse.  A party moving for summary judgment can establish that a cause of action has no merit if “one or more of the elements of the cause of action cannot be separately established.”  (Code of Civ. Proc., §437c, subd. (o)(1).)  A defendant may satisfy their burden on summary adjudication pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 437c subdivision (o)(1) by showing that the claim “cannot be established” because of the lack of evidence on some essential element of the claim.  (Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 574, 590.)  A defendant does not meet its burden under Code of Civil Procedure section 437c subdivision (o)(1) by merely “pointing out” or “arguing” that the plaintiff does not possess or cannot reasonably obtain necessary evidence. 

Defendants failed to submit any evidence regarding ratification.  Defendants cannot satisfy their burden by simply stating that “Plaintiffs cannot prove that any officer, director or managing agent of Defendant ratified its agents or employees’ alleged intentional misconduct.”  (Motion, 19:17, 20:1-2.)    

The court finds that Defendants have not met their burden of showing that the first cause of action for elder abuse has no merit, because Defendants have not shown ratification is an element of elder abuse or that Plaintiffs cannot establish ratification.  The court therefore also denies Defendants’ motion for summary adjudication of the first cause of action for elder abuse on this ground.

2.     Fifth Cause of Action for Violations of Residents Rights (pursuant to Health & Safety Code Section 1430(b))

Health and Safety Code section 1430(b) expressly authorizes a current or former resident or patient of a skilled nursing facility to bring a civil action against the licensee of a facility who violates any rights of the resident or patient as set forth in the Patient’s Bill of Rights in 22 California Code of Regulations section 72527, or any other right provided for by federal or state law or regulation.

Plaintiffs allege Leon’s patient rights under Title 22 of California Code of Regulations section 72527, Health & Safety Code §1599.1, Title 42 of the Code of Federal Regulations sections 483.10, 483.13, 483.15, 483.20 and other state and federal laws and regulations.  (Complaint, ¶¶118-119.)  These rights include the right to be given care to prevent pressure sores (¶118(b)(iii), (v)), the right to be informed and to have her physician informed of any need to alter treatment (¶118(c)(ix)), the right to be cared for in a manner and in an environment that promotes maintenance or enhancement of Leon’s life (¶118(e)(i)), the right to receive necessary care and services to attain or maintain the highest practicable physical, mental, and psychosocial well-being, in accordance with comprehensive assessment and plan of care (¶118(g)), the right to receive sufficient fluid intake to maintain proper hydration and health (¶118(g)(viii)), and the right to a patient care plan and implementation of that plan (¶118(j)).

Defendants discuss the causes of action for elder abuse cause and violation of residents rights per Health & Safety Code Section 1430(b) together.  Defendants rely on the exact same arguments and evidence in moving for summary adjudication of both causes of action.  For the same reasons discussed above in connection with the first cause of action for elder abuse, the court finds that Defendants have not met their burden of showing that the fifth cause of action for violation of residents rights pursuant to Health & Safety Code Section 1430(b) has no merit, because Defendants have not shown that the element of a violation of Leon’s rights cannot be established.

3.     Second Cause of action for Negligence

Defendants’ Notice of Motion does not include the second cause of action for negligence among the issues to be adjudicated.  Defendants’ Separate Statement lists several issues for adjudication that were not listed in the Notice of Motion:  “Issue 2:  Defendants did not neglect Perla Susana Leon,” “Issue 3:  Defendants’ Care and Treatment of Perla Susana Leon at all times, complied with the standard of care,” and “Issue No. 4:  Defendants did not cause or substantially contribute to Perla Susana Leon’s Alleged Injuries or Death.”  “If summary adjudication is sought, whether separately or as an alternative to the motion for summary judgment, the specific cause of action, affirmative defense, claims for damages, or issues of duty must be stated specifically in the notice of motion and be repeated, verbatim, in the separate statement of undisputed material facts.”  (Cal. Rules of Court Rule 3.1350, subd. (b).)  By failing to include these issues in the Notice of Motion, Plaintiffs were not given notice that they were to be adjudicated and addressing them on their merits would be a violation of due process.    

Defendants did not move for adjudication of the second cause of action for negligence.  The second cause of action for negligence is therefore outside the scope of the motion and not subject to adjudication.

 

 

4.     Fourth Cause of Action for Wrongful Death

The elements of a wrongful death action are a tort (negligence or other wrongful act), resulting death (causation), and loss (damages) suffered by the heirs. When the tort is negligence, plaintiff must, of course, prove defendant's duty and breach thereof.  (Lattimore v. Dickey (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 959, 968.)

The elements of a medical malpractice cause of action are (1) the duty of the professional to use such skill, prudence, and diligence as other members of his profession commonly possess and exercise; (2) a breach of that duty; (3) a proximate causal connection between the negligent conduct and the resulting injury; and (4) actual loss or damage resulting from the professional's negligence.  (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 468 fn 2.) 

            The standard of skill, knowledge and care prevailing in a medical community is ordinarily a matter within the knowledge of experts.  (Jambazian v. Borden (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 836, 844.)  “The standard of care for physicians is the reasonable degree of skill, knowledge and care ordinarily possessed and exercised by members of the medical profession under similar circumstances.  The test for determining familiarity with the standard of care is knowledge of similar conditions.  Geographical location may be a factor considered in making that determination, but, by itself, does not provide a practical basis for measuring similar circumstances.”  (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center, supra, at 159 Cal.App.4th at p. 470.)

            Whether the standard of care in the community has been breached presents a basic issue of fact in a malpractice action which can only be proved by expert opinion testimony unless the medical question is within the common knowledge of laypersons.  (Id. at p. 470, fn. 1; see also 1 Witkin, Cal. Evid. (6th ed. 2021), Opinion, §52.)  “[A]n opinion unsupported by reasons or explanations does not establish the absence of a material fact issue for trial, as required for summary judgment.”  (Golden Eagle Refinery Co., Inc. v. Associated Intern. Ins. Co. (2001) 85 Cal.App.4th 1300, 1315 (citing Kelley v. Trunk (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 519, 524).  Accordingly, where a plaintiff provides proper opposing expert testimony by a declaration, a triable issue of material fact is raised which precludes summary judgment.  (Jambazian, supra, 25 Cal.App.4th at p. 844.)  The absence of opinion evidence on this issue is fatal to the plaintiff’s cause of action.  (Willard v. Hagemeister (1981) 121 Cal.App.3d 406, 412.)  An expert declaration is admissible as long as Defendants’ expert states in detail their qualifications and the factual basis for their opinion.  (Kelley, supra, 66 Cal.App.4th at p. 524.) 

            Expert testimony is also required to establish the element of causation in a medical malpractice action.  “The law is well settled that in a personal injury action causation must be proven within a reasonable medical probability based on competent expert testimony. Mere possibility alone is insufficient to establish a prima facie case. That there is a distinction between a reasonable medical probability and a medical possibility needs little discussion. There can be many possible causes, indeed, an infinite number of circumstances that can produce an injury or disease. A possible cause only becomes probable when, in the absence of other reasonable causal explanations, it becomes more likely than not that the injury was a result of its action. This is the outer limit of inference upon which an issue may be submitted to the jury.”  (Jennings v. Palomar Pomerado Health Systems, Inc. (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 1108, 1118 (trial court properly struck medical expert testimony on issue of causation where expert’s opinion failed to provide a reasoned explanation regarding the etiology connecting plaintiff’s infected subcutaneous tissue to the nidus inside peritoneal cavity where retractor was retained).   

            Defendants’ Notice of Motion does include the wrongful death cause of action among the issues to be adjudicated.  The separate statement, however, does not specifically state the fifth cause of action wrongful death and the material facts that are relevant to the wrongful death claim are not identified.  Defendants’ motion for summary adjudication of the fifth cause of action for wrongful death may be denied on procedural grounds at the court’s discretion based on Defendants’ failure to comply with rule 3.1350.  (Truong v. Glasser (2009) 181 Cal.App.4th 102, 118.) 

            The motion for summary adjudication must also be denied as to the fourth cause of action for wrongful death on substantive grounds.  As discussed in connection with the first cause of action for elder abuse and the fifth cause of action for violation of patient rights under Health and Safety Code section 1430, subd. (b), a triable issue of material fact exists as to whether Leon was neglected.  Both parties submit competing expert declarations regarding Defendants’ alleged negligence and whether Defendants’ treatment and care of Leon was within the applicable standard of care.  (Defendants’ Evidence, Hibbs Dec., ¶¶48, 52, 53, 60; Rosen Dec., ¶¶48, 53, 61; Plaintiffs’ COE, Ex. B, ¶¶10-50; Ex. C, ¶¶19, 23-26, 39-40, 44-50.)  The existence of dueling expert opinions on the essential elements of breach and causation precludes summary adjudication of the wrongful death cause of action, which incorporates the elements of Plaintiffs’ negligence claim. 

The court finds that Defendants have not met their burden of showing that the fourth cause of action for wrongful death has no merit because Defendants have not shown that negligence cannot be established.  Even if Defendants had met their burden of showing that the fourth cause of action for wrongful death has no merit because Defendants had shown that negligence cannot be established with expert evidence, the court finds that Plaintiffs have met their burden to show that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the element of Defendants’ negligence.  (Defendants’ Evidence, Hibbs Dec., ¶¶48, 52, 53, 60; Rosen Dec., ¶¶48, 53, 61; Plaintiffs’ COE, Ex. B, ¶¶10-50; Ex. C, ¶¶19, 23-26, 39-40, 44-50.) 

The court therefore denies Defendants’ motion for summary adjudication as to the fourth cause of action for wrongful death. 

5.     Third Cause of Action for Negligent Hiring, Supervision and Retention (CACI 426)

The elements of a claim for negligent hiring, supervision or retention are set forth in CACI 426.  The elements are (1) employer defendant hired/supervised/retained an employee; (2) the employee was or became unfit or incompetent to perform work for which the employee was hired; (3) employer defendant knew or should have known that employee was or became unfit or incompetent and that this unfitness or incompetence created a particular risk to other; (4) that the employee’s unfitness or incompetence harmed plaintiff; and (5) employer defendant’s negligence in hiring, supervising or retaining the employee was a substantial factor in causing plaintiff’s harm.  (CACI 426.)

            “California case law recognizes the theory that an employer can be liable to a third person for negligently hiring, supervising, or retaining an unfit employee.”  (Doe v. Capital Cities (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 1038, 1054.) 

            Defendants rely on the exact same material facts submitted in support of the motion for summary adjudication of the first cause of action for elder abuse to satisfy their burden on summary adjudication of the negligent hiring, retention and supervision cause of action.  (Defendant’s Separate Statement, Issue 1, UMF Nos. 1-60; Issue 5, UMF Nos. 1-60.)  Defendants argue they are entitled to summary adjudication of the third cause of action for negligent hiring, retention and supervision because their experts opine that the care provided to Leon was within the applicable standard of care and, without evidence of incompetent care by the nurses, there is no basis to find negligent hiring, supervision or retention. 

For the reasons discussed above in connection with the first cause of action for elder abuse, the court finds Defendants have not met their burden of showing that the third cause of action for negligent hiring, supervision and retention has no merit because Defendants have not shown that the element of negligence cannot be established.  Even if Defendants had met their burden of showing that the third cause of action has no merit because the element of negligence cannot be established, the court finds that Plaintiffs have met their burden to show that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the element of Defendant’s negligence for the reasons set forth above.    

6.     Punitive Damages

“Under the clear and convincing standard, the evidence must be so clear as to leave no substantial doubt  and sufficiently strong to command the unhesitating assent of every reasonable mind.  Although the clear and convincing evidentiary standard is a stringent one, it does not impose on a plaintiff the obligation to ‘prove’ a case for punitive damages at summary judgment or summary adjudication.  Even so, where the plaintiff’s ultimate burden of proof will be by clear and convincing evidence, the higher standard of proof must be taken into account in ruling on a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication, since if a plaintiff is to prevail on a claim for punitive damages, it will be necessary that the evidence presented meet the higher evidentiary standard.  Likewise, in reviewing the trial court’s denial of PG&E’s motion for summary adjudication, we view the evidence with the higher burden of proof in mind.  Summary judgment or summary adjudication on the issue of punitive damages is proper only when no reasonable jury could find the plaintiff’s evidence to be clear and convincing proof of malice, fraud or oppression.”  (Pacific Gas and Electric Company v. Superior Court (2018) 24 Cal.App.5th 1150, 1158-1159.)

Malice is conduct that is intended by the defendant to cause the plaintiff harm or despicable conduct carried out with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of that person. (Civ. Code, §3294, subd. (c).)  “‘Recklessness’ refers to a subjective state of culpability greater than simple negligence, which has been described as a ‘deliberate disregard’ of the ‘high degree of probability’ that an injury will occur. Recklessness, unlike negligence, involves more than ‘inadvertence, incompetence, unskillfulness, or a failure to take precautions’ but rather rises to the level of a ‘conscious choice of a course of action ... with knowledge of the serious danger to others involved in it.’”  (Delaney, supra, 20 Cal.4th at pp. 31-32.)

Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication of the punitive damages claim is based on the exact same 60 material facts submitted in support of the first cause of action for elder abuse.  For the same reasons stated in connection with the elder abuse cause of action, Defendant fails to negate Plaintiff’s allegation that Defendants knowingly neglected Leon and engaged in despicable conduct carried out with willful and conscious disregard of Leon’s safety and well-being.  (Complaint, ¶¶75, 76, 79, 102.)  Defendants failed to establish as an issue of law that they provided for Leon’s basic needs, including hydration and prevention of bed sores, and any failure to do so was not the result of despicable conduct carried out with willful and conscious disregard of Leon’s safety and well-being.    

 The court finds that Defendants have not met their burden of showing that Plaintiffs’ punitive damages claim has no merit because Defendants have not shown that the element of malice cannot be established. 

The court therefore denies Defendants’ motion for summary adjudication as to the punitive damages claim.

ORDER

            The court denies Defendant’s motion for summary judgment or, in the alternative, summary adjudication.     

The court orders Plaintiffs to give notice of this ruling.
