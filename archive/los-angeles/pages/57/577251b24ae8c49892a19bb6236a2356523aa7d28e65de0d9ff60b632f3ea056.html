Case Number: 23AHCV01272    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANT’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO SPECIAL INTERROGATORY and GRANTING MONETARY SANCTIONS  

I.         INTRODUCTION

Plaintiffs, Scott Marshall-Kimball, and Julie Kimball, as Co-Trustees of the Gail Diann Fromm Family Trust dated November 11, 1980 (“Plaintiffs”), filed this action on June 5, 2023, against Defendants Cheryl L. Cuccia and Layne M. Cosloff, as Trustees of their respective trusts (“Defendants”). On November 22, 2023, Plaintiffs filed their First Amended Complaint, asserting causes of action for partition, accounting, breach of fiduciary duty, and declaratory relief. The dispute arises from the co-ownership of a multi-unit commercial property located at 9331 East Valley Blvd., Rosemead, California (“Subject Property”), where Plaintiffs seek a partition by sale due to irreconcilable differences regarding the management and disposition of the property.

On June 25, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to compel a further response to Defendant’s Special Interrogatory (“SPROG”) and for monetary sanctions. No opposition has been filed.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

A motion to compel a further response to interrogatories or production of documents must be noticed within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the propounding party and the responding party have agreed in writing. (Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. §§ 2030.300(c), 2031.310(c); see also Sexton v. Superior Court (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1409; Vidal Sassoon, Inc. v. Superior Court (1983) 147 Cal.App.3d 681, 685).) Otherwise, the propounding party waives any right to compel further responses. (Id.) The 45-day time limit is mandatory and jurisdictional. (Sexton, supra,58 Cal. App. 4th at 1410.) 

C.C.P. §2033.290 provides, in pertinent part, as follows: 

(a) On receipt of a response to requests for admissions, the party requesting admissions may move for an order compelling a further response if that party deems that either or both of the following apply: 

(1) An answer to a particular request is evasive or incomplete. 

(2) An objection to a particular request is without merit or too general

III.      ANALYSIS

Defendant moves to compel Plaintiff to provide a further response to SPROGs, Set One, No. 2, and requests monetary sanctions in the amount of $3,762 against Plaintiff and his counsel of record, Stone & Sallus LLP, jointly and severally. (Notice of Motion, p. 2.)

The motion is unopposed.

Defendant submits a separate statement showing that SPROG No. 2 requests that Plaintiff “SET FORTH all attorney’s fees incurred by YOU for the COMMON BENEFIT.” “Common benefit” is defined as attorney’s fees Plaintiff contends are recoverable as costs of partition under Code of Civil Procedure section 874.010. The interrogatory further defines “set forth” as providing the date the fees were incurred, a description of the attorney work performed, the time spent, the attorney who performed the work, and the attorney’s hourly rate. (SS, p. 2.)

Plaintiff responded solely with objections based on the attorney-client privilege and attorney work-product doctrine. Here the Court finds a further response warranted. Plaintiff seeks to recover attorney’s fees allegedly incurred for the common benefit in connection with the partition. Thus, the amount and basis of the fees Plaintiff contends are recoverable as costs of partition are directly at issue. Plaintiff may not withhold all responsive fee information merely by asserting the attorney-client privilege and work-product doctrine. To the extent a description of attorney work would disclose privileged communications, attorney impressions, or litigation strategy, such information may be redacted.

Thus, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s motion to compel further response to SPROG No.2. Plaintiff to provide further responses within 20 days’ notice of this order.

Sanctions

            Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300, subdivision (d), requires the Court to impose monetary sanctions against the party who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further responses unless the Court finds substantial justification or other circumstances make sanctions unjust. 

Defendant seeks $3,762 in monetary sanctions against Plaintiff and his counsel. Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s refusal to provide the requested information constitutes a misuse of the discovery process and lacks substantial justification. Defendant’s counsel declares that his billing rate is $495 per hour and that he spent 4.6 hours researching and drafting the motion and anticipated an additional four hours reviewing an opposition, preparing a reply, and attending the hearing. (Niman Decl., ¶ 9.)

Here, the Court finds monetary sanctions warranted. However, because Plaintiff did not file an opposition and Defendant therefore did not file a reply, the Court finds the amount requested excessive. The Court finds two hours at counsel’s hourly rate of $495 reasonable, for a total sanction award of $990.

Thus, the Court further GRANTS Defendant’s request for monetary sanctions in the amount of $990, payable by Plaintiff and his counsel of record, Stone & Sallus LLP, jointly and severally.

            IV.       CONCLUSION

            The Court GRANTS Defendant’s motion to compel a further response to SPROG No. 2. Plaintiff shall provide a further verified response within 30 days of notice of this order.

The Court further GRANTS Defendant’s request for monetary sanctions in the amount of $990, payable by Plaintiff and his counsel of record, Stone & Sallus LLP, jointly and severally within 30 days’ notice of this order.

Defendant to give notice.

Case Number: 23GDCV02700    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING NON-PARTY ESLY FOMBONA’S MOTION TO INTERVENE

I.         INTRODUCTION

This is a sexual abuse action where Plaintiffs Jane Doe KC 1, Jane Doe 3, Jane Doe 4 allege Defendants Gloria De Los Angeles Trevino Ruiz aka Gloria Trevi (herein “Defendant Ruiz” or “Ms. Ruiz”); Sergio Gustavo Andrade Sanchez aka Sergio Andrade; and Maria Raquenel Portillo Jimenez aka Mary Boquitas and Mary Raquenel (herein “Defendant Andrade” and Defendant Portillo”) sexually abused Plaintiffs while Plaintiffs were children. 

On August 14, 2026, nonparty Esly Fombona (“Fombona”), in pro per, filed the instant motion for leave to intervene pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 387. On September 2, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an opposition. On September 8, 2026, Fombona filed a reply.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

“An intervention takes place when a nonparty, deemed an intervenor, becomes a party to an action or proceeding between other persons by doing any of the following: (1) Joining a plaintiff in claiming what is sought by the complaint. (2) Uniting with a defendant in resisting the claims of a plaintiff. (3) Demanding anything adverse to both a plaintiff and a defendant.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 387 subd. (b)(1)-(3).) “A nonparty shall petition the court for leave to intervene by noticed motion or ex parte application. The petition shall include a copy of the proposed complaint in intervention or answer in intervention and set forth the grounds upon which intervention rests.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 387 subd. (c).)  

“The court shall, upon timely application, permit a nonparty to intervene in the action or proceeding if either of the following conditions is satisfied: (A) A provision of law confers an unconditional right to intervene. (B) The person seeking intervention claims an interest relating to the property or transaction that is the subject of the action and that person is so situated that the disposition of the action may impair or impede that person's ability to protect that interest, unless that person's interest is adequately represented by one or more of the existing parties.” (Code Civ. Pro. § subd. 387(d)(1)(A)-(B).) “The court may, upon timely application, permit a nonparty to intervene in the action or proceeding if the person has an interest in the matter in litigation, or in the success of either of the parties, or an interest against both.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 387 subd. (d)(2).)  

“If leave to intervene is granted by the court, the intervenor shall do both of the following: (1) Separately file the complaint in intervention, answer in intervention, or both. (2) Serve a copy of the order, or notice of the court's decision or order, granting leave to intervene and the pleadings in intervention as follows: (A) A party to the action or proceeding who has not yet appeared shall be served in the same manner for service of summons pursuant to Article 3 (commencing with Section 415.10) of Chapter 4 of Title 5 of Part 2. (B) A party who has appeared in the action or proceeding, whether represented by an attorney or not represented by an attorney, shall be served in the same manner for service of summons pursuant to Article 3 (commencing with Section 415.10) of Chapter 4 of Title 5 of Part 2,or in the manner provided by Chapter 5 (commencing with Section 1010) of Title 14 of Part 2. (Code Civ. Pro. § 387 subd. (e).)  

The court permits intervention when the following factors are met: (1) the proper procedures have been followed; (2) the nonparty has a direct and immediate interest in the action; (3) the intervention will not enlarge the issues in the litigation; and (4) the reasons for the intervention outweigh any opposition by the parties presently in the action. (Siena Court Homeowners Ass’n v. Green Valley Corp. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1416, 1428. CCP § 387 should be liberally construed in favor of intervention. (Simpson Redwood Co. v. State of California (1987) 196 Cal.App.3d 1192, 1200.)

III.      ANALYSIS

Fombona argues that he should be granted leave to intervene under Code of Civil Procedure section 387 and thereafter permitted to strike allegedly false and defamatory statements from the record under section 436. Fombona states that he is an independent journalist residing in Arizona and contends that Jane Doe 3 made false statements about him in an August 3, 2026, declaration, including accusations of harassment and stalking. Fombona argues he has a fundamental interest in protecting his reputation, livelihood, and safety. (Motion, pp. 3-4.)

Fombona argues that he has a direct and immediate interest in this action because the challenged statements concern him personally and have been placed in the public record. He further argues that the existing parties do not adequately represent his interests because none has an incentive to defend his reputation or address the alleged harm caused by the statements. (Id. at p.4.)

The Court is not persuaded.

First, Fombona failed to comply with the mandatory requirements for intervention. Specifically, Code of Civil Procedure section 387, subdivision (c) requires that the petition include a copy of the proposed complaint in intervention or answer in intervention. Fombona’s moving papers included neither. Although Fombona attaches a proposed limited complaint in intervention to his reply, submission of the proposed pleading for the first time on reply deprives the opposing parties of an opportunity to address the pleading in their opposition. The Court therefore declines to treat the belated filing as curing the defect.

Even assuming the procedural defect could be cured, Fombona fails to establish the direct and immediate interest necessary for intervention.

It is well settled that the intervener's interest in the matter in litigation must be direct, not consequential, and that it must be an interest which is proper to be determined in the action in which intervention is sought.” (Simpson Redwood Co. v. State of California (1987) 196 Cal.App.3d 1192, 1199-1200.) Furthermore, “the interest which entitles a party to intervene must be an interest in the matter in litigation in the suit as originally brought, and of such a present, direct and immediate character that the intervener will either gain or lose by the direct effect of the judgment. [Citations.] An intervener cannot be permitted to broaden the scope or function of such special proceeding by urging claims or contentions which have their proper forum elsewhere.” (People v. Brophy (1942) 49 Cal.App.2d 15, 34-35 (emphasis in original).)  

An interest is consequential and thus insufficient for intervention when the action in which intervention is sought does not directly affect the proposed intervenor, although the results of the action may indirectly benefit or harm the proposed intervenor. (Continental Vinyl Products v. Mead Corp. (1972) 27 Cal.App.3d 543, 550.) “One cardinal rule which is established by the cases is that an intervener's interest must be more direct and immediate than that of a simple creditor of one of the parties.” (California Physicians' Service v. Superior Court (1980) 102 Cal.App.3d 91, 95-96.)  

Here, the subject of this action concerns Plaintiffs' claims against Defendants arising from alleged sexual abuse, exploitation, trafficking, and related misconduct. Fombona is not a plaintiff or defendant to those claims, no cause of action is asserted by or against him, and he claims no interest in the property or transactions underlying Plaintiffs' causes of action. The disposition of Plaintiffs' claims will neither impose liability upon Fombona nor adjudicate or extinguish any right belonging to him. The Court finds that these asserted interests are consequential rather than direct and immediate. Although Fombona may personally or professionally be affected by statements contained in the record, his interests will not be affected by the direct legal operation of the judgment in this action. Whether Plaintiffs or Defendants ultimately prevail will not determine whether the statements concerning Fombona are true, whether Fombona engaged in harassment or stalking, whether his activities constitute legitimate journalism, or whether he has suffered reputational or professional harm. Thus, Fombona will neither gain nor lose by the direct effect of the judgment.

Further, permitting intervention would substantially enlarge the issues in this action. Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that Fombona has failed to satisfy the requirements for either mandatory or permissive intervention under Code of Civil Procedure section 387. The motion for leave to intervene is DENIED.

IV.       ORDER

             The Court DENIES Fombona’s motion to intervene.

Clerk is to give notice.

Case Number: 25NNCV02495    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANT’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO FORM INTERROGATORIES, SET TWO IN PART and GRANTING REQUESTS FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS IN PART

I.         INTRODUCTION

This is an action for negligence and premises liability arising from a slip and fall injury Plaintiff Lilia Quentero alleges she suffered on property owned by Defendant Jean P. Arslan. Plaintiff alleges she slipped on an unknown substance going down the stairs. 

On April 11, 2025, Plaintiff filed her Complaint against Defendant alleging two causes of action for negligence and premises liability. 

On May 21, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to compel further responses to Form Interrogatories (FROGs), Set Two, and for monetary sanctions. On September 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition, and on September 4, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

A motion to compel a further response to interrogatories or production of documents must be noticed within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the propounding party and the responding party have agreed in writing. (Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. §§ 2030.300(c), 2031.310(c); see also Sexton v. Superior Court (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1409; Vidal Sassoon, Inc. v. Superior Court (1983) 147 Cal.App.3d 681, 685).) Otherwise, the propounding party waives any right to compel further responses. (Id.) The 45-day time limit is mandatory and jurisdictional. (Sexton, supra,58 Cal. App. 4th at 1410.) 

C.C.P. §2033.290 provides, in pertinent part, as follows: 

(a) On receipt of a response to requests for admissions, the party requesting admissions may move for an order compelling a further response if that party deems that either or both of the following apply: 

(1) An answer to a particular request is evasive or incomplete. 

(2) An objection to a particular request is without merit or too general.

Code of Civil Procedure sections 2030.300, subdivision (d), and 2031.310, subdivision (h), provide that the Court shall impose a monetary sanction against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further responses unless the Court finds the party acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of sanctions unjust.

III.      ANALYSIS

Plaintiff initially served objections-only responses to Form Interrogatory No. 17.1 on May 11, 2026. Because responses containing only objections need not be verified, the lack of verification did not render those responses a nullity.

After the motion was filed, Plaintiff served substantive supplemental responses on June 1, 2026. Defendant acknowledges receipt of those responses but argues the motion is not moot because the supplemental responses remain unverified.

The Court declines to determine the substantive sufficiency of the June 1 responses because those responses were served after the motion was filed and were not the responses challenged in Defendant’s separate statement. Any dispute concerning their substantive sufficiency may be addressed separately. However, because the June 1 responses contain substantive answers, they must be verified.

Thus, the motion is GRANTED in part. Plaintiff is ordered to provide a verification of her June 1, 2026, supplemental response to Form Interrogatory No. 17.1 within 10 days’ notice of this ruling.

Sanctions

Here, both parties request monetary sanctions. The Court DENIES Plaintiff’s request and GRANTS Defendant’s request in part against Plaintiff only.

Although Plaintiff served supplemental substantive responses on June 1, 2026, those responses were unverified. Thus, Defendant was justified in pursuing the motion to obtain verified responses. The Court finds one hour of attorney’s time at Defendant’s counsel’s rate of $450/hour reasonable.  (Monadjemi Decl., ¶ 13.)

Thus, monetary sanctions GRANTED against Plaintiff only in the amount of $450.

IV.       ORDER

            The Court GRANTS in part motion to compel further responses to FROG, Set Two. Plaintiff is ordered to provide a verification of her June 1, 2026, supplemental responses within 10 days’ notice of this order.

The Court further GRANTS monetary sanctions in the amount of $450 against Plaintiff only to be paid within 30 days’ notice of this order.

Defendant is to give notice.

Case Number: 25NNCV08348    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER OVERRULING DEFENDANTS’ DEMURRER AND DENYING MOTION TO STRIKE

I.         INTRODUCTION

            This action commenced on November 20, 2025. On May 12, 2026, Plaintiff Mohammed Sadeghi (“Plaintiff”) filed the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging causes of action for: (1) breach of contract (insurance policy); (2) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing (contractual and tortious bad faith insurance); (3) financial elder abuse in violation of Welfare and Institutions Code sections 15610.30 and 15657.5; and (4) violation of the Unfair Competition Law under Business and Professions Code section 17200.

On June 16, 2026, Defendant California Automobile Insurance Company (“Defendant” or “CAIC”) filed the instant motion to strike portions of the FAC. On June 17, 2026, Defendant filed the instant demurrer to the FAC. On September 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed oppositions to both motions. On September 10, 2026, Defendant filed replies.

Meet and confer

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a), the demurring party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading at least five days before the responsive pleading is due. This must occur in person, telephone or by video conference with the aim of resolving any objections raised in the demurrer. Additionally, the demurring party must file and serve a declaration detailing the meet and confer efforts. It should be noted, however, that the failure to meet and confer is not itself grounds for sustaining or overruling a demurrer or granting or denying a motion to strike. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a)(4), 435.5, subd. (a)(4).)

Here, Defendant’s counsel attempted to meet and confer prior to filing the demurrer. On June 5, 2026, Defendant’s counsel sent Plaintiff a letter outlining the grounds for the demurrer and requesting a response by June 12, 2026. (Kim Decl., ¶ 4.) Plaintiff did not respond. (Kim Decl., ¶ 6.) On June 15, 2026, Defendant’s counsel sent a follow-up email but received no response. (Kim Decl., ¶¶ 5–6.)

Thus, the requirement is not satisfied, however, the Court elects to consider the demurrer on the merits.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.) 

CCP section 430.10(f) provides that a pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous and unintelligible. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(f).) “A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) “A demurrer for uncertainty will be sustained only where the complaint is so bad that defendant cannot reasonably respond—i.e., he or she cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him or her.” (Weil & Brown, Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group) § 7:85 (emphasis in original).) “The objection of uncertainty does not go to the failure to allege sufficient facts.” (Brea v. McGlashan (1934) 3 Cal.App.2d 454, 459.) “It goes to the doubt as to what the pleader means by the facts alleged.” (Id.) “Such a demurrer should not be sustained where the allegations of the complaint are sufficiently clear to apprise the defendant of the issues which he is to meet.” (People v. Lim (1941) 18 Cal.2d 872, 882.)  

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

Further, the court may, upon motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false, or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.) 

Civ. Code §3294: 

(a) In an action for the breach of an obligation not arising from contract, where it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice, the plaintiff, in addition to the actual damages, may recover damages for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant. 

(c) As used in this section, the following definitions shall apply: 

(1) “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others. 

(2) “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights. 

(3) “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury. 

III.      ANALYSIS

            Defendant demurs to the first cause of action for breach of contract, the second cause of action for breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and the third cause of action for financial elder abuse. Defendant also demurs to all causes of action asserted against Mercury Insurance Services, LLC (“Mercury Services”) and Mercury General Corporation (“Mercury General”).

First and second causes of action: breach of contract and breach of implied 

“The standard elements of a claim for breach of contract are: ‘(1) the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) damage to plaintiff therefrom.’” (Wall Street Network, Ltd. v. New York Times Co. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1171, 1178.) 

The elements for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing are: (1) existence of a contract between plaintiff and defendant; (2) plaintiff performed his contractual obligations or was excused from performing them; (3) the conditions requiring defendant’s performance had occurred; (4) the defendant unfairly interfered with the plaintiff’s right to receive the benefits of the contract; and (5) the plaintiff was harmed by the defendant’s conduct. (Merced Irr. Dist. V. County of Mariposa (E.D. Cal. 2013) 941 F.Supp.2d 1237, 1280 [discussing California law].) 

Here, Defendant demurs to these causes of action primarily on the ground that they are barred by the Policy’s one-year contractual limitations period. Defendant argues, and provides a chart illustrating, that Plaintiff discovered the alleged loss on December 15, 2023, reported the claim on January 17, 2024, and that any tolling of the contractual limitations period ended when Defendants closed the claim on February 19, 2024. Defendant further argues that, by the time the claim was reopened on January 28, 2025, the one-year limitations period had already expired and that the subsequent closure of the claim on February 14, 2025, did not restart or otherwise extend the limitations period. Thus, Defendant contends that the limitations period had expired well before Plaintiff commenced this action on November 24, 2025. (Demurrer, pp. 8–11.)

The FAC alleges that the February 19, 2024, closure was not a denial of coverage. Rather, Plaintiff alleges that the claim was closed based on Plaintiff’s purported failure to respond regarding rescheduling an inspection and that, on March 5, 2024, Defendants’ adjuster informed Plaintiff that Defendants “can still re-open the claim and continue with the claims process.” (FAC, ¶ 17(c).) The FAC further alleges that Defendants subsequently reopened the claim and continued adjusting it. Specifically, on January 28, 2025, an adjuster contacted Plaintiff regarding “the claim you reopened” and requested additional documentation to “move the claim forward.” (FAC, ¶ 17(e).) Plaintiff further alleges that Defendants again closed the claim on February 14, 2025, but continued to identify items necessary to consider the claim for further review. (FAC, ¶ 17(f).)

Under California law, a contractual one-year suit limitation period is equitably tolled from the time the insured gives notice of the claim until the insurer formally denies the claim in writing. (Prudential-LMI Commercial Insurance v. Superior Court (1990) 51 Cal.3d 674, 678.) To terminate the tolling period, the insurer’s written denial must be unequivocal. (Aliberti v. Allstate Ins. Co. (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 138, 148.) Although a denial need not use the specific words “deny” or “denial,” the communication must clearly convey that the insurer has rejected the claim. (Migliore v. Mid-Century Ins. Co. (2002) 97 Cal.App.4th 592, 605.) An insurer’s willingness to reconsider an otherwise unequivocal denial does not render the denial equivocal. (Id. at pp. 605–606.)

Here, the SOL argument depends on Defendants’ contention that the February 19, 2024, closure of Plaintiff’s claim terminated the equitable tolling period. The allegations of the FAC, however, do not establish as a matter of law that the February 19, 2024, communication constituted an unequivocal denial of Plaintiff’s claim. Rather, the FAC alleges that the February 19, 2024, letter closed the claim based on Plaintiff’s purported failure to respond regarding rescheduling and did not deny coverage. Plaintiff further alleges that, on March 5, 2024, Defendants’ adjuster informed Plaintiff that Defendants “can still re-open the claim and continue with the claims process.” (FAC, ¶ 17(c).)

Moreover, the FAC alleges that Defendants subsequently reopened the claim and continued the adjustment process. (See FAC ¶ 17e-f.) On demurrer, the Court accepts properly pleaded material facts as true, and accepting the allegation of the FAC as true, the Court cannot determine as a matter of law that February 19, 2024, closure constituted an unequivocal written denial that terminated equitable tolling. 

Further, as to the second cause of action, the Court finds Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged unreasonable claims handling. The FAC alleges Defendants failed to pay any portion of Plaintiff’s claim despite timely notice and documentation, failed to timely inspect the property and conduct a complete invitation, issued successive procedural closures (See FAC, ¶ 31(a)-(h). Accepting these allegations as true, it is enough to allege Defendant unreasonably interfered with Plaintiffs right to receive benefits under the policy. 

Thus, the Court OVERRULES the demurrer as to the first and second causes of action

Third cause of action: financial elder abuse

Defendant demurs to the third cause of action for financial elder abuse, arguing that the claim is likewise barred by the Policy’s one-year contractual limitations period. Defendant further argues that the allegations underlying the claim are the same allegations supporting Plaintiff’s bad faith claim and are insufficient to establish elder abuse. (Demurrer, p. 11.)

First, as discussed further above, the Court cannot determine as a matter of law at the demurrer stage that the Policy’s one-year contractual limitations period expired before Plaintiff commenced this action. Thus, OVERRULED in part on this ground. 

Next, To establish financial elder abuse, the plaintiff must allege that the defendant took or retained the plaintiff’s property; that the plaintiff was 65 years of age or older at the time of the conduct; that the defendant took or retained the property for a wrongful use or with the intent to defraud; that the plaintiff was harmed; and that the defendant’s conduct was a substantial factor in cause the plaintiff’s harm. (See CACI, § 3100.) An insurer’s wrongful withholding of insurance benefits may constitute a retention of an elder’s property where the insurer acted unreasonably or in bad faith. (Paslay v. State Farm General Ins. Co. (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 639, 658-659.)

Here, the FAC alleges that Plaintiff is over 65 years of age and that Defendants wrongfully retained Plaintiff’s property by delaying and refusing to pay benefits owed under the Policy. (FAC, ¶¶ 35–36.) Plaintiff further alleges that Defendants knew or should have known that their conduct was likely to harm Plaintiff and that Plaintiff suffered damages as a result. (FAC, ¶¶ 37, 39.) As discussed above, the FAC also sufficiently alleges unreasonable claims handling at the pleading stage. The Court therefore finds these allegations sufficient to state a cause of action for financial elder abuse.

Thus, the Court OVERRULES Defendant’s demurrer as to the third cause of action.

Mercury Insurance Services, LLC and Mercury General Corporation

            Finally, Defendant demurs to all causes of action asserted against Mercury Services and Mercury General, arguing that Plaintiff has not alleged a contractual relationship with either entity. (Demurrer, pp.11-12.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues alter ego allegations here are sufficient. The FAC alleges unity of interest and ownership, commingled funds, overlapping officers, shared offices and personnel, inadequate capitalization, use of the entities as mere shells and conduits, and that respecting separate existence would produce an inequitable result. (FAC ¶ 6.) (Opposition, pp.7-8.)

“In California, two conditions must be met before the alter ego doctrine will be invoked.  First, there must be such a unity of interest and ownership between the corporation and its equitable owner that the separate personalities of the corporation and the shareholder do not in reality exist.  Second, there must be an inequitable result if the acts in question are treated as those of the corporation alone.”  (Sonora Diamond Corp. v. Superior Court (2000) 83 Cal.App 4th 523, 538; Mesler v. Bragg Management Co. (1985) 39 Cal.3d 290, 300.) 

Here, the FAC alleges that CAIC issued the Policy, Mercury Services administered and adjusted Plaintiff’s claim, and Mercury General is the parent corporation of CAIC and Mercury Services and exercised substantial control over their claims-handling policies and practices. The FAC further alleges unity of interest and ownership among Defendants, including commingling of funds and assets, overlapping officers and personnel, shared offices and resources, inadequate capitalization, disregard of corporate formalities, and use of the entities as shells or conduits for one another. Plaintiff further alleges that discovery is expected to disclose additional facts supporting alter ego liability. (FAC, ¶¶ 2, 6.)

The Court finds these allegations sufficient, at the pleading stage, to allege alter ego liability. Thus, the Court OVERRULES Defendant’s demurrer as to Mercury Services and Mercury General

Motion to strike

            Defendant moves to strike paragraphs 1, 2, and 7 of Plaintiff’s prayer for relief. Specifically, Defendant seeks to strike Plaintiff’s requests for compensatory damages, Brandt attorney’s fees, and punitive damages.

Brandt fees - Paragraph 1

As a preliminary matter, paragraph 2 of Plaintiff’s prayer for relief seeks Brandt attorney’s fees as an element of tort damages incurred to obtain policy benefits under the second cause of action. Defendant identifies paragraph 2 as seeking Brandt attorney’s fees but does not separately address why such fees should be stricken.

When an insurer tortiously withholds policy benefits and reasonably compels the insured to retain an attorney to obtain the benefits due under the policy, the attorney’s fees incurred to obtain those benefits are recoverable as tort damages. (Brandt v. Superior Court (1985) 37 Cal.3d 813, 817.)

As discussed above, Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged a cause of action for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. Thus, the Court DENIES the motion to strike paragraph 2 of Plaintiff’s prayer for relief.

Punitive damages - paragraph 7

As to punitive damages, paragraph 7, as discussed above in connection with the demurrer, Plaintiff alleges more than a mere disagreement concerning coverage. The FAC alleges that Defendants knew Plaintiff was elderly and suffered from chronic medical conditions, failed to appear for a scheduled inspection and then falsely represented that the adjuster had appeared, used expiring portal links and communications that obscured the adjuster’s identity, repeatedly closed the claim while continuing to request information, and later invoked the contractual limitations period. (Opposition, pp. 4-5.)

At the pleading stage, and accepting these allegations as true, the Court finds the FAC sufficiently alleges facts supporting Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages. Thus, the Court DENIES to strike paragraph 7. 

Compensatory damages - paragraph 1 

Finally, as to compensatory damages, Defendant argues that paragraph 1 of Plaintiff’s prayer improperly seeks compensatory, consequential, incidental, and general damages under the fourth cause of action for violation of the UCL, for which such damages are unavailable. (Motion, p. 7.)

Here, Plaintiff’s first three causes of action seek damages, while Plaintiff separately seeks restitution and injunctive relief under the fourth cause of action. (Opposition, p.3.) The Court declines to strike Plaintiff’s request for “compensatory, consequential, incidental, and general damages in an amount according to proof at trial” based merely on Defendant’s presumption that the request applies to the UCL cause of action. Thus, the Court DENIES to strike paragraph 1.

The motion to strike is DENIED in its entirety.

IV.       ORDER

            The Court OVERRULES Defendant’s demurrer to the first, second and third causes of action. The Court further DENIES Defendant’s motion to strike in its entirety.

Defendant is to give notice.
