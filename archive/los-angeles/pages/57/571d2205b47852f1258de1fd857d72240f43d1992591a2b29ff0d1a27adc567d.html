DEPARTMENT 734 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV32585    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: 734

The following constitutes the Court's ruling on the ex parte application of Defendant General Motors LLC.  The ruling is posted here for the convenience of the parties.  The matter will not be called for hearing on September 18, 2026.

The Court has reviewed the ex parte application filed by Defendant General Motors LLC,and finds the matter suitable for decision in chambers without argument.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 166(a)(1).)

Defendant requests an order consolidating all hearings on its several recently filed discovery motions, or alternatively, continuing or vacating the trial date.

The application is DENIED.  The court understands Defendant’s desire to have the motion heard before trial.  However, the limit on the number of available motion hearings is necessary to manage the court’s resources.  These limits work to the benefit of litigants by ensuring that the court has adequate time to consider each motion that is brought.  A request for a specially set hearing is essentially a request to cut in line ahead of parties who have anticipated the need for motion practice and timely reserved their hearings.  A specially set hearing takes away precious calendar time from parties who have timely scheduled their motions, and often delays the start of trials or other proceedings.  Defendant has failed to show good cause for the request.  It is incumbent upon counsel to plan their discovery in a manner that anticipates the necessary lead time for any motions to be heard.  Failure to do so is not an exigent circumstance that justifies extraordinary relief pursuant to Rule of Court 3.1202.  Moreover, the papers presented by Defendant do not show why, in the exercise of due diligence, the discovery motion at issue could not have been brought sooner. 

Unless the time for discovery is extended by stipulation of the parties or an order of the court, general discovery ends 30 days before “the date initially set for the trial of the action” and discovery motions must be heard by the 15th day before trial.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 2024.020.)  The trial date in this case is October 6, 2026, meaning that discovery motions must be heard no later than September 21, 2026.  Defendant’s motions to compel were filed on September 4, 2026, and served by electronic mail, such that the earliest the motion could be heard with statutory notice is September 30, 2026 (16 court days notice (Code Civ. Proc. § 1005) plus two days for service by email (Code Civ. Proc. § 1010.6.)  

Since the motions were not filed in time to be heard before the discovery motion cutoff, Defendant would receive a windfall if the Court were to hear the motion without requiring Defendant to make the requisite showing of good cause for extending the discovery motion cutoff, including filing of the meet and confer declaration required by Code of Civil Procedure section 2024.050 when a motion to extend discovery cutoffs is filed.  (See Pelton-Shepherd Indus. v. Delta Packaging Prods. (2008) 165 Cal.App. 1568, 1586-87 (holding that trial court erred by ruling on post-cutoff discovery motions without first deciding whether good cause existed to reopen discovery.)

            Plaintiff alleges that he was subjected to sexually harassing comments by two supervisors and Defendant City of Los Angeles did nothing to prevent such harassment from continuing.

 

Defendant City of Los Angeles demurs to the First Amended Complaint.

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

Defendant City of Los Angeles’ demurrer to First Amended Complaint is SUSTAINED without leave to amend as to the second cause of action, unless Plaintiff can demonstrate a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. Otherwise, Defendant is to answer the remaining allegations of the First Amended Complaint within 10 days.

 

ANALYSIS

 

Meet and Confer

 

            The Declaration of Warren M. Williams reflects that Defendant’s counsel satisfied the meet and confer requirement set forth in Civ. Proc. Code, § 430.41.

 

Discussion

 

            The Court has reviewed the moving, opposing and reply briefs filed by the parties, but only addresses the points which the Court deems to be material to the disposition of this motion.

 

Defendant City of Los Angeles demurs to the First Amended Complaint as follows:

 

1.         Second Cause of Action (Retaliation in Violation of FEHA—Gov. Code § 12940 et seq.)

 

            Defendant argues that what Plaintiff complains of is a failure to act on complaints of harassment, which is not an adverse employment action.

 

            Plaintiff argues that, taken together, Defendant’s toleration of harassment amounts to an adverse employment action.

 

            Defendant argues that simply labeling something retaliatory does not make it so.

 

            Defendant cites the California Supreme Court’s opinion in Bailey v. San Francisco Dist. Attorney’s Office (2024) 16 Cal.5th 611. The Court agrees that the principles set forth therein lead to the conclusion that Plaintiff has not pled actionable retaliation under the FEHA.

 

It is unlawful for an employer to “discharge, expel, or otherwise discriminate against any person because the person has opposed any practices forbidden under this part or because the person has filed a complaint, testified, or assisted in any proceeding under this part.” (§ 12940, subd. (h).) This type of unlawful employment practice is known simply as “retaliation.” (Citation omitted.)  To establish a prima facie case of retaliation under FEHA, an employee must show that (1) she engaged in a “protected activity,” (2) the employer subjected her to an adverse employment action, and (3) a causal link existed between the protected activity and the employer’s action. (Ibid.)

 [*637]

. . .

The phrase “adverse employment action” does not appear in FEHA but “has become a familiar shorthand expression referring to the kind, nature, or degree of adverse action against an employee that will support a cause of action under a relevant provision of an employment discrimination statute.” (Citation omitted.) We have held that the language of section 12940, subdivision (h)—making it unlawful to “discharge, expel, or otherwise discriminate against” an employee—refers to and encompasses the same forms of adverse employment actions that are actionable in FEHA discrimination claims under section 12940, subdivision (a). (Citation omitted.)  Thus, for both discrimination and retaliation claims, an adverse employment action is one that “materially affects the terms, conditions, or privileges of employment.” (Id. at p. 1051.)8

Although “a mere offensive utterance or even a pattern of social slights by either the employer or coemployees” is not actionable, “the phrase ‘terms, conditions, or privileges’ of employment must be interpreted liberally and with a reasonable appreciation of the realities of the workplace in order to afford employees the appropriate and generous protection against employment discrimination that the FEHA was intended to provide.” (Citation omitted.) “Minor or relatively trivial adverse actions or conduct by employers or fellow employees that, from an objective perspective, are reasonably likely to do no more than anger or upset an employee cannot properly be viewed as materially affecting the terms, conditions, or privileges of employment and are not actionable . …” (Citation omitted.) But adverse treatment that is reasonably likely to impair an employee’s job performance or prospects for advancement in their career falls within the reach of FEHA’s antiretaliation provision. (Citation omitted.)

We have recognized that “[r]etaliation claims are inherently fact-specific, and the impact of an employer’s action in a particular case must be evaluated in context,” considering “the unique circumstances of the affected employee as well as the workplace context of the claim.” (Citation omitted..) “[T]he significance of particular types of adverse actions must be evaluated by taking into account the legitimate interests of both the employer and the employee.” (Citation omitted.) Additionally, the alleged retaliatory acts are to be considered “collectively,” rather than individually. (Citation omitted.) Retaliatory acts may take the form of “a series of subtle, yet damaging, injuries,” and “[e]nforcing a requirement that each act separately constitute an adverse employment action would subvert the purpose and intent of the statute.” (Citation omitted.)

(Bailey v. San Francisco Dist. Attorney’s Office (2024) 16 Cal.5th 611, 638 [bold emphasis added].)

 

Critically, this course of conduct was undertaken, not by a coworker, but by the human resources manager responsible for receiving complaints of harassment and discrimination in the workplace. It bears repeating that  the opportunity to seek, obtain, and hold employment without discrimination because of race is a civil right. (§ 12921, subd. (a).) A reasonable trier of fact could find that Taylor-Monachino’s course of conduct, in light of her position, effectively sought to withdraw Bailey’s means of reporting and addressing workplace discrimination and harassment. This is not a “[m]inor or relatively trivial” action that does no more than anger or upset the affected employee. (Citaiton omitted.) Rather,  the withdrawal of an employee’s right to avail themselves of the HR process typically available to other employees materially affects the “terms, conditions, or privileges” of their employment. (Citations omitted.) Such treatment is reasonably likely to impair the affected employee’s job performance insofar as it leaves them unprotected from the very harms FEHA was designed to eliminate. Pointed threats compounded the harm of Taylor-Monachino’s conduct. (Citation omitted.)

To be clear, our opinion today does not hold that an employer’s mere inaction (e.g., the failure to investigate a claim of racial harassment or take corrective action)—which separately may bear on the employer’s liability for the harassment itself—constitutes an act of retaliation. Had the City merely failed to further investigate Larkin’s alleged harassment following Bailey’s report of the same on January 29, no actionable retaliation would appear. The instant claim of retaliation, however, is not based on mere inaction. It is based on an HR manager’s purposeful obstruction of Bailey’s complaint, which included an admonition that Bailey might create a hostile work environment for her harasser if she persisted. And it is based on Taylor-Monachino’s escalating threats in August, when she mouthed the words, “you are going to get it.” Though ultimately it is for a jury to decide whether Taylor-Monachino’s conduct rises to the level of an adverse employment action in this case, such conduct could be understood as quintessentially retaliatory. That is, it appears designed to punish Bailey for engaging in protected activity (i.e., pursuing her complaint of harassment) and threatens further punishment should she persist. Where a supervisor or other person of authority obstructs and threatens to punish a reporting employee if she persists in bringing a complaint to higher level officials, such acts may be considered by a jury to constitute actionable retaliation.

 

(Bailey supra, 16 Cal.5th at 639-40 [bold emphasis and underlining added].)

 

            The Court addresses the alleged retaliatory acts identified by Plaintiff in the opposition:

 

¿        Retaliatory Act No 1: “In retaliation, Lt. Clancy failed to follow the LAPD manual and abide by the alleged zero tolerance policy to initiate the required personnel complaint which could have prevented future harassing conduct which Plaintiff faced.” (FAC at ¶ 22)

 

This is mere inaction in the form of failure to initiate a personnel complaint as required by the LAPD zero tolerance policy. This is not retaliation. ((Bailey supra, 16 Cal.5th at 639-41.)

 

¿        Retaliatory Act No 2: “Lt. Clancy was essentially condoning and creating an ongoing hostile work environment for Plaintiff. Failing to properly investigate was in addition to forcing Plaintiff to endure the hostile work environment. The tolerance in allowing the sexual harassment and hostile work environment to persist altered the conditions of Plaintiff’s employment such that it was further adverse employment action.” (FAC at ¶ 22)

 

Again, this is mere inaction in the form of failure to investigate or address the alleged hostile work environment. This is not retaliation. ((Bailey supra, 16 Cal.5th at 639-41.)

 

 

¿        Retaliatory Act No 3: “Throughout, the Department failed to take proper precautionary measures, take corrective actions, or properly discipline or separate the Lieutenants from Plaintiff, thereby encouraging their behavior and subjecting Plaintiff to harm.” (FAC at ¶ 23)

 

Again, this is mere inaction in the form of failure to investigate or address the alleged hostile work environment. This is not retaliation. ((Bailey supra, 16 Cal.5th at 639-41.)

 

¿        Retaliatory Act No 4: “Due to the lack of any appropriate response, the sexual harassment and hostile work environment continued. From July through August 2024, Lt. Cornwell discussed with Plaintiff how she was dating a new real estate guy and in doing so she went into detail about their specific sexual exploits. Talking about sexual exploits in Plaintiff’s presence was further sexual harassment. Plaintiff was uncomfortable with this conversation from his supervisor.” (FAC at ¶ 24)

 

and

 

¿        Retaliatory Act No 5: “From June -August 2024, Lt. Cornwell, further sexually harassed Plaintiff by making comments stating that Plaintiff looked good for his age and asked on various occasions why he didn’t ask her out, or why Plaintiff hasn’t “thrown it at her.” Plaintiff was uncomfortable with this conversation from his supervisor. During this time, she said she prefers to date men in their 30’s and Plaintiff may be too old for her. Plaintiff never asked her out or flirted with her.” (FAC at ¶ 25)

 

and

 

¿        Retaliatory Act No 6: “From June- September 2024, Lt. Cornwell knew Plaintiff played golf and continued the harassment and would ask Plaintiff to come help her with her swing off duty. She asked Plaintiff if they could play together and after a round they should hit up the 19th hole to have drinks. Plaintiff was uncomfortable with this conversation from his supervisor. Plaintiff never accepted these advances, but Lt. Cornwell would persist.” (FAC at ¶ 26)

 

These are actions by Lt. Cornwell who were engaging in interpersonal behavior which was not an official action by the City as employer that constitutes an adverse employment action.

 

Our precedent explains that the primary difference between discrimination claims and harassment claims is that discrimination claims “address[] only explicit changes in the ‘terms, conditions, or privileges of employment’ [citation]; that is, changes involving some official action taken by the employer.” (Citation omitted.) “In the case of an institutional or corporate employer, the institution or corporation itself must have taken some official action with respect to the employee, such as hiring, firing, failing to promote, adverse job assignment, significant change in compensation or benefits, or official disciplinary action.” (Ibid.)

(Pollock v. Tri-Modal Distribution Services, Inc. (2021) 11 Cal.5th 918, 931-32.)

 

¿        Retaliatory Act No 7: “In further retaliation, Plaintiff was threatened by one of the Lieutenants who warned that she had “friends in high places,” fueling Plaintiff’s worries about future retaliation.” (FAC at ¶ 28)

 

and

 

¿        Retaliatory Act No 8: “In further retaliation, on or about November of 2025, Lt. Darcy Cornwell initiated a baseless misconduct complaint against Plaintiff attempting to use the Department’s personnel complaint system as a weapon against Plaintiff for his prior engagement in protected activity against her exposing her sexual harassment. Lt. Cornwell’s baseless misconduct complaint was unfounded.” (FAC at ¶ 30)

 

There is no allegation that this veiled threat by one of the Lieutenants or the misconduct complaint filed by Lt. Cornwell was an official action by the City as employer that constitutes an adverse employment action. (Pollock, supra, 11 Cal.5th at 931-32.)

 

¿        Retaliatory Act No 9: “In further retaliation, Plaintiff has been ostracized since his reporting and subjected to ongoing isolation. Many co-workers no longer speak to Plaintiff in the same way and look down upon Plaintiff for making a sexual harassment complaint as a male. Co-workers feel they can no longer trust Plaintiff after Plaintiff reported the sexual harassment.” (FAC at ¶ 31) 

 

            Ostracization by co-workers is not an actionable adverse employment action:

 

Although “a mere offensive utterance or even a pattern of social slights by either the employer or coemployees” is not actionable, “the phrase ‘terms, conditions, or privileges’ of employment must be interpreted liberally and with a reasonable appreciation of the realities of the workplace in order to afford employees the appropriate and generous protection against employment discrimination that the FEHA was intended to provide.” (Yanowitz, supra, 36 Cal.4th at p. 1054.) “Minor or relatively trivial adverse actions or conduct by employers or fellow employees that, from an objective perspective, are reasonably likely to do no more than anger or upset an employee cannot properly be viewed as materially affecting the terms, conditions, or privileges of employment and are not actionable . …” (Id. at p. 1054.) But adverse treatment that is reasonably likely to impair an employee’s job [*638]  performance or prospects for advancement in their career falls within the reach of FEHA’s antiretaliation provision. (Yanowitz, at pp. 1054–1055.)

 

(Bailey v. San Francisco Dist. Attorney’s Office (2024) 16 Cal.5th 611, 637-38 [bold emphasis added].)

 

¿        Retaliatory Act No 10: “Despite Plaintiff’s complaints regarding the subject Lieutenants, sexual harassment and/or retaliation, and corroboration from other supervisors, the Department continued their own retaliation against Plaintiff by failing to properly investigate” (FAC at ¶ 32)

 

Again, this is mere inaction in the form of failure to investigate or address the alleged hostile work environment. This is not retaliation. ((Bailey supra, 16 Cal.5th at 639-41.)

 

¿        Retaliatory Act No 11: “failing to” “ properly punish these Lieutenants as required.” (FAC at ¶ 32)

 

Again, this is mere inaction in the form of failure to investigate or address the alleged hostile work environment. This is not retaliation. ((Bailey supra, 16 Cal.5th at 639-41.)

 

            The foregoing list of alleged non-actionable conduct does not become an adverse employment action simply because Plaintiff lists them in series. What Plaintiff complains of is the failure to prevent harassment, which is a separate violation of the FEHA and is already pled in the third cause of action. “The FEHA makes it a separate unlawful employment practice for an employer to “fail to take all reasonable steps necessary to prevent discrimination and harassment from occurring.” (§ 12940, subd. (k).)”  (State Dept. of Health Services v. Superior Court (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1026, 1040.) “The employer’s duty to prevent harassment and discrimination is affirmative and mandatory.” (Nazir v. United Airlines, Inc. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 243, 288.)

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the demurrer to the second cause of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend, unless Plaintiff can demonstrate a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. Otherwise, Defendant is to answer the remaining allegations of the First Amended Complaint within 10 days.

Discussion

Defendants Brett Daniels, Atlas Real Estate Corporation and Ian Daniels, Trustee of the 2010 Brett Daniels Grantor Trust demur to the First Amended Complaint as follows:

 

1.         First Cause of Action (Civil Conspiracy).

 

            Defendants argue that this purported cause of action is not recognized in California, thus providing no subject matter jurisdiction to be adjudicated.

 

“Conspiracy is not a cause of action, but a legal doctrine that imposes liability on persons who, although not actually committing a tort themselves, share with the immediate tortfeasors a common plan or design in its perpetration. … [¶] Standing alone, a conspiracy does no harm and engenders no tort liability. It must be activated by the commission of an actual tort.” (Citation omitted.) “A conspiracy cannot be alleged as a tort separate from the underlying wrong it is organized to achieve.” (Citation omitted.)

(Moran v. Endres (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 952, 954-55.)

 

            The Complaint alleges:

 

135. Plaintiff is informed and believes, and thereon alleges, that Defendant ERROL FENTY (“FENTY”), a licensed real estate professional, acted as a “facilitator” in the conspiracy between Defendant ANDERSON and Defendant DANIELS to illegally vacate the Subject Property.

. .

 

138. Plaintiff is informed and believes, and thereon alleges, that FENTY’S motive for participating in this conspiracy was financial gain, specifically to assist Defendant DANIELS in clearing the property of long-term tenants to maximize the profit of a future “flip” sale to a third party.

. . .

 

200. Plaintiff incorporates by reference all prior allegations. Plaintiff is informed and believes, and thereon alleges, that Defendants ANDERSON, MMCC, DANIELS, and NOLA INVESTMENT SOLUTIONS, LLC, ANDERSON DANIELS-STOCK, ARAUJO and FENTY entered into

a knowing and intentional agreement to engage in a “constructive eviction” scheme.

 

201. The objective of this conspiracy was to harass and coerce Plaintiff into vacating the premises during the COVID-19 pandemic, thereby bypassing formal eviction protections and allowing Defendants to “flip” the property for a higher profit.

 

202. In furtherance of this conspiracy, Defendants committed several overt acts, including but not limited to:

 

¿  The Intentional Exposure: Authorizing uncontained power blasting of lead-based paint to create a toxic and uninhabitable environment.

 

¿  The Denial of Notice: Failing to provide mandated EPA lead-hazard disclosures to keep Plaintiff unaware of the risks.

 

¿  Political Concealment: Defendant ANDERSON using his position of authority to shield the operation from public scrutiny to protect his City Council District 10 candidacy.

 

(1AC,  ¶¶ 135, 138, 200  - 202 [bold emphasis added].)

 

            In this regard, if at least one theory based on illegal action intended to force Plaintiff to vacate the survives demurrer, then the conspiracy allegation would also survive demurrer.

 

            Defendant’s argument that the conspiracy is inadequately pled is not persuasive. As discussed above, the objective of the conspiracy is pled, in part, as Defendant Daniels wanting to illegally vacate the property to flip it. Plaintiff would be entitled to conduct discovery to obtain evidence of such alleged conspiracy.

 

To plead civil conspiracy liability, the complaint must allege: 1) Defendant’s agreement to the course and objective to injure; 2) wrongful act pursuant to such agreement; and 3) resulting damage. (Berg & Berg Ent., LLC v. Sherwood Partners, Inc. (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 802, 823.) In pleading conspiracy, the allegations cannot be based on “bare legal conclusions, inferences, generalities, presumptions and conclusions”. 

 

(See Nicholson v. McClatchy Newspapers (1986) 177 Cal.App.3d 509, 521.)

 

            The demurrer to the first cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

2.         Second Cause of Action (Breach of Implied Warranty of Habitability—Civil Code § 1941.1).

 

Defendants argue that this cause of action is barred by the applicable statute of limitations.

 

            The second cause of action is based on the following allegation:

 

219. By power-blasting the exterior for 47 days and releasing high levels of lead dust (as confirmed by the lab report, Defendants breached this warranty [of habitability].

 

(1AC, ¶ 219.)

 

The applicable statute of limitations appears to be the three-year statute of limitations set forth in Code Civ. Proc. § 338(a) applicable to an action upon a liability created by statute, other than a penalty or forfeiture. Under a three-year statute of limitations, this action was timely filed on December 1, 2025 measured from the May 2023 to June 2023 period of the alleged sandblasting of lead paint that took place between May 22, 2023 and June 2023. (1AC, ¶¶ 108 – 128.)

 

Defendant also argues that Civil Code § 1941.1 applies to a landlord. Daniels[1] is alleged to be a real estate professional who was engaged in the conspiracy (see discussion above) and the Brett Daniels 2010 Trust is alleged to be the owner of the subject property. (1AC, ¶¶ 19 – 27.) There are sufficient allegations to state a cause of action against demurring Defendants.

 

            This ground for demurrer is not persuasive.

 

            The demurrer to the second cause of action is OVERRULED.

The Report confirmed the presence of hazardous materials, stating: “The laboratory results indicate Lead-Based Painted Surfaces (LBPs) and/or Lead- Containing Paint (LCPs) and or Materials were identified in the area(s) of proposed disturbance.” A true and correct copy of this Report is attached hereto as Exhibit 11 and incorporated by reference.

 

118. Following the receipt of the Report, Plaintiff suffered, and continues to suffer, severe emotional distress and physical anxiety due to the confirmed presence of lead-based toxins in the immediate living environment.

 

(1AC, ¶¶ 109 – 118 [bold emphasis added].)

 

            In that all of the above conduct occurred in 2023, the Complaint in this action was filed on December 1, 2025, which is timely under the three-year statute of limitations set forth in LAMC § 45.35(G). This cause of action is not time-barred.

 

Moreover, as discussed above re: the second cause of action, Daniels is alleged to be a real estate professional who was engaged in the conspiracy (see discussion above) and the Brett Daniels 2010 Trust is alleged to be the owner of the subject property. (1AC, ¶¶ 19 – 27.) There are sufficient allegations to state a cause of action against demurring Defendants.

 

            The demurrer to the fourth cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

5.         Sixth Cause of Action (Violation of California Civil Code § 1940.2).

 

Defendants argue that this cause of action is barred by the applicable statute of limitations.

 

            The sixth cause of action—for violation of a statute[2]—is based on the following allegations:

 

251. Under Civil Code § 1940.2, it is unlawful for a landlord to use fraud, intimidation, or menacing conduct to influence a tenant to vacate a dwelling.

 

252. Defendants violated this statute by intentionally exposing a disabled adult child to toxic lead dust and weaponizing the daughter’s professional credentials through false allegations of violence.

 

            The Court incorporates by reference its discussion above re: the second cause of action. For those same reasons, the demurrer to the sixth cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

6.         Seventh Cause of Action (Negligence and Negligence Per Se).

 

Defendants argue that this cause of action is barred by the applicable statute of limitations.

 

            Defendants also argue that the power-blasting and toxic dust activities were performed by persons other than demurring parties.

 

            This cause of action is based upon the following allegations:

 

256. Defendant CARLA ARILA SILVA ARAUJO self-identified as the contractor responsible for the exterior renovation and power-blasting of the premises. As a contractor and agent for the owners, she owed a duty of care to Plaintiff to perform such work in a safe, professional manner that complied with all health and safety regulations.

 

257. Under California Health and Safety Code § 17920.10, it is a violation of law to disturb lead-based paint without following specific lead-safe work practices. Under Negligence Per Se, Defendant Araujo’s violation of this statute constitutes a breach of her duty of care as a matter of law.

 

258. Defendants personally directed and/or authorized a 47-day power-blasting campaign without proper containment, HEPA vacuuming, or notification to the tenants.

. . .

 

266. As a direct result, Plaintiff and her child were exposed to toxic lead dust, causing severe physical risk and extreme mental anguish.

 

(1AC, ¶¶ 256 – 258, 266.)

 

            The foregoing occurred no later than July 7, 2023, when Plaintiff learned of the lead report results. (1AC, ¶¶ 109 – 118.) The two-year statute of limitations for personal injury, set forth in Code Civ. Proc. § 335.1 applies. The Complaint in this action was not filed until December 1, 2025. This cause of action is time-barred.

 

            The demurrer to the seventh cause of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

 

7.         Eighth Cause of Action (Malicious Prosecution).

 

Defendants argue that this cause of action is barred by the applicable statute of limitations.

 

            The 1AC alleges as follows:

 

274. Defendants initiated or “instigated” Case No. 23CHR001672 against Plaintiff without probable cause. Defendants knew the allegations of violent assault were false and had no reasonable basis to believe a crime had occurred.

 

275. Defendants acted with malice, intending to harm Plaintiff’s reputation and cause the revocation of her daughter’s teaching credentials.

 

276. The prior action terminated in Plaintiff’s favor on November 27, 2023, when the court ruled that the allegations were not believable.

 

277. As a result, Plaintiff suffered severe emotional distress, and reputational harm.

 

(1AC, ¶¶ 274 – 277 [bold emphasis added].)

 

            The elements of a malicious prosecution cause of action were complete on November 27, 2023.

 

“To establish a cause of action for the malicious prosecution of a civil proceeding, a plaintiff must plead and prove that the prior action (1) was commenced by or at the direction of the defendant and was pursued to a legal termination in his, plaintiff’s, favor [citations]; (2) was brought without probable cause [citations]; and (3) was initiated with malice [citations].” (Citation omitted.)

(Lanz v. Goldstone (2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 441, 458.)

 

The two-year statute of limitations period set forth in Code Civ. Proc. § 335.1 applies to malicious prosecution claims. (Escamilla v. Vannucci (2025) 17 Cal.5th 571, 578.) The Complaint in this action was not filed until December 1, 2025. As such, this cause of action is time-barred.

 

The demurrer to the eighth cause of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

 

8.         Ninth Cause of Action (Abuse of Process).

 

Defendants argue that this cause of action is barred by the applicable statute of limitations.

 

The two-year statute of limitations period set forth in Code Civ. Proc. § 335.1 applies to abuse of process claims. (Cantu v. Resolution Trust Corp. (1992) 4 Cal. App. 4th 857, 886-87 [applying former one-year statute of limitations applicable to personal injury claims].)

 

279. Defendants utilized the “judicial machinery” for an ulterior purpose for which it was not designed.

 

280 The primary purpose of Case No. 23CHR001672 was not to seek justice for an assault, but to extort a vacancy and harass the Plaintiff into moving out.

 

281. Defendants committed a willful act in the use of the process—recruiting a loan broker to validate false testimony—that was improper in the regular conduct of a legal proceeding.

 

282. As a direct result, Plaintiff suffered damages, including the 90-day “terror” period.

 

(1AC, ¶¶ 280 – 282.)

 

The common law tort of abuse of process arises when one uses the court’s process for a purpose other than that for which the process was designed. (Citations omitted.) It has been “interpreted broadly to encompass the entire range of ‘procedures’ incident to litigation.” (Citation omitted.)

 

“[T]he essence of the tort [is] … misuse of the power of the court; it is an act done in the name of the court and under its authority for the purpose of perpetrating an injustice.” (Citation omitted.) To succeed in an action for abuse of process, a litigant must establish that the defendant (1) contemplated an ulterior motive in using the process, and (2) committed a willful act in the use of the process not proper in the regular conduct of the proceedings. (Citation omitted.)

 

(Rusheen v. Cohen (2006) 37 Cal.4th 1048, 1056-57.)

 

            As discussed above re: the eighth cause of action Case No. 23CHR001672 was terminated in Plaintiff’s favor on November 27, 2023. (1AC, ¶ 276.)

 

The Complaint in this action was not filed until December 1, 2025. As such, this cause of action is time-barred.

 

The demurrer to the ninth cause of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

 

9.         Tenth Cause of Action (Conspiracy To Commit Extrinsic Fraud (fraud on the court).

 

            Defendants argue that this purported cause of action is not recognized in California, thus providing no subject matter jurisdiction to be adjudicated.

 

This cause of action is based upon the following allegation:

 

284. Civil Conspiracy to commit extrinsic fraud (fraud on the court) that resulted in wrongful eviction/displacement. Plaintiff asks for a combination of actual, special, and punitive damages, along with potential treble damages if the fraud qualifies as theft under California Penal Code § 496(c).

 

(1AC, ¶ 284.)

 

            However, Plaintiff does not have standing to assert a private cause of action for fraud on the court. While extrinsic fraud is a basis for voiding a judgment[3], this situation is not presented in this case.

 

            The Court finds that the statute of limitations argument is moot because there is no cause of action for civil conspiracy to commit extrinsic fraud upon the court.

 

            The demurrer to the tenth cause of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

 

10.       Eleventh Cause of Action (Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress).

 

Defendants argue that this cause of action is barred by the applicable statute of limitations.

 

This cause of action is based on the allegation that:

 

For a period of 90 days, Defendants pursued a campaign of high-stakes terror. Specifically, Defendants fabricated a narrative that Plaintiff ordered her disabled daughter and a neighbor to commit a violent assault against the owner’s family member.

 

(1AC, ¶ 287.)

 

            This occurred no later than November 27, 2023, when Case No. 23CHR001672 was ordered terminated because the court ruled that the allegations of violent assault were not believable. (1AC, ¶¶ 274, 276.)

 

Causes of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress are governed by the two-year statute of limitations set forth in Code Civ. Proc., § 335.1. (Pugliese v. Superior Court (2007) 146 Cal.App.4th 1444, 1450.) Because the Complaint in this action was not filed until December 1, 2025, this cause of action is time-barred.

           

The demurrer to the eleventh cause of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

Extrinsic fraud is a broad concept that tends to encompass almost any set of extrinsic circumstances which deprive a party of a fair adversary hearing. (Citations omitted.) Generally, it arises when one party has in some way fraudulently been prevented from presenting his or her claim or defense. (Citations omitted.) "No abstract formula exists for determining whether a particular case involves extrinsic, rather than intrinsic, fraud.
