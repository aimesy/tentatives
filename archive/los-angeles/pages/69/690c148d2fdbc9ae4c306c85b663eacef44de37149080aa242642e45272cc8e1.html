ORDER DENYING PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION FOR

LEAVE TO FILE A FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT

 

 

 

I.               BACKGROUND 

This case arises from a motor vehicle collision between Wendell Gayares (“Wendell”) and Milagros Gayares (“Milagros”) (jointly “Plaintiffs”) and Defendants Hratch Dzhanoyan and A & A West Coast Distribution Inc. (jointly “Defendants”). Plaintiffs allege that on November 11, 2024, Wendell was driving a Kia Forte with Milagros as a passenger when Defendant Hratch Dzhanoyan rear-ended their vehicle as Wendell signaled to change lanes. (See Compl. at 5.) Plaintiffs further allege Defendant Dzhanoyan failed to maintain a safe speed and following distance, causing Plaintiffs to sustain injuries. (See id. at 5.)

Plaintiffs now seeks leave of this Court to file a first amended complaint.

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On May 22, 2025, Plaintiffs filed their Complaint, alleging (1) Motor Vehicle; and (2) General Negligence.

On February 18, 2026, Defendants filed their Answer. 

On May 14, 2026, the Court granted the parties’ joint stipulation to continue trial.

On June 29, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant Motion for Leave to File a First Amended Complaint. 

On July 15, 2026, Defendants filed their Opposition.

On July 23, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their Reply.

III.         LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure § 473(a)[1] provides: “The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 473(a)(1); see also Code Civ. Proc. § 576.)¿¿¿¿ ¿¿ 

The court has broad discretion to permit amendments to pleadings, and “the court’s discretion will usually be exercised liberally to permit amendment of the pleadings.” (Howard v. County of San Diego (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 1422, 1428.)¿“The policy favoring amendment is so strong that it is a rare case in which denial of leave to amend can be justified.”¿(Id.)¿“If the motion to amend is timely made and the granting of the motion will not prejudice the opposing party, it is error to refuse permission to amend.”¿(Morgan v. Superior Court (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 527, 530.) Prejudice includes “delay in trial, loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation.” (Solit v. Tokai Bank, Ltd. New York Branch (1999) 68 Cal.App.4th 1435, 1448.)¿¿ 

A motion for leave to amend a pleading must comply with the procedural requirements of Rules of Court, rule 3.1324, under which the motion must include (1) a copy of the proposed and numbered amendment, (2) specifications by reference to pages and lines the allegations that would be deleted and added, and (3) a declaration specifying the effect, necessity and propriety of the amendments, date of discovery and reasons for delay. (See Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a), (b).)¿  

IV.          ANALYSIS

Plaintiffs seek leave to file a first amended complaint to add allegations supporting a claim for punitive damages against Defendants. Plaintiffs argue that, through further investigation and discovery, they learned additional facts regarding the collision, including that Defendant Dzhanoyan was driving at an unsafe speed, failed to maintain a safe following distance, rear-ended Plaintiffs’ vehicle, and was operating the vehicle with an expired driver’s license. (See Mot. at 3:7-11.) Plaintiffs further argue Defendant A & A West Coast Distribution Inc. knew or should have known of Dzhanoyan's unfitness to operate the vehicle, supporting allegations that Defendants acted with conscious disregard for Plaintiffs’ safety under Civil Code § 3294. (See id. at 3:14-18.) Plaintiffs contend amendment is proper because trial is not set until February 24, 2027, and Defendants will have an opportunity to conduct discovery and respond to the amended pleading. (See id. at 8:5-8.)

Defendants oppose the motion, arguing Plaintiffs failed to comply with California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324 because Plaintiffs’ counsel’s declaration does not explain when the facts supporting the proposed amendment were discovered, why the amendment was not sought earlier, the effect of the amendment, or why the amendment is necessary. (See Opp. at 2:21-24, 3:6-8.) Defendants further argue Plaintiffs failed to comply with rule 3.1324(a)(2)-(3) because the proposed amended pleading does not identify the allegations to be deleted or added, or specify where those allegations are located within the pleading. (See id. at 3:27-28, 4:1-8.) Moreover, Defendants contend the proposed amendments fail to state a viable claim for negligent entrustment or punitive damages because the allegations are conclusory and insufficient to establish malice, oppression, or fraud under Civil Code § 3294. (See id. at 9:9-28.) Finally, Defendants argue they will suffer prejudice if leave to amend is granted because discovery has been conducted based on the existing pleadings, and they would incur additional costs and burdens in conducting discovery, revising their litigation and settlement strategy, preparing defenses against what they characterize as unsupported claims, and retaining new counsel. (See id. at 13:1-10, 13:24-28.)

In reply, Plaintiffs argue California law strongly favors granting leave to amend and that Defendants have failed to demonstrate actual prejudice sufficient to justify denial of the motion. (See Reply at 2:19-27.) Plaintiffs contend the proposed amendment merely clarifies and supplements existing allegations and liability theories arising from the same collision, does not introduce new facts or claims unrelated to the action, and will not delay trial or otherwise prejudice Defendants because discovery remains ongoing. (See id. at 3:3-18, 4:1-10.) Plaintiffs further argue Defendants’ challenges to the sufficiency of the proposed allegations go to the merits of the amended pleading rather than whether leave to amend should be granted. (See id. at 4:25-27.) Finally, Plaintiffs contend any procedural deficiencies in the moving papers are curable and do not warrant denial of leave to amend. (See id. at 5:2-5.)

After reviewing Plaintiffs’ Motion and the Declaration of Christopher C. Lee, the Court finds Plaintiff has failed to comply with Rule of Court, rule 3.1324(a) and (b). First, Mr. Lee’s declaration does not “specify … [w]hen the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and the reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier.” (Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324(b)(3)-(4).) Because rule 3.1324(b) states a separately filed declaration “must accompany [a] motion” to amend a pleading before trial and “must specify” these facts, it is not discretionary. Here, the timing of Plaintiffs’ discovery that Defendant Dzhanoyan was allegedly operating the vehicle with an expired driver’s license is especially significant, as that allegation appears to be the primary new factual basis for the proposed punitive damages claim and is therefore central to the Court’s prejudice analysis. Further, Plaintiffs’ motion fails to comply with Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324(a)(2)-(3), which requires moving parties to “State what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are located” and “State what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.” Defendants’ arguments concerning the legal sufficiency of the proposed punitive damages and negligent entrustment allegations go to the merits of the proposed amended pleading, which are not presently before the Court.

Accordingly, the Motion is procedurally defective and the Court DENIES it.

V.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES Plaintiffs’ Motion for Leave to File a First Amended Complaint.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

I.               BACKGROUND 

This is a lemon law action. Plaintiff Mardy Fish (“Plaintiff”) alleges he leased a 2023 Land Rover Range Rover (“Subject Vehicle”) from Galpin Jaguar Lincoln Mercury Inc. Plaintiff alleges the Subject Vehicle was delivered to Plaintiff with serious defects and nonconformities to warranty and developed other serious defects and non-conformities to warranty such as electrical and safety related system defects.

Defendant now demurrers to and moves to strike portions of the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”).

On April 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Complaint alleging (1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act - Breach of Express Warranty; (2) Violation of Song-Beverly Act - Breach of Implied Warranty; and (3) Violation of The Song Beverly Act Section 1793.2.

On June 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed his FAC alleging (1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act - Breach of Express Warranty; (2) Violation of Song-Beverly Act - Breach of Implied Warranty; (3) Violation of The Song Beverly Act Section 1793.2; and (4) Fraud-Fraudulent Inducement-Concealment.

Before filing a demurrer or motion to strike, the moving party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer or motion to strike for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer or motion to strike. IV.          DEMURRER

A.             Legal Standard

A demurrer may be brought if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 430.10(e).)[1] “[T]he court gives the complaint a reasonable interpretation, and treats the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded.”¿(Id.) Allegations are to be liberally construed. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 452.) In testing the sufficiency of the complaint, the court must assume the truth of (1) the properly pleaded factual allegations; (2) facts that can be reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded; and (3) judicially noticed matters. (See Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (See SKF Farms v. Superior Court¿(1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) Accordingly, “[w]hether the plaintiff will be able to prove the pleaded facts is irrelevant to ruling upon the demurrer.” (Stevens v. Superior Court¿(1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 605, 609-610.) A demurrer can only be sustained when it disposes of an entire cause of action.¿(See Poizner v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redev. Agency (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1046.)

¿Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court a pleading can be amended successfully. (See id.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.)

B.             Judicial Notice

The Court grants Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice of a court order in Case No. 25VECV05217. However, judicial notice of other court records and files is limited to indisputably true matters, as distinguished from the contents of documents filed. (See Fremont Indemnity Co. v. Fremont Gen. Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 113.)

C.             Analysis

1.               Fraudulent Concealment

 “[T]he elements of a cause of action for fraud based on concealment are: ‘(1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.’” (Kaldenbach v. Mutual of Omaha Life Ins. Co. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 830, 850, quoting Roddenberry v. Roddenberry (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 634, 665-666.)¿¿¿ 

a.               Duty to Disclose

 One party’s failure to disclose material facts unknown to another party is not fraud unless “unless there is¿some relationship¿between the parties which gives rise to a duty to disclose such known facts.” (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 337.) “In transactions which do not involve fiduciary or confidential relations, a cause of action for non-disclosure of material facts may arise in at least three instances: (1) the defendant makes representations but does not disclose facts which materially qualify the facts disclosed, or which render his disclosure likely to mislead;¿(2)¿the facts are known or accessible only to defendant, and defendant knows they are not known to or reasonably discoverable by the plaintiff;¿(3) the defendant actively conceals discovery from the plaintiff.” (Warner Constr. Corp. v. City of Los Angeles (1970) 2 Cal.3d 285, 294.)¿“Circumstances [(1), (2), (3)] presuppose a preexisting relationship between the parties, such as ‘between seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement. [Citation.] All of these relationships are created by transactions between parties from which a duty to disclose facts material to the transaction arises under certain circumstances.’ [Citation.] ‘Such a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.’” (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 40-41.)

Defendant argues the FAC fails to plead a direct transactional relationship between the parties necessary to establish a duty to disclose. (See Dem. at 4:15-16, citing Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 312.) Defendant argues Plaintiff does not allege the third-party dealership he leased his vehicle from was a subsidiary or agent of Defendant’s such that a relationship with Defendant is alleged under Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 844. (See Dem. at 4:21-26.)

Plaintiff argues his alleged warranty contract with Defendant is sufficient to establish a preexisting relationship with Defendant under Rattagan. (See Opp. at 10:19-24.) Plaintiff also argues under OCM Principal Opportunities Fund, L.P. v. CIBC World Markets Corp. (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 835, 851, a direct transactional relationship is not required because Defendant had a duty to disclose to subsequent purchasers of its vehicles. (See Opp. at 6:11-24.) Finally, Plaintiff argues the FAC pleads Defendant had exclusive knowledge of and actively concealed the true extent of the Engine Defect. (See id., at 12:5-7.)

In reply, Defendant argues Dhital’s finding the plaintiff’s allegations sufficient at the pleading stage was limited to the parties’ arguments before it. (See Reply at 1:23-28, citing Dhital, 84 Cal.App.5th at 844.) Further, Defendant argues the Supreme Court in Rattagan reaffirmed the direct transaction requirement for a duty to disclose. (See Reply at 2:11-22.) Defendant argues a warranty contract does not establish a direct transactional relationship. (See id., at 2:27-28.) Defendant argues Plaintiff cannot rely on an agency relationship between the dealership and Defendant as Courts have consistently held that automobile dealerships are not agents of the manufacturer for purposes of retail sales. (See id., at 3:21-28 [citing non-binding authority].) Moreover, Defendant contends the FAC does not contain agency allegations comparable to the Dhital plaintiffs who alleged the manufacturer’s “authorized dealerships are its agents for purposes of the sale of [the manufacturer’s] vehicles to consumers.” (See id., at 4:2-5, citing Dhital, 84 Cal.App.5th at 844.) Finally, Defendant argues Plaintiff’s reliance on OCM is misplaced as that case found a duty to disclose because the defendant went beyond general advertising directed at the public. (See Reply at 4:7-25.)

The Court finds Plaintiff sufficiently alleges his fourth cause of action for fraudulent concealment. Defendant’s reliance on Bigler-Engler is misplaced. First, Bigler-Engler is factually distinguishable from this case, as that action was, at its heart, a medical malpractice case in which the plaintiff was a patient who sued, among others, the manufacturer of the medical device involved. (Bigler-Engler, 7 Cal.App.5th at 284.) Here, however, Plaintiff is a direct purchaser of the product himself, not merely the person on whom the product was used. (See FAC, ¶¶ 2, 5.) Second, Bigler-Engler held that ordinarily, a duty to disclose absent a fiduciary relationship “presupposes the existence of [a] relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise.” (Bigler-Engler, 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 311.) Defendant’s reliance on this general rule as stated in Bigler-Engler is misplaced because it ignores California courts have developed a distinct doctrine that applies when, as here, an original seller manufactures or sells a product to whom it reasonably expects the product to be resold.

A vendor has a duty to disclose material facts “not only to immediate purchasers, but also to subsequent purchasers when the vendor has reason to expect that the item will be resold.” (OCM Principle Opportunities Fund v. CIBC World Markets Corp. (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 835, 859-60.) The duty to disclose to subsequent purchasers is limited: the plaintiff must show that the seller had special “reason to expect” that the fraud will be passed onto subsequent purchasers. (Geernaert v. Mitchell (2007) 31 Cal.App.4th 601, 607-08.) In determining whether the seller had “reason to expect” that the fraud will be passed onto subsequent purchasers, courts generally consider two factors: “(1) the extent of the seller's knowledge of resale to a particular person or class of persons and (2) the likelihood that the particular misrepresentation (or concealment) would be passed on to them.” (Id. at 608.)

The FAC alleges Defendant had reason to expect the fraud could be passed onto Plaintiff, as Defendant manufactured the Subject Vehicle knowing it had defective systems and would be sold by a dealership to a subsequent purchaser. The FAC alleges Defendant engaged in pre-sale communications with Defendant’s authorized sales agents, Plaintiff entered into a warranty agreement with Plaintiff regarding the Subject Vehicle, Defendant manufactured the powertrain and electrical systems Plaintiff complained about, and Defendant was exclusively aware the systems were defective. (See FAC, ¶¶ 3, 17, 30-32, 37.) The Court is unpersuaded by Defendant’s reading that OCM only found a duty to disclose because the defendant actively advised potential purchasers. The OCM Court primarily found a duty to disclose because the defendant had reason to expect the unregistered notes it sold would be resold to subsequent purchasers and “moreover, it actively advised potential purchasers regarding the registered notes.” (OCM, 157 Cal.App.4th at 860.) Thus, because the FAC alleges Defendant had reason to expect the fraud would be passed on to a subsequent purchaser, it alleges Defendant had a duty to disclose on this basis.

Moreover, the FAC alleges a preexisting buyer-seller relationship sufficient to support a duty to disclose. In Dhital, the Court found the plaintiff’s allegations that they “bought the car from a Nissan dealership, that Nissan backed the car with an express warranty, and that Nissan’s authorized dealerships are its agents for purposes of the sale of Nissan vehicles to consumers” were sufficient at the pleading stage to allege a relationship requiring defendant to disclose known defects. (See Dhital, 84 Cal.App.5th at 844.) Here, the FAC similarly alleges Plaintiff, a buyer, engaged in pre-sale communications with Keyes European sales personnel, who are authorized agents and representatives of Defendant. (See id., ¶ 3, 6, 17 [the latter two paragraphs support the inference that “Keyes European” refers to Galpin Jaguar Lincoln Mercury Inc].) The FAC also alleges the type of system defects and their specific effects. (See FAC, ¶¶ 34-36, 102-103.) The FAC alleges Defendant exclusively knew of these material defects “from pre-production testing, internal investigations, consumer complaints, warranty data, dealership repair orders, and communications with the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration.” (See id., ¶¶ 30-31, 106.) The FAC alleges Defendant marketed the Subject Vehicles before, during, and after the sale as technologically advanced instead of disclosing the Subject Vehicle’s defects. (See id., ¶ 110, 112.) Plaintiff also alleges he would not have purchased the Subject Vehicle had he been aware of the defects. (See id., ¶ 112.) Thus, the Court finds Plaintiff also sufficiently alleges a duty to disclose by virtue of the parties’ relationship and defendant’s exclusive knowledge of the material defect.

b.              Specificity

Defendant argues the FAC fails to satisfy the general rule requiring particularized allegations of who, what, when, where, and how when pleading fraud against a corporation. (See Dem. at 5:16-19.) Defendant argues Plaintiff alleges in a conclusory manner it concealed facts of which it possessed exclusive knowledge. (See id., at 5:17-20.) Defendant also argues Plaintiff does not allege any specifics with respect to the contents of the marketing materials he allegedly reviewed and relied on in his purchasing experience. (See id., at 6:2-7.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues he sufficiently pleads fraud with specificity, as his allegations are similar to those found to be sufficient in Dhital. (See Opp. at 10:3-5.)

In reply, Defendant argues Plaintiff’s allegations are not comparable to those in Dhital, Plaintiff’s allegations concerning the selling dealership are inconsistent, and Plaintiff alleges only generic marketing directed at the public at large. (See Reply at 5:18-27.)

The Court finds Plaintiff pleads his claim with specificity. The general rule for pleading fraud against a corporation applies differently when the fraud claim is based upon an assertion of concealment. (See Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Association, Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384 [“How does one show ‘how’ and ‘by what means’ something didn’t happen, or ‘when’ it never happened, or ‘where’ it never happened?”].) Instead, such details, in the context of a fraudulent concealment claim, “are properly the subject of discovery, not demurrer.” (Id., 171 Cal.App.4th at 1384-85.) Additionally, the specificity requirement is “relaxed when it is apparent from the allegations that the defendant necessarily possesses knowledge of the facts.” (Quelimane Co. v. Steward Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 27.) That is the case here. 

Moreover, the FAC’s allegations are similar to those in Dhital.  In Dhital, the Court found allegations “the CVT transmissions were defective in that they caused such problems as hesitation, shaking, jerking, and failure to function. The SAC also alleged Nissan was aware of the defects as a result of premarket testing and consumer complaints that were made both to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration and to Nissan and its dealers” were sufficiently specific. (See Dhital, 84 Cal.App.5th at 845.) Here, the FAC alleges the type of system defects and their specific effects. (See FAC, ¶¶ 34-36, 102-103.) The FAC alleges Defendant exclusively knew of these material defects “from pre-production testing, internal investigations, consumer complaints, warranty data, dealership repair orders, and communications with the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration.” (See id., ¶¶ 30-31, 106.) The FAC alleges Defendant marketed the Subject Vehicles before, during, and after the sale as technologically advanced instead of disclosing the Subject Vehicle’s defects. (See id., ¶ 110, 112.) Plaintiff also alleges he would not have purchased the Subject Vehicle had he been aware of the defects. (See id., ¶ 112.) The Court finds these allegations are sufficiently pled with the requisite specificity.

Thus, the Court OVERRULES Defendant’s Demurrer to the sixth cause of action for fraudulent concealment.

V.             MOTION TO STRIKE

A.             Legal Standard

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 436(a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (See id. at § 436(b).) This provision is for “the striking of a pleading due to improprieties in its form¿or in the procedures¿pursuant to which it was filed.”¿ (Ferraro v. Camarlinghi¿(2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 509, 528 [emphasis in original].) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (See Code Civ. Proc. at § 437.)

“The appropriate procedural device for challenging a portion of a cause of action seeking an improper remedy is a motion to strike.” (Caliber Bodyworks, Inc. v. Superior Court (2019) 134 Cal.App.4th 365, 385 [court directed striking alleged civil penalties from causes of action, not drawn in conformity with the laws, due to failure to plead compliance with statutory notice and exhaustion requirements], disapproved of on other grounds by ZB, N.A. v. Superior Court (2019) 8 Cal.5th 175. Cf. Royals v. Lu (2022), 81 Cal.App.5th 328, 353 [court properly denied motion to strike allegations sufficient to support statutory penalties under applicable statutory sections].) 

B.             Analysis

Defendant moves to strike Plaintiff’s claim for civil penalties including:

(1) Paragraph 73, in its entirety,

(2) Paragraph 100, in its entirety; and

(3) Prayer for Relief, Page 19, Item 5, which reads: “For civil penalty in the amount of two times Plaintiff’s actual damages.”

Defendant argues § 871.24(h) clearly states a plaintiff who initiates an action without first providing the required notice “shall not seek civil penalties, whether by amendment of the complaint or otherwise.” Defendant argues Plaintiff is not entitled to civil penalties because the FAC fails to allege compliance with Code of Civil Procedure § 871.24. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 871.24.) (See Mot. at 3:1-5.) Defendant argues civil penalties under the SBA are akin to punitive damages and thus, the reasoning for requiring factual allegations to support a claim for punitive damages is also applicable to a claim for civil penalties. (See id., at 3:18-19, 4:5-10, citing Kwan v. Mercedes-Benz of North America, Inc. (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 174, 184 [the penalty under § 1794(c), like other civil penalties, are akin to punitive damages].)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues Defendant offers no authority to support its position that compliance with Code of Civil Procedure § 871.24(a) must be plead to allow recovery of civil penalties. (See Opp. at 3:19-20.) Plaintiff argues whether he has made a sufficient pre-suit restitution request pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 871.24 is a factual matter that cannot be resolved at the pleading stage. (See Opp. at 2:17-19; RJN, ¶ 1, Ex. 1.) Plaintiff also argues he sufficiently pleads Defendant’s failure to comply with its statutory repurchase or replacement obligations was willful and therefore he has pled sufficient support for his civil penalty claim under Civil Code § 1794(c). (See Opp. at 4:16-18, 6:11-12.) Finally, Plaintiff argues § 871.24 should be interpreted as creating an additional statutory prelitigation procedure and not to restrict the civil penalty remedy available under Civil Code § 1794(c). (See id., at 10:7-12, relying on § 871.28 [“duties and obligations imposed by this chapter are cumulative with duties or obligations imposed under any other law and shall not be construed to relieve any party from any duties or obligations imposed under any other law.”].)

Defendant argues the order Plaintiff relies on in his request for judicial notice is not binding on this Court and should be disregarded. (See Reply at 1:13-16.) Defendant argues willfulness under § 1794(c) and satisfaction of the prelitigation procedure under § 871.24 are separate, cumulative requirements; a plaintiff must satisfy both before civil penalties may be pursued. (See id., at 3:15-17.) Defendant also argues the prelitigation requirements of § 871.24 are not rendered optional by § 871.28, which preserves a manufacturer’s obligations under the SBA and does not discuss civil penalties. (See id., at 3:26-4:3.)

The Court agrees with Plaintiff that compliance with section 871.24’s prelitigation procedures is a question of fact not suitable for disposition at the pleading stage where a plaintiff has failed to allege compliance.

The Court DENIES Defendant’s Motion to Strike.

VI.          CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court OVERRULES Defendant’s Demurrer and DENIES Defendant’s Motion to Strike.

Defendant is ordered to file an Answer only on or before August 10, 2026.
