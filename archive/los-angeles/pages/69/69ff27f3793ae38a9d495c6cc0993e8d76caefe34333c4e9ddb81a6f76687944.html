Because these motions concern substantially similar subject matter and overlapping materials, the Court will address them together. The parties did not file any opposition briefs.

California Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d) provides:

A court may order that a record be filed under seal only if it expressly finds facts that establish:

(1) There exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record;

(2) The overriding interest supports sealing the record;

(3) A substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed;

(4) The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and

(5) No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest.

The Court finds an overriding interest overcomes the right of public access to these documents. Plaintiff states that the information they seek to sealed includes confidential “contract terms, contracting strategy, and confidential, sensitive financial reimbursement data and recruitment information.” (Fuller Decl., ¶ 2.) Similarly, Defendant claims that the information they seek to be sealed contains “non-public, confidential commercial and financial information” detailing its reimbursement methodology. (Abhasakun Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) “[C]onfidential matters relating to the business operations of” a party can constitute an overriding interest that justifies sealing of records when “public revelation of [the] matters would interfere with its ability to effectively compete in the marketplace… .” (Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Super. Ct. (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 1273, 1286.)  Here, the parties have presented evidence to support their significant interest in maintaining the confidentiality of their sensitive business information.

This overriding interest supports sealing the record. Here, the parties’ right to protect their confidential business information trumps the public’s minimal need to access this information. A substantial probability exists that publicly disclosing the information will prejudice both parties’ overriding interest and harm them in the marketplace, at least at this stage of the case.

In addition, the proposed sealing is narrowly tailored. The parties move to seal only those portions of exhibits and briefs that reference this confidential information. No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interests in confidentiality.

Plaintiff Pomona Valley Hospital Medical Center’s motion to seal documents filed in connection with motions in limine is granted.

No. 13 – Payam Benyamini v. Lundquist Institute for Biomedical Innovation at Harbor-UCLA Medical Center, et al.

Defendant Lundquist Institute for Biomedical Innovation at Harbor-UCLA Medical Center’s Motion to Compel Written Responses and Produce Documents to Supplemental Requests

Defendant Lundquist Institute for Biomedical Innovation at Harbor-UCLA Medical Center moves for an order compelling Plaintiff Payam Benyamini to provide responses to its supplemental requests for production.

A party must respond to written discovery “[w]ithin 30 days after service.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.260(a).) When the responding party fails to serve a timely response to interrogatories or requests for production, the requesting party may move for an order compelling responses. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300(b).)

On June 17, 2026, the parties discussed this issue with the Court at an Informal Discovery Conference. Plaintiff promised to produce native files of an overtime list and overtime calendar in response to the requests. Plaintiff also agreed at the IDC to provide a verified response to the supplemental requests by June 29, 2026. Defendant indicates that, since the IDC, Plaintiff has only served the two native file documents, and no responses to the supplemental discovery. (Supp. Grutman Decl., ¶¶ 4-5.) Plaintiff did not oppose the motion to compel responses to the supplemental discovery. Therefore, the Court will order Plaintiff to serve verified responses without objection to Defendant’s supplemental requests.

Sanctions

Defendant also requests monetary sanctions against Plaintiff. Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.300(c) provides, “[T]he court shall impose a monetary sanction … against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” Plaintiff failed to provide verified responses by the deadline, and failed to do so again even after promising to do so at the Informal Discovery Conference. There is no evidence that Plaintiff acted with substantial justification.

The Court finds sanctions are appropriate. Defendant did not, however, reasonably incur $2,510 in expenses in preparing this motion. (Grutman Decl., ¶ 7.) The Court exercises its discretion to reduce the hours requested by Defendant by one-half. 4.5 hours at a rate of $270 per hour is $1,215. The Court will also award the $80 filing fee.

Disposition

Defendant Lundquist Institute for Biomedical Innovation at Harbor-UCLA Medical Center’s motion for an order compelling Plaintiff Payam Benyamini to provide  responses to its supplemental requests for production is granted.

Plaintiff Payam Benyamini is hereby ordered to serve verified responses without objection to Defendant’s supplemental requests within 10 days.

Plaintiff and his counsel are further ordered to pay Defendant $1,295 in sanctions to Defendant in 21 days.

No. 16 - Flosieta Davis v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc.

    Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.'s Motion for Summary Judgment or Summary Adjudication

Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. moves for summary judgment or, in the alternative, summary adjudication of each cause of action brought by Plaintiff Flosieta Davis.

Request for Judicial Notice

          Plaintiff requests judicial notice of 10 exhibits, including five technical service bulletins and five NHTSA reports and communications. The Court declines to take judicial notice of these documents as they are irrelevant to deciding the instant motion. The request for judicial notice is denied.

Evidentiary Objections

          Defendant makes 20 objections to the Declaration of Benjamin Fuchs. Objection nos. 1 and 3-20 are sustained. The remaining objection is overruled.

Legal Standard

A defendant moving for summary adjudication of a cause of action must show “that one or more elements of the cause of action… cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) Once the defendant does so, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show a triable issue of at least one material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 849.)

1st-3rd Causes of Action: Claims Under Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act

Defendant is entitled to summary adjudication of Plaintiff’s first three causes of action. The first three causes of action are based on Civil Code section 1793.2, a provision of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act: (1) failure to repair or replace after a reasonable number of attempts (§ 1793.2(d)); (2) failure to commence repairs within a reasonable time (§ 1793.2(b)); and (3) failure to make available sufficient literature for repairs (§ 1793.2 (a)(3)). 

“A plaintiff pursuing an action under the Song-Beverly Act has the burden to prove the following elements: (1) the product had a defect or nonconformity covered by the express warranty; (2) the product was presented to an authorized representative of the manufacturer for repair; and (3) the manufacturer or its representative did not repair the defect or nonconformity after a reasonable number of repair attempts.” (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 798-799 (Robertson); see also Civ. Code, § 1793.2(d)(1) [“if the manufacturer or its representative in this state does not service or repair the goods to conform to the applicable express warranties after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer shall either replace the goods or reimburse the buyer”]. )

“ ‘Attempts’ is plural. The statute does not require the manufacturer to make restitution or replace a vehicle if it has had only one opportunity to repair that vehicle.” (Silvio v. Ford Motor Co. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1208.) “At a minimum there must be more than one opportunity to fix the nonconformity.” (Robertson, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at p. 799.)

Here, Plaintiff provides no admissible evidence that there were any repair attempts on the subject vehicle for the sensing defect identified in the complaint. Rather, Defendant shows that the only documented repair on the vehicle involved repair of a side mirror that was broken off. (See Def. Ex. 6.) In opposition, Plaintiff merely argues that the absence of a documented repair does not mean no defect existed. This argument misses the point. Plaintiff has the burden to show that triable issues exist as to whether the vehicle suffered a defect covered by the warranty, and whether Plaintiff presented the vehicle for a reasonable number of repair attempts. She does not provide any admissible evidence to meet her burden.

Further, statements made in Plaintiff’s counsel’s declaration do not meet this burden. Counsel declares: “Nothing on file with Plaintiff’s counsel SLP supports that the body of repair orders currently on file with Plaintiff’s counsel constitutes a comprehensive detailing of all the issues Plaintiff encountered during her ownership of the Vehicle. . . . Due to the ongoing status of discovery, it is premature to conclude that there is no additional evidence of repair presentations.” (Fuchs Decl., ¶¶ 9, 11.) Counsel’s speculation that there may be evidence of requisite repair attempts in the future is not itself admissible evidence of a triable issue of material fact.

4th Cause of Action: Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability

Defendant is entitled to summary adjudication of this cause of action. Under the Song-Beverly Act, the implied warranty of merchantability “may be breached by a latent defect undiscoverable at the time of sale.” (Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1297, 1304.) Here, Plaintiff provides no admissible evidence that creates a triable issue of material fact as to whether her vehicle had a latent defect. Instead, Plaintiff argues that technical bulletins and NHTSA reports establish that Defendant’s “Accord product line suffered from a systematic latent defect capable of causing sudden unexpected braking.” (Opp., at p. 6.) This again misses the point. Plaintiff has the burden of showing that there is a triable issue of fact as to whether her car had a latent defect, not that other, similar cars did. Plaintiff provides no evidence or personal declaration attesting to this fact. Further, evidence that other cars of similar models had this defect does not show that Plaintiff’s specific vehicle had this defect.

5th Cause of Action: Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment

Defendant is also entitled to summary adjudication of this cause of action. Fraud by concealment requires that: (1) defendant omitted or concealed a material fact; (2) defendant had a duty to disclose the fact to Plaintiffs; (3) defendant intentionally omitted or concealed the fact with intent to defraud Plaintiffs; (4) Plaintiffs must have been unaware of the fact and would have acted otherwise if he had known of the concealed fact; and (5) the omission caused damages. (Boschma v. Home Loan Center, Inc. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 230, 248.)

Plaintiff does not show a triable issue of material fact as to the fourth element: that she was unaware of a material fact, and that she would have acted otherwise if she had known of the fact. The only evidence Plaintiff submits in opposition, ten bulletins and one counsel declaration, do not reveal Plaintiff’s reliance on any concealed fact. Nor is there evidence that Plaintiff’s vehicle had a defect, such that any information was in fact concealed.

Disposition

          Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.’s motion for summary judgment is granted. 


Case Number: 25STCV15857    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 15 - Michael Ventura v. City of Los Angeles, et al.

Defendant City of Los Angeles’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Third Amended Complaint

          Defendant City of Los Angeles demurs to the first and only cause of action for violation of Labor Code section 1102.5 alleged by Plaintiff Michael Ventura in his third amended complaint (TAC).

Labor Code § 1102.5, commonly referred to as the whistleblower statute, provides protection for employees in reporting unlawful activities of their employers. To make a prima facie showing of a violation of 1102.5 a plaintiff must show they (1) engaged in a protected activity, (2) their employer subjected them to an adverse employment action, and (3) there is a causal link between the two. (McVeigh v. Recology San Francisco¿(2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 443, 468 (McVeigh).)

Plaintiff alleges sufficient facts to support a cause of action for whistleblower retaliation against Defendant City of Los Angeles. Plaintiff alleges that he believed his fellow employees’ lack of fare evasion enforcement was itself illegal. The TAC states: “On or about May 15, 2024, Plaintiff disclosed information to the LAPD and the City of Los Angeles that LAPD Officers [sic] failure to enforce fare evasion was endangering employees and patrons who ride on the Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transit Authority (“MTA”) buses and trains. Plaintiff reasonably believed that said disclosure constituted a violation of [various regulations and ordinances].” (TAC, ¶ 24 [emphasis added].) These allegations of wrongful conduct by LAPD Officers, essentially his co-workers, were not included in prior versions of the complaint. This reasonable belief of an employee’s illegal conduct constitutes protected activity as articulated in McVeigh v. Recology San Francisco¿(2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 443. (See id., at p. 471 [“We conclude . . . that Labor Code section 1102.5, subdivision (b) protects employee reports of unlawful activity by third parties such as contractors and employees, as well unlawful activity by an employer.”].)

          Defendant argues in reply that Plaintiff was not complaining about illegal conduct, but was merely disagreeing with internal policies about fare evasion enforcement. Further, Defendant argues governmental immunity would protect it from being liable for any alleged failure to enforce a law. These arguments are irrelevant here. To state a claim under the whistleblower statute, Plaintiff needs to allege “only that he or she reasonably believed that there was a violation of a statute, rule, or regulation.” (Nejadian v. County of Los Angeles (2019) 40 Cal.App.5th 703, 719.) “ ‘[A]n employee need not prove an actual violation of law; it suffices if the employer fired him for reporting his “reasonably based suspicions” of illegal activity.’ ” (Ferrick v. Santa Clara University (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 1337, 1345.) Thus, Plaintiff’s allegations that he reasonably believed his fellow employees’ conduct violated the law is sufficient at the pleading stage.

Disposition

Defendant City of Los Angeles’s demurrer to Plaintiff Michael Ventura’s third amended complaint is overruled. Defendant is to file its answer to the third amended complaint within 21 days.


Case Number: 25STCV32534    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 12 - George Jones v. Luxurious Properties, LLC, et al.

          Plaintiff George Jones’s Motion to Enforce Settlement Agreement

Plaintiff George Jones moves to enforce the settlement agreement and enter judgment against Defendant Luxurious Properties, LLC.

As an initial matter, the Court notes that Defendant Luxurious Properties, LLC’s opposition brief was untimely served on Plaintiff. Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1005(b), all papers opposing a motion must be served at least 9 court days before the hearing. Here, the opposition papers were served on Plaintiff on June 25, 2026, only 7 court days before the hearing. There is no explanation for this delay in Defendant’s papers. However, the Court will exercise its discretion to consider Defendant’s untimely opposition when ruling on this motion.

Entry of Judgment

Plaintiff shows sufficient grounds to enter judgment of $8,333.33 against Defendant Luxurious Properties, LLC. Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6, subdivision (a) provides in relevant part, “If parties to pending litigation stipulate, in a writing signed by the parties outside the presence of the court... for settlement of the case, or part thereof, the court, upon motion, may enter judgment pursuant to the terms of the settlement.” 

Plaintiff and Defendants Luxurious Properties, LLC and Family Dollar, LLC entered a written agreement to settle the action. (Chernoff Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 1, Settlement Agreement.) The agreement requires Defendant Luxurious Properties to pay $8,333.33 “no later than the first business day thirty (30) days after the full execution of this Agreement.” (Settlement Agreement, ¶ 5.) The agreement was fully executed on March 9, 2026, and payment was due by April 8, 2026. (Settlement Agreement, at p. 8; Chernoff Decl., ¶ 10.)

Here, Defendant Luxurious Properties, LLC has still not paid the $8,333.33 amount due to Plaintiff. (Chernoff Decl., ¶¶ 10, 12.) Thus, pursuant the terms of the settlement agreement, the unpaid settlement amount is due to Plaintiff. In its opposition, Defendant does not contest that it owes this amount.

Attorney Fees

          Plaintiff is entitled to recoverable attorney fees and costs incurred in bringing this motion. The settlement agreement provides, “In the event of breach of this agreement, the prevailing party is entitled to recovery of their attorney fees and costs in any proceeding concerning breach of contract or enforcement of this agreement.” (Settlement Agreement, ¶ 12.)

Plaintiff’s counsel did not, however, reasonably incur $3,451 in fees and costs in preparing this motion. The time spent meeting and conferring, drafting this motion, preparing a reply brief, and attending the future hearing does not reasonably require a total of 5.8 hours of work at a rate of $595 per hour. (Chernoff Decl., ¶ 17.) The motion to enforce settlement is only six pages long. The Court exercises its discretion to not award fees for 0.6 hours spent meeting and conferring. The Court will also reduce Plaintiff counsel’s remaining hours by one-half. This totals $1,547 in attorney fees. Plaintiff is entitled to recover its $60 filing fee for the motion.

Disposition

          Plaintiff George Jones’s motion to enforce settlement agreement is granted. Plaintiff is ordered to submit a modified proposed judgment in the amount of $8,333.33, plus $1,607 in attorney fees and costs, against Defendant Luxurious Properties, LLC for the Court’s signature forthwith.
