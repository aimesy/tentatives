DEPARTMENT N LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 23SMCV01266    Hearing Date: July 29, 2026    Dept: N

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff Boyd Kestner’s Motion for Reconsideration of Order Denying Motion for Leave to File Second Amended Complaint and Sustaining Demurrer Without Leave to Amend is DENIED.

Plaintiff Boyd Kestner to give notice. 

REASONING

Plaintiff Boyd Kestner (“Plaintiff”) moves the Court for an order reconsidering the Court’s January 12, 2026 order sustaining Defendant Estate of Mark Kornwasser (“Defendant”)’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint without leave to amend and denying Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to File Second Amended Complaint. Plaintiff argues here that Plaintiff can now present new and different facts that were not presented with the original motion and that could not have been presented earlier with reasonable diligence. Specifically, Plaintiff provides a declaration with the motion, evidence and explanation regarding the actual incident date, evidence showing the purported dangerous condition and subsequent repairs, and identification of new witnesses. The Court notes that Plaintiff’s motion is 23 pages long, which exceeds the page limit set forth in rule 3.1113(d) of the California Rules of Court. However, the Court has opted to consider the memorandum in full to dispose of this motion on its merits.

Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a), allows a Court to reconsider a prior order as follows:

When an application for an order has been made to a judge, or to a court, and refused in whole or in part, or granted, or granted conditionally, or on terms, any party affected by the order may, within 10 days after service upon the party of written notice of entry of the order and based upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, make application to the same judge or court that made the order, to reconsider the matter and modify, amend, or revoke the prior order. The party making the application shall state by affidavit what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown.

The Court initially sustained the demurrer to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint on the ground that the initial complaint was filed beyond the two-year statute of limitations set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1, which provides that any action for personal injury must be filed within two years, and the Court also denied Plaintiff’s motion for leave to file an amended complaint partly on procedural grounds and partly because Plaintiff’s proposed allegation that the incident occurred on September 27, 2020 conflicted with two prior pleadings, an ex parte application representation, and opposition to the demurrer with allegations that the subject incident occurred on September 23, 2020. Plaintiff concedes here that the incident occurred on September 23, 2020. This does not explain in any way how Plaintiff’s claim would fall within the statute of limitations.

As the Court previously stated, assuming Plaintiff has six additional days to allege his claim, as he contends he was unconscious until September 29, 2020, he would be permitted an additional eight days pursuant to Emergency Rule 9, not a full six months to include time before the incident occurred. (Committee for Sound Water & Land Development v. City of Seaside (2022) 79 Cal.App.5th 389, 403 [limitations period stops running during the tolling event, i.e., the only time tolled is time after the incident occurs].) Thus, Plaintiff had two years from September 23, 2020 to file his complaint, for a deadline of September 23, 2022, plus an additional eight days from Emergency Rule 9, for a deadline of October 1, 2022, plus an additional six days for incapacity, for a deadline of October 9, 2022, which fell on a Sunday, for a final deadline of October 10, 2022. Plaintiff’s complaint was not filed until March 23, 2023. Again, given that Plaintiff’s initial pleading was not timely filed, there is no basis to conclude that any of the proposed new claims may relate back properly, as relation back applies only where there is an earlier, timely filed pleading. (Davaloo v. State Farm Insurance Co. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 409, 415 [“An amended complaint relates back to a timely filed original complaint, and thus avoids the bar of the statute of limitations, only if it rests on the same general set of facts and refers to the same ‘offending instrumentalities,’ accident and injuries as the original complaint”].)

Insofar as Plaintiff provides evidence of the dangerous condition here, this does nothing to change the Court’s initial conclusion. The ruling sustaining the demurrer and denying leave to amend was not based on the merits of Plaintiff’s claim on injury; rather, it was clear that Plaintiff’s claim fell outside the statute of limitations, and evidence of the condition cannot change that conclusion. Accordingly, Plaintiff Boyd Kestner’s Motion for Reconsideration of Order Denying Motion for Leave to File Second Amended Complaint and Sustaining Demurrer Without Leave to Amend is DENIED.

Case Number: 25SMCV00866    Hearing Date: July 29, 2026    Dept: N

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff Zarina Karim’s Motion for a New Trial is DENIED.

Plaintiff Zarina Karim to give notice.

REASONING

Request for Judicial Notice
Defendant City of Santa Monica (“the City”) requests judicial notice of four court records in this action. The City’s request is GRANTED pursuant to Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (d).

Analysis
On May 8, 2026, this Court granted the City’s Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication. On May 19, 2026, Plaintiff Zarina Karim (“Plaintiff”) filed a notice of intent to move for new trial; on May 26, 2026, judgment was entered in the City’s favor; and the Court electronically served the Notice of Entry of Judgment on the parties on May 28, 2026. On May 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed another notice of intent to move for new trial, and on June 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed its motion for new trial on the ground that insufficient evidence justifies the decision, the decision is against law, and error of law. First, insofar as the City argues that the motion is untimely because Plaintiff failed to file her motion within 10 days of filing her notice of intent to move for new trial, Plaintiff’s counsel credibly states that he attempted to file the motion on May 29, 2026, but the system was down. (Reply, Vafaee Decl. ¶¶ 2-4.)

California Rules of Court, rule 5.560(f) provides that “[c]lerical errors in judgments, orders, or other parts of the record may be corrected by the court at any time on the court’s own motion or on motion of any party and may be entered nunc pro tunc.” 

Los Angeles Superior Court Local Rule 3.4(c) states, “Any document received electronically before midnight on a court day is deemed to have been filed on that court day if accepted for filing. Any document received on a non-court day is deemed to have been filed on the next court day if accepted for filing.” While the provision is no longer in effect, the Court’s prior order regarding electronic filing, Los Angeles Superior Court’s First Amended General Order re: Mandatory Electronic Filing, recently rescinded in favor of Los Angeles Superior Court Local Rule 3.4, made clear in section 7(a)(i) that “[n]otwithstanding any other provision of this order, if a digital document is not filed in due course because of: (1) an interruption in service; (2) a transmission error that is not the fault of the transmitter; or (3) a processing failure that occurs after receipt, the Court may order, either on its own motion or by noticed motion submitted with a declaration for Court consideration, that the document be deemed filed and/or that the document’s filing date conform to the attempted transmission date.” The Court finds that prior rule to provide guidance here, as it is clear that the Court intended to allow relief for e-filing issues in certain circumstances. These rules make it such that the Court can conclude that Plaintiff’s motion is properly deemed file as of May 29, 2026, due to an interruption in service that was not the fault of Plaintiff’s counsel.

Code of Civil Procedure section 657 sets forth the grounds upon which a verdict may be vacated, in whole or in part, and a new or further trial granted on all or part of the issues. “The determination of a motion for a new trial rests so completely within the court’s discretion that its action will not be disturbed unless a manifest and unmistakable abuse of discretion clearly appears.” (Jiminez v. Sears, Roebuck & Co. (1971) 4 Cal.3d 379, 387.) It is well established that “[a] motion for a new trial is appropriate following an order granting summary judgment.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 858.)

In this motion, Plaintiff argues that the case of Stack v. City of Lemoore (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 102, 107 weighed against finding a sidewalk condition of 1.75-inch displacement to be trivial as a matter of law, such that the Court’s decision finding that a sidewalk displacement of one and one-half inches was trivial to be in error. Notably, the Court relied on Stack v. City of Lemoore in making its decision. In its ruling, the Court noted that in that case, the appellate court stated that “a holistic multifactor framework for assessing triviality” made it such that the size of the defect remained the most important of the dangerous condition factors, but it was still just one of many circumstances to be considered. (Id. at p. 114.) Within that framework, a height differential of one and three-quarter inches weighed against finding the sidewalk condition trivial as a matter of law, such that a strong showing of other circumstances that made the defect not dangerous was required, but in that case, the plaintiff showed that the defect had a rough edge, there were back-to-back defects, and the defect was partially obstructed with pine needles and debris. (Id. at pp. 114, 123.) The evidence before the Court with this motion showed only that there was a one and one-half inches height differential in the sidewalk; there were no other conditions that would support a conclusion that the condition was dangerous, such that the City could not be held liable for injury due to that condition. Specifically, while the displacement was one and one-half inches in height, the defect was not camouflaged, Plaintiff was familiar with the area, and there were no prior complaints.

In opposition to the motion for summary judgment, Plaintiff provided evidence that the City’s work-order system has no standardized written measuring protocol for sidewalk inspectors, i.e., inspectors could rely on informal visual references to estimate priority for any condition, it is not clear which photographed displacement was the Priority 2 condition or which specific displacement was hot patched on June 28, 2022, and when more than one sidewalk displacements exist at a single address, the City cannot determine which specific condition was identified as hazardous or whether a particular condition was repaired. The Court found that this evidence does not weigh in favor of establishing that the condition was more than trivial, i.e., Plaintiff focused on notice, she provided no evidence that the condition was concealed in any way or that there were broken pieces in the area, Plaintiff testified that she frequently walked in this area, the incident occurred at 3:00 p.m., and the defect had not caused another incident like the one alleged here. Thus, while the size of the defect was the most important factor for the Court to consider, it was not the only factor, and Plaintiff provided no other evidence to support a conclusion that the defect is dangerous as a matter of law.

Put simply, Plaintiff is seeking a second bite at the proverbial apple in this motion, making the same arguments already considered and rejected by the Court in its prior ruling. Again, the one and one-half height differential alone did not create a triable issue of material fact that the displacement was more than trivial given the total lack of other conditions that would weigh in favor of finding that the displacement was a dangerous condition. It follows that Plaintiff has failed to provide a basis for the Court to reverse its prior ruling. Accordingly, Plaintiff Zarina Karim’s Motion for a New Trial is DENIED.

Case Number: 26SMCV00320    Hearing Date: July 29, 2026    Dept: N

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant Global Integrity Realty Corporation’s Demurrer to Complaint is SUSTAINED with thirty (30) days leave to amend as to the second, third, fifth, sixth, and seventh causes of action and SUSTAINED without leave to amend as to the fourth cause of action.

Defendant Global Integrity Realty Corporation’s Motion to Strike Portions of Complaint is GRANTED with thirty (30) days leave to amend as to the claims and prayer for punitive damages.

Plaintiffs Michael Lewis, Shannon Cummings, Alexander Cummings, Austin Cummings, Cecilia Sabillon, and Serafin Hill Hernandez may amend their complaint only as authorized by the Court’s order and may not amend the complaint to add a new party or cause of action without having obtained permission to do so. (Harris v. Wachovia Mortgage, FSB (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1018, 1023.)

Defendant Global Integrity Realty Corporation to give notice. 

REASONING

Legal Standard
“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967), but the Court does not “assume the truth of contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law” (Moore v. Regents of University of California (1990) 51 Cal.3d 120, 125).

Further, the court may, upon motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false, or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

Demurrer
Second Cause of Action: Negligence
To state a claim for negligence, Plaintiffs Michael Lewis, Shannon Cummings, Alexander Cummings, Austin Cummings, Cecilia Sabillon, and Serafin Hill Hernandez (“Plaintiffs”) must allege the elements of (1) “the existence of a legal duty of care,” (2) “breach of that duty,” and (3) “proximate cause resulting in an injury.” (McIntyre v. Colonies-Pacific, LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 664, 671.) “The negligence per se doctrine, as codified in Evidence Code section 669, creates a presumption of negligence if four elements are established: (1) the defendant violated a statute, ordinance, or regulation of a public entity; (2) the violation proximately caused death or injury to person or property; (3) the death or injury resulted from an occurrence of the nature of which the statute, ordinance, or regulation was designed to prevent; and (4) the person suffering the death or the injury to his person or property was one of the class of persons for whose protection the statute, ordinance, or regulation was adopted.” (Spates v. Dameron Hospital Association (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 208, 218, quotation marks omitted.) “The doctrine of negligence per se is not a separate cause of action, but creates an evidentiary presumption that affects the standard of care in a cause of action for negligence.” (Johnson v. Honeywell International Inc. (2009) 179 Cal.App.4th 549, 555, quotation marks and brackets omitted.)

In the second cause of action, Plaintiffs allege that Defendant was required by law to prevent harm to Plaintiffs, which they breached by violating the ICRAA’s notice and checkbox requirements. (Compl. ¶¶ 41-44.) Plaintiffs allege harm in the form of “emotional distress, the invasion of privacy rights, and the denial of reports that Plaintiffs had a statutory right to obtain, which harmed Plaintiffs rights in those property interests.” (Compl. ¶ 46.)

Put simply, the facts alleged do not support a finding of damages and harm by Plaintiffs. The Court cannot conceive of, because Plaintiffs fail to allege, how Plaintiffs would have suffered cognizable emotional distress or invasion of privacy rights in a manner that caused monetary damages when the alleged conduct is mere failure to provide reports to Plaintiffs or failure to notify Plaintiffs that such reports were being obtained. For that reason, Defendant’s demurrer to the second cause of action is SUSTAINED with thirty (30) days leave to amend.

Third Cause of Action: Violations of Unfair Competition (“UCL”) – Cal. Bus. & Profs. Code §§ 17200, et seq.
To set forth a claim for a violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200, Plaintiffs must establish Defendant was engaged in an “unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising” and certain specific acts. (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200.) A cause of action for unfair competition “is not an all-purpose substitute for a tort or contract action.” (Cortez v. Purolator Air Filtration Products Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 163, 173.)

In the third cause of action, Plaintiffs allege that they paid a $30 application screening fee to cover the costs of reports from investigative reporting agencies, but Defendant refused to provide a copy of the report to Plaintiffs. (Compl. ¶¶ 48-49.) Plaintiffs do not allege that the fee being charged is itself the violation; rather, it is the failure to provide a copy of the report, and it remains that Plaintiffs have failed to allege cognizable damages from this violation. Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer to the third cause of action is SUSTAINED with thirty (30) days leave to amend.

Fourth Cause of Action: Violations of the Consumer Legal Remedies Act (“CLRA”) – Cal. Civ Code §§ 1750, et seq.
Plaintiffs state in their opposition to Defendant’s demurrer that they “do not oppose the demurrer to this cause of action, as it is not applicable to transactions involving real property.” (Opp’n, p. 6, ll. 9-10.) While Plaintiffs provide argument stating that this claim has been sufficiently pled, the Court understands this concession as seeking to voluntarily dismiss this claim. Thus, Defendant’s demurrer to the fourth cause of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

Fifth Cause of Action: Invasion of Privacy
“An actionable claim [for invasion of privacy] requires three essential elements: (1) the claimant must possess a legally protected privacy interest; (2) the claimant’s expectation of privacy must be objectively reasonable; and (3) the invasion of privacy complained of must be serious in both its nature and scope.” (County of Los Angeles v. Los Angeles County Employee Relations Commission (2013) 56 Cal.4th 905, 926, citations omitted.) “An otherwise actionable invasion of privacy may be legally justified if it substantively furthers one or more legitimate competing interests. Conversely, the invasion may be unjustified if the claimant can point to ‘feasible and effective alternatives’ with ‘a lesser impact on privacy interests.’” (Ibid., citation omitted.)

In the fifth cause of action, Plaintiffs allege that they had a reasonable expectation that Defendant would not solicit information concerning their character unless Defendant complied with legal requirements, and obtaining reports in violation of law invaded Plaintiffs privacy and deprived them of the ability to scrutinize the reports. (Compl. ¶¶ 68-70.) It is not clear why Plaintiffs would have had such a reasonable expectation of privacy when they voluntarily applied to rent at the units, and while Defendant may have violated legal requirements, the trier of fact could not conclude that Plaintiffs’ privacy was violated in a serious manner under these facts. Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED with thirty (30) days leave to amend.

Sixth Cause of Action: False Advertisement
The False Advertising Law prohibits advertising that contains “any statement . . . which is untrue or misleading, and which is known, or . . . should be known, to be untrue or misleading . . . .” Bus. & Prof. Code §17500.

In the sixth cause of action, Plaintiffs allege that Defendant represented there was an opportunity for applicants to obtain a copy of investigative consumer reports procured with application processes, but Defendant did not provide such reports. (Compl. ¶¶ 75-76.) The nature of the purported advertisement is unclear, as Plaintiffs refer only generally to “application materials and related communications” (Compl. ¶ 75), and it is unclear how those statements would have induced Plaintiffs to apply, as they allege (Compl. ¶ 77). Thus, Defendant’s demurrer to the sixth cause of action is SUSTAINED with thirty (30) days leave to amend.

Seventh Cause of Action: Fraud
“The elements of fraud are (a) a misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) scienter or knowledge of its falsity; (c) intent to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Ctr. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) The facts constituting the alleged fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.) To properly allege fraud against a corporation, the plaintiffs must plead the names of the persons allegedly making the false representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written. (Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157.)

In the seventh cause of action, Plaintiffs refer only generally to “false representations” that were made about obtaining a copy of investigative reports (Compl. ¶ 81) and their reliance on the same (Compl. ¶ 84). This does not meet the heightened specificity requirement for a fraud claim, particularly one against a corporate defendant, as Plaintiffs do not allege who said what, what specifically was said, how it was communicated, and when it was communicated. For that reason, Defendant’s demurrer to the seventh cause of action is SUSTAINED with thirty (30) days leave to amend.

Motion to Strike
Punitive damages may be recovered upon a proper showing of malice, fraud, or oppression. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) “Malice” is defined as conduct intended to cause injury to a person or despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard for the rights or safety of others. (Turman v. Turning Point of Cent. Cal., Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) “Oppression” means despicable conduct subjecting a person to cruel and unjust hardship, in conscious disregard of the person’s rights. (Ibid.) “Fraud” is an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known by defendant, with intent to deprive a person of property, rights or otherwise cause injury. (Ibid.) Conclusory allegations, devoid of any factual assertions, are insufficient to support a conclusion that parties acted with oppression, fraud, or malice. (Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1042.)

Plaintiffs seek punitive damages, but they fail to allege any malice, oppression, or fraud by Defendant. Given that it appears Plaintiffs seek to hold Defendant liable for the acts of specific individuals, such as employees, who made certain representations, corporate employers like Defendant may be liable for punitive damages only where the acts supporting the award were performed by or with knowledge of or with ratification by corporate officials. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (b).) Plaintiffs have alleged no facts that would allow the trier of fact to conclude that corporate officials were aware of the likelihood of injury to Plaintiffs, particularly because the nature of the purported injury is unclear, or that those officials ratified the wrongful conduct which ultimately caused harm to Plaintiffs. Upon amending the complaint, Plaintiffs must allege specific facts in this regard to support an award for punitive damages, and conclusory statements will not suffice. Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to strike is GRANTED with thirty (30) days leave to amend as to Plaintiffs’ claims and prayer for punitive damages.
