Tentative Ruling

Shirian v. Fox Capital, Case no. 23VECV01359

Hearing date September 29, 2026

Defendant Fox Capital’s Motion for Leave to File Cross-Complaint

Plaintiff Shirian sued defendants Fox Capital, LLC, Rediger Investment Corporation and K. and S. Kamat (collectively “Kamat defendants”) for negligence and premises liability, alleging injury when a portion of the mezzanine in a leased commercial unit collapsed. Plaintiff dismissed the Kamat defendants and Rediger on 9/9/26; Rediger’s cross-complaint was dismissed the same day. Fox Capital, the sole remaining defendant, moves for leave to file a compulsory cross-complaint. Plaintiff opposes.

Permission to file a cross-complaint is discretionary. Orient Handel v. United States Fid. & Guar. Co. (1987) 192 Cal.App.3d 684, 701. A defendant may file a cross-complaint against a third party if the cause of action asserted arises out of the same transaction, occurrence or series of transactions or occurrences as set forth in the complaint. Code Civ. Proc. §428.10. A compulsory cross-complaint under section 426.30 concerns a “related cause of action” that a defendant has “against the plaintiff” at the time of answering the complaint. Code Civ. Proc. §426.30(a). Per section 428.50(c), a cross-complaint may be filed at any time "in the interests of justice.”¿

Defendant moves for leave to file a cross-complaint against plaintiff and nonparty JAJ Enterprises, Inc., asserting causes of action for express indemnity, equitable apportionment, equitable indemnity, declaratory relief and breach of contract. See Decl. Partida, exh. A. The proposed cross-complaint alleges plaintiff is the principal of JAJ Enterprises, the entity that leased the premises. Id. at para. 4, exh. A. Defendant’s proposed cross-complaint alleges JAJ Enterprises agreed to indemnify and hold defendant harmless in the event of injury on the premises. Id.

Plaintiff argues defendant delayed in bringing this motion. Plaintiff argues trial is 10/19/26, and being forced to defend against five previously unraised causes of action and an unnamed entity is prejudicial. Plaintiff argues this would stall a case pending for nearly 3.5 years, and defendant did not act in good faith, waiting 18 months after answering to seek to file a cross-complaint.

The proposed cross-complaint is a compulsory one, containing causes of action related to the lease of the premises and plaintiff’s injuries and claims. See Decl. Partida, exh. A; see also Code Civ. Proc. §426.30(a). Defendant’s moving papers and supporting affidavit fail to explain the 18-month delay between filing the answer and this motion. The facts giving rise to the proposed cross-complaint were, or should have been, known to defendant from the inception of this lawsuit. Defendant’s assertions that “litigation counsel” associated into this matter 8/20/26 and subsequently “in good faith immediately brought this motion to file a cross-complaint” fail to explain this delay, as defendant has been represented by counsel Mokhtarzadeh since the answer was filed almost a year and a half ago. See 3/17/25 Answer.

Defense counsel must explain the delay in filing this compulsory cross-complaint. Absent a satisfactory explanation, the motion must be denied.

Tentative Ruling

Mares v. American Honda Motor Co., Case no. 26VECV02560

Hearing date September 29, 2026

Defendant Honda’s Motion to Compel Arbitration

Plaintiff Mares sues defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc., alleging violations of the Song-Beverly Act. Defendant moves to compel arbitration. Plaintiff opposes.

Defendant offers the declaration of counsel Pacheco. Plaintiff objects. Obj. 1-6 OVERRULED.

California public policy strongly favors arbitration. Code Civ. Proc. §1280 et seq., Madden v. Kaiser Found. Hosps. (1976) 17 Cal.3d 699, 706. Under the California Arbitration Act, if there is a valid written agreement to arbitrate, arbitration is mandatory. Code Civ. Proc. §1281.2. The opposing party bears the burden of proving defenses to arbitrability, including unconscionability. See Pinnacle Museum Tower Ass’n. v. Pinnacle Mkt. Dev. (US), LLC (2012) 55 Cal.4th 223, 236. “[T]he doctrine of unconscionability has both a procedural and substantive element, the former focusing on oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power, the latter on overly harsh or one-sided results.” Sanchez v. Valencia Holding Co., LLC (2015) 61 Cal. 4th 899, 910.

The threshold issue is whether a valid agreement to arbitrate exists. See Mitsubishi Motors Corp. v. Soler Chrysler-Plymouth, Inc. (1985) 473 U.S. 614, 626. Under California law, the party seeking to compel arbitration has the burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence. Rosenthal v. Great W. Fin. Sec. Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413. Defendant offers an unsigned warranty handbook exemplar, purportedly included with each vehicle sold in the glovebox, which contains an arbitration clause. See Decl. Pacheco, paras. 3, 6-7, exh. A. Plaintiff purchased the subject vehicle in August 2024. Id. at para. 4. Defendant asserts the subject vehicle would have come with a copy of the warranty handbook. Id.

Plaintiff admittedly purchased the vehicle in August 2024 but disputes being shown or informed of the purported warranty handbook or included arbitration clause. Decl. Mares, paras. 3-6. Plaintiff never signed an agreement containing an arbitration provision between plaintiff and defendant. Id. at para. 3-4. Plaintiff never expressed verbal or written agreement to be bound by the purported arbitration agreement within the warranty handbook. Id. at paras. 6-9.

Defendant offers no signed, written agreement containing an arbitration provision, nor any communications or other acts by plaintiff demonstrating the parties entered into an agreement to arbitrate. The law is clear: an essential element of any contract is the consent of the parties, or mutual assent, which must be communicated by each party to the other. See Donovan v. RRL Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 261, 270; Civ. Code, §1565. Defendant offers no evidence, such as a dealership affidavit, establishing the handbook was actually provided to plaintiff. Regardless, a unilateral arbitration clause in an unsigned warranty handbook is not sufficient to find that an agreement to arbitrate was formed. Defendant failed to meet its burden. DENIED.


Case Number: 26VECV03635    Hearing Date: September 29, 2026    Dept: B

Tentative Ruling

Boyadzhyan v. Abovian, Case no. 26VECV03635

Hearing date September 29, 2026

Plaintiff Boyadzhyan’s Motion to Disqualify Defendant Abovian’s Counsel

Plaintiff Boyadzhyan sues defendants Abovian and G. and R. Ouzouian, alleging defamation, referring to plaintiff as a “drug addict,” “fraud” and “killer.” Plaintiff moves to disqualify Abovian’s counsel Geragos & Geragos, APC (“Geragos”), asserting a prior substantial attorney-client relationship. Defendant Abovian opposes.

The court has authority to determine, in its own discretion, the disqualification of counsel. People ex rel. Department of Corporations v. SpeeDee Oil Change Systems, Inc. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1135. Disqualification of counsel is “a drastic course of action that should not be taken simply out of hypersensitivity to ethical nuances or the appearance of impropriety.” Roush v. Seagate Technology, LLC (2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 210, 219. Disqualification is required where there is a “substantial risk” that confidential information obtained in a prior representation “would be used to the disadvantage of the former client.” City and County of San Francisco v. Cobra Solutions, Inc. (2006) 38 Cal.4th 839.

Plaintiff brought this motion on an ex parte basis. See 9/3/26 Ex Parte. The court denied the ex parte, setting a noticed hearing with the ex parte papers to serve as moving papers, with opposition and reply per Code. See 9/8/26 Min. Order. Plaintiff filed a second set of moving papers on 9/11/26, including a supplemental declaration of counsel Sacks, without permission. Abovian objects. The unauthorized filings are disregarded per Code Civ. Proc. §1005 and CRC rule 3.1300(a). Abovian’s objections 1-8 SUSTAINED.

The dispute arises from Geragos’ prior representation of plaintiff and subsequent litigation between plaintiff and Geragos, LASC Case no. BC671202; the court takes judicial notice of that matter sua sponte per Evid. Code §452(d). Plaintiff argues Geragos represented him in a business sale dispute, and plaintiff later sued Geragos regarding a fee dispute. In the course of the prior representation and fee dispute, Geragos allegedly received, reviewed and retained confidential medical records, personal financial information and other sensitive private communications. Plaintiff argues such information falls within the definition of substantial relation per Flatt v. Superior Court (1994) 9 Cal.4th 275, 283, which held “access to confidential information by the attorney in the course of the first representation (relevant, by definition, to the second representation) is presumed and disqualification of the attorney's representation of the second client is mandatory; indeed, the disqualification extends vicariously to the entire firm.”

Defendant Abovian does not dispute that Geragos previously represented plaintiff but argues the course and scope of that representation does not constitute a substantial relation for purposes of this matter. Abovian argues Geragos represented plaintiff in a “dispute with the buyer of his business, concerning the purchase and sale agreement, the operating agreement, the monthly payments, and related agreements.” Opp., 2:16-18. Abovian argues this case arises from statements allegedly made by defendants to third parties in 2025-26, so is unrelated to the prior representation. Abovian argues general familiarity with plaintiff arising from a prior representation does not warrant disqualification, citing Khani v. Ford Motor Co. (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 916. Khani is inapposite, as it concerned a corporate vehicle warranty claim, where no allegations of personal medical or financial knowledge were made.

Plaintiff disclosed private medical and financial information to Geragos. Decl. Boyadzhyan, para. 3. Plaintiff argues Geragos, without permission, disclosed that information in a 2019 complaint in the fee dispute matter. Plaintiff asserts information regarding his health and alleged alcohol use goes directly to the truth of some of the allegedly defamatory statements at issue in this case, and truth is a defense against a defamation claim. Plaintiff argues Geragos possessing knowledge of his personal financial information is prejudicial. He further argues Geragos’ inappropriate 2019 disclosure demonstrates Geragos’ willingness to weaponize plaintiff’s private information against him.

Abovian argues plaintiff’s fee dispute lawsuit against Geragos constitutes waiver of the attorney-client privilege under Evid. Code §958. This unreasonably expands the scope of section 958, which provides a narrow, restricted exception to the privilege, stating there is no privilege as to a communication “relevant to an issue of breach of a duty arising out of the lawyer-client relationship.” See Fox Searchlight Pictures, Inc. v. Paladino (2001) 89 Cal.App.4th 294, 310. California courts restrict section 958 disclosures to information directly necessary to adjudicate the specific billing or malpractice dispute. Glade v. Superior Court (1978) 76 Cal.App.3d 738, 746. Section 958 does not provide enduring carte blanche to reveal or weaponize a former client’s confidential communications or private information in other disputes or litigation. There has been no waiver of the attorney-client privilege between plaintiff and Geragos. Geragos’ knowledge of plaintiff’s personal medical history, private financial information and general litigation approach is substantial and requires disqualification.

Aside from mandatory disqualification, trial courts have inherent power to disqualify where the "representation would undermine the integrity of the judicial process." Geragos’ representation of Abovian bears the appearance of impropriety in violation of Code Civ. Proc. §128(a)(5). The allegations directly concern, in part, the truthfulness of statements allegedly made by Abovian concerning plaintiff’s substance use. Geragos possesses protected information regarding said topic and demonstrated a history of disclosing such information in public filings when such information should not have been disclosed or at least should have been filed under seal. See CRC rule 1.9(c)(1); see also JN, 8/7/17 Compl., para. 23. Permitting Geragos to continue to represent Abovian would appear improper to the reasonable observer, undermining the integrity of the judicial process.

The motion to disqualify is GRANTED. Geragos & Geragos, APC is disqualified from serving as counsel for any party in this matter effective immediately. The court will discuss further steps to allow Abovian to retain new counsel.
