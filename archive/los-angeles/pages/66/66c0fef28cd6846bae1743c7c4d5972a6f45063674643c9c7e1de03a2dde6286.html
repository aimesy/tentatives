Case Number: 25STCV14758    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: 507

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Rolf M. Treu, Department 507

 

 

HEARING DATE:     July 21, 2026                          TRIAL DATE:  December 8, 2026

                                                          

CASE:                         Jorge Vargas v. Melissa Solis, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                 25STCV14758

 

 

MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

MOVING PARTY:               Plaintiff Jorge Vargas 

 

RESPONDING PARTIES: Defendants Melissa Solis and Maria Vargas

 

CASE HISTORY:

  • 5/20/2025: Complaint filed.

 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

           

Plaintiff Jorge Vargas alleges that he and Defendant Melissa Solis each own 50% of the single-family residence located at 4309 Lindsey Ave, Pico Rivera, CA 90660. Plaintiff filed this action to have the property partitioned.

 

            Plaintiff Jorge Vargas moves for summary adjudication.

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Plaintiff Jorge Vargas’ motion for summary adjudication is DENIED.

 

            Plaintiff to give notice.

 

DISCUSSION:

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

Defendants’ objections are OVERRULED.

Plaintiff’s objections are OVERRULED. As to the objections to Ricardo Solis and Leehmus declarations, while the Court “must” rule on all evidentiary objections made at the summary judgment stage, it is permitted to focus its attention on those which are “important.” (See Reid v. Google, Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 512, 532; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (q) [the court need only rule those objections to evidence that were material in the disposition of the MSJ].) The Court did not rely on this evidence and thus, does not rule on the objections.

Request for Judicial Notice

 

            Plaintiff seeks judicial notice of (1) Plaintiff’s Complaint in this action; (2) Defendant Vargas’ answer; (3) Defendants Solis and Ricardo Solis’ Answers; (4) Grant Deed; (5) Interspousal Transfer Deed; (6) Deed of Trust; and (7) Vargas’ declaration in a dissolution proceeding.

 

The requests are granted. It is well-established that courts may take judicial notice of the existence of recorded deeds (Evans v. California Trailer Court, Inc. (1994) 28 Cal.App.4th 540, 549,) but not as to hearsay statements or disputable facts asserted therein. (Poseidon v. Woodland (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 1106, 1117.) Accordingly, the Court will judicially notice the existence of the grant deeds and the Court filings, but not the truth of the material asserted therein.

 

Defendant seeks judicial notice of this Court’s 3/26/26 minute order.

 

The request is granted.

 

Motion For Summary Adjudication

 

            Plaintiff seeks summary adjudication on his sole cause of action for partition.

 

“Under California law, the term ‘partition’ signifies ‘the procedure for segregating and terminating common interests in the same parcel of property.’¿ [Citation.]”¿ (14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Ass’n v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396, 1404-1405 (Moorpark).)¿

 

¿Code of Civil Procedure section 872.720(a) declares that “[i]f the court finds that the plaintiff is entitled to partition, it shall make an interlocutory judgment that determines the interests of the parties in the property and orders the partition of the property.” (§ 872.720, subd. (a).) When the trial court “determines the interests of the parties in the property and orders the partition of the property,” it shall decide the manner of partition “unless [this] is to be later determined.” (§ 872.720, subd. (a).)  “The manner of partition may be ‘in kind’—i.e., physical division of the property [citation] according to the parties’ interests as determined in the interlocutory judgment. [Citations.] Alternatively, if the parties agree or the court concludes it ‘would be more equitable,’ the court may order the property sold and the proceeds divided among the parties.”  The third option, “[w]hen the interests of all parties are undisputed or have been adjudicated,” and the parties agree (§ 873.910), is partition by appraisal. (See § 873.910 et seq.) (Cummings v. Dessel (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 589; § 872.820.)

 

The co-owner of a property has “an absolute right to partition unless barred by a valid waiver.”¿ (LEG Investments v. Boxler (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 484, 493.)

 

Based on the above, Plaintiff must prove the following: (1) Plaintiff has an ownership interest in the Property; (2) a partition by sale of the Property is more equitable than a partition in kind; and (3) Plaintiff did not waive the right to partition the Property.  

 

As to the first element, Plaintiff offers the grant deed in support of his argument that he had 50% ownership of the property and Defendant Solis has 50% ownership. (RJN 4.) Plaintiff contends that the presumption of title in Evidence Code section 662 applies here.

 

Evidence Code section 662 states that the owner of legal title to property is presumed to be the owner of the beneficial title. This presumption may be rebutted only by clear and convincing proof. (Evidence Code section 662.) To rebut the presumption, the opposing party must show that the character of the property was held differently, including by establishing that the property's character was changed by an agreement or mutual understanding. (Pearce v. Briggs (2021) 68 Cal. App. 5th 466, 477.) Section 662 thus codifies the common law rule that oral trusts in derogation of title are disfavored and must be proved by clear and convincing evidence. “Allegations that deeds absolute are actually mortgages, that conveyances are subject to a trust, and that legal title does not represent beneficial ownership have ... been historically disfavored because society and the courts have a reluctance to tamper with duly executed instruments and documents of legal title.” (quoting Weiner v. Fleischman (1991) 54 Cal.3d 476, 489, People v. Semaan (2007) 42 Cal. 4th 79, 88, 163 P.3d 949, 954.)

 

First, while the existence of the document is judicially noticeable, the truth of the material therein is not. Plaintiff did not separately authenticate this document, and thus, the Court cannot consider the truth of the matters in the deed, including who the legal owners are. Next, while Plaintiff submits his declaration that the only co-owners of record title for the property are Plaintiff and Defendant Melissa Solis as joint tenants (Vargas Decl., ¶ 2), this is a conclusory statement. As such, Plaintiff has not met his initial burden on summary judgment to show there are no triable issues of material fact as to whether he is entitled to partition.

 

Even if Plaintiff met his burden on summary judgment to show that he is the co-owner as indicated on the deed, Defendants have met their burden to present clear and convincing evidence that there was a mutual understanding that the character of the property was to be held differently. As such, triable issues of material fact exist as to the parties’ interests in the property as Maria and Melissa have submitted testimony that the intent was for Plaintiff and Defendant to hold title for safekeeping until she passes, and Maria is the beneficial owner and she has treated the property as her own by paying the mortgage and bills and by living there. This evidence, all together, shows there are triable issues of fact.

 

Specifically, Defendants submit evidence that the home has been Defendant Maria’s primary residence since she purchased it with her former husband in 1979. (M. Solis Decl., ¶ 2; M. Vargas Decl., ¶ 2.) In 2007, the parents transferred bare legal title to two of the children – Plaintiff and Defendant – as joint tenants, for safekeeping and estate-planning purposes. (M. Solis Decl., ¶ 4; M. Vargas Decl., ¶ 3.) The agreement was that only upon death of both of their parents, would Plaintiff and Defendant sell the home and divide the proceeds equally among the children. The parents chose to place title in only Plaintiff and Defendant, rather than including their other siblings because of concerns of the other siblings’ personal circumstances at the time. (Id.) Maria Vargas is primarily Spanish speaking and did not fully understand the legal implications of the deed at that time; had she understood the transfer could be treated as an outright gift, she would have never signed it. (M. Vargas Decl., ¶ 4.) Plaintiff and Defendant did not believe they had any right to sell the property while their parents were alive. It was the parents’ home. (M. Solis Decl., ¶ 5.) The mortgage, property taxes, homeowners insurance, and utilities were paid by their parents and were in one of both of their names. Defendant occasionally helped starting in 2023 because the mother was on a fixed disability income. (M. Solis Decl., ¶ 6; M. Vargas Decl., ¶ 5.) Neither Plaintiff nor Defendant paid to acquire the property. (Id.) Plaintiff paid the mortgage a few times without Maria’s permission after he filed suit in 2024. (M. Vargas Decl., ¶ 5.) In 2024, Plaintiff asked Maria for permission to claim the home on his income taxes, and she said no. She states that he would not have needed to ask her permission if he believed the home belonged to him—he asked because he understood the home is Maria’s. After she said no, he became hostile towards her, and once it became abusive, he moved out of the home in 2024. (M. Vargas Decl., ¶ 9.)

 

Plaintiff contends in the motion that even if Defendants are arguing an oral trust, any such oral agreement would be unenforceable under the statute of frauds. (Civ. Code § 1624(a)(3).) Civil Code section 1624(a)(3) states: “The following contracts are invalid, unless they are in writing and subscribed by the party to be charged …. An agreement for … the sale of real property; such an agreement, if made by the party sought to be charged, is invalid, unless the authority of the agent is in writing, subscribed by the party sought to be charged.” But simply because an oral agreement for the sale of property would be invalid as a contract does not mean it cannot be offered in evidence in opposition to a motion for summary judgment to show the parties’ intent.

 

            Inconsistent Discovery Responses

 

Plaintiff also argues that Defendant Solis’ verified discovery response, she admits that Plaintiff is a co-owner of the property. (U.F. No. 5.) For example, Request for Admission No. 2 states: admit that Plaintiff is a co-owner of the property, which Defendant Solis responded “admit”. (Plaintiff’s Exs. 9-10.) Request for Admission No. 1 stated: admit that Plaintiff is listed as a co-owner of record on the grant deed to the property, to which Defendant Solis responded “admit”. (Id.) Thus, Plaintiff contends this evidence affirmatively establishes that Plaintiff holds ownership interest.

 

In opposition, Defendants argue that these admissions speak to record title, not beneficial ownership, and are expressly qualified by Defendants’ other verified responses describing the family trust arrangement. (UMF Nos. 7, 9, 16.)

 

Plaintiff cites to no caselaw for the proposition that inconsistent responses in discovery entitles Plaintiff to summary adjudication or would allow Plaintiff to meet his burden. It would be one thing if there were no other discovery responses denying Plaintiff’s ownership. But in fact, Defendant Solis responded to Defendant’s form interrogatories that although the deed was transferred to them, it was for safekeeping (UMF No. 7), which is entirely consistent with their position in opposition to this motion. Further, Defendants argue that the admission was for legal title. Thus, it appears, at the very least, that there was a miscommunication or misunderstanding in the discovery response.

 

            Judicial Estoppel

 

Next, Plaintiff contends that Defendant Vargas made a prior judicial statement that she has no interest in any real property in her dissolution proceedings. (U.F. No. 22.) Thus, he argues that judicial estoppel bars her from taking an inconsistent position.

 

“Judicial estoppel is an equitable doctrine that precludes a party from gaining an advantage by asserting one position, and then later seeking an advantage by taking a clearly inconsistent position.” (Hamilton v. State Farm Fire & Casualty Co. (9th Cir. 2001) 270 F.3d 778, 782.) Factors in determining whether to apply judicial estoppel include: (1) whether a party’s later position is clearly inconsistent with its earlier position; (2) whether the party has succeeded in persuading a court to accept that party’s earlier position so that judicial acceptance of an inconsistent position would create the perception that either the first or the second court was misled; and (3) whether the party seeking to assert an inconsistent position would derive an unfair advantage or impose an unfair detriment on the opposing party if not estopped. (See id.)  

 

            Plaintiff argues that Vargas’ position in the dissolution proceedings that she has no interest in any property is clearly inconsistent with her position now that she is the 100% owner of the property. However, there is no dispute that Vargas did not hold legal title. Thus, the declaration in the dissolution proceeding where she wrote N/A under the column that seeks a description of property owned is not entirely inconsistent with her position here. The dissolution declaration did not ask if she had a beneficial interest in property. Further, even if it was inconsistent, for judicial estoppel to apply, Plaintiff is required to show Vargas succeeded in persuading a court to accept her position so that judicial acceptance of an inconsistent position would create the perception that either the first or the second court was misled. Plaintiff has not addressed this factor. Thus, judicial estoppel does not apply here.

 

            Plaintiff’s Argument Regarding a Rebuttable Gift Presumption in the Reply

 

            For the first time in the Reply, Plaintiff argues that the challenged transfer raises a rebuttable gift presumption. As this argument was made for the first time in the Reply, Defendants have not had an opportunity to respond to it and provide evidence. Thus, it cannot be considered. Plaintiff’s motion addressed the arguments Defendants made in opposition, namely that the transfer was not intended to be a gift and it was for safekeeping as Plaintiff presented this evidence in his separate statement, and thus, he could have included this argument in the moving papers, but did not. Even if the Court did consider it, Defendants’ evidence that the transfer was not intended to be a gift rebuts the presumption.

 

            Because there are triable issues of material fact as to the first element, the Court does not reach the other elements. Plaintiff’s motion for summary adjudication is DENIED.

 

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV15459    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: 507

Tentative Ruling 

 

Judge Rolf M. Treu, Department 507 

 

 

HEARING DATE:     July 21, 2026              TRIAL DATE: May 18, 2027 

 

CASE:                         Dragana Stijepovic v. Davita Inc.  

 

CASE NO.:                 25STCV15459

 

 

MOTION TO COMPEL DEPOSITION AND REQUEST FOR SANCTIONS

 

 

MOVING PARTIES:           Defendants Davita Inc. and total Renal Care, Inc.

 

RESPONDING PARTY:      Plaintiff Dragana Stijepovic

  

CASE HISTORY: 

·        05/28/2025: Complaint filed.  

 

TENTATIVE RULING: 

 

Defendants Davita Inc. and total Renal Care, Inc.’s Motion to Compel Deposition is GRANTED. Plaintiff is ordered to appear for her deposition as agreed upon by the parties.

 

Defendants’ request for sanctions is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $2,940.50 against Plaintiff and counsel of record, jointly and severally, to be paid within 20 days of this order.

 

Moving Party to give notice. 

 

BACKGROUND  

This is an employment action. Plaintiff alleges she suffered numerous acts of discrimination and that Defendants retaliated against Plaintiff because of her cancer diagnosis. Plaintiff also alleges that when she resisted Defendants’ unlawful directives that jeopardized patient well-being and conflicted with her responsibilities as a social worker, she faced retaliation, and that in 2024, she was demoted and her pay was significantly reduced.

DISCUSSION: 

 

            As an initial matter, Plaintiff’s opposition is untimely. The Court will exercise its discretion to consider it.

Defendants move to compel Plaintiff’s deposition. Defendants provide evidence that Plaintiff’s deposition was noticed six times, and Plaintiff canceled last minute each time. (Lu Decl., Exs. A-N.) At the last cancelation, Defendant re-noticed Plaintiff’s deposition for April 23, 2026. (Lu Decl., Ex. R.)

“If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action … without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice, the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent's attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.”  (CCP § 2025.450, subd. (a).)

The motion must be accompanied by a good faith meet and confer declaration under section 2016.040 or, “when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.”  (CCP § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).) 

 

            In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the motion is moot as Plaintiff has sat for her first session of her deposition, and her second session is scheduled for August 6, 2026. Plaintiff argues that all of the delays, except for one, in completing Plaintiff’s deposition were necessary due to her cancer treatment.

 

            However, three of the cancellations were not due to Plaintiff’s cancer treatment, but counsel’s mistake in calendaring and twice for Plaintiff’s counsel’s trial conflicts. (Lu Decl., Exs. D, N, Q.) Further, the motion is not moot as Plaintiff has another deposition to complete in August. Moreover, Defendants argue in the Reply that after this motion was filed, Plaintiff rescheduled her first deposition session from April 23, 2026 to May 7, 2026, and she also failed to appear for her second deposition session on June 16, 2026, notifying Defendant only the day before. Thus, this motion is not moot and in fact, necessary. As Defendants served deposition notices and Plaintiff failed to proceed with her deposition, the motion is granted. Plaintiff is ordered to proceed with the deposition as agreed upon.

 

            Sanctions

 

Defendants seek sanctions in the amount of $5,060.5, which consists of 4 hours of attorney time at a rate of $1,060 an hour, and $820.50 for the non-appearance. (Lu Decl., Ex. S.)

 

If this motion is granted, the court shall impose a monetary sanction in favor of the party who noticed the deposition and against the deponent or the party with whom the deponent is affiliated, unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. (CCP § 2025.450(g)(1).)

 

While Plaintiff argues that the delays were due to her cancer, as noted above, three of the depositions were canceled due to Plaintiff’s counsel’s own conflicts. Further, as Defendant noted in the Reply, Plaintiff did not appear for her second deposition session. Nevertheless, because some of the cancelations were due to Plaintiff’s cancer treatment, the Court will reduce the sanctions amount awarded. Thus, the Court imposes sanctions in the amount of $2,940.50 ($820.50 for the non-appearance invoice, and 2 hours at $1,060 an hour), against Plaintiff and counsel of record, jointly and severally, to be paid within 20 days of this order.

 

CONCLUSION:  

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ Motion to Compel Deposition is GRANTED. Plaintiff is ordered to appear for her deposition as agreed upon by the parties.

 

Defendants’ request for sanctions is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $2,940.50 against Plaintiff and counsel of record, jointly and severally, to be paid within 20 days of this order.

 

Moving Party to give notice. 

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV19266    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: 507

Tentative Ruling 

 

Judge Rolf M. Treu, Department 507 

 

 

HEARING DATE:     July 21, 2026              TRIAL DATE: July 13, 2027 

 

CASE:                         Elisabetta Rogiani v. Elizabeth V. Gordon, et al.  

 

CASE NO.:                 25STCV19266  

 

MOTION FOR SANCTIONS FOR MISUSE OF THE DISCOVERY PROCESS AND TO COMPEL FULL AND COMPLETE DISCLOSURE OF ALL APPLICABLE INSURANCE POLICIES

 

 

MOVING PARTY:               Plaintiff Elisabetta Rogiani 

 

RESPONDING PARTIES: Defendants Elizabeth V. Gordon and Eric Fonseca 

  

CASE HISTORY: 

·        07/02/25: Complaint filed.  

 

TENTATIVE RULING: 

 

Rogiani’s Motion for Sanctions and to Compel Full and Complete Disclosure is DENIED.

 

Moving Party to give notice. 

 

BACKGROUND 

 

This is a personal injury action. Plaintiff alleges that she was drugged and sexually assaulted at a social gathering, causing her severe and debilitating injuries.  

 

DISCUSSION: 

Plaintiff moves for sanctions and to compel full disclosure of all of Defendant Gordon’s applicable insurance policies, arguing that Defendant Gordon’s discovery responses concealed the full scope of available insurance coverage from Plaintiff. Plaintiff argues that Code of Civil Procedure section 2017.210 expressly authorizes discovery of any insurance policy that may provide coverage for a judgment sought in the action. Plaintiff argues that Defendant denied having any applicable insurance in her original responses, then in supplemental discovery, disclosed only a $500,000 homeowner's policy while not disclosing a $3,000,000 excess policy, then sat for her deposition and, when directly asked about policy limits, confirmed only the $500,000 Standard Fire coverage without ever disclosing the Markel excess policy. Plaintiff argues that Defendant served amended responses in May 2026 disclosing the $3,000,000 for the first time.

CCP section 2023.030 authorizes the court to impose a monetary sanction, issue sanction, evidence sanction, or terminating sanction against any party who engages in conduct that is misuse of discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030(a)-(d).)

 

Misuses of the discovery process include, but are not limited to, the following:

 

(a)  Failing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery.

 

(b)  Making an evasive response to discovery.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010.)

 

Courts have recognized the terminating sanction as a drastic penalty that should be used sparingly because the sanction “eliminates a party’s fundamental right to a trial, thus implicating due process rights.” (Lopez v. Watchtower Bible and Tract Society of New York, Inc. (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 566, 604.) Discovery sanctions are incremental, starting with monetary sanctions and ending with termination sanctions. (Id.) “[T]erminating sanction[s] should generally not be imposed until the court has attempted less severe alternatives and found them to be unsuccessful and/or the record clearly shows lesser sanctions would be ineffective.” (Id.) 

 

Ultimate discovery sanctions are justified where there is a willful discovery order violation, a history of abuse, and evidence showing that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with discovery rules.  (Van Sickle v. Gilbert (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1495, 1516.)  “[A] penalty as severe as dismissal or default is not authorized where noncompliance with discovery is caused by an inability to comply rather than willfulness or bad faith.”  (Brown v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 701, 707.) 

In opposition, Defendant argues that Plaintiff ignores Defendant’s contemporaneous verified interrogatory responses, which stated that Defendant was searching and would supplement Form Interrogatory No. 4.1.

First, Plaintiff has not shown that the failure to supplement the discovery response earlier was willful. Further, there is no discovery order violation, or a history of abuse. Further, a motion for issue or evidentiary sanctions requires a separate statement. (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1345(a)(7).) No separate statement has been filed. Thus, terminating, issue or evidentiary sanctions will not be imposed.

Although Defendant has failed to explain why it did not disclose the $3,000,000 insurance policy earlier, the Court agrees with Defendant that Defendant’s voluntary supplementation of discovery should not be sanctionable. As Defendant points out, Defendant stated in discovery that she was searching for the insurance policies. (Morris Decl., Exh. A.) Thus, it appears Defendant was not initially aware of the excess/umbrella policy, especially considering it was an excess policy, and not the primary policy. Plaintiff has not shown that Defendant and her counsel of record were aware they had the excess/umbrella policy and willfully failed to disclose it. Rather, it appears that the discovery responses were not evasive, but inaccurate based on the lack of information. Further, the deposition questioning cited only asked about the $500,000 policy. Moreover, the fact that Defendant did not disclose the excess policy at deposition may have been because she was unaware of it at that time.

Next, the Court also cannot compel responses when no discovery has been presented. It is unclear whether Plaintiff is seeking further responses to certain discovery she has already served or if Plaintiff is seeking an order for Defendant to provide responses to discovery not ever served. If Plaintiff seeks further responses, she must file the appropriate motion, along with a separate statement, and must have complied with the procedural requirement of meeting and conferring, and it must also be timely. If she seeks to compel Defendant’s response to discovery that was never served, there is no authority for such relief.

CONCLUSION:  

 

Accordingly, Plaintiffs Motion for Sanctions and to Compel Full and Complete Disclosure is DENIED.  

 

Moving Party to give notice.
