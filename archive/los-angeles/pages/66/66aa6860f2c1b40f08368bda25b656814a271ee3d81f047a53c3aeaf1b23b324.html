22STCV00671

JESUS MARTINEZ vs CITY OF LOS ANGELES, et al.

August 12, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: DEFENDANT CITY OF LOS ANGELES’ MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT.

 

RULING:

The Court denies the Motion.

 The Court overrules Plaintiff’s Evidentiary Objections, filed July 23, 2026, as to numbers 4-5, 7-10, 12, 14-17, 21-22, and 24-28, and otherwise sustains them.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On January 6, 2022, JESUS MARTINEZ (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against CITY OF LOS ANGELES and LOS ANGELES POLICE DEPARTMENT (Defendants), identifying one Cause of Action for, “Retaliation in Violation of Labor Code § 1102.5.”

Plaintiff alleges that, on or around August 12, 2020, in retaliation for reporting illegal activity, Plaintiff was removed from his elite position and experienced several other adverse employment actions, including IA investigations, a demotion, a suspension, an administrative transfer, loss of overtime opportunities, loss of promotion opportunities, discipline, loss of pay, reprimands, and a retaliatory work environment. (Complaint, ¶¶ 12-33.)

On February 5, 2026, CITY OF LOS ANGELES (Defendant) filed the Motion for Summary Judgment, on grounds that (1) there are no cognizable adverse employment actions alleged, (2) Plaintiff’s disclosure about misconduct of Captain Griffith was not a contributing factor to any adverse actions, and (3) the same employer decision would have been made based on Plaintiff’s participation in an officer-involved shooting.

Plaintiff opposes, based on arguments that numerous triable issues exist as to those issues that Defendant raised.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

“In an employment discrimination case, the employer, as the moving party, has the initial burden to present admissible evidence showing either that one or more elements of plaintiff's prima facie case is lacking or that the adverse employment action was based upon legitimate, nondiscriminatory factors.” (Hicks v. KNTV Television, Inc. (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 994, 1003. Accord Avila v. Continental Airlines, Inc. (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1237, 1247.) Where employers present admissible proof that at least one prima facie element is lacking, or that the adverse employment action was based upon nondiscriminatory factors, employers are entitled to summary judgment, unless plaintiffs present admissible evidence showing a triable issue of material fact related to the employers’ showing. (Arteaga v. Brink's, Inc. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 327, 344. “[M]any employment cases present issues of intent, and motive, and hostile working environment, issues not determinable on paper. Such cases … are rarely appropriate for disposition on summary judgment….” (Nazir v. United Airlines, Inc. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 243, 286.)

Plaintiffs must produce substantial, responsive evidence that the employer's showing was untrue or pretextual. (Addy v. Bliss & Glennon (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 205, 215.) “[E]vidence that the employer's claimed reason is false—such as that it conflicts with other evidence, or appears to have been contrived after the fact—will tend to suggest that the employer seeks to conceal the real reason for its actions, and this in turn may support an inference that the real reason was unlawful.” (Mamou v. Trendwest Resorts, Inc. (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 686, 715.)

The burden of establishing a prima facie case of discrimination is fairly minimal, such that the temporal proximity between employees’ protected activities, and a subsequent adverse action, may satisfy the causation requirement. (Arteaga v. Brink's, Inc. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 327, 353.) “A plaintiff can satisfy his or her initial burden under the test by producing evidence of nothing more than the employer's knowledge that the employee engaged in protected activities and the proximity in time between the protected action and the allegedly retaliatory employment decision.” (McRae v. Dept. of Corrections and Rehab. (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 377, 388.) The element of causation may be based upon inferences from circumstantial evidence, including employer's knowledge of the employee’s protected activity, and the time between the protected act and the employment decision. (Morgan v. Regents of Univ. of Cal. (2000) 88 Cal.App.4th 52, 69-70.) Employment termination only a few months after employees’ protected activities can circumstantially evidence causation as to retaliation claims. (Flait v. No. Amer. Watch Corp. (1992) 3 Cal.App.4th 467, 478.)

“[W]hen an employment decision is influenced by several people, “ ‘a decision maker's ignorance does not “ ‘categorically shield the employer from liability if other substantial contributors to the decision bore the requisite animus….” ’ ” (Avila v. Continental Airlines, Inc. (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1237, 1251. Accord Reeves v. Safeway Stores, Inc. (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 95, 109.) “[S]howing that a significant participant in an employment decision exhibited discriminatory animus is enough to raise an inference that the employment decision itself was discriminatory, even absent evidence that others in the process harbored such animus.” (DeJung v. Superior Court (2008) 169 Cal.App.4th 533, 551.)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

Defendant summarizes the arguments, as follows:

“First, Plaintiff’s claims fail because he alleges several non-cognizable adverse actions.  He alleges ‘adverse actions’ that are not cognizable under § 1102.5.  Second, his disclosure about Griffith was not a ‘contributing factor’ to any adverse action.  The record contains no evidence that Plaintiff ever suffered an adverse action because of his disclosure.  His belief that he was retaliated against is entirely speculative.  Lastly, the clear and convincing evidence confirms that Plaintiff would still have received the same treatment for legitimate and independent reasons, i.e., his participation in an officer-involved shooting.”

(Motion, 1:8-14.)

Plaintiff counterargues that there are disputes as to the above-referenced issues, including the following facts:

1.      Decision-makers were not ignorant of Plaintiff’s disclosure. Defendant’s claims are contradicted by DC Scott who testified that since Brockway never told DC Scott about Martinez’s complaint, she never told Brockway that what Griffith allegedly did was not misconduct or that a complaint was not warranted and the matter did not need to be investigated.

2.      Brockway testified that Griffith knew of Martinez/Davila’s complaint, because Brockway confronted Griffith within days of the April 24, 2020 incident, and details of the conversation clearly show that Martinez complained about Griffith. 

3.      Plaintiff would not have been disciplined the same absent the protected disclosure. Chief Moore deviated from the chain of command’s recommendation and imposed a uniquely harsh penalty on Martinez than other officers/supervisors, despite all engaging in the alleged acts.

4.      Moore admitted that his disciplinary decision was only as good as the information on which it was based and conceded the core facts he relied on for discipline-- that Martinez ignored advance radio warnings of armed suspects and that Martinez shot an unarmed man were false.

5.      The timing between knowledge of Plaintiff’s disclosure and adverse actions infers causation.

6.      Plaintiff suffered adverse actions including lost promotion opportunities.

 

The elements of the Cause of Action for Retaliation under Labor Code section 1102.5, subdivision (b) (Whistleblowing), are the following:

  1. Retaliation against an employee's protected activity;
  2. that was a contributing factor for causing an adverse employment action.

(Lawson v. PPG Architectural Finishes, Inc. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 703, 718 [citing Lab. C. § 1102.6 as burden of proof, and holding retaliation against employees’ protected activities must have been a contributing factor to the adverse employment action]; ibid., p. 712 [“employer bears ‘the burden of proof to demonstrate by clear and convincing evidence’ that the alleged adverse employment action would have occurred ‘for legitimate, independent reasons’ even if the employee had not engaged in protected whistleblowing activities.”]. See also Francis v. City of Los Angeles (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 532, 540–541 [“To prove a claim of retaliation under this statute [Labor Code section 1102.5], the plaintiff ‘must demonstrate that he or she has been subjected to an adverse employment action that materially affects the terms, conditions, or privileges of employment.’ ”]; Ververka v. Dep't of Veterans Affs. (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 162, 176 [an employer's same-decision defense that is not pretext could bar a whistleblower claim].)

What constitutes an adverse employment action is not susceptible to a mathematically precise test, but is one that materially affects terms and conditions of employment, considering the complainant’s collective allegations and the totality-of-the-circumstances. (Brown v. Los Angeles Unified Sch. Dist. (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 1092, 1106.) In determining whether there was an adverse employment action to show discrimination or retaliation, courts look at the totality of circumstances and not just at each isolated act. (Yanowitz v. L'Oreal USA, Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1055 [“we need not and do not decide whether each alleged retaliatory act constitutes an adverse employment action in and of itself.”]. See also Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water & Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1231, disapproved on other grounds by Jones v. The Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1174.) Adverse employment actions materially affect terms, conditions, or privileges of employment caused by actions reasonably likely to adversely and materially affect an employee's job performance or opportunity for advancement, such as hiring, firing, failing to promote, adverse job assigning, significantly changing compensation or benefits, or disciplining. (Doe v. Dep't of Corr. & Rehab. (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 721, 734.) Even a lateral transfer can constitute an adverse employment action as to retaliation claims, where the employment actions are reasonably likely to adversely and materially affect job performance or opportunities for advancement in one’s career, and the test must be interpreted liberally with a reasonable appreciation of workplace realities. (Patten v. Grant Joint Union High School Dist. (2005) 134 Cal.App.4th 1378, 1389-1390, disapproved on other grounds by Lawson v. PPG Architectural Finishes, Inc. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 703, 718.) Lateral transfers, suspensions, assignments without training, and a series of subtle discriminatory acts may constitute an adverse employment action in support of a claim of discrimination. (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State Etc. (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 374.) Adverse employment actions can include demotions, reassignments, refusals to promote, unwarranted evaluations, tolerating harassment by coworkers, reprimands and suspensions. (Yanowitz v. L'Oreal USA, Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1061.) Examples of adverse employment actions “include discharge, demotions, refusal to hire, nonrenewal of contracts, and failure to promote.” (Wilson v. Murillo (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1124, 1135.) A transfer to a less-preferred position alone would not constitute an adverse employment action, so long as the employee receives equivalent pay, benefits and promotional opportunities, and experiences no hostile environment. (Malais v. L. A. City Fire Dept. (2007) 150 Cal. App.4th 350, 357-358.)

Preliminarily, the Court does not consider the Reply declaration of Ha Nguyen, filed July 31, 2026, as to Defendant’s burden of proof. “The [summary judgment] reply shall not include any new evidentiary matter, additional material facts, or separate statement submitted with the reply and not presented in the moving papers or opposing papers.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(4).) Courts cannot consider moving parties’ new evidence filed for the first time after the opposition to a summary judgment or adjudication motion has been filed, without first considering opposing parties’ due-process rights, or allowing a continuance for a supplemental opposition. (E.g., Wall Street Network, Ltd. v. N. Y. Times Co. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1171, 1183, 1191, fn. 11; Plenger v. Alza Corp. (1992) 11 Cal.App.4th 349, 362.)

As for the merits, the Court determines that there are triable issues of material fact as to all of the issues raised, involving whether retaliation against Plaintiff’s protected whistleblowing was a contributing factor to adverse employment actions, and whether the actions would have occurred in any event for legitimate and independent reasons. (See, e.g., Moving and Opposing Separate Statements, fact numbers 12 (same-decision pretext), 16 (adverse actions including demotion), 23 (pretext inferred by disproportionate discipline), 27 (multiple decision-makers, not only ignorant ones), 28 (extent of knowledge of Plaintiff’s disclosure), 29 (adverse actions, including lost promotional opportunities); Plaintiff’s additional fact numbers 17-18 (discriminatory animus), 33-39 (temporal proximity between disclosure and employer adversities), 54 (decision-maker knowledge of protected disclosure), 65-66 (same-decision defense disputed by disproportionate disciplining), 75-76 (inconsistent explanations for disciplining, inferring pretext), 83-85 (inconclusive investigation as to the officer-involved shooter’s identity), 86-90 (decision-making participants’ knowledge); and, proof referenced thereat.)

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

Accordingly, the Court denies the Motion, for reasons set forth herein.

ROBERT CROWELL vs ONE ELECTRIC LLC

August 12, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: DEFENDANT’S MOTION FOR AN ORDER COMPELLING PLAINTIFF ROBERT CROWELL TO PROVIDE RESPONSES TO PLAINTIFF’S FORM INTERROGATORIES SET NO. TWO.  DEFENDANT’S MOTION FOR AN ORDER COMPELLING PLAINTIFF ROBERT CROWELL TO PROVIDE FURTHER RESPONSES TO DEFENDANT’S SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES, SET NO. TWO AND IMPOSING MONETARY SANCTIONS IN THE AMOUNT OF $3,360.00, JOINTLY AND SEVERALLY, AGAINST PLAINTIFF AND PLAINTIFF’S COUNSEL.  DEFENDANT’S MOTION FOR AN ORDER COMPELLING PLAINTIFF ROBERT CROWELL TO PROVIDE FURTHER RESPONSES TO DEFENDANT’S REQUESTS FOR ADMISSIONS, SET NO. TWO AND IMPOSING MONETARY SANCTIONS IN THE AMOUNT OF $5,600.00, JOINTLY AND SEVERALLY, AGAINST PLAINTIFF AND PLAINTIFF’S COUNSEL.

 

RULING:

The Court grants the Motion to Compel Further Responses to Form Interrogatories, set number two, limited to 70 Admission Request items pursuant to the June 30, 2026 Protective Order.

The Court denies the Motion to Compel Further Special Interrogatories, set number two, pursuant to the waiver stated in that Protective Order.

The Court grants the Motion to Compel Further Responses to Requests for Admissions, set number two, limited to 70 items pursuant to the same Protective Order.

The Court denies all sanctions requests, it finding substantial justification for some opposing positions, primarily related to the partially granted Protective Order.

On or before September 11, 2026, Plaintiff ROBERT CROWELL shall serve initial responses, without objections, and in full compliance with the California Discovery Act (Code Civ. Proc. § 2016.010 et seq.), as to Defendant’s Form Interrogatory number 17.1, and code-compliant responses, without objections, to Defendant’s 70 selected Requests for Admissions.

Defendant to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On June 12, 2025, ROBERT CROWELL (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against ONE ELECTRIC, LLC (Defendant), listing Causes of Action for:

1.       WRONGFUL TERMINATION IN VIOLATION OF PUBLIC POLICY

2.       DISABILITY DISCRIMINATION IN VIOLATION OF FAIR EMPLOYMENT AND HOUSING ACT

3.       FAILURE TO ENGAGE IN GOOD FAITH INTERACTIVE PROCESS IN VIOLATION OF FAIR EMPLOYMENT AND HOUSING ACT

4.       FAILURE TO PROVIDE REASONABLE ACCOMMODATION IN VIOLATION OF FAIR EMPLOYMENT AND HOUSING ACT

5.       RETALIATION IN VIOLATION OF THE FAIR EMPLOYMENT AND HOUSING ACT

6.       FAILURE TO PREVENT DISCRIMINATION AND RETALIATION IN VIOLATION OF FAIR EMPLOYMENT AND HOUSING ACT.

Plaintiff alleges that Defendant wrongfully terminated his employment as a chief electrical estimator, instead of engaging in the interactive process and providing reasonable accommodations after Plaintiff’s stent procedure and vascular surgery.

On June 30, 2026, the Court entered a Minute Order, nunc pro tunc, at page 5 granting Plaintiff’s Motion for Protective Order, as follows: “Defendant shall select 70 maximum Requests for Admissions, out of the subject discovery, and serve a Notice thereof upon Plaintiff, such that Plaintiff is informed of which item numbers to serve responses to, which responses Plaintiff shall serve within 30 days after such notice. [¶] The Motion is otherwise denied.” That Order is not modified in any way by statute or by Defendant’s prior discovery requests or Plaintiff’s prior discovery responses. There is an absolute limit of 70 Requests for Admission.

On June 17, 2026, Defendant filed three discovery Motions to compel further responses to (1) Form Interrogatories, (2) Special Interrogatories, and (3) Requests for Admission. Defendant also requests monetary sanctions, as to the second and third motions.

Plaintiff opposes, based upon counterarguments such as that the Protective Order has limited the Requests for Admissions to 70, as related to Form Interrogatory 17.1, and has ordered waived the Special Interrogatories.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

Where respondents object or respond inadequately to discovery requests, a motion lies to compel further responses, as to which respondent has the burden to justify the objections. (Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (2000) 22 Cal.4th 245, 255; Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, subd. (a) [motion to compel further responses lies “[o]n receipt of a response to interrogatories….”]; and 2031.310, subd. (a) [motion to compel further responses lies “[o]n receipt of a response to an inspection demand….”].) A propounding party's remedy when it deems objections in discovery responses to be without merit is to move for an order compelling further responses. (Catalina Island Yacht Club v. Superior Court (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1127.)

Monetary discovery sanctions are mandatory as to parties losing discovery motions, unless courts find substantial justification or other injustice. (Deck v. Devs. Inv. Co. (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 808, 830.) “‘[T]he phrase “substantial justification” has been understood to mean that a justification is clearly reasonable because it is well grounded in both law and fact.’” (Kwan Software Engineering, Inc. v. Hennings (2020) 58 Cal.App.5th 57, 75.) “If the party seeking a monetary sanction meets its burden of proof, the burden shifts to the opposing party attempting to avoid a monetary sanction to show that it acted with ‘substantial justification.’” (Doe v. U.S. Swimming, Inc. (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1424, 1435.) Courts may determine substantial justification based on the “whole picture” of the discovery dispute, including where parties partially prevailed as to some discovery items in dispute, or where discovery concessions occurred only after improperly asserted objections. (In re Marriage of Moore (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 1275, 1297.)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

Defendant advocates an Order compelling Plaintiff’s initial Form Interrogatory responses and further responses without objections to Special Interrogatories and Requests for Admission, and imposing monetary sanctions.

Plaintiff advocates continuing or ruling on the Motions, in accordance with the Protective Order. Plaintiff also argues against sanctions, asserting that there is substantial justification for the discovery responding and opposing positions.

 

A.    Form Interrogatories

Defendant requests an Order compelling Plaintiff to serve an initial verified response to Form Interrogatory No. 17.1, without objections, as to the 70 selected Requests for Admissions, and also numbers 47, 128, 138, 142, 154, 169, and 195, which Defendant contends are unaffected by the Protective Order.

Plaintiff maintains that Form Interrogatory No. 17.1 is derivative of the Requests for Admission that have been limited to 70.

If interrogatory responses lack specificity, then parties may move to compel further responses under Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300, subdivision (a), providing for motions to compel, where parties deem that an answer is evasive, incomplete, or inadequate as to specification of documents. (Best Products, Inc. v. Superior Court (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 1181, 1190.) “While the party propounding interrogatories may have the burden of filing a motion to compel if it finds the answers it receives unsatisfactory, the burden of justifying any objection and failure to respond remains at all times with the party resisting an interrogatory.” (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541.)

Parties failing to serve timely responses to discovery requests waive any objections thereto. (Pelton v. Delta Packaging Prods. (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1658, 1577, fn.13; Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pac. Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 404. Courts, on motion, may relieve parties from waivers of discovery objections caused by late responses, if parties subsequently have served responses in substantial compliance with applicable statutory provisions, and their failures resulted from mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Pelton v. Delta Packaging Prods. (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1568, 1577, fn.13 [citing Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300]; Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pac. Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 407.)  Parties may obtain protective orders even after  waiving discovery objections. (Stadish v. Superior Court (1999) 71 Cal.App.4th 1130, 1144 [addressing documents responses].)

Admission requests and Judicial Council Form Interrogatory number 17.1 together require respondents to state all facts that support the contentions. (Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 573, 580.) Form Interrogatory number 17.1 is a contention interrogatory. (See, ibid. [“Those admissions requests and Judicial Council form interrogatory number 17.1 required plaintiffs to state all of the facts which supported the contention….”].)

Here, the Court finds that number 17.1 must be initially answered, by addressing only the 70 Requests for Admissions Defendant selected, and that all potential discovery objections have been waived by Plaintiff’s not timely serving responses, but subject to a possible Motion for Relief from Waivers. While the Protective Order did not directly affect number 17.1, by design it is interrelated with the Requests for Admissions, which here are limited to 70.

 

B.     Special Interrogatories

Defendant requests an Order compelling Plaintiff to serve verified, code-compliant, further responses to Special Interrogatories numbers 79, 89, 92, 98, 104, 108, 111, 112, 114, 115, 119, 121, 130, 137, and 151, and opines that the Protective Order has had no effect on this Motion.

Plaintiff counterargues that Defendant expressly waived the Special Interrogatories at the hearing about the Protective Order.

“The same rules apply in ascertaining the meaning of a court order or judgment as in ascertaining the meaning of any other writing.” (Mendly v. County of L. A. (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1205.) Courts have the inherent power to correct clerical errors in their records to reflect the true facts, even pending an appeal, on its own motion, at any time. (People v. Mitchell (2001) 26 Cal.4th 181, 185.)

Courts may accept representations of attorneys as being the equivalent of a binding stipulation. (Roman v. Liberty University, Inc. (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 670, 683.) “[A]n oral statement by counsel in the same action is a binding judicial admission if the statement was an unambiguous concession of a matter then at issue and was not made improvidently or unguardedly.” (Fassberg Constr. Co. v. Hous. Auth. of City of Los Angeles (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 720, 752.)

Estoppel in litigation occurs where a party’s statement intentionally led another to believe a particular thing true and to act upon such belief. (State Compensation Ins. Fund v. Workers' Comp. Appeals Bd. (1985) 40 Cal.3d 5, 16; Padron v. Osoy (2025) 110 Cal.App.5th 677, 691; Evid. Code § 623 [“Whenever a party has, by his own statement or conduct, intentionally and deliberately led another to believe a particular thing true and to act upon such belief, he is not, in any litigation arising out of such statement or conduct, permitted to contradict it.”].) A party to a stipulation is conclusively estopped from taking a position contrary to a stipulation in the litigation. (Palmer v. City of Oakland (1978) 86 Cal.App.3d 39, 44.) “By expressly consenting … an appellant acquiesces in it and thereby loses the right to attack the error on appeal.” (Stebbins v. White (1987) 190 Cal.App.3d 769, 782.) “‘It is a fundamental rule of appellate procedure that an order will not be disturbed on an appeal prosecuted by a consenting party. A stipulation is a consent within the meaning of this rule.’” (Bowden v. Green (1982) 128 Cal.App.3d 65, 72.)

Here, the Court unequivocally ordered that all Special Interrogatories were waived, without any exceptions or refinements, pursuant to defense counsel’s statement at the June 30, 2026 hearing. Such ruling and statements are binding upon Defendant.

Therefore, the Motion to Compel Responses to Special Interrogatories is denied entirely.

 

C.     Requests for Admissions

“Defendant … seeks verified further responses only to RFA Nos. 47, 128, 138, 142, 154, 169, and 195. Each asks Plaintiff to admit the genuineness of an attached document that he signed, provided, received, or was sent. Section 2033.030, subdivision (a), expressly excludes requests concerning document genuineness from the numerical limit applicable to other RFAs, and the Court did not decide these seven requests.” (Reply, 2:15-20.) Additionally, Defendant argues in favor of monetary sanctions, partly because Plaintiff was not substantially justified for repeating the same objections and avoiding substantive responses.

Plaintiff argues that Defendant's Motion has been procedurally superseded by the Protective Order, necessitating a revised Motion addressing Requests for Admissions reduced to 70 total.

Where respondents timely responded to requests for admissions, requesting parties may move to compel further answers based upon a showing of some deficiency. (Wimberly v. Derby Cycle Corp. (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 618, 636; Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.290.)

A response to a request for admission must be an admission, denial or statement of inability to admit or deny. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.220, subd. (b).) Respondents must admit the part of an admission request that is true (as expressed in the request, or as reasonably and clearly qualified), deny the part that is untrue, and specify the part as to which the responding party lacks sufficient information. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.220, subd. (b); Applera Corp. v. MP Biomedicals, LLC (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 769, 778-779, fn.5.) Where a response indicates an inability to admit or deny a request for admission, the responding party must also state that a reasonable inquiry was made as to information known or readily obtainable to obtain sufficient information. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.220, subd. (c).) “It has been said that a denial of all or portion of the request must be unequivocal.” (American Federation of State, County & Municipal Employees v. Metropolitan Water Dist. (2005) 126 Cal.App.4th 247, 268.) Requests for admissions may require respondents to apply laws to facts or state opinions related to facts. (Garcia v. Hyster Co. (1994) 28 Cal.App.4th 724, 735; Miller v. American Greetings Corp. (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 1055, 1066.) “‘[W]hen a party is served with a request for admission concerning a legal question properly raised in the pleadings he cannot object simply by asserting that the request calls for a conclusion of law.’” (Joyce v. Ford Motor Co. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1478, 1488.)

Further, where general boilerplate objections were made, courts have authority to compel further responses. (Best Products, Inc. v. Superior Court (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 1181, 1189.)

Here, the Court finds that Plaintiff must serve code-compliant responses to the 70 Requests for Admission numbers that Defendant selects, whether or not that 70 total includes numbers 47, 128, 138, 142, 154, 169, and 195. Further, the Court overrules all discovery objections in Plaintiff’s response, the Court finding that those were grossly overbroad, boilerplate objections, and that there is no substantial justification for failures to provide the substantive responses authorized by the California Discovery Act.

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

The Court partially grants the Motions regarding Form Interrogatories and Requests for Admissions, as specified herein. The Court denies the Motion to Compel Further Responses to Special Interrogatories, and all sanctions requests.
