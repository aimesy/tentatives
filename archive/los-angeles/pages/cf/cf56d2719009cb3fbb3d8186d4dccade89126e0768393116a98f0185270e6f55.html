I.                BACKGROUND

This case is a wrongful death action filed by Plaintiffs Ali Salehpour (“Ali”) and Suzanne Salehpour (“Suzanne”), individually and as personal representatives of the Estate of Amelia Salehpour (“Amelia”), deceased, against Defendants Rising Roads Recovery Services for Women, LLC (“Rising Roads”); Recovery Solutions, LLC d/b/a Saddleback Recovery (“Saddleback”); Bold Health Medical Group, a Professional Corporation (“Bold Health”); Katy Alexander (“Alexander”); Brandon Owens (“Owens”); Amelia Pietraccini (“Pietraccini”); Andrew Schneider (“Schneider”); Nicole Cavanagh (“Cavanagh”); Llysa Lott (“Lott”); Kimiko Miller (“Miller”); City of Costa Mesa (“Costa Mesa”); City of Los Angeles (“City LA”); Jose Monzon (“Monzon”); County of Los Angeles (the “County”); Kyle Butler (“Butler”); Michel R. Moore (“Moore”); Dominic Choi (“Choi”); Alfred Labrada (“Labrada”); and Odey C. Ukpo (“Ukpo”).

On July 2, 2024, Ali filed his original complaint.

On October 21, 2024, Ali and Suzanne (hereafter, “Plaintiffs”) filed their first amended complaint.

On April 1, 2025, Plaintiffs filed their second amended complaint (the “SAC”) against Defendants Rising Roads, Saddleback, Bold Health, Alexander, Owens, Pietraccini, Schneider, Cavanagh, Lott, Miller, Costa Mesa, City LA, Monzon, the County, and Butler, alleging: (1) professional negligence; (2) negligence per se; (3) negligence; (4) violation of mandatory duty under Government Code section 815.6; (5) negligence; (6) negligent hiring, training, and supervision; (7) continuation of Amelia’s causes of action (survival action); (8) wrongful death; (9) intentional misrepresentation/concealment; and (10) intentional misrepresentation/concealment.

II.             LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer is a procedure to object to a pleading set forth under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10,¿et seq.¿¿Because a demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters, a demurrer analysis considers only those defects which appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed.¿¿SKF Farms v. Superior Court¿(1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.¿¿When considering demurrers, courts assume the truth of the facts alleged in the pleading and read the allegations liberally and in context.¿¿Schifando v. City of Los Angeles¿(2003) 31 Cal.4th 1074, 1081.

A demurrer for sufficiency attacks a pleading on the ground that it does not¿contain¿facts sufficient to¿state¿a cause of action.¿ Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10 (e);¿Hahn v. Mirda¿(2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.¿ Parties may also demur to causes of action individually.¿ In either case, the procedure is also called a ‘demurrer for sufficiency,’ and it tests the pleading by itself, alone and disconnected from extraneous matters.¿¿Hahn,¿at p. 747.

III.           REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

City LA and Monzon request that the Court take judicial notice of Ali’s tort claim for damages submitted on July 2, 2024.  City LA and Monzon also request that the Court take judicial notice of certain LAPD “Body Worn Videos.”

As to Ali’s claim for damages, Plaintiffs oppose the request on grounds that the exhibit is incomplete.  Specifically, Plaintiffs complain that box 2, 3, and 4 of the claim state, “SEE ATTACHMENT,” but no attachment is provided.  City LA and Monzon assert that while the document may refer to an attachment, no attachment is included as part of the document received through discovery.  Thus, according to City LA and Monzon, the tort claim attached to their request for judicial notice is not incomplete.  (See Reply re: RJN, p. 1:6-11.) 

Because there is no dispute that the document submitted to the Court as RJN, Exhibit 1 represents at least part of Ali’s government tort claim, the Court will take judicial notice of said document as submitted.  However, given that there appears to be some dispute as to whether that document represents the whole of Ali’s government claim, the Court will not take judicial notice of the presence or absence of additional attachments.

As to the “Body Worn Videos” lodged with the Court, the Court disagrees with City LA and Monzon that such evidence is the proper subject of judicial notice.  The videos are not, as City LA and Monzon assert, “business records of a public entity.”  (Reply re: RJN, p. 1:16.)  Rather, the videos were purportedly downloaded by an LAPD detective who provided them to litigation counsel during discovery.  The videos’ authenticity and what the videos depict are matters that are reasonably subject to dispute, thus judicial notice would be improper.  See Evid. Code, § 452.  As to “Body Worn Videos,” the request for judicial notice is denied.

IV.           DISCUSSION

City LA and Monzon demur to each cause of action alleged against them in the TAC: (4) violation of mandatory duty under Government Code section 815.6; (5) negligence; (7) continuation of Amelia’s causes of action (survival action); (8) wrongful death; and (10) intentional misrepresentation/concealment.  City LA and Monzon also move to strike portions of the TAC related to seeking punitive damages.

a.     Review of Allegations in the TAC

Plaintiffs plead that between July 22, 2023, and July 26, 2023, Amelia was trafficked from a licensed treatment facility in Orange County to a residence in Los Angeles that functioned as a hub for narcotics trafficking, prostitution, human trafficking, and other criminal activity.  Plaintiffs allege Amelia was held against her will at the residence and was murdered there on July 26, 2023.  Plaintiffs are Amelia’s surviving parents.  (TAC, ¶¶ 1, 6, 36-38.)

Plaintiffs plead that:

·       In May 2023, Amelia was admitted to Rising Roads, an all-female in-patient substance abuse treatment facility in Costa Mesa.   

·       Amelia’s mental health deteriorated substantially while there and that she required a higher level of care.   

·       Rising Roads discharged Amelia to Saddleback, a subcontractor facility.  

·       Five days after entering Saddleback, Amelia was permitted to leave against medical advice despite her mental impairments and despite being a danger to herself.

·       Saddleback released Amelia without money, a cellular telephone, identification, transportation, food, or water and failed to timely notify Plaintiffs of her departure.  

·       Amelia’s family attempted to engage the Costa Mesa Police Department to locate Amelia.  Costa Mesa’s failures resulted in Amelia being transported and trafficked by gang members and ultimately taken to Los Angeles.  

·       Plaintiffs were able to pinpoint Amelia’s location at the Los Angeles residence and repeatedly advised law enforcement that Amelia was being held there against her will.  

·       LAPD knew or should have known that the residence was associated with narcotics trafficking, prostitution, and the recent false imprisonment of another young woman.  Plaintiffs allege LAPD failed to follow its own protocols applicable to at-risk and critical missing persons.  

(TAC, ¶¶ 26-38, 88.)

Plaintiffs plead that on July 24, 2023, a report was made to LAPD concerning Amelia’s disappearance.  Plaintiffs allege LAPD had a mandatory duty to treat Amelia as an at-risk or critical missing person under its policies because she was mentally impaired, required medical attention, and was the victim of a crime.  Plaintiffs allege LAPD failed to comply with mandatory policies governing at-risk missing persons, including dispatch and notification requirements.  (TAC, ¶¶ 91-95.)

Plaintiffs plead that on July 24, 2023, LAPD officers David Garcia and Gabriel Garcia responded to the residence at approximately 10:00 P.M.  Plaintiffs allege the officers were uncertain how to proceed, failed to recognize the exigent circumstances confronting them, and informed Plaintiffs that all they could do was knock on the exterior gate of the property.  Plaintiffs allege the officers did not enter the property or otherwise investigate further.  (TAC, ¶¶ 96-99.)

Plaintiffs plead that on July 25, 2023, after a second 911 call regarding Amelia, LAPD specifically promised Plaintiffs that officers would return to the residence and conduct a complete search for Amelia.  Plaintiffs allege that, in reliance on these representations, they refrained from entering the property themselves or directing others to do so.  (TAC, ¶¶ 100-103.)

Plaintiffs plead that LAPD subsequently returned to the residence, detained an individual wanted on drug charges, and obtained consent to enter the property.  Plaintiffs allege that contrary to LAPD’s representations, officers never conducted a meaningful search of the property.  Instead, body-worn camera footage purportedly shows that officers merely walked exterior pathways, knocked on doors, failed to enter any rooms, failed to open any doors, failed to inspect any structures on the property, and failed to search the garage where Amelia was located.  Plaintiffs allege LAPD thereafter falsely represented to Plaintiffs that officers had searched the property and that Amelia was not there.  (TAC, ¶¶ 104-108, 126-131.)

Plaintiffs allege that Amelia was alive and inside the garage at the time of LAPD’s purported search and that opening the garage door would have led to her rescue.  Plaintiffs allege that only a minimal effort would have been required to locate and save Amelia.  (TAC, ¶¶ 131-133.)

Plaintiffs plead that less than 24 hours after LAPD’s purported search, a 911 call was placed from the residence to report a “dead girl.”  Amelia was subsequently found deceased in the garage.  Plaintiffs allege the officers’ presence and near discovery of Amelia panicked the occupants of the residence and caused them to conclude that Amelia had become a liability, resulting in the decision to murder her.  (TAC, ¶¶ 107, 134-136.)

Plaintiffs plead Amelia was murdered by Hernandez, who strangled and sexually assaulted Amelia.  Plaintiffs allege Hernandez attempted to conceal the circumstances of Amelia’s death.  Plaintiffs allege Amelia had been trafficked to the residence by individuals including Marlon Mancillas and Gerson Perez as part of an ongoing criminal enterprise involving narcotics and prostitution.  (TAC, ¶¶ 36-38, 136-140.)

Plaintiffs plead that on July 26, 2023, LAPD represented to Plaintiffs that there was no indication of foul play and that Amelia’s death appeared accidental.  Plaintiffs allege these representations were false and were made despite highly suspicious circumstances surrounding Amelia’s death.  Plaintiffs allege any investigation was negligently supervised by Sergeant Monzon and that Monzon and other officers were motivated to minimize or mischaracterize the circumstances of Amelia’s death because their prior failures had contributed to it.  (TAC, ¶¶ 109-113.)

Plaintiffs plead that despite the suspicious circumstances surrounding Amelia’s death, LAPD failed to conduct an adequate homicide investigation and communicated its conclusions to the Los Angeles County Medical Examiner-Coroner (“LACMEC”).  Plaintiffs allege LACMEC failed to conduct an adequate independent investigation and initially accepted LAPD’s conclusion that Amelia had died of an overdose.  (TAC, ¶¶ 7, 114-115.)

Plaintiffs further allege that a subsequent autopsy determined Amelia’s death was a homicide caused by strangulation and documented evidence of sexual assault and blunt-force injuries.  Plaintiffs allege that the initial failures of law enforcement and LACMEC resulted in the loss of valuable and perishable evidence.  (TAC, ¶¶ 8, 141-144.)

Plaintiffs plead that LAPD is a government entity and an agency of the City of Los Angeles and that Monzon was employed by LAPD as a police officer and supervisor.  Plaintiffs allege they timely submitted a claim for damages under the Government Claims Act, which the City rejected.  (TAC, ¶¶ 13-14.)

Plaintiffs plead that the Van Nuys Narcotics Enforcement Detail independently investigated the residence where Amelia died and uncovered evidence consistent with Plaintiffs’ allegations concerning her trafficking and murder.  Plaintiffs allege LAPD withheld critical evidence and documents from the investigators, delaying their ability to connect Amelia’s death to an existing criminal investigation that ultimately resulted in criminal charges against multiple defendants.  (TAC, ¶¶ 114, 116.)

Plaintiffs plead that LAPD concealed body-worn camera footage and other evidence concerning the July 25, 2023 search for more than two (2) years, including from other investigators and prosecutors.  Plaintiffs allege the concealed footage demonstrates that officers never conducted the search they represented had occurred.  (TAC, ¶¶ 126-131.)

Plaintiffs further allege that Detectives Verdin and Tan recognized that Amelia’s death was a homicide but were instructed by superior officers to cease investigating the matter because LAPD did not want to assist the family in pursuing claims against the City.  Plaintiffs allege the detectives were retaliated against after attempting to investigate the matter further.  (TAC, ¶¶ 145-150.)

Plaintiffs also allege that body-worn camera footage captured statements by Sergeant Monzon and other officers demonstrating indifference to the search effort, including statements such as, “Well, we tried. A for effort,” and “Voila. No more contact.”  Plaintiffs allege these statements are indicative of LAPD’s failure to undertake a meaningful search and subsequent efforts to conceal that failure.  (TAC, ¶¶ 128-130.)

b.     Plaintiffs’ claims against City LA and Monzon are barred by public entity immunity.

City LA and Monzon demur to each of Plaintiffs’ claims against them in the TAC on grounds that they are barred by public entity immunity.

“Sovereign immunity is the rule in California.”  Sonoma Ag Art v. Department of Food & Agriculture (2004) 125 Cal.App.4th 122, 125, quoting Colome v. State Athletic Com. (1996) 47 Cal.App.4th 1444, 1454-1455.  “[T]he intent of the [Government Claims Act] is not to expand the rights of plaintiffs in suits against governmental entities, but to confine potential governmental liability to rigidly delineated circumstances: immunity is waived only if the various requirements of the Act are satisfied.”  Williams v. Horvath (1976) 16 Cal.3d 834, 838.  The Government Claims Act “permits private tort actions against government entities and employees where permitted by statute, but otherwise retains the general concept of immunity for government entities.”  DeJung v. Superior Court (2008) 169 Cal.App.4th 533, 543.  Thus, all public entity and public employee tort liability is dependent on the existence of an authorizing statute.  See Gov. Code, § 815 (a).

“A tort, whether intentional or negligent, involves a violation of a legal duty, imposed by statute, contract or otherwise, owed by the defendant to the person injured. Without such a duty, any injury is ‘damnum absque injuria’ -- injury without wrong.”  Nally v. Grace Community Church (1988) 47 Cal.3d 278, 292 (italics original; quotations omitted).  When alleging liability based upon a statute, this requires at a minimum, that the litigant “specifically allege the applicable statute or regulation” that was allegedly violated.  Lehto v. City of Oxnard (1985) 171 Cal.App.3d 285, 292.  “Duty cannot be alleged simply by stating ‘defendant had a duty under the law’; that is a conclusion of law, not an allegation of fact. The facts showing the existence of the claimed duty must be alleged.”  Searcy v. Hemet Unified School Dist. (1986) 177 Cal.App.3d 792, 802.

“Except as otherwise provided by statute, a public employee is not liable for an injury resulting from his act or omission where the act or omission was the result of the exercise of the discretion vested in him, whether or not such discretion be abused.”  Gov. Code, § 820.2.  “Except as otherwise provided by statute, a public employee is not liable for an injury caused by the act or omission of another person. Nothing in this section exonerates a public employee from liability for injury proximately caused by his own negligent or wrongful act or omission.”  Gov. Code, § 820.8.  “Except as otherwise provided by statute (including Section 820.2), a public employee is liable for injury caused by his act or omission to the same extent as a private person.”  Gov. Code, § 820 (a).

“Where a public entity is under a mandatory duty imposed by an enactment that is designed to protect against the risk of a particular kind of injury, the public entity is liable for an injury of that kind proximately caused by its failure to discharge the duty unless the public entity establishes that it exercised reasonable diligence to discharge the duty.”  Gov. Code, § 815.6.  “Enactment” as used in Government Code section 815.6 is a defined as a “constitutional provision, statute, charter provision, ordinance or regulation.”  Gov. Code, § 810.6.  “A statute is deemed to impose a mandatory duty on a public official only if the statute affirmatively imposes the duty and provides implementing guidelines. If a statute does not require that a particular action be taken, [] section 815.6 does not create the right to sue a public entity.”  O'Toole v. Superior Court (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 488, 510 (internal quotations and citations omitted).

Plaintiffs allege City LA and Monzon are liable under Government Code section 815.6 because they owed Plaintiffs and Amelia a mandatory duty under Penal Code section 14211 and the LAPD Policy Manual section 4/712.10.  Plaintiffs also cite Government Code sections 27491, 27491.5 and 27520.  (TAC, ¶ 368.)  Plaintiffs argue that those statutes’ use of the word “shall” conferred upon LAPD and City LA a mandatory duty to take steps to search for and rescue Amelia.

Government Code sections 27491, 27491.5, and 27520 pertain to the conduct of a coroner’s office.  Here, the coroner’s office at issue is LACMEC, which is part of the County.  The County is no longer a defendant in this case.  These sections of the Government Code do not support imposing liability against City LA and Monzon.

As was also the case in its ruling on City LA and Monzon’s earlier motion for judgment on the pleadings, the Court disagrees with Plaintiffs’ broad interpretation of Penal Code section 14211 as conferring on LAPD a mandatory duty to take any of the specific actions that Plaintiffs complain LAPD failed to take in this case.  The statute requires police to accept reports of missing persons; advise persons making such reports of the name and telephone number of the police or sheriff’s department with jurisdiction of the missing person’s residence; “make an assessment of reasonable steps to be taken to locate the person by using...report forms, checklists, and guidelines”; “broadcast a ‘Be On the Lookout’ bulletin”; and “within two hours after the receipt of the report, electronically transmit the report to the Department of Justice.”  Pen. Code, § 14211 (a)-(e).

Similarly, LAPD Policy Manual section 4/712.10 states that an “employee … shall complete a Missing/Found Persons Investigation, Form 03.16.00, without delay and immediately notify and forward a copy of the report to the outside agency having jurisdiction over the person’s residence address and, if applicable, the jurisdiction where the missing person was last seen.”

Penal Code section 14211 and the LAPD Policy Manual do not impose on LAPD a mandatory duty to conduct a warrantless search and rescue of the residence where an “at risk” person is believed to be held.  Nor do those sections impose on LAPD a mandatory duty to open closed doors or look in particular places when searching for an “at risk” person inside a residence.  Rather, those sections require that LAPD accept and prioritize missing persons reports, as well as broadcast a “Be On the Lookout” bulletin.  Here, Plaintiffs do not complain that Amelia died because LAPD failed to accept a report or broadcast a bulletin.  Rather, Plaintiffs complain Amelia died because LAPD failed to sufficiently search the property at Plaintiffs’ request.  See Shelton v. City of Westminster (1982) 138 Cal.App.3d 610, 623 (Shelton) (under Gov. Code, § 815.6, “liability is only for the particular harm caused by the failure to perform the mandatory duty”).  This is insufficient to show a violation of Plaintiffs’ cited statutes.

The TAC includes additional allegations that LAPD concealed body-worn camera footage and other evidence concerning the July 25, 2023 search for more than two (2) years, including from other investigators and prosecutors.  Plaintiffs allege the body-worn camera footage captured statements by Monzon and other officers demonstrating indifference to the search effort, including statements such as, “Well, we tried. A for effort,” and “Voila. No more contact.”  Plaintiffs allege these statements are indicative of LAPD’s failure to undertake a meaningful search and subsequent efforts to conceal that failure.  (TAC, ¶¶ 126-131.)

City LA disagrees with Plaintiffs that their interpretation of LAPD’s efforts as insufficient to meet their demands and as demonstrating indifference to Amelia’s wellbeing support imposing liability against a public entity.  City LA’s departments and employees have immunity for their decision as to whether to respond to a particular scene, what type of investigation, if any, to conduct, and the manner in which to conduct any investigation.  See Gov. Code, §§ 820.2, 845.  Decisions that require “comparisons, choices, judgments, and evaluations, comprises the very essence of the exercise of ‘discretion’ … are immunized under section 820.2.”  Thompson v. County of Alameda (1980) 27 Cal.3d 741, 749 (Thompson).  The scope of any LAPD search at the property and subsequent choices and whether and how to continue investigating Amelia’s death are plainly entitled to public entity immunity under Government Code section 820.2.

Plaintiffs complain that Monzon promised Plaintiffs LAPD would search the property, but “[a]ll they did was walk up and down two exterior outdoor walkway.”  (TAC, ¶ 24.)  Again, the decision as to whether and how to enter the property and investigate certain rooms is necessarily one over which police officers exercise a substantial amount of discretion.  There is no specific right or obligation (statutory or otherwise) that requires police officers to conduct a specific type of search or to conduct a search with a certain degree of thoroughness.  See Thompson, supra, 27 Cal.3d at p. 749; see also Conway v. County of Tuolumne (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 1005, 1014 (“California’s common law has long provided personal immunity from lawsuits challenging a governmental official’s discretionary acts within the scope of his or her authority”).

LAPD is not liable for failing to open closed doors at the residence where there is no specific statutory obligation that LAPD conduct a specific type of search.  This is especially the case where the dispatched officers appear to have had reasonable grounds for believing that opening a closed door would have violated residents’ Fourth Amendment rights.  See People v. Celis (2004) 33 Cal.4th 667, 676-677 (Fourth Amendment proscribes warrantless searches unless certain conditions are satisfied).

In sum, Plaintiffs are incorrect that Penal Code section 14211 via Government Code section 815.6 imposed on LAPD a mandatory duty to conduct a full search of the house unbounded by the constitutional limits of the Fourth Amendment as to the home’s property owners and residents.  Plaintiffs are similarly incorrect that those sections imposed on LAPD a mandatory duty to rescue and to otherwise prevent harm to Amelia.  In short, Penal Code section 14211 does not impose the mandatory duties that Plaintiffs have read into that section.  As such, Plaintiffs cannot seek liability against LAPD for violations of Penal Code section 14211 and/or Government Code section 815.6.

Plaintiffs’ claims against City LA and Monzon are barred by public entity immunity.

c.      Plaintiffs’ allegations fail to show a legal duty or breach thereof by City LA and/or Monzon.

Plaintiffs argue LAPD may be liable for their failures to locate Amelia before her death under a “special relationship” theory of liability.

Generally, a person who has not created a peril has no duty to come to the aid of another unless a “special relationship” exists.  “[W]hen the state, through its agents, voluntarily assumes a protective duty toward a certain member of the public and undertakes action on behalf of that member, thereby inducing reliance, it is held to the same standard of care as a private person or organization.”  Williams v. State of California (1983) 34 Cal.3d 18, 24.  The necessary elements for a special relationship are: (1) that the officer took an affirmative action which contributed to, increased, or changed a risk which would have otherwise existed; or (2) that the officer voluntarily assumed responsibility to protect the individual; or (3) that the individual detrimentally relied on the conduct of the officer in statements made by him which induced a false sense of security, thereby worsening the individual’s position.  Id. at pp. 27–28.

Plaintiffs argue the special relationship in this case is analogous to the special relationship in Arista v. County of Riverside (2018) 29 Cal.App.5th 1051 (Arista).  In Arista, a plaintiff’s husband was late returning from a bike ride in the mountains where he had suffered injuries after a fall.  After the plaintiff called 911, police officers dispatched to her home, collected information, and told her they would handle the situation while she waited.  The plaintiff relied on the officers’ statements and waited.  However, the officers did not go out to look for the husband until the next morning, despite predictions of freezing cold overnight.  The husband died of hypothermia where he might have otherwise been found and saved if the officers had searched earlier.  Arista, at pp. 1055-1056.

The Arista Court held the requisite special relationship was established because the deputies, “through their actions, undertook the responsibility of rescuing the victim because the deputies were actively involved in all aspects of locating the victim, and by appointing an Incident Commander, the deputies signaled that they were taking control of the rescue. Therefore, the deputies had the duty to exercise due care in performing the rescue, which means (a) using reasonable care not to increase the risk of harm, and (b) following through in a reasonable manner after inducing reliance on the rescue.”  Arista, supra, 29 Cal.App.5th at p. 1061.  The Arista Court held the plaintiff had “sufficiently pled that the deputies undertook rescue efforts and induced reliance on those efforts.”  Id. at p. 1062.

This case is distinguishable from Arista in that Plaintiffs in this case have not alleged a deferred undertaking by police that contributed to Amelia’s death.  Rather, Plaintiffs concede LAPD did conduct a search of the property.  Unlike in Arista where the officers promised to undertake a search and failed to do so, LAPD’s alleged failure to act in this case centers on the officers’ decision not to open closed door while conducting a search.  Arista does not stand for the position that police officers may incur liability when they take affirmative acts to rescue a person by conducting a search but that search is not thorough enough to locate the missing person.  Nor does Arista stand for the position that a police department may be civilly liable for failing to search every room of a property when such failure was the result of their reasonable calculation that opening a closed door might violate the residents’ Fourth Amendment right against warrantless searches.

Where there is no special relationship imposing a duty of care on a responding officer, the responding officer generally does not have a duty to protect a citizen against harms by third parties.  See Brown v. USA Taekwondo (2021) 11 Cal.5th 204, 214; see also Shelton, supra, 138 Cal.App.3d at p. 613 (public policy warrants against imposing liability on allegedly negligent police investigation of missing person based on police department’s representation that the “missing person report would be fully and completely investigated”).  Sovereign immunity is the rule unless there is a statute authorizing the cause of action.  See Sonoma Ag Art v. Department of Food & Agriculture (2004) 125 Cal.App.4th 122, 125.  Because there was no statutory or “special relationship” duty owed by LAPD to Plaintiffs, sovereign immunity defeats all of Plaintiffs’ alleged claims against City LA and/or Monzon.

The demurrer is sustained as to Ali’s claims against City LA and Monzon.

d.     The demurrer will be sustained, without leave to amend.  The motion to strike will be denied as moot.

Plaintiffs seek leave to further amend their pleading to state a cause of action against City LA and Monzon.  Plaintiffs argue City LA and Monzon’s argument “rests entirely on [their] disputed reading of the [Body Worn Videos], which the Court cannot weigh on demurrer.”  (Opposition, p. 15:13-15.)

The Court disagrees with Plaintiffs that sustaining City LA and Monzon’s demurrer requires evaluating the Body Worn Videos.  Rather, the substance of Plaintiffs’ allegations as laid out in the TAC (and without any reliance on the body cam footage) reveals that Plaintiffs’ claims arise from allegations that City LA and Monzon undertook certain discretionary acts in a manner that Plaintiffs found insufficient and as demonstrating indifference to Amelia’s wellbeing.  Because City LA and Monzon had no statutory obligation to conduct a specific type of search, Plaintiffs’ claims as alleged in the TAC fail as a matter of law.  This is true regardless of what is on the body cam videos.

As to whether to grant Plaintiffs’ additional leave to amend on other grounds, the TAC marks Plaintiffs’ fourth attempt at pleading.  The Court already gave Plaintiffs leave to correct the defects identified herein when it ruled on City LA and Monzon’s earlier motion for judgment on the pleadings.  While the TAC includes additional specificity and supplemental allegations that were missing from the SAC, none of the additional allegations cures the defects identified in the Court’s earlier ruling.  Under these circumstances, further amendment would be futile.  The demurrer will be sustained, without leave to amend.  Obviously, the allegations tell the story of a tragic and horrific series of events.  However, the law does not hold City LA and Monzon liable for those events.    

Because the Court has determined that each of Plaintiffs’ claims against City LA and Monzon fails as a matter of law based on public entity immunity, the Court need not evaluate City LA and Monzon’s other related arguments, e.g., the sufficiency of Plaintiffs’ concealment allegations and the sufficiency of Ali’s tort claim for damages.

Because the Court has determined that Plaintiffs have no claim against City LA and Monzon as a matter of law, the motion to strike will be denied as moot.

V.              CONCLUSION

Defendants City of Los Angeles and Jose Monzon’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ Third Amended Complaint is SUSTAINED, without leave to amend.

 

Defendants City of Los Angeles and Jose Monzon are ORDERED to give notice.

I.                BACKGROUND

            This case is a lemon law action filed by Plaintiff Jorge A. Antuna Garcia against Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.

            On May 5, 2025, Plaintiff filed his complaint.

            On June 12, 2025, Defendant filed its answer to the complaint.

            On June 24, 2025, Plaintiff first sought to meet and confer with Defendant regarding taking the deposition of Defendant’s person most qualified (“PMQ”).  Defendant did not immediately respond.  (Alemi decl., ¶ 3.)

            On September 8, 2025, Plaintiff noticed the deposition of Defendant’s PMQ, with deposition set for October 8, 2025.  (Alemi decl., ¶ 4.)

On October 1, 2025, Defendant served objections to the deposition notice.  The parties subsequently met and conferred to find a mutually agreeable date for the deposition to take place.  The parties agreed on June 4, 2026, as the deposition date despite Plaintiff’s concerns that the selected date was just several months before trial.  (Alemi decl., ¶¶ 5-6.)

On January 7, 2026, Plaintiff served an amended notice of deposition of Defendant’s PMQ, with deposition set for June 4, 2026.  (Alemi decl., ¶ 7.)
               On May 28, 2026, Plaintiff received Defendant’s objections to the amended deposition notice, which stated that Defendant would instead be producing its PMQ for deposition on June 5, 2026.  (Alemi decl., ¶ 8.)

On May 29, 2026, Defendant informed Plaintiff that it would not be producing its PMQ for deposition on June 5, 2026, and instead offered August 24, 2026, a date just one (1) month before trial.  (Alemi decl., ¶ 9.)

On June 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion to compel the deposition of Defendant’s PMQ.

On June 10, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s ex parte application for an order shortening time to hear the motion.

On June 16, 2026, Defendant filed its opposition brief.

On June 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed his reply brief.

II.             LEGAL STANDARD

Where a would-be deponent fails to appear, proceed with, or produce documents for deposition in a duly noticed deposition notice, the noticing party may move for an order to compel said deponent’s attendance, testimony, and/or production for inspection of any document described in the deposition notice.¿ Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450 (a).

III.           DISCUSSION

            Plaintiff moves to compel the deposition of Defendant’s PMQ.

Plaintiff first noticed the deposition of Defendant’s PMQ on September 8, 2025, nearly ten (10) months ago.  After Plaintiff served an amended deposition notice on January 7, 2026, the parties agreed the deposition should take place on June 4, 2026.  The June 4, 2026 date was agreed on despite Plaintiff’s concerns that said date was only several months before the parties’ FSC and trial dates.  Defendant later requested that it present its PMQ for deposition on June 5, 2026, rather than on June 4, 2026.  Defendant then requested that it present its PMQ for deposition on August 24, 2026, rather than on June 5, 2026.

Under these circumstances, Plaintiff has sufficiently attempted to meet and confer with Defendant in good faith.  Given that trial in this case is set for September 28, 2026, Defendant is technically correct that a deposition conducted on August 24, 2026, would be timely under the 30-day discovery cutoff.  See Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.020 (discovery shall be completed 30 days before trial).  Even so, the Court agrees with Plaintiff that Defendant’s repeated rescheduling and months-long delays in presenting a PMQ for deposition have been unreasonable.  Given the timeline of this case and Plaintiff’s good faith efforts to depose the PMQ at an earlier date, Plaintiff should be able to take the PMQ’s deposition earlier than Defendant’s selected date of August 24, 2026.

The motion is granted.  Defendant is ordered to produce its PMQ for deposition no later than Friday, July 31, 2026, or on another date mutually agreeable to both sides.

As to the substance of Defendant’s objections to the deposition notice, the parties’ primary disagreement appears to be about scheduling the deposition rather than about the objections themselves.  For now, it is sufficient that the Court order the deposition to proceed.  If Plaintiff takes issue with specific objections asserted during the deposition, such issues will need to be addressed by later motion.  The Court strongly encourages both sides to engage in good faith during the deposition to avoid future motion practice. 

As to whether to award monetary sanctions, the Court recognizes the considerable constraints likely placed on Defendant’s PMQ given the volume of lemon law cases proceeding in California trial courts.  Ultimately, Defendant did offer Plaintiff a deposition date within the discovery cut-off.  Under these circumstances, Defendant cannot be said to have misused the discovery process, and, thus, the Court does not award of sanctions against Defendant at this time.

IV.           CONCLUSION

Plaintiff Jorge A. Antuna Garcia’s motion to compel the deposition of Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.’s person most qualified is GRANTED.

 

Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. is ORDERED to produce its person most qualified for deposition no later than Friday, July 31, 2026, or on another date mutually agreeable to both sides.

 

No sanctions will issue at this time.

 

Plaintiff Jorge A. Antuna Garcia is ORDERED to give notice.

I.                BACKGROUND

This case is a lemon law action filed by Plaintiff Armen Khanyan against Defendant Hyundai Motor America.

On January 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed his complaint against Defendant, alleging breaches of warranties in violation of the Song-Beverly Act.

On February 27, 2026, Defendant filed its motion to compel arbitration.

On June 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed his opposition.

On June 23, 2026, Defendant filed its reply.

II.             LEGAL STANDARD

Parties may be compelled to arbitrate a dispute upon a finding that: (1) there was a valid agreement to arbitrate between the parties; and (2) the agreement covers the controversy or controversies in the parties’ dispute.  Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961 (Omar).

III.           REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Defendant requests that the Court take judicial notice of the complaint.  The Court takes judicial notice as requested.

IV.           DISCUSSION

Defendant moves to compel arbitration on the ground that there exists an agreement to arbitrate in the Owner’s Handbook & Warranty Information booklet that accompanied Plaintiff’s vehicle (the “Warranty Booklet”). 

Defendant argues Plaintiff is bound to arbitrate his dispute with Defendant because the Warranty Booklet states that the vehicle’s warranty is subject to the terms of a binding arbitration provision:

“IF YOU PURCHASED OR LEASED YOUR VEHICLE IN CALIFORNIA, YOUR WARRANTY IS MADE SUBJECT TO THE TERMS OF THIS BINDING ARBITRATION PROVISION. BY USING THE VEHICLE, OR REQUESTING OR ACCEPTING BENEFITS UNDER THIS WARRANTY, INCLUDING HAVING ANY REPAIRS PERFORMED UNDER WARRANTY, YOU AGREE TO BE BOUND BY THESE TERMS. IF YOU DO NOT AGREE WITH THESE TERMS, PLEASE CONTACT US AT [email protected] WITHIN THIRTY (30) DAYS OF YOUR PURCHASE OR LEASE TO OPT-OUT OF THIS ARBITRATION PROVISION.” 

(Ameripour decl., Exh. 2, p. 14.) 

Defendant argues Plaintiff should be estopped from asserting that the absence of his signature on a written contract precludes its enforcement, citing Boucher v. Alliance Title Co., Inc. (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 262, 269.  However, this principle only applies where there is an enforceable contract that is defective because one of the parties failed to sign.  Here, Defendant presents insufficient evidence that the Subject Vehicle’s warranty constitutes an enforceable contract under basic principles of contract formation. 

Plaintiff attests that: (1) he never noticed the Warranty Booklet inside a package in his vehicle’s glove compartment, which also included pamphlets and other booklets, until months after purchase; (2) he was never told such documents were located there, nor that he was required to opt-out of any post-sale arbitration agreement contained in the Warranty Booklet; (3) prior to Defendant filing the instant motion, he was unaware that pages 12-14 of the Warranty Booklet (which is approximately 48 pages long) included a post-sale arbitration agreement.  The Warranty Booklet is unsigned by the parties and does not include a signature line.  (Khanyan decl., ¶¶ 3-12; Ameripour decl., Exh. 2.)

Defendant fails to show that the arbitration provision contained in the Warranty Booklet constituted an offer to which Plaintiff assented.  Thus, the statements included in the Warranty Booklet do not amount to an enforceable contract.  See Leslie v. Brown Bros. Inc. (1929) 208 Cal. 606, 621 (offer cannot be turned into agreement merely because the person to whom it is made makes no reply, even where offer states that silence will be taken as consent); see Norcia v. Samsung Telecommunications America, LLC (9th Cir. 2017) 845 F.3d 1279, 1291 (offeree, regardless of apparent manifestation of his consent, is not bound by inconspicuous contractual provisions of which he was unaware, contained in a document whose contractual nature is not obvious); see Golden Eagle Ins. Co. v. Foremost Ins. Co. (1993) 20 Cal.App.4th 1372, 1385 (silence or inaction do not constitute acceptance).

Defendant argues the Court should find otherwise, relying on the case Sanchez v. Valencia Holding Co., LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 899 (Sanchez).  In Sanchez, the Supreme Court stated that a defendant “was under no obligation to highlight the arbitration clause of its contract, nor was it required to specifically call that clause to [the plaintiff’s] attention.”  Id. at 914.  Critically, however, the Supreme Court found that the subject arbitration provision was included within the text of an enforceable contract.  Id. at p. 907.  The issue of whether the defendant had an obligation to highlight the contract’s arbitration provision did not come up until the unconscionability stage of the Supreme Court’s analysis.  Id. at pp. 910-915.

This case is readily distinguishable from Sanchez because the Court has already determined that the Warranty Booklet containing the language regarding arbitration is not an enforceable contract under basic principles of contract formation.  Because there is no contract, there is no need to consider whether the contract is unconscionable.  Thus, it is irrelevant whether Defendant called Plaintiff’s attention to the existence of the arbitration provision, as the provision is not included in an enforceable contract.

In sum, the text of the Warranty Booklet does not constitute an enforceable contract under principles of estoppel or foundational theories of contract formation.  Because Defendant is without evidence that the statements regarding arbitration in the Warranty Booklet amount to a contract, those statements are not grounds on which the Court will order Plaintiff to arbitrate his dispute with Defendant.  See Omar, supra, Cal.App.4th at p. 961. L.A. Pacific Ctr., Inc. (2006) 144 Cal. App.4th 754, 758); see also Norcia v. Samsung Telecommunications America, LLC (9th Cir. 2017) 845 F.3d 1279 (“[l]anguage in a written warranty agreement is [only] ‘contractual’ in

the sense that it creates binding, legal obligations on the seller, but a warranty does not impose binding obligations on the buyer.”)

Defendant presents no alternative theory for compelling arbitration.  The motion is denied.

V.              CONCLUSION

Defendant Hyundai Motor America’s motion to compel Plaintiff Armen Khanyan to arbitrate his complaint against Defendant Hyundai Motor America is DENIED.

 

Plaintiff Armen Khanyan is ORDERED to give notice.
