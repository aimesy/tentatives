 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Attorney’s Fees Upon Expungement of Lis Pendens – A party to an action who asserts a real property claim may record a notice of pendency of action (lis pendens) in which that real property claim is alleged, to be recorded in the office of the recorder of each county in which all or part of the real property is situated. (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.20.) At any time after the lis pendens has been recorded, any party, or nonparty with an interest in the real property affected, may apply to the court in which the action is pending to expunge the notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.30.) However, any non-party moving to expunge the lis pendens shall obtain leave to intervene from the court at or before the time the party brings the motion to expunge the notice. (Id.)

With regard to attorney's fees in connection with a motion to expunge, section 405.38 provides:

The court shall direct that the party prevailing on any motion under this chapter be awarded the reasonable attorney's fees and costs of making or opposing the motion unless the court finds that the other party acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of attorney's fees and costs unjust.

(Code Civ., Proc., § 405.38.)

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Tang moves to expunge the lis pendens on the Property pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 405.31, 405.32, 128, subdivision (a), and for an award of attorney’s fees and costs as prevailing party in the amount of $4,323.70 incurred in bringing the present motion pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 405.38. (Notice of Motion, p. 2:15-3:7.)

 

 

As a preliminary matter, Tang’s motion to expunge the lis pendens is moot. On July 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed a notice of filing of recorded withdrawal of the lis pendens on the Property, attached to which is a copy of the withdrawal, recorded on July 24, 2026. (Plaintiff’s Notice of Filing, Ex. 1.) The recording of a withdrawal of a lis pendens by the party who recorded the lis pendens in the office of the recorder in which the lis pendens was recorded sufficiently withdraws and extinguishes the lis pendens. (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.50; Federal Deposit Ins. Corp. v. Charlton (1993) 17 Cal.App.4th 1066, 1069.) The parties do not dispute the mootness of Tang’s motion for expungement. (See Opp., p. 2:9-13; Reply, p. 9:12-13.)

 

Therefore, Tang’s motion is DENIED as moot. However, despite the filing of the withdrawal, the Court may still rule on Tang’s request for attorney’s fees as prevailing party in bringing the motion to expunge.

 

 

 

As to the question of sanctions, the trial court has the discretion to consider whether, “in light of all the circumstances, the imposition of fees would otherwise be unjust.” (Castro v. Superior Court (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 1010, 1024-25.) Castro, supra, 116 Cal.App.4th at 1023.)

 

Plaintiff argues that he acted with substantial justification in recording the lis pendens and that his prompt, voluntary withdrawal delivered Tang relief on a faster timeline than the motion itself could have achieved, rendering the imposition of fees unjust. (Opp., p. 3:2-8.) Plaintiff asserts he had a reasonable basis in law and fact for recording the lis pendens, and his decision to withdraw the notice voluntarily to spare the parties and court the expense of litigating the motion does not convert a justified recording into a sanctionable act. (Opp., p. 6:12-18.)

 

The Court also considers the conduct of both sides throughout the course of this litigation. The Court exercises its discretion to deny the request for attorneys’ fees. The Court will award $88.70 in filing costs

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Meet and Confer – A motion to compel further responses must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration showing “a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2016.040, 2030.300, subd. (b)(1), 2031.310, subd. (b)(2).)

 

Defense counsel has set forth sufficient information that meet and confer efforts occurred to informally resolve the discovery disputes with Defense counsel, but that no resolution was reached. (Lopez Decl., ¶¶ 7-12.) The Court finds this requirement satisfied.

 

Timeliness of Plaintiff’s Reply  – All reply papers are due five court days before the hearing on the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1005, subd. (b).) Due to the Court holiday on Monday, September 7, 2026, Plaintiff’s reply was due on or before August 31, 2026. Therefore, Plaintiff’s reply is untimely.

 

However, in the absence of any prejudice to Defendant, and in the interests of adjudicating the issues raised on the merits, the Court will exercise its discretion and consider the reply on the merits.

 

Issues Raised in Defendant’s Opposition – The Court notes a number of procedural issues raised by Defendant in opposition to the motion, including Plaintiff’s failure to set an IDC prior to filing the motions to compel, singular filing for multiple discovery requests, and failure to define at-issue terms. The Court notes that it does not require the parties to set an IDC before filing a motion to compel, or at any time for that matter (Defendant is referring to the procedures of the judicial officer previously in this Department). Aside from that, Defendant is correct that Plaintiff’s motion fails to comply with a number of procedural specifications. However, the Court does not find that these procedural deficiencies are prejudicial or that they warrant outright denial of the motion.

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Motions to Compel Further Discovery Responses - Interrogatories – In responding to a set of interrogatories, a party must provide one of the following: (1) an answer “containing the information sought to be discovered”; (2) “an exercise of the party’s option to produce writings”; or (3) an objection to the particular interrogatory. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.210, subd. (a).) Each answer to an interrogatory “shall be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220, subd. (a).) If any objections are made, “the specific ground for the objection shall be set forth clearly in the response.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.240, subd. (b).) 

Upon receipt of responses to interrogatories, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if they deem the responses to be “evasive or incomplete” or if “[a]n objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a)(1), (a)(3).) A motion to compel further responses to interrogatories must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration showing “a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2016.040, 2030.300, subd. (b)(1).) The motion must also be accompanied by a separate statement containing the text of the interrogatories at issue, the text of each response at issue, and a statement of the reasons why a further response is required, though the court may permit a party to submit “a concise outline of the discovery request and each response in dispute” in lieu of a separate statement. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (b)(2); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345.)  

A motion to compel further response lies where the party to whom the interrogatories were directed gave responses deemed improper by the propounding party; e.g., objections, or evasive or incomplete answers. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300.) Unlike a motion to compel further responses to a request for production, there is no “good cause” requirement applicable to a motion to compel further responses to interrogatories. “The ruling usually is based on consideration of the following factors:  the relationship of the information sought to the issues framed in the pleadings; the likelihood that disclosure will be of practical benefit to the party seeking discovery; the burden or expense likely to be encountered by the responding party in furnishing the information sought.” (Weil, et al., Cal. Prac. Guide, Civil Procedure Before Trial, Ch. 8F-7, ¶ 8:1181; see also Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc v. Superior Court (1968) 263 Cal.App.2d 12, 18–19 [“[I]n deciding a motion under [former] section 2030 the trial court must, of necessity, consider not only the stated objections to the interrogatories, but also the interrogatories themselves, as well as the pleadings, and the contentions of the interrogating party as to the purpose and validity of the interrogatories. If the interrogatories stray too far and seek information which cannot reasonably serve the acknowledged purposes of pretrial discovery, the motion should be denied.”].) If a timely motion to compel has been filed, the burden is on the responding party to justify any objection or failure to fully answer. (Coy v. Superior Court of Contra Costa County (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220–221.) 

If a party fails to serve a timely response to interrogatories, the party waives the right to exercise the option to produce writings under Section 2030.230 and waives any objection to the demand, including objections based on privilege or work product. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).) The Court may relieve a party of this waiver only if the party has subsequently served a substantially code-compliant response and the party’s failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a)(1)-(2).)

Standard for Motions to Compel Further Discovery Responses – Requests for Production of Documents – Motions to compel further responses to requests for production must set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the request. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) To establish good cause, a discovery proponent must identify a disputed fact that is of consequence in the action and explain how the discovery sought will tend in reason to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact. (Digital Music News LLC v. Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 216, 224 (disapproved on other grounds by Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531).)

A demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to RPDs if (1) a statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete, (2) a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive, or (3) an objection in the response is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).) “A representation of inability to comply with the particular demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand. This statement shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of the responding party. The statement shall set forth the name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.230.)

If a party fails to serve a timely response to a request for production, the party waives any objection to the demand, including objections based on privilege or work product. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).) The Court may relieve a party of this waiver only if the party has subsequently served a substantially code-compliant response and the party’s failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a)(1)-(2).)

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Plaintiff moves to compel Defendant’s further responses to Special Interrogatories, Set One and Requests for Production of Documents, Set One, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 2030.220, subdivisions (a)-(c) and 2030.300, et seq. on the grounds that Defendant was properly served discovery requests on October 21, 2025 and has failed to provide full and code-compliant answers responsive to the request and has asserted meritless objections. (Notice of Motion, p. 2:12-24.)

 

The relevant factual background is set forth by Plaintiff as follows:

 

Plaintiff served the  discovery requests on Defendant on October 21, 2025. (Lopez Decl., ¶¶ 3-4, Ex. A, B.) On November 18, 2025, Defendant served its responses. (Lopez Decl., ¶¶ 5-6, Ex. C, D.) On December 11, 2025, Plaintiff’s counsel emailed Defense counsel to meet and confer regarding the responses. (Lopez Decl., ¶ 7, Ex. E.) On December 17 and 18, 2025, Defense counsel requested, and Plaintiff’s counsel granted, a two week mutual extension for further responses and for Plaintiff to bring a motion to compel. (Lopez Decl., ¶ 8, Ex. F.) On January 2, 2026, counsel granted another mutual two week extension. (Lopez Decl., ¶ 9, Ex. G.) On January 30, 2026, counsel granted another mutual extension, making the due date for further responses February 6, 2026 and a motion to compel February 20, 2026. (Lopez Decl., ¶ 10, Ex. H.) On February 6, 2026, Defendant served further responses to both requests and a meet and confer letter explaining why further responses were not provided for some requests. (Lopez Decl., ¶ 11, Ex. I.) Plaintiff’s counsel sent correspondence regarding the missing responses on February 10, 2026 and requested an IDC but received no response. (Lopez Decl., ¶ 12, Ex. J.) Plaintiff thereafter filed the present motion to compel further responses to the Special Interrogatories and Requests for Production.

 

a.     Special Interrogatories, Set One

 

At issue are Defendant’s supplemental responses to Interrogatory Nos. 10-12, 14-17, 32, 34, 45, and 64. (Plaintiff’s Sep. Statement.) These interrogatories include six requests seeking for information dating back 10 years  before the subject incident, including the dates and times of any and all inspections and maintenance done on the Property and the name, address, and contact information for any individual who inspected the Property within the past 10 years, two requests seeking a description of the Property’s and floor’s “condition,” and one requesting any and all falling incidents in which Defendant was involved prior to the incident without limitation.

 

Defendant objected to these requests, in part, on the grounds that they are overly broad, burdensome, oppressive, and not properly limited in time or scope. Defendant also objected on the grounds that certain requests were duplicative, and on the grounds that the undefined term “condition” was vague, ambiguous, unintelligible, and calls for speculation. Plaintiff argues these objections are evasive and meritless.

 

In opposition, Defendant asserts that the present motion is moot because it provided Plaintiff with amended responses to all discovery requests on August 24, 2026 based on the fact that Daniel Food Enterprises, Inc. is the proper defendant that should have been served the requests, as Daniel controlled, managed, and operated the Property at the time of the incident. (Opp., p. 4:19-25, 7:15-20.)

 

On reply, Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s supplemental responses are not code-compliant because Defendant failed to provide any evidence supporting its burden objection and asserts meritless vagueness objections. (Reply, p. 4:19-26.)

 

The Court notes that the present motions are not moot.  Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390 expressly authorizes the Court to hear a motion to compel initial responses even where a party filed untimely responses after the motion to compel was filed but before the hearing. The Sinaiko Court reasoned that to accept that a court has no discretion to hear and rule on a motion to compel initial responses after untimely responses are provided “would remove an important incentive for parties to respond to discovery in a timely fashion” and “a party to whom interrogatories were directed could wait until the hearing on a section 2030.290 motion was imminent, then serve a set of evasive and incomplete responses, and thereby unilaterally deprive the trial court of authority to hear the motion.” (Sinaiko, supra, 148 Cal.App.4th at 408.) Therefore, “once a party has failed to serve timely [discovery] responses, the trial court has the authority to hear a propounding party's motion to compel responses… regardless of whether a party serves an untimely response.” (Ibid.)

 

The County of San Benito v. Superior Court (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 243, 256 echoed Sinaiko’s reasoning in holding the same as to motions to compel further responses, stating that to adopt such an “interpretation of the discovery act—that a party opposing a motion to compel further responses may unilaterally divest the court of authority to rule on the motion by serving a further response in the interim—would permit a party faced with a motion to compel to delay its adjudication indefinitely by successive supplemental but inadequate responses, each prompting a further motion to compel, each of which in turn would be defeated by piecemeal supplementation.”

 

However, the Court also finds that Defendant’s objections to the interrogatories at issuehave merit.. Many of Plaintiff’s interrogatories request detailed information, including the date and time of all inspections and maintenance of the entire premises, and the name, address, and telephone number of each person who performed maintenance or inspection, in the ten years preceding the subject incident. Many of the interrogatories propounded are unreasonable in temporal scope and are facially burdensome. The interrogatories also request all falling incidents involving Defendant with no limitation on date, time, or location. While Plaintiff argues that 10 years of records of inspections and maintenance are necessary to establish a pattern of injury or substandard inspection of the Property, the Court disagrees that 10 years of detailed information regarding, in part, inspections that take place every 30 minutes, is necessary for  Plaintiff to prove her case or even tangentially relevant.

 

The Court does agree, however, that Defendant’s supplemental responses, aside from its otherwise meritorious objections, on their own are deficient and not code compliant. In responding to interrogatories, a party’s answer must provide one of the following: (1) an answer “containing the information sought to be discovered”; (2) “an exercise of the party’s option to produce writings”; or (3) an objection to the particular interrogatory. (Code Civ. Proc.,  § 2030.210, subd. (a).) Each answer to an interrogatory “shall be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220, subd. (a).) 

 

Here, Defendant’s supplemental responses restate its objections, with the following included language: “Vallarta Food Enterprises, Inc., did not control, manage, or operate the subject Vallarta Supermarkets located at 440 East Palmdale Blvd., Palmdale, California 93550. Defendant, Daniel Food Enterprises, Inc., is the correct legal entity that controlled, managed, and operated the subject Vallarta Supermarkets located at 440 East Palmdale Blvd., Palmdale, California 93550, and all responsive information will be in Daniel Food Enterprises, Inc.’s possession, custody, or control.” (See Stone Decl., Ex. E.) The Court notes that this statement, though it may well be true, does not comply with the requirements for code-compliance responses to interrogatories. The Court also finds that Defendant’s objections as to vagueness for a number of terms unmeritorious, including as to the words “inspection,” “maintenance,” and “condition” in context of the underlying action and the interrogatories propounded, and if appropriately limited. That said, because the Court finds Defendant’s objections to broadness and scope of most of the interrogatories warranted, it does not find that Defendant’s responses as they stand are evasive or non-code compliant.

 

Plaintiff’s request is GRANTED in part. Interrogatory Nos. , 12,  16, and 17 shall be limited in time for a period of three months preceding the subject incident, limited to the subject floor at issue, and to include within “inspections” safety checks, maintenance reviews, walkthroughs, or condition assessments. Interrogatory No. 64 shall be limited in time for a period of three years preceding the subject incident and limited to slip and fall accidents at the store at issue. To the extent any other Special Interrogatory is not expressly addressed, the motion is DENIED

 IV.          Legal Standard

Standard for Demurrer – A party against whom a complaint has been filed may object by demurrer to the pleading on the ground that the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations reasonably, liberally, and in context. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) Courts must “treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at 318.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 126.) The threshold for meeting this burden is low, and “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

 

Standard for Motion to Strike – A motion to strike lies where either (1) there is “irrelevant, false or improper matter inserted in any pleading,” or (2) where a pleading or part thereof is “not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule or order of court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The Court has the authority to strike out any part of a pleading not filed in conformity with an order of the court. (See Ibid; Ferraro v. Camarlinghi (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 509, 528.) 

When the pleadings are defective, “the defect may be raised by demurrer or motion to strike, or by motion for judgment on the pleadings.” (Coyne v. Krempels (1950) 36 Cal.2d 257, 262.) The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or form any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (a).) A matter to be judicially noticed shall be specified in the notice of motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (b).) The court then may strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading and strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend. (Perlman v. Municipal Court (1979) 99 Cal.App.3d 568, 575.)

    V.          Discussion

 

Application – Red Lion demurs to each cause of action asserted against it in the FAC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e) on the grounds that the FAC fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Demurrer, p. 5:2-25.) Red Lion also moves to strike paragraphs 8-9, 80-81, 83-86, 94-98, and Prayer for Relief ¶ 3 from the FAC on the grounds that they are improper and not drawn in conformity with the laws of this state. (Motion, p. 5:2-6:27.) The causes of action asserted against Red Lion are: (1) First Cause of Action – Negligence, (2) Third Cause of Action – Premises Liability, (3) Fourth Cause of Action – Survival, (4) Fifth Cause of Action – Wrongful Death.

 

a.     Red Lion’s Demurrer

 

Red Lion argues none of Plaintiffs’ causes of action can withstand demurrer as Red Lion had no duty to Decedents, Plaintiffs fail to plead which acts and omissions alleged in the FAC are attributable to Red Lion, and the FAC is devoid of factual allegations to support the existence of a causal connection between Red Lion’s conduct and the Decedents’ deaths. (Demurrer, p. 6:6-14.)

 

                                                  i.     First Cause of Action – Negligence

 

To state a cause of action for negligence, Plaintiff must plead the elements of (1) “the existence of a legal duty of care,” (2) “breach of that duty,” and (3) “proximate cause” (4) “resulting in an injury.” (McIntyre v. Colonies-Pacific, LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 664, 671.)

 

Red Lion argues Plaintiffs’ first cause of action for negligence fails because the FAC (1) does not allege Red Lion owed a duty to Decedents, (2) does not allege specific facts regarding Red Lion’s particular acts or omissions constituting a breach of any duty owed, and (3) does not allege facts to support the contention that Red Lion’s actions were the proximate cause of Decedents’ injuries and deaths or Plaintiffs’ damages. (Demurrer, p. 9:14-19, 13:23-26, 14:7-9.)

a.     Red Lion’s Ownership and Control of the Property

 

As a preliminary matter, Red Lion also argues that Plaintiffs fail to plead facts showing Red Lion owned, possessed, managed, or controlled the property, was involved in the hiring, training, or supervision of hotel security, staff, or otherwise had involvement with implementing any security protocols on the property, and that it is insufficient for Plaintiffs to rely exclusively on an unsubstantiated conclusion that Red Lion owns, manages, or operates the premises without any allegation that Red Lion exercised control over the property. (Demurrer, p. 9:17-18, 15:14-17, 16:5-9.) On the contrary, the FAC states that “Red Lion…owns, manage[s] and operate[s] the subject premises,” that Red Lion “employed security, staff and its regular employees mentioned herein,” and that “Red Lion…[has] control over the safety and condition of the subject premises.” (FAC, ¶¶ 54, 68, 71.) These allegations are sufficient to survive demurrer, as “the complaint need only allege [ultimate] facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff's proof need not be alleged.” (Ferrick v. Santa Clara University (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 1337, 1341.) Moreover, “‘less particularity is required where the defendant may be assumed to have knowledge of the facts equal to that possessed by the plaintiff. [Citation.]’… There is no need to require specificity in the pleadings because ‘modern discovery procedures necessarily affect the amount of detail that should be required in a pleading.’” (Roger v. County of Riverside (2020) 44 Cal. App. 5th 510, 533.) Here, the Court can assume Red Lion has knowledge of facts regarding its own ownership, operation, and control of the property.

 

Red Lion’s argument that the FAC indicates that BGHG was the entity that controlled, managed, and maintained access to the property at all relevant times is unconvincing. (Demurrer, p. 12:17-27.) Red Lion points to paragraphs 51-53 and 55-63 in the FAC, all pled as to the second cause of action for negligent supervision and training against BGHG only. Red Lion, however, omits any mention of paragraph 54 which reads “Defendant RED LION employed security, staff and its regular employees mentioned herein this Complaint.” Red Lion also skips over paragraph 59, which reads: “Most importantly, Defendant BG[HG] is responsible for the harm and damages because it owns Defendant RED LION and has control over its independent contractors and employees but failed to investigate the subject premises this recklessly allowing unleashed violence to occur within the subject premises placing many customers in harms’ way.” Reading the other allegations against BGHG in context, it appears BGHG’s alleged liability and negligence is based upon its ownership of Red Lion, its knowledge of the wrongful conduct of both its own employees and those of Red Lion, and its failure “to investigate, provide advice and engage with its subsidiaries regarding the hiring of said security, staff, and employees.” (FAC, ¶ 55.)

 

Furthermore, while allegations regarding Red Lion’s ownership, management, operation, and control over the safety and condition of the premises are not incorporated or pled within Plaintiffs’ first cause of action, the Court must “not only treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but also ‘give the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.’” (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.) Here, Plaintiffs have sufficiently pled Red Lion’s ownership and control over the property in the FAC and  referred to its ownership and control throughout the FAC.. Plaintiffs have sufficiently pled that Red Lion owned, operated, managed, and controlled the property, and the Court must take this allegation as true on demurrer.

 

b.     Duty of Care

 

Existence of a duty is a question of law to be resolved by the Court. (Jimenez v. Hayes Apartment Homes, LLC (2025) 115 Cal.App.5th 1072, 1084.)

 

Plaintiffs’ FAC alleges that Red Lion, doing business as American Value Inn & Suites, pursuant to California Civil Code section 1714 owed a duty to ensure the patrons at the premises were safe and protected, to protect patrons and customers from violence, and to use due care in preventing persons from entering the premises with deadly weapons so as not to cause injury to themselves or others. (FAC, ¶¶ 9, 38(a), 40-42.)

 

Civil Code section 1714 provides the general negligence framework that “[e]veryone is responsible, not only for the result of his or her willful acts, but also for an injury occasioned to another by his or her want of ordinary care or skill in the management of his or her property or person, except so far as the latter has, willfully or by want of ordinary care, brought the injury upon himself or herself.” (Civ. Code, § 1714, subd. (a).) Here, read in context of Plaintiffs’ allegations that Red Lion was negligent because security and employees allowed an unidentified man to enter the premises with a gun that could kill any person on the premises and failed to recognize a robbery and gun shooting on the premises, it appears Plaintiffs’ negligence cause of action is based upon Red Lion’s status as either owner, operator, or manager of the real property on which the subject incident occurred, and on Red Lion’s willful acts or want of ordinary care or skill in the management of its property, resulting in Decedents’ being killed by unnamed third parties.

 

“A landowner owes a duty to exercise reasonable care to maintain his or her property in such a manner as to avoid exposing others to an unreasonable risk or injury. [Citations.] The failure to fulfill the duty is negligence. [Citation.]” (Barnes v. Black (1999) 71 Cal.App.4th 1473, 1478 (citations omitted).) “In general, each person has a duty to act with reasonable care under the circumstances. [Citations.] However, ‘one owes no duty to control the conduct of another, nor to warn those endangered by such conduct.’ [Citation.] ‘A person who has not created a peril is not liable in tort merely for failure to take affirmative action to assist or protect another unless there is some relationship between them which gives rise to a duty to act.’” (Regents of University of California v. Superior Court¿(2018) 4 Cal.5th 607, 619;¿Delgado v. Trax Bar & Grill¿(2005) 36 Cal.4th 224, 235 [“A defendant may owe an affirmative duty to protect another from the conduct of third parties if he or she has a ‘special relationship’ with the other person.”].)¿

 

Courts apply a two-step inquiry to determine “whether a defendant has a legal duty to take action to protect the plaintiff from injuries caused by a third party.” (Brown v. USA Taekwondo (2021) 11 Cal.5th 204, 209.) “First, the court must determine whether there exists a special relationship between the parties or some other set of circumstances giving rise to an affirmative duty to protect. Second, if so, the court must consult the factors described in Rowland¿[v. Christian (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108] to determine whether relevant policy considerations counsel limiting that duty.” ¿(Ibid.)¿The two-step inquiry is based on the premise that “even when two parties may be in a special relationship, the unforeseeability of the kind of harm suffered by the plaintiff or other policy factors may counsel against establishing an affirmative duty for one party to protect another.” (Brown, supra, 11 Cal.5th at 219.) “A court might conclude that duty should not be imposed because, for example, the type of harm the plaintiff suffered was unforeseeable, or because there was no moral blameworthiness associated with defendant's conduct, notwithstanding the defendant's special relationship to the plaintiff. Put differently, even when a special relationship gives rise to an affirmative duty to protect, a court must still consider whether the policy considerations set out in¿Rowland¿warrant a departure from that duty in the relevant category of cases.” (Id. at 222.)¿ 

 

a.      First Step – Special Relationship

 

Cases in which a special relationship exists generally involve some kind of dependency or reliance. (Melton v. Boustred (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 521, 535.) "Courts have found such a special relationship in cases involving the relationship between business proprietors such as shopping centers, restaurants, and bars, and their tenants, patrons, or invitees. In addition, such special relationships triggering a duty to protect another from foreseeable injury caused by a third party have been found in other contexts, including those of (i) common carriers and passengers, (ii) innkeepers and their guests, and (iii) mental health professionals and their patients." (Id. at 535 (citations omitted, emphasis added).)

 

Specifically, a commonly recognized special relationship that may give rise to a legal duty in a particular case is that "between a possessor of land and members of the public who enter in response to the landowner's invitation," particularly in the context of a hotel owner and its patrons. (Peterson v. San Francisco Community College Dist. (1984) 36 Cal.3d 799, 806, 808-09 [enrolled student using a parking lot in exchange for a fee was an invitee to whom a possessor of the premises would owe a duty of care]; see also Gray v. Kircher (1987) 193 Cal.App.3d 1069, 1073 [“[t]he relationship between innkeeper and guests is recognized as sufficiently ‘special’ to impose liability under certain circumstances within the parameters of that relationship,” and “it is undisputed that owners or possessors of land, and particularly innkeepers, have a duty of care to protect invitees or tenants from the reasonably foreseeable criminal or tortious conduct of third persons”]; Lawrence v. La Jolla Beach & Tennis Club, Inc. (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 11, 22 [“[h]otel proprietors have a special relationship with their guests that gives rise to a duty”]; Delgado v. Trax Bar & Grill (2005) 36 Cal.4th 224, 237 [commercial proprietors’ duty of care to maintain land in reasonably safe condition includes taking reasonable steps to secure the premises against foreseeable third party criminal acts].)

 

Red Lion asserts that the FAC pleads “zero facts” showing Red Lion had any special relationship with Decedents. (Demurrer, p. 5:25-6:1, 12:15-17.) The FAC, in fact, alleges that (1) Red Lion owns, employs, funds and operates and conducts business in Los Angeles, California and Los Angeles County and owned, managed, and operated the subject property (FAC, ¶¶ 9, 68), (2) Red Lion employed security, staff and its regular employees on the premises (FAC, ¶ 54), (3) Red Lion had control over the safety and condition of the premises (FAC, ¶ 71), (4) Decedent Luther Crayton IV was a resident on the property, operating as “American Value Inn & Suites” (FAC, ¶ 21), (5) Decedent Shane Wallace, brother of Decedent Crayton, was visiting Crayton in his motel room (FAC, ¶ 21), (6) the subject incident resulting in the robbing, shooting, and killing of Decedents occurred on the property while Decedents were in the motel room and after Decedent Wallace ran outside to the parking lot (FAC, ¶¶ 22-27, 81), (7) Red Lion owns, manages, and operates the parking lot on the premises (FAC, ¶ 68), and (8) both Decedents were lawfully on the premises as patrons (FAC, ¶ 90).

 

Plaintiffs have sufficiently pled facts supporting the existence of a special relationship between Red Lion as owner and operator of a motel and Decedents as patrons thereof, a relationship recognized by law and which triggers a legal duty on Red Lion’s part.

 

b.     Second Step – Rowland Factors

 

Even where a special relationship exists, the scope of a landowner’s duty to protect from third party criminal conduct requires balancing the foreseeability of harm against the burden to be imposed. (Castaneda v. Olsher (2007) 41 Cal.4th 1205, 1213.) “The following factors determine the scope of the landowner's duty of care under the relevant circumstances: the foreseeability of harm to the injured party; the degree of certainty he or she suffered injury; the closeness of the connection between the defendant's conduct and the injury suffered; the moral blame attached to the defendant's conduct; the policy of preventing future harm; the extent of the burden to the defendant and the consequences to the community of imposing a duty of care with resulting liability for breach; and the availability, cost, and prevalence of insurance for the risk involved. [Citation.]” (Rowland v. Christian (1968) 69 Cal. 2d 108, 113.)

 

“Foreseeability and the extent of the burden to the defendant are ordinarily the crucial considerations, but in a given case one or more of the other¿Rowland¿factors may be determinative of the duty analysis.” (Castaneda, supra, 41 Cal.4th at 1213.)¿”In each case, however, the existence and scope of a property owner's duty to protect against third party crime is a question of law for the court to resolve.” (Ibid.) As the Brown Court stated, “even when two parties may be in a special relationship, the unforeseeability of the kind of harm suffered by the plaintiff or other policy factors may counsel against establishing an affirmative duty for one party to protect the other.” (Brown, supra, 11 Cal.5th at 219.)¿ “[A] court might conclude that duty should not be imposed because, for example, the type of harm the plaintiff suffered was unforeseeable, or because there was no moral blameworthiness associated with the defendant's conduct, notwithstanding the defendant's special relationship to the plaintiff. Put differently, even when a special relationship gives rise to an affirmative duty to protect, a court must still consider whether the policy considerations set out in Rowland warrant a departure from that duty in the relevant category of cases.” (Id. at 222.)  

 

Red Lion argues the FAC fails to plead sufficient allegations as to the foreseeability of the criminal conduct that caused Decedents’ death, and that under the Rowland factors and as currently pled, the FAC does not give rise to any duty owed by Red Lion. (Demurrer, p. 13:1-15.) The Court agrees.

 

Before imposing a duty of care, a plaintiff must establish a high degree of foreseeability of harm before imposing a duty of care. “[T]he requisite degree of foreseeability rarely, if ever, can be proven in the absence of prior similar incidents of violent crime on the landowner’s premises.¿To hold otherwise would be to impose an unfair burden upon landlords and, in effect, would force landlords to become the insurers of public safety, contrary to well established policy in this state.”¿(Ann M. v. Pacific Plaza Shopping Ctr. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 666, 679, footnote omitted.)¿“Heightened foreseeability is satisfied by a showing of prior similar criminal incidents (or other indications of a reasonably foreseeable risk of violent criminal assaults in that location) and does not require a showing of prior nearly identical criminal incidents.”¿ (Delgado, supra, 36 Cal.4th at 245.)¿

 

Courts have consistently held that even in the event of prior similar occurrences known to the defendant, “more than a general prospect of danger is required ‘before we can hold a defendant liable for the criminal acts of third parties.’” (Hanouchian v. Steele (2020) 51 Cal.App.5th 99, 111; see, e.g., Wiener v. Southcoast Childcare Centers, Inc. (2004) 32 Cal.4th 1138, 11150-51 [“it is difficult if not impossible in today's society to predict when a criminal might strike,” and a duty cannot be imposed on a defendant “[w]ithout prior similar criminal acts, or even any indication of prior criminal acts or intrusions of any type in the surrounding businesses”]; Margaret W. v. Kelley R. (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 141, 156 [“foreseeability must be measured by what the defendant actually knew” before a duty attaches, and no case has held “that a defendant owed a duty to take steps to prevent or respond to third party crime on the basis of constructive knowledge or information the defendant should have known.”]; Williams v. Fremont Corners, Inc. (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 654, 668 [evidence of three prior similar instances of third party conduct insufficient to establish foreseeability because general awareness of the possibility of future incidents and general knowledge of the possibility of violent criminal conduct is not enough to create a duty].) Thus “[t]o establish heightened foreseeability for third party criminal conduct, our authorities have consistently required actual knowledge—not constructive, inferential, or knowledge by association—to impose a burdensome legal duty.” (Hanouchian, supra, 51 Cal.App.5th at 111.)¿

¿

Applying the foregoing authorities to this case, the question is whether Plaintiffs allege facts in the FAC demonstrating a high degree of foreseeability that harm would come to Decedents if adequate security measures were not taken to protect them from the harm suffered. Such facts are not alleged. There are no allegations of any prior similar incidents in the area, in or around the property, or any prior criminal incidents at all. There is no allegation or fact pled that Red Lion knew of any such instances, that any had ever been reported, or that the same or similar criminal conduct had ever transpired to give Red Lion reason to know of the risk of harm to its patrons. The FAC is completely silent on the issue of whether the third party criminal conduct at issue in this case was even remotely foreseeable to Red Lion. The FAC also does not plead facts sufficient to attach any moral blame on Red Lion for its alleged conduct, because there are no factual allegations indicating that Red Lion’s implementing enhanced security measures, such as hiring additional security or conducting searches of its patrons or their personal belongings, was in any way warranted. (See FAC, ¶ 75.)

 

Accordingly, the FAC fails to plead facts sufficient to establish that Red Lion owed any duty to Decedents. It follows that Plaintiffs have also failed to adequately plead Red Lion’s breach of any duty, or that any breach was the cause of Decedents’ and Plaintiff’s injuries and damages.

 

Accordingly, Red Lion’s demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the first cause of action.

 

                                                ii.     Third Cause of Action – Premises Liability

 

“The elements of a cause of action for premises liability are the same as those for negligence: duty, breach, causation, and damages.” (Castellon v. U.S. Bancorp (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 994, 998.) For the reasons stated above, because the FAC does not plead facts sufficient to establish any duty owed by Red Lion, breach of any such duty, or causation, Plaintiffs’ third cause of action fails.

 

Red Lion’s demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the third cause of action.

 

                                              iii.     Fourth Cause of Action – Survival

 

A survival action is a "separate and distinct cause of action which belonged to the decedent before death but, by statute, survives the event." (Quiroz, supra, 140 Cal. App. 4th at 1264.) For the reasons stated herein, the FAC fails to allege facts sufficient to give rise to a cause of action against Red Lion based in negligence, as the FAC does not plead facts sufficient to impose any duty on Red Lion owed to Decedents prior to or at the time of their deaths.

 

Red Lion also argues Plaintiffs do not have standing to bring this claim. (Demurrer, p. 16:22.) Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 377.30, “[a] cause of action that survives the death of the person entitled to commence an action or proceeding passes to the decedent's successor in interest… and an action may be commenced by the decedent's personal representative or, if none, by the decedent's successor in interest.” Any person who seeks to commence a survival action must execute and file an affidavit or declaration stating (1) the decedent’s name, (2) the date and place of decedent’s death, (3) that “No proceeding is now pending in California for administration of the decedent's estate,” (4) if the decedent's estate was administered, a copy of the final order showing the distribution of the decedent's cause of action to the successor in interest, (5) either “The affiant or declarant is the decedent's successor in interest (as defined in Section 377.11 of the California Code of Civil Procedure) and succeeds to the decedent's interest in the action or proceeding” or “The affiant or declarant is authorized to act on behalf of the decedent's successor in interest (as defined in Section 377.11 of the California Code of Civil Procedure) with respect to the decedent's interest in the action or proceeding” with facts in support thereof, (6) that “No other person has a superior right to commence the action or proceeding or to be substituted for the decedent in the pending action or proceeding,” and (7) that “The affiant or declarant affirms or declares under penalty of perjury under the laws of the State of California that the foregoing is true and correct.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 377.32, subd. (a).) Where more than one person executes the affidavit or declaration, the statements required by subdivision (a) shall be modified as appropriate, and a certified copy of the decedent’s death certificate must be attached. (Code Civ. Proc., § 377.32, subd. (b)-(c).)

 

No Plaintiff has filed an affidavit as required to bring this action. Therefore, Plaintiffs have failed to establish that they have the requisite standing to assert this claim on behalf of Decedents.

Accordingly, the demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the fourth cause of action.

 

                                               iv.     Fifth Cause of Action – Wrongful Death

 

“The elements of the cause of action for wrongful death are the tort (negligence or other wrongful act), the resulting death, and the damages, consisting of the pecuniary loss suffered by the heirs.” (Quiroz v. Seventh Ave. Center (2006) 140 Cal. App. 4th 1256, 1263.) “In any action for wrongful death resulting from negligence, the complaint must contain allegations as to all the elements of actionable negligence. [Citation.]” (Jacoves v. United Merchandising Corp. (1992) 9 Cal.App.4th 88, 105.) The FAC bases the wrongful death claim on Red Lion’s negligent, reckless, and careless ownership, operation, control, maintenance, repair, permission, management, supervision, and facilitation of the operation of the Property, causing a dangerous condition to exist which caused Decedents’ deaths. (FAC, ¶ 92.) For the reasons stated herein, the FAC does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for negligence or other tort against Red Lion. Therefore, Plaintiffs’ fifth cause of action for wrongful death fails.

 

Red Lion’s demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the fifth cause of action.

 

                                                v.     Leave to Amend

 

Red Lion requests that the demurrer to each cause of action be sustained without leave to amend because Plaintiffs have already had one opportunity to cure the deficiencies in the complaint and failed to do so. (Demurrer, p. 6:2-5.) Red Lion’s argument is contrary to California’s liberal policies of amendment. While the FAC was purportedly filed in response to ongoing meet and confer efforts, no demurrer has ever been previously filed in this case by either Defendant.,. Plaintiffs have amended once, without leave of court, as a matter of right. Furthermore, the Court finds a reasonable possibility of amendment. The defects in the FAC identified herein relate to (1) failure to plead certain facts, which may exist and are not precluded by the FAC as pled, and (2) lack of standing due to Plaintiffs’ failure to file an affidavit establishing standing for the survival cause of action pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 377.32. These deficiencies may reasonably be cured.

 

Accordingly, Red Lion’s demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Timeliness of Defendants’ Demurrer – When a party amends a pleading as a matter of right, the time for responding to an amended pleading shall be computed from the date of service of the amended pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 472.) A defendant has 30 days from the date of personal service to file a responsive pleading to an amended complaint. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.40, subd. (a).)

 

On April 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed two proofs of service. The first is verified by a registered process served, establishing that U.S. Bank’s agent for service of process, CT Corporation System, was personally served the amended summons and FAC on April 6, 2026. Personal service is complete the moment of deliver, rendering service on U.S. Bank complete on April 6, 2026. (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.10.) The second is verified under oath by an individual who is not a registered California process server, but who declares that Selene was served via substituted service on its registered agent for service of process Corporation Service Company on April 8, 2026 with an attached declaration of mailing. Substituted service is complete on the 10th day after mailing, rendering service on Selene complete on April 18, 2026. (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.20, subd. (b).)

 

Therefore, U.S. Bank’s deadline to file a demurrer to the FAC was on May 6th. Selene’s deadline to file a demurrer to the FAC was on May 18th. The present demurrer was not filed until May 29, 2026, rendering it untimely as to both Defendants.

 

However, an untimely demurrer may be considered by the Court in its discretion. (Jackson v. Doe (2011) 192 Cal.App.4th 742, 750.) Here, Plaintiff does not object to the timeliness of Defendants’ demurrer, has filed a substantive opposition thereto, and Defendants had already previously opposed Plaintiff’s ex parte application a month before the demurrer was filed. In the absence of any objection from or cognizable prejudice to Plaintiff, who never took steps to obtain default judgment against either Defendant while actively pursuing default against RASC, the Court will consider the demurrer on the merits. (Ibid.; Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a)(1).)

 

Meet and Confer Requirement – Before filing a demurrer, the demurring party is required to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due, in person, by telephone, or by video conference, with the party who filed the pleading demurred to for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) The demurring party shall file and serve with the demurrer a declaration stating the means by which the parties met and conferred and that the parties did not reach an agreement to resolve the issues raised, or that the party who filed the pleading demurred to failed to respond or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith. (Ibid.)

 

Defendants provide the Declaration of Counsel Jonathan D. Marvisi, in which he declares that on May 5, 2026, he met and conferred with Plaintiff’s counsel by telephone, but after two hours no agreement was reached. (Marvisi Decl., ¶ 2.) The Court finds this requirement satisfied.

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Demurrer – A party against whom a complaint has been filed may object by demurrer to the pleading on the ground that the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action and that the pleading is uncertain, ambiguous, and unintelligible. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subds. (e)-(f).) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations reasonably, liberally, and in context. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) Courts must “treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at 318.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 126.) The threshold for meeting this burden is low, and “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendants demur to all eight causes of action in the FAC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivisions (e)-(f) on the grounds that each cause of action fails to set forth sufficient facts to constitute a cause of action and is uncertain, ambiguous, and unintelligible.

 

a.     Uncertain, Ambiguous, and Unintelligible

 

As a preliminary matter, Defendants demur to all causes of action in the FAC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (f) on the basis that the FAC and allegations within are uncertain, ambiguous, and unintelligible. Specifically, Defendants demur on these grounds because the FAC refers to “Defendants” generally in its allegations without identifying which Defendant allegedly committed which act. (Demurrer, p. 5:21-22.) For example, Defendant notes that the FAC does not allege which Defendant misapplied payments, failed to send notices, denied accommodations, made false statements, breached contractual obligations, or engaged in unlawful conduct. (Demurrer, p. 5:23-28.) Defendants assert that such collective pleading is improper and subject to demurrer on uncertainty grounds. (Demurrer, p. 6:1-2.) Plaintiff’s opposition is silent as to this argument.

 

“A defendant may demur to a complaint… on the ground that the ‘pleading is uncertain.’ (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) The term ‘uncertain’ includes ‘ambiguous” and ‘unintelligible.’ (Ibid.).” (Van Horn v. Dep't of Toxic Substances Control (2014) 231 Cal. App. 4th 1287, 1295.) A “[d]emurrer for uncertainty is directed at the allegations actually made. [Citation.] Demurrer for uncertainty does not lie as to immaterial matter. [Citation.] Nor will a demurrer for uncertainty lie where the facts are presumptively within the knowledge of the demurring parties. [Citation.]” (Dumm v. Pac. Valves (1956) 146 Cal. App. 2d 792, 799.) “A special demurrer on the ground that an answer is (a) ambiguous, (b) unintelligible, or (c) uncertain is insufficient unless the demurrer points out specifically wherein the pleading is ambiguous, uncertain or unintelligible. [Citation.].” (Coons v. Thompson (1946) 75 Cal. App. 2d 687, 690 (citation omitted).)

 

Here, the Court does not find that the FAC is so uncertain, ambiguous, or unintelligible that Defendants are not on notice of what is being alleged. While the FAC does collectively refer to “Defendants,” the FAC also defines Selene as the mortgage servicer, U.S. Bank as claimed beneficiary, and RASC as the recorder of the Notice of Default (NOD). (FAC, ¶¶ 2-4.) The FAC thereafter alleges specific conduct attributable to the individually named or described Defendants throughout. (See, e.g., FAC ¶ 9, 11, 12, 14, 18, 23(4), 24(3), 28(3).) Moreover, “‘less particularity is required where the defendant may be assumed to have knowledge of the facts equal to that possessed by the plaintiff. [Citation.]’… There is no need to require specificity in the pleadings because ‘modern discovery procedures necessarily affect the amount of detail that should be required in a pleading.’” (Roger v. County of Riverside (2020) 44 Cal. App. 5th 510, 533.)

 

Therefore, Defendant’s demurrer is not sustained on this ground.

 

b.     First Cause of Action – Violation of Civil Code § 2924.17 (Selene Only)

Defendant Selene demurs to the first cause of action asserted against it on the grounds that it alleges in conclusory fashion that the NOD contains incorrect, missing, and unverified information, but concedes that RASC, not Selene, recorded the NOD. (Demurrer, p. 6:4-10.) Defendant Selene further asserts that even if Plaintiff alleges Selene was involved in the preparation of the NOD, he does not identify what arrears were incorrect, what payments were omitted, what information was inaccurate, or how Selene failed to review competent evidence. (Demurrer, p. 6:10-13.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff cites to the specific defects as alleged in the FAC, and notes that the FAC also alleges that Plaintiff was never served with the NOD and that materiality of Selene’s wrongdoing is sufficiently pled. (Opp., p. 2:12-20.) Plaintiff claims Exhibit B contains servicer correspondence showing inconsistent arrears and missing credits. (Opp., p. 2:20-23.)

 

A NOD recorded by or on behalf of a mortgage servicer in connection with a foreclosure shall be accurate and complete and supported by competent and reliable evidence. (Civ. Code, § 2924.17, subd. (a).) Therefore, before recording or filing a NOD, a mortgage servicer must ensure it has reviewed competent and reliable evidence to substantiate the borrower’s default and the right to foreclose, including the borrower’s loan status and loan information. (Civ. Code § 2924.17, subd. (b).) Under the Homeowners Bill of Rights (HBOR), which includes Civil Code section 2924.17, a borrower may bring an action to enjoin a material violation of Section 2924.17. (Civ. Code, § 2924.12, subd. (a)(1).) “[B]y its terms, the HBOR creates liability only for material violations that have not been remedied before the foreclosure sale is recorded. A material violation is one that affected the borrower's loan obligations, disrupted the borrower's loan-modification process, or otherwise harmed the borrower.” (Billesbach v. Specialized Loan Servicing LLC (2021) 63 Cal. App. 5th 830, 837.)

 

Plaintiff has failed to state facts sufficient to state a cause of action for violation of Civil Code section 2924.17 as against Selene. To start, whether Plaintiff was served with the NOD is irrelevant to Civil Code section 2924.17. Plaintiff does not plead any facts to support the allegation that Selene failed to review competent and reliable evidence substantiating his default and the right to foreclose before recording related documents. Plaintiff asserts that the NOD recorded on February 13, 2026 contains inaccurate arrears, missing credits, and unverified information. (FAC, ¶ 23(3).) However, Plaintiff provides no factual allegations to support this assertion, such as which arrears were inaccurate, how they were inaccurate and by what amount, what credits are missing, or what information contained on the NOD is unverified. Plaintiff also alleges that Selene failed to review accurate loan data before recording the NOD, but provides no further factual basis for this claim. (FAC, ¶ 23(4).) Plaintiff does not allege what loan data was reviewed, how it was inaccurate, identify accurate loan data that establishes that the information on the NOD is inaccurate, or otherwise plead any allegations to support this statement. The FAC is also devoid of any factual allegations that the information on the NOD constituted a material violation that affected his loan obligations, disrupted any loan-modification process, or otherwise harmed him. The FAC also alleges that no Notice of Trustee’s Sale has issued (FAC, ¶ 13) and that RASC recorded the NOD, not Selene. (FAC, ¶ 4).

 

As to Plaintiff’s reference to Exhibit B, the Court notes that no arrear or credit information is contained in this correspondence. Rather, Plaintiff’s Exhibit B is an email from U.S. Bank clarifying its role as trustee for the mortgage-backed-securitization trust (MBS Trust) that owns the mortgage with an attached brochure detailing the role of trustees of MBS trusts.

 

Because the deficiencies of the first cause of action arise entirely from insufficient factual allegations, the Court finds a reasonable possibility of amendment to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer is SUSTAINED as to Selene for the first cause of action with leave to amend.

 

c.      Second Cause of Action – Violation of Civil Code § 2923.7 (Selene Only)

 

Defendant Selene demurs to the second cause of action (which is asserted against Selene alone) on the grounds that the FAC does not allege sufficient facts that Plaintiff ever requested a foreclosure prevention alternative and that he alleges insufficient facts regarding his requests for a single point of contact (SPOC) and fails to allege any prejudice. (Demurrer, p. 6:25-7:11.) In opposition, Plaintiff points to specific allegations set forth in the FAC regarding his alleged requests for assistance and again points to Exhibit B, which he claims contains correspondence showing Plaintiff’s repeated attempts to obtain assistance. (Opp., p. 2:26-3:6.)

 

“When a borrower requests a foreclosure prevention alternative, the mortgage servicer shall promptly establish a single point of contact and provide to the borrower one or more direct means of communication with the single point of contact.” (Civ. Code, § 2923.7, subd. (a).) “‘Foreclosure prevention alternative’ means a first lien loan modification or another available loss mitigation option.” (Civ. Code., § 2920.5, subd. (b).) Reinstatement is not a “foreclosure prevention alternative.” (See McAfee v. PHH Mortgage Corporation (N.D. Cal., Oct. 8, 2019) 2019 WL 13203931, at *2 [citing other authority in holding that “[p]aying off a delinquent loan to bring that loan current is not a ‘foreclosure prevention alternative’”]; see also Civ. Code, §§ 2924.9-2924.11 [describing requirements for foreclosure prevention alternatives].)

 

The FAC fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for violation of Civil Code section 2923.7 against Selene. Prompt establishment of a SPOC is an obligation imposed on a mortgage servicer only when a borrower requests a foreclosure prevention alternative. Here, the FAC alleges that Plaintiff repeatedly attempted to resolve the issues with Selene, including sending requests for accounting, correction of errors, and dispute resolution, that Selene failed to correct the defects, and that Plaintiff repeatedly requested that a SPOC be established but Selene failed to assign one. (FAC, ¶¶ 14, 24(2)-(3).) The issues Plaintiff states he attempted to resolve with Selene include the allegedly incorrect information on the NOD, the non-issuance of the NTS, servicing errors, misapplied payments, contradictory statements, defective notice, and failure to serve the NOD. (FAC, ¶¶ 9-14.)

 

There is no allegation that Plaintiff requested a foreclosure prevention alternative or that Selene’s failure to establish a SPOC was material for the purposes of the HBOR. Therefore, the allegations in the FAC do not establish any statutory duty by Selene to appoint a SPOC. However, because the deficiencies of the second cause of action arise entirely from insufficient factual allegations regarding Plaintiff’s request for a foreclosure prevention alternative, the Court finds a reasonable possibility of amendment to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action if he did in fact request an alternative.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer to the second cause of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

 

d.     Third Cause of Action – Negligence (Selene Only)

 

Defendant Selene demurs to the third cause of action on the grounds that Sheen v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 905 expressly forecloses negligence claims against mortgage servicers on the grounds that lenders and servicers do not owe borrowers a general duty of care arising from loan servicing activities, and absent any special relationship or conduct exceeding the scope of conventional loan servicing activities, no duty arises. (Demurrer, p. 7:13-20.) Defendant further argues that the FAC fails to allege facts to support breach of any duty, causation, or damages, as he does not identify what payments were misapplied, when they were misapplied, or how Selene caused compensable damages. (Demurrer, p. 7:20-8:2.)

 

Plaintiff argues the FAC alleges conduct exceeding the scope of ordinary loan servicing, including failure to serve the NOD, defective statutory notices, misapplied payments, failure to correct errors, disability related failures, and violation of statutory duties under the HBOR, which exist independently of the loan contract. (Opp., p. 3:9-18.) Plaintiff again asserts his Exhibit B establishes errors and failure to take corrective action.

 

"The elements of a cause of action for negligence are well established. They are '(a) a legal duty to use due care; (b) a breach of such legal duty; [and] (c) the breach as the proximate or legal cause of the resulting injury.'" (Ladd v. County of San Mateo (1996) 12 Cal.4th 913, 917, internal citation omitted.) In California, the general rule is that “a financial institution owes no duty of care to a borrower when the institution's involvement in the loan transaction does not exceed the scope of its conventional role as a mere lender of money.” (Nymark v. Heart Fed. Savings & Loan Assn. (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1089, 1096; Sheen, supra, 12 Cal.5th at 928.) A lender is acting within the scope of its conventional role as a lender and owes no duty to the borrower in pursuing its own economic interests in lending money, approving a loan, negotiating with the borrower, or in offering, considering, approving, or modifying a loan, exploration of foreclosure alternatives. (Perlas v. GMAC Mortgage, LLC (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 429, 436; Lueras v. BAC Home Loans Servicing, LP (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 49, 67.) Therefore, the general notion is that a lender does “not have a common law duty of care…to offer [the borrower] alternatives to foreclosure. Likewise, [mortgage lenders do] not have a duty of care to handle [the borrower’s] loan ‘in such a way to prevent foreclosure and forfeiture of his property.’” (Lueras, supra, 221 Cal.App.4th at 68.) “[A]bsent special circumstances… a loan transaction is at arm's length and there is no fiduciary relationship between the borrower and lender. [Citations.]” (Oaks Management Corporation v. Superior Court (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 453, 466.)

 

Here, Plaintiff has failed to identify any act, omission, conduct, or wrongdoing on behalf of Selene outside of the scope of its general role as lender that would support any duty owed by Selene to Plaintiff. The FAC’s allegations as to Selene relate directly to its involvement in servicing Plaintiff’s loan within its conventional role as a lender, including generally servicing the loan, application of payments, providing notices to and serving documents on Plaintiff, recording the NOD, transferring service, calculation of arrears, providing contact information for the loan servicer, compliance with various statutory obligations imposed on all mortgage lenders in servicing a loan, communication with Plaintiff as a borrower, and responding to disputes regarding his default and money owed on the loan. Taking the FAC as true, none of these factual allegations give rise to any special relationship between Plaintiff and Selene, and thus, do not give rise to any duty owed by Selene to Plaintiff in servicing the loan, noticing Plaintiff’s default, or initiating foreclosure proceedings. For this reason, the FAC fails to allege facts sufficient to impose any duty on Selene, and Plaintiff’s negligence cause of action fails.

 

Regarding leave to amend, courts have held that “a lender does owe a duty to a borrower to not make material misrepresentations about the status of an application for a loan modification or about the date, time, or status of a foreclosure sale,” as the “law imposes a duty not to make negligent misrepresentations of fact.” (Lueras, supra, 221 Cal.App.4th at 69.) Therefore, where a complaint subject to demurrer alleges facts that may give rise to a negligent misrepresentation claim, such as where a lender’s communications about a foreclosure sale or the status of a loan modification application are inaccurate or untimely, or where a complaint generally alleges that a lender failed to exercise reasonable care in timely and accurately responding to customer requests and inquiries, leave to amend is appropriate. (Ibid.)

 

Here, the FAC alleges numerous servicing errors, misapplied payments, contradictory statements, defective notices, failure to serve Plaintiff with the NOD, misrepresentations of arrears owed, failure to credit payments, and receipt of inaccurate information from Selene which resulted in financial harm, credit damage, and exposure to wrongful foreclosure. Thus, if Plaintiff can supply further factual allegations as to the incorrect information he received, the defective notices, and negligently incorrect information provided by Selene regarding the status of his loan, it is reasonably possible that Plaintiff can state a cause of action for negligent misrepresentation against Selene.

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

 

e.      Fourth Cause of Action – Breach of Contract (Both Defendants)

 

Defendants demur to the breach of contract cause of action on the grounds that Plaintiff fails to adequately plead the existence of a contract, Plaintiff’s performance or excuse for non-performance, or Defendants’ breaches of the contract. (Demurrer, p. 8:4-7.) Defendants further argue that Plaintiff has not attached the Deed of Trust (DOT) to the FAC or identified specific contractual provisions breached, does not allege that both Selene and U.S. Bank are parties to the DOT, does not allege whether he performed under the DOT, which includes payment obligations, and does not allege facts establishing that any alleged breach caused him damages. (Demurrer, p. 8:7-19, 9:1-5.) In opposition, Plaintiff argues his allegations of a valid DOT, Defendants’ failure to provide required notices, failure to apply payments correctly, and initiation of foreclosure without compliance are sufficient to withstand demurrer. (Opp., p. 3:24-4:4.)

 

"To prevail on a cause of action for breach of contract, the plaintiff must prove (1) the contract, (2) plaintiff's performance of the contract or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant's breach, and (4) resulting damage to the plaintiff." (Richman v. Hartley (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1182, 1186.)

 

Here, the FAC fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for breach of contract.  The FAC does not contain any allegations that Plaintiff entered into a contract with both Defendants, does not put forth any terms of the contract or attach the contract itself, does not allege that Plaintiff performed or was excused from performing under the contract, does not allege what term of the contract was breached by Defendants or how, and does not establish that the alleged breach of the contract was the cause of any harm suffered by Plaintiff.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the fourth cause of action with leave to amend.

 

f.      Fifth Cause of Action – Unfair Business Practices (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200) (Both Defendants)

 

Defendants argue the fifth cause of action fails because the FAC does not plead with any specificity or clarity any viable unlawful conduct, does not specify which prong of Section 17200 Plaintiff relies on, fails to plead fraud under the UCL, and is derivative of other failed claims. (Demurrer, p. 9:16-10:15.)

 

Plaintiff argues that his allegations of Defendants’ violations of Civil Code sections 2923.5, 2923.55, 2924, 2924.17, 2923.7, misapplication of payments, and servicing errors sufficiently establish a cause of action for unfair business practices. (Opp., p. 4:9-18.)

 

Business and Professions Code section 17200 describes unfair competition as “any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising and any act prohibited by Chapter 1 (commencing with Section 17500) of Part 3 of Division 7 of the Business and Professions Code.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200.) An “unlawful business act” includes “anything that can properly be called a business practice and that at the same time is forbidden by law.” (People v. McKale¿(1979) 25 Cal.3d 626, 632.) A claim under the California's Unfair Competition Law, Business & Professions Code §§17200 et seq. ("UCL"), requires the allegation of particular facts showing the statutory elements of ongoing unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business acts on the part of the defendant with “reasonable particularity.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 619.) Claims by private plaintiffs can only be brought by “a person who has suffered injury in fact and has lost money or property as a result of the unfair competition.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17204.) Fraudulent business practices requires a showing that members of the public are likely to be deceived, meaning “members of the public must have had an expectation or an assumption about” the matter in question.” (Daugherty v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc., 144 Cal. App. 4th 824, 838.) “[A]n ‘unfair’ business practice occurs when that practice ‘offends an established public policy or when the practice is immoral, unethical, oppressive, unscrupulous or substantially injurious to consumers.’ [Citation.]” (Smith v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Ins. Co. (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 700, 718–719.)

 

The FAC does not plead with any particularity facts showing ongoing unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business acts, that Plaintiff has lost money or property as a result of the unfair competition, that members of the public are likely to be deceived by Defendants’ alleged wrongful conduct, or that Defendants’ conduct offends established public policy or is immoral, unethical, oppressive, unscrupulous, or substantially injurious to consumers. Plaintiff has also failed to allege that Defendants deprived him of any specific money or property, or that any loss of money was a result of Defendants’ business practices as opposed to mere default on the mortgage loan. Plaintiff’s HBOR violation claims are also insufficient to establish any cause of action under the UCL, as they fail to state facts sufficient to constitute causes of action for violations thereof and do not rise to the level of unfairness or fraudulent necessary to maintain this cause of action.

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

 

g.     Sixth Cause of Action – Failure to Accommodate Disability / Unlawful Practices (Selene Only)

 

Defendant Selene argues the  sixth cause of action fails because it does not identify any cognizable legal theory, statute violated, legal duty owed, applicable disability law, specific accommodation request, when any request was made, or how any request was denied. (Demurrer, p. 10:17-23.) Plaintiff argues he sufficiently pleads a cause of action because the FAC alleges that Plaintiff is a disabled senior citizen, notified Selene of disability-related communication needs, Selene failed to accommodate those needs, and alleges harm as a result.

 

The FAC only alleges that Plaintiff is a disabled senior citizen, that he informed Selene of his disability-related communication needs, including that he requires written and email communications and additional time and clarity to understand and respond to loan-related issues, and that Selene failed to provide reasonable accommodations by failing to provide written communication and consistent contact, causing Plaintiff confusion, stress, and risk of wrongful foreclosure. (FAC, ¶¶ 17-18, 28.) For the reasons put forth by Defendants, Plaintiff’s sixth cause of action fails. Plaintiff puts forth no disability-related law imposing a duty on Selene, breach of that duty, or other recognized legal violation.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is SUSTAINED to the sixth cause of action. The Court will grant leave to amend to provide Plaintiff an opportunity to identify any applicable disability laws violated relating to his accommodation requests and plead facts sufficient to support a legally viable cause of action, if any exist.

 

h.     Seventh Cause of Action – Accounting (Both Defendants)

 

Defendants argue the seventh cause of action fails because Plaintiff identifies no clear fiduciary duty between himself and Defendants and does not allege that ordinary discovery would be insufficient to determine disputed amounts. (Demurrer, p. 11:1-10.)

 

To plead a cause of action for accounting, a plaintiff must demonstrate (1) “that a relationship exists between the plaintiff and defendant that requires an accounting” and (2) ‘that some balance is due the plaintiff that can only be ascertained by an accounting.” (Sass v. Cohen (2020) 10 Ca1.5th 861, 869.) An action for an accounting is a means for plaintiff to recover funds owed. (Tesselle v. McLoughlin (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 156, 180 (“the purpose of the accounting is, in part, to discover what, if any, sums are owed to the plaintiff.”).) An accounting is not available where a party seeks to recover a sum certain or where the amount owed can be determined through ordinary discovery and loan records. (Jolley v. Chase Home Finance, LLC (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 872, 910.) The plaintiff must show that there is no legal remedy available, such as where the books and records are so complicated that an action demanding a fixed sum is appropriate. (Prakashpalan v. Engstrom, Lipscomb & Lack (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 1105, 1137.)

 

This cause of action fails for two reasons. First, Plaintiff has not established that a legal remedy is inadequate or that accounting is appropriate because no ascertainable sum is owed. (See ibid [“If an ascertainable sum is owed, an action for an accounting is not proper.”].) It appears Plaintiff’s accounting cause of action merely rests on his assertion that he disputes the charges, application of payments, and balances on his loan account. (FAC, ¶ 29.) This is insufficient to establish that an accounting is necessary or an available remedy, and the amount owed by Plaintiff appears from the complaint to be readily ascertainable, given that the dispute relates to a straightforward mortgage balance and interest accrued thereon. The date of the loan, the amount, the interest rates, the amounts owed, and the amounts paid are calculable and discernable. No allegations in the FAC indicate otherwise. Second, “[g]enerally, an underlying fiduciary relationship, such as a partnership, will support an accounting, but the action does not lie merely because the books and records are complex. [Citations.] Some underlying misconduct on the part of the defendant must be shown to invoke the right to this equitable remedy.” (Ibid.) Plaintiff has failed to allege any cognizable fiduciary relationship between himself and Defendants, nor any misconduct or wrongdoing on the part of Defendants. To the extent Plaintiff is able to amend the FAC to allege sufficient facts to support some special relationship with Defendants, a duty owed, and wrongdoing to support his other causes of action, the Court will grant leave to amend to allow Plaintiff an opportunity to plead a cause of action for accounting.

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to the seventh cause of action.

 

i.       Eighth Cause of Action – Injunctive Relief (Both Defendants)

 

“Injunctive relief is a remedy and not, in itself, a cause of action, and a cause of action must exist before injunctive relief may be granted.” (Shell Oil Co. v. Richter (1942) 52 Cal.App.2d 164, 168.)

 

The Court sustains Defendants’ demurrer as to all of Plaintiff’s causes of action. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s eighth cause of action for injunctive relief necessarily fails.

 

Defendants’ demurrer to the FAC is therefore SUSTAINED in its entirety with leave to amend.

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Meet and Confer Requirement – Before filing a motion for judgment on the pleadings, the moving party is required to meet and confer at least five days before the date the motion is filed in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading subject to the motion for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 439 , subd. (a).) The moving party shall file and serve with the motion a declaration stating the means by which the parties met and conferred and that the parties did not reach an agreement to resolve the issues raised, or that the party who filed the pleading failed to respond or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith. (Ibid.)

 

Here, Defendant has not put forth any declaration or statement indicating any attempt to meet and confer was made. Defendant’s argument on reply that the parties have sufficiently met and conferred through filings in this case is unmeritorious, as the language of the meet and confer requirement set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 439 expressly requires that the moving party must meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference to try and come to an agreement. Furthermore, Defendant’s meet and confer declaration references only dates of correspondence predating the filing of the operative Complaint in this case. Such communications do not establish any valid meet and confer effort regarding deficiencies in the pleadings, as no pleadings had been filed. Defendant has not asserted he has made any effort to comply with the meet and confer requirement, and Plaintiff’s counsel confirms that at no point and in no manner since the filing of the Complaint has Defendant attempted to initiate any further meet and confer regarding the causes of action or allegations asserted in the Complaint. (Daneshi Decl., ¶ 6.) Thus, the Court finds the meet and confer requirement has not been satisfied.

 

However, a determination that the meet and confer process was not sufficient is not grounds to deny a motion for judgment on the pleadings. (Code Civ. Proc., § 439, subd. (a)(4).) Therefore, Defendant’s failure to even attempt meet and confer is not a sufficient basis on which the Court may deny the motion. The Court notes that meeting and conferring here likely could have avoided needless motion practice.

California Evidence Code section 452 permits judicial notice being taken of records of any court of California and any facts and propositions not reasonably subject to dispute and capable of immediate and accurate determination by resort to sources of reasonably indisputable accuracy. (Evid. Code., § 452, subd. (c), (h).) California Evidence Code section 453 mandates a court take judicial notice of a matter specified in section 452 if a party so requests, gives the adverse party sufficient notice through pleadings or otherwise, and furnishes the court with sufficient information to enable it to take judicial notice. (Evid. Code, § 453, subd. (a), (b).) A court may also properly take judicial notice of recorded documents. (Md. Casualty Co. v. Reeder (1990) 221 Cal.App.3d 961, 977; Evans v. California Trailer Court, Inc. (1994) 28 Cal.App.4th 540, 549 (disapproved in part on other grounds) [courts may take judicial notice of recorded deeds].) The Court may take judicial notice “not only of the existence and recordation of recorded documents but also of a variety of matters that can be deduced from the documents.” (Fontenot v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 256, 265 (overruled in part on other grounds).)

 

Defendant’s request is therefore GRANTED and the Court takes judicial notice of the fact of the listed filings.  The Court does not take judicial notice of any disputed facts within.

(1978) 77 Cal.App.3d 481, 486; see also Cal. Rules of Court, rule 81115(b).)

    V.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings – A defendant may move for judgment on the pleadings where “[t]he complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 438(c)(1)(B)(ii).) “A motion for judgment on the pleadings may be made at any time either prior to the trial or at the trial itself. [Citation.]” (Ion Equipment Corp. v. Nelson (1980) 110 Cal.App.3d 868, 877 (disapproved on other grounds).) The grounds for the motion must appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice, or from matter of which the court is permitted to take judicial notice pursuant to Evidence Code section 452 or 453 if specified in the notice of motion or in the supporting points and authorities. (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (d).)

 

“A motion for judgment on the pleadings performs the same function as a general demurrer, and hence attacks only defects disclosed on the face of the pleadings or by matters that can be judicially noticed. Presentation of extrinsic evidence is therefore not proper on a motion for judgment on the pleadings.” (Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 999 (citations omitted).) “The standard for ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings is essentially the same as that applicable to a general demurrer, that is, under the state of the pleadings, together with matters that may be judicially noticed, it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Bezirdjian v. O'Reilly (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 316, 321–322 (citing Schabarum v. California Legislature (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1216).) 

“If a pleading is defective but amendable, judgment on the pleadings should be granted but with leave to amend.” (Olsen v. Lockheed Aircraft Corp. (1965) 237 Cal.App.2d 737, 742.)

 VI.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendant moves for Judgment on the Pleadings pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 438 and 439, and for Declaratory Relief determining the validity, enforceability, scope, and legal effect of the memorandum and that Plaintiff’s asserted rights were extinguished when the rental agreement was canceled and the lease was forfeited pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1060.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1060 provides that any person interested under a written instrument or contract, or who desires a declaration of his or her rights or duties with respect to another, or in respect to, in, over or upon property, may, in cases of actual controversy relating to the legal rights and duties of the respective parties, bring an original action or cross-complaint in the superior court for a declaration of his or her rights and duties in the premises, including a determination of any question of construction or validity arising under the instrument or contract. He or she may ask for a declaration of rights or duties, either alone or with other relief; ¿and the court may make a binding declaration of these rights or duties, whether or not further relief is or could be claimed at the time. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1060.) The declaration may be either affirmative or negative in form and effect, and the declaration shall have the force of a final judgment. (Id.)

 

a.     Defendant’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

 

As a preliminary matter, the Court notes that Defendant’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings appears to be a hybrid motion, seeking both judgment on the pleadings on somewhat uncertain grounds, along with declaratory relief, essentially amounting to a complete adjudication of the Declaratory Relief causes of action asserted in his Cross-Complaint. The Court will first address Defendant’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings to the extent that the Court cansort out the arguments. Before reaching the merits of Defendant’s arguments, the Court addresses the substantive procedural issues in the Motion itself.

 

                                                  i.     The Notice of Motion is Defective

 

As Plaintiff points out in opposition, Defendant’s notice of motion is defective. A motion for judgment on the pleadings may only be made by a defendant on the grounds that the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the complaint or that the complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant. (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (c)(1)(B).) The motion may be made as to the entire complaint, or as to any of the causes of action stated therein. (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (c)(2)(A).)

 

Defendant’s notice of motion states that he is moving for “judgment on the pleadings and declaratory relief resolving the threshold legal issue that controls this case: whether [Plaintiff] has any presently enforceable right of first refusal or fuel-supply covenant burdening [Defendant’s Property] after the former retailer’s lease was judicially canceled and forfeited.” (Notice of Motion, p. 2:6-9.) Defendant’s notice of motion does not specify on what grounds he is moving, or whether he brings the motion to the entire Complaint or as to any certain causes of action therein. The Notice, therefore, violates Code of Civil Procedure section 1010, which requires notices state “the grounds upon which it will be made,” California Rules of Court, rule 3.1110(a), which requires that a notice of motion “state in the opening paragraph the nature of the order being sought and the grounds for issuance of the order,” and Rules of Court rule 3.1112(d)(4), which requires that a motion must, “[i]f a pleading is challenged, state the specific portion challenged.”

 

“A basic principle of motion practice is that the moving party must specify for the court and the opposing party the grounds upon which that party seeks relief.” (Luri v. Greenwald (2003) 107 Cal. App. 4th 1119, 1125.) “Only the grounds specified in the notice of motion may be considered by the trial court.” (Gonzales v. Superior Court (1987) 189 Cal. App. 3d 1542, 1545.) However, “[a]n omission in the notice may be overlooked if the supporting papers make clear the grounds for the relief sought.” (Luri, supra, 107 Cal.App.4th at 1125.) “Even though the notice of motion fails to state a particular ground for the motion, where the notice states…that the motion is being made upon the notice of motion and accompanying papers and the record, and these papers and the record support that particular ground, the matter is properly before the court and the defect in the notice of motion should be disregarded.” (Carrasco v. Craft (1985) 164 Cal. App. 3d 796, 808.)

 

The Notice states that it is based on the Notice, accompanying Memorandum of Points and Authorities, Request for Judicial Notice, and various other extrinsic evidence (addressed below). All of these documents, including the Motion itself, omit any clear assertion that either the Complaint or certain causes of action fail to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. Within the Memorandum of Points and Authorities, Defendant asserts that each cause of action asserted in the Complaint depends on the premise that the Memorandum remains enforceable. (Motion, p. 5:6-9.) The Motion, generally, argues the opposite. The Court is left to assume, then, that the Motion is brought against the Complaint in its entirety for failing to state facts sufficient to constitute any cause of action on the grounds that the Memorandum is, as a matter of law, unenforceable, and all causes of action therefore must fail. The Motion, however, goes far beyond this assumption. For example, the Motion argues that the LOIs received by Defendant were preliminary and not final matchable transactions (Motion, p. 6:23-24), that Plaintiff failed to provide agreements as requested by Defendant (Motion, p. 7:18-22), that the Property is in dire need of securement and restoration for safe, productive use (Motion, p. .7:24-8:6) and that Plaintiff’s interpretation creates an unreasonable restraint on alienation, and as such, “the Court should construe the Memorandum narrowly” as “[t]he correct construction is simple: no current ROFR exercise right exists absent a final matchable agreement; no supply covenant runs with the fee estate; and any lease-dependent fuel-operation rights ended with the lease forfeiture” (Motion, p. 12:7-14.)

 

As discussed, a motion for judgment on the pleadings is limited only to the face of the challenged pleading and judicially noticeable documents. To the extent the Motion attempts to argue the interpretation of the Memorandum the Court should adopt, that the Court should grant Defendant’s Motion because the Property is in need of remediation, or that the LOIs Defendant received should be considered preliminary and non-binding, these arguments are improper and are disregarded.

 

However, in the interests of adjudicating the present motion, the Court will exercise its discretion to consider the Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings to the extent that it is permissibly argued and intelligible. The Court will rule on the only cognizable interpretation, i.e., that the Motion is brought to the Complaint as a whole on the grounds that “[e]very pleaded cause of action depends on [Plaintiff] proving a surviving, presently enforceable ROFR or Supply Covenant,” and that all causes of action fail because the Complaint fails to state facts sufficient to support an enforceable agreement exists, as all such rights were extinguished by the former tenant’s forfeiture.

 

                                                ii.     Evidence Presented in Support of the Motion

 

The next glaring procedural issue with Defendant’s motion is his attempt to introduce extrinsic evidence to counter the factual allegations in the Complaint. For example, Defendant’s Request for Judicial Notice states “Defendant separately submits Exhibits E and J through the Declaration of Phoenix Thottam for evidentiary purposes: April 6, 2026 correspondence with Anabi's counsel and the nonbinding replacement-operator LOI materials. Defendant also authenticates the factual observations concerning the condition of the Property in his declaration, without asking the Court to judicially notice the truth of disputed facts contained in the other case file.” (RJN, p. 18:2-4 (emphasis added).) Defendant’s Motion states that it is based, in part, on “the exhibits filed concurrently.” (Motion, p. 3:2-5.) Defendant’s Proposed Order, filed concurrently with his Motion, reads, in pertinent part: “Having considered the papers, arguments, pleadings, judicially noticeable records, and evidence submitted…the Court orders as follows...” (Proposed Order, p. 25:6-7.)

 

A motion for judgment on the pleadings on the grounds that a complaint fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, like a demurrer, must be based only on the pleading itself and judicially noticeable documents. Presentation of extrinsic evidence is improper on a motion for judgment on the pleadings, the Court’s consideration thereof is not permitted, and any unresolved factual issues may not be considered. (Sykora, supra, 225 Cal.App.4th at 1534-35.) Much of Defendant’s Motion, all exhibits provided of which the Court did not take judicial notice, and virtually all of Defendant’s Reply must be disregarded for the purposes of ruling on Defendant’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings as they do not speak to the sufficiency of the Complaint, but rather, attempt to persuade the Court to adjudicate disputed factual issues one way or another. Such a request is improper.


Therefore, Defendant’s factual arguments and non-judicially noticed evidence presented in support of the Motion are disregarded. All allegations pled in Plaintiff’s Complaint will be taken as true.

 

                                              iii.     Defendant’s Supplemental Filings, Declarations, and Notices

 

Plaintiff filed separate objections to Defendant’s numerous filings since the filing of the present Motion. The Court does not consider any of Defendant’s filings, all of which pertain to irrelevant circumstances extrinsic to a motion for judgment on the pleadings.

 

                                               iv.     Merits of Defendant’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

 

As discussed, Defendant’s motion rests on one issue: whether the Complaint fails to establish the existence of a legally enforceable ROFR or Supply Covenant.

 

Turning to Defendant’s few permissible arguments regarding the sufficiency of Plaintiff’s Complaint, Defendant contends that “[t]he lease forfeiture is dispositive of all lease-dependent and retailer-dependent rights,” that California law does not permit a party to preserve dependent leasehold interests after the underlying leasehold is properly terminated, and “[t]o the extent [Plaintiff’s] claimed rights depended on the former retailer’s leasehold or operations, they fell with that leasehold.” (Motion, 9:5-16.) Defendant also cites to numerous cases supporting his argument that a ROFR is not an essential term that carries into a holdover tenancy unless the parties so indicate, and Plaintiff’s option rights did not survive the end of the lease relationship with Defendant’s former tenant. (Motion, p. 9:17-24.)

 

The Court notes that there is no final judgment terminating the prior tenancy. In Case No. 26AVCV00038, default judgment was entered in favor of Defendant Thottam and against his former tenant Saib Alrabadi on February 10, 2026, ordering that Defendant is the party entitled to possession of the Property, that the rental agreement was canceled, and the lease was forfeited. Alrabadi’s motion to set aside and vacate the judgment was denied on March 26, 2026. On May 26, 2026, Alrabadi filed a notice of his appeal of the Court’s denial of his motion on March 26, 2026. As of the date of this Order, his appeal is still pending.

 

An action is pending from the time of commencement until its final determination on appeal. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1049.) “[A] judgment does not become final so long as the action in which it was rendered is pending and an action is deemed pending until it is finally determined on appeal or until the time for appeal has passed. The determination of the issue in the case is held in abeyance until the appeal is finally decided by an appellate court and the appeal operates to ‘keep alive the case … as it existed before the judgment was rendered.’” (McKee v. National Union Fire Ins. Co. (1993) 15 Cal. App. 4th 282, 288.)

 

Defendant’s entire argument fails on this ground alone. Defendant requests the Court make a finding that any once-valid ROFR or Supply Covenant granted by Defendant to Plaintiff was terminated upon the default judgment entered in Defendant’s favor. However, this is not a final judgment. If, for any reason, the reviewing court agrees with Alrabadi that the Court erred in denying his motion to vacate the judgment terminating his leasehold, his leasehold would not, in fact, be terminated or forfeited, and any Order of this Court predicated on that termination or forfeiture would necessarily be vacated. The Court therefore declines to entertain this argument.

 

Even if the Court were to consider Alrabadi’s leasehold conclusively terminated, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s Complaint states facts sufficient to establish a valid and enforceable ROFR and Supply Covenant exist. Turning to the Complaint itself, Plaintiff alleges the following relevant facts: (1) Defendant executed the Memorandum of Supply Covenant and ROFR in favor of Plaintiff which was duly recorded against the Property, (2) the Memorandum reflects a direct grant by Defendant of enforceable rights to Plaintiff until at least May 31, 2031, (3) the Memorandum is a binding agreement between Plaintiff and Defendant and applies to the Property and all future transfers, leases, and operations thereof, (4) the rights granted to Plaintiff in the Memorandum are not limited to any particular tenant or leasehold estate, but govern Defendant's conduct as the owner and grantor with respect to the Premises, (5) Defendant is obligated under its terms to provide Plaintiff notice and opportunity to exercise its ROFR in connection with any proposed transfer, lease, or disposition of interest, (6) the ROFR is triggered when Defendant receives or makes a bona fide offer related to such a  transaction (7) the Memorandum obligates Defendant to act in good faith in administering Plaintiff’s rights, and (8) Plaintiff has at all times been ready, willing, and able to exercise its rights under the Memorandum and has acted in good faith to preserve and enforce those rights. (Compl., ¶¶ 14-18.)

 

The recorded Memorandum at issue is attached to the Complaint as Exhibit A, and provided by Defendant as Exhibit A to his Request for Judicial Notice. The Memorandum was recorded on October 12, 2022 and reads, in relevant part, as follows:

Defendant’s argument that the Memorandum terminated upon the termination of Alrabadi’s leasehold is  contradicted by the terms of the Memorandum itself, and as construed in favor of Plaintiff. First, there is no language stating or implying that the Memorandum was in effect only so long as Alrabadi’s leasehold was in effect. As Defendant failed to move for judgment on the pleadings on any other cognizable basis, the Court ends its analysis there. Defendant’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is DENIED.

 

b.     Defendant’s Request for Declaratory Relief

 

As discussed, Defendant’s Motion is largely not focused on the sufficiency of the Complaint itself, but rather, is targeted at resolving factual disputes regarding the parties’ rights and obligations under the Memorandum and seeks various forms of declaratory relief.

 

“In an action for declaratory relief, either a general demurrer or a motion for summary judgment is an appropriate proceeding to declare the rights of the parties if they can be determined as a matter of law.” (Arroyo v. Regents of University of California (1975) 48 Cal. App. 3d 793, 795-796.) "Strictly speaking, a general demurrer is not an appropriate means of testing the merits of the controversy in a declaratory relief action because plaintiff is entitled to a declaration of his rights even if it be adverse. [Citation.] However, where the issue is purely one of law, if the reviewing court agreed with the trial court's resolution of the issue it would be an idle act to reverse the judgment of dismissal for a trial on the merits. In such cases the merits of the legal controversy may be considered on an appeal from a judgment of dismissal following an order sustaining a demurrer without leave to amend and the opinion of the reviewing court will constitute the declaration of the legal rights and duties of the parties concerning the matter in controversy. [Citations].” (Taschner v. City Council (1973) 31 Cal.App.3d 48, 57.)

 

Therefore, the Court may award declaratory relief where a Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is granted without leave to amend and granted as a matter of law. Here, Defendant’s motion is denied. Defendant is not entitled to any declaratory relief related to his Motion.

 

Furthermore, Defendant is incorrect in arguing that Code of Civil Procedure section 1060 permits a standalone request or motion be brought for declaratory relief. Section 1060’s language only permits a party seeking declaratory relief to “bring an original action or cross-complaint in the superior court for a declaration of his or her rights and duties in the premises.” There is no reading of Section 1060 that gives rise to the notion that a party may bring a specific motion or request for declaratory relief, and Defendant has not identified, nor is the Court aware, of any such avenue for relief. Section 1060 gives rise to a party’s right to bring an action for declaratory relief. Defendant has done so in his Cross-Complaint, which is permitted. What Defendant is not permitted to do is intertwine his own causes of action with a motion for judgment on the pleadings in an attempt to obtain a more expeditious result on disputed factual issues that are not able to be adjudicated as a matter of law, which is precisely what Defendant requests the Court do here.

 

Indeed, Defendant’s Motion expressly states: “To the extent the Court can resolve the issue from the face of Anabi's pleading, the recorded Memorandum attached to it, and judicially noticeable court records, the Court may grant judgment on the pleadings under Code of Civil Procedure section 438. To the extent the Court considers declarations, LOIs, April 6 correspondence, and the post-judgment property-condition record, Defendant requests declaratory relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 1060 determining the present validity, enforceability, and scope of the Memorandum.” (Motion, p. 5:14-19.) Defendant’s Motion effectively concedes that his requests for declaratory relief are (1) not determinable as a matter of law, and (2) exceed the scope of the Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings.

 

 

All of Defendant’s request for relief are DENIED.
