DEFENDANTS CREATIVE INVESTMENT GROUP, INC. AND ALL COUNTIES TRUSTEE SERVICE COMPANY’S DEMURRER TO PLAINTIFF’S COMPLAINT

 

Background

On November 12, 2025, Plaintiff Fredrick Douglas Steen (“Steen”) filed this action against Defendants Creative Investment Group, Inc. and All Counties Trustee Service Company (jointly, “Defendants”), alleging eight causes of action for (1) wrongful foreclosure, (2) violation of California Civil Code §§ 2924, 2924c, 2924f, (3) breach of contract, (4) negligence, (5) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (6) unfair business practices (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200), (7) declaratory belief, and (8) injunctive relief.

Defendants demur to Steen’s complaint in its entirety. Steen did not file an opposition.

The Court previously ordered Defendants’ counsel to meet and confer with Steen. Defendants’ counsel filed a declaration on June 22, 2026 attesting to his efforts. Counsel attests that “[p]ursuant to the Court’s order requiring the parties to meet and confer prior to the filing of this Demurrer, I attempted to contact Plaintiff by telephone on June 14, 2026, at approximately 1:03 p.m. again June 17, 2026, at approximately 2:03 p.m., and again on June 19, 2026, at approximately 3:23 p.m. to discuss the deficiencies in the Second Amended Complaint. Plaintiff did not answer any of my calls. I was unable to leave a voicemail message on all three occasions because Plaintiff's voicemail mailbox was full. Despite my good faith efforts to initiate the meet-and-confer process, Plaintiff did not respond, and I was therefore unable to resolve the issues raised in this Demurrer informally.” (Zaki Decl., ¶ 2.) Counsel further states that he has “not heard back from Plaintiff since Plaintiff and Defendant’s counsel last appeared at the Demurrer hearing on June 10, 2026, in Department 508.” (Zaki Decl., ¶ 3.)

The Court notes that Defendants’ counsel cannot meet and confer if Steen does not return his phone calls. Accordingly, the efforts are sufficient, and the Court will hear the demurrer.

Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda¿(2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) In testing the sufficiency of the complaint, the court assumes the truth of properly pleaded factual allegations, facts reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded, and judicially noticed matters. ((Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the¿evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed.” (SKF Farms v. Superior Court¿(1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) Accordingly, “[w]hether the plaintiff will be able to prove the pleaded facts is irrelevant to ruling upon the demurrer.” (Stevens v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 605, 609-10.)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10 subdivisions (e) and (f), a demurrer may be filed if the pleading is uncertain or does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.) A special demurrer for uncertainty, Code of Civil Procedure Section 430.10 subdivision (f), is strictly construed and will only be sustained where the respondent cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him or her. (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Moreover, even if the pleading is somewhat vague, “ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Ibid.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment, otherwise, it is abuse of discretion. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.)

Discussion

Defendants demur to each cause of action of Steen’s complaint for failing to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action and for uncertainty.

A.    Allegations of the Complaint

Steen alleges the following, inter alia, that “[t]his action arises out of the wrongful and illegal foreclosure proceedings initiated by Defendants against Plaintiff’s real property.” (Compl., ¶ 1.) “Plaintiff brings this complaint to challenge the unlawful and procedurally defective foreclosure initiated by Creative Investment Group, Inc. (‘Creative Investment’) through its trustee, All Counties Trustee Service Company (‘All Counties Trustee’), in violation of the California Civil Code and California Homeowner Bill of Rights.” (Compl., ¶ 2 [emphasis omitted].) “On September 28, 2020, Plaintiff executed a Deed of Trust in favor of Creative Investment Group, Inc. securing a loan in the principal sum of $240,000, recorded October 1, 2020,” and “All Counties Trustee Service Company was named and acted as the trustee under the Deed of Trust.” (Compl., ¶¶ 9-10 [emphasis omitted].)

Steen alleges that he “made regular monthly payments and attempted to resolve minor delinquencies through communication with the lender,” but “[o]n October 8, 2024, without proper notice or accounting, Creative Investment Group, Inc. executed a Declaration of Default and Demand for Sale falsely alleging that Plaintiff was in default in the sum of $14,647.25 as of that date.” (Compl., ¶¶ 11-12 [emphasis omitted].) “The Notice of Default was recorded October 11, 2024, by All Counties Trustee Service Company, which later recorded a Notice of Trustee’s Sale dated October 23, 2025, setting a sale for November 18, 2025.” (Compl., ¶ 13 [emphasis omitted].)

Steen further alleges that “[t]he amount claimed in default and the reinstatement figure were grossly inaccurate, and despite Plaintiff’s written and verbal requests, neither the lender nor trustee provided a valid reinstatement or payoff quote, in violation of Civil Code § 2924c.” (Compl., ¶ 14 [emphasis omitted].) “Plaintiff was never properly served with the Notice of Default and Sale as required by Civil Code § 2924b (personal service, posting, and certified mailing).” (Compl., ¶ 15 [emphasis omitted].) “Plaintiff attempted to cure the alleged default but was denied due to Defendants’ failure to provide accurate accounting and reinstatement amounts.” (Compl., ¶ 16.) “Defendants continued foreclosure proceedings despite knowing Plaintiff’s dispute regarding the balance, rendering the foreclosure process void or voidable under California law.” (Compl., ¶ 17 [emphasis omitted].)

B.    Lack of Supporting Facts

Defendants assert that “[a]ll causes of action in the Complaint rely exclusively on the scant ‘Statement of Facts’ for support, which contains only two substantive allegations.” (Dem., 5:1-2.) “Firstly, Plaintiff alleges that the Notice of Default recorded on October 11, 2024, incorrectly stated that the amount in default at that time was $14,647.25. Complaint ¶ 12. Plaintiff does not allege what the correct amount was at the time, only that it was not $14,647.25, and therefore it was ‘invalid.’ Complaint ¶ 14. Plaintiff alleges that he did not cure the default because the amount alleged was inaccurate. Complaint ¶ 16. Plaintiff alleges that this inaccuracy excuses his obligation to tender the amounts due.” (Dem., 5:3-8.) “Secondly, Plaintiff alleges that ‘Plaintiff was never properly served with the Notice of Default and Sale as required by Cal. Civ. Code § 2924b (personal service, posting, and certified mailing).’ Complaint ¶ 15. Plaintiff does not allege how the documents were served or when or how he received them, only that the service upon him was not done ‘properly.’” (Dem., 5:9-12.)

Defendants contend that “[s]pecific facts are missing here that are necessary to any cause of action that Plaintiff might seek: a showing of tender by himself as trustor and prejudice to him by the errors alleged. By failing to allege what the correct amount was, that he offered to pay the correct amount, and that [he] was prevented from reinstatement by the inclusion of the incorrect amount in the notice of default, the Complaint is fatally defective. Furthermore, because Plaintiff has affirmatively alleged that he has NOT tendered any amount to Defendant, it cannot be cured.” (Dem., 5:13-18.)

The Court agrees that the complaint fails to assert sufficient facts and rests on two substantive allegations, as noted by Defendants.

C.    First, Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Seventh, and Eighth Causes of Action

Defendants first contend that “[g]iven the information by the deed of trust beneficiary as to the amount due, the trustor seeking to prevent nonjudicial foreclosure is required to project the amount presently due and to tender that amount as a cure of the default.” (Dem., 5:22-24; see Crossroads Investors, L.P. v. Federal National Mortgage Assn. (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 757, 790-791 [“It is obvious even the owner of the property cannot redeem it from a lien without tendering the amount due under its terms, including the terms governing a default.”].)

“The basic elements of a tort cause of action for wrongful foreclosure track the elements of an equitable cause of action to set aside a foreclosure sale. They are: ‘(1) the trustee or mortgagee caused an illegal, fraudulent, or willfully oppressive sale of real property pursuant to a power of sale in a mortgage or deed of trust; (2) the party attacking the sale (usually but not always the trustor or mortgagor) was prejudiced or harmed; and (3) in cases where the trustor or mortgagor challenges the sale, the trustor or mortgagor tendered the amount of the secured indebtedness or was excused from tendering.’” (Miles v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 394, 408.)

Steen did not allege that he tendered the amount of debt, nor did he allege that he attempted to do so.

Defendants also assert that “[i]f we accept as true Plaintiff’s factual allegation that the notice of default incorrectly stated the amount due, and that he was unable to obtain an accurate reinstatement quote, these facts alone do not state a cause of action. Plaintiff relies on two statutes, §§ 2924(a)(1)(C) and 2924c. Plaintiff has not pleaded facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action predicated on either statute.” (Dem., 5:26-6:2.)

“‘Civil Code sections 2924 through 2924k ... govern nonjudicial foreclosure sales pursuant to a power of sale contained in a deed of trust.’” (Matson v. S.B.S. Trust Deed Network (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 33, 40.) “The primary purpose of a notice of default is to provide notice of the amount in arrears and an opportunity to cure the default. [Citation.] In order for a defect in the notice of default to be material, it must cause prejudice.” (Ram v. OneWest Bank, FSB (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 1, 17.) “Civil Code section 2924c allows the borrower to cure the default, reinstate the loan, and avoid foreclosure by paying the amount in default, plus specified fees and expenses.” (Taniguchi v. Restoration Homes LLC (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 478, 481.)

Defendants contend that “[n]either statute that creates a private right of action for the violation he alleges; neither section 2924 nor section 2924c, subdivision (e) does so. And neither statute is among those identified in section 2924.12, which expressly creates a private right of action for material violation of specified foreclosure provisions.” (Dem., 6:12-15; see Lucioni v. Bank of America, N.A. (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 150, 158-159 [“In the [Homeowner Bill of Rights], the Legislature authorized a private right of action to enjoin a nonjudicial trustee’s sale when a lender violates any one of nine statutory provisions. Under section 2924.12(a)(1), a homeowner may bring an action for injunctive relief due to a material violation of section 2923.55, 2923.6, 2923.7, 2924.9, 2924.10, 2924.11, or 2924.17. Under section 2924.19(a)(1), a homeowner also may bring an action for injunctive relief for a material violation of section 2923.5 or 2924.18… [A] foreclosure may be enjoined due to a material violation of the statutory provisions that the Legislature has chosen to list, but not due to a violation of unlisted provisions.”].)

Further, Defendant contends that “[t]o the extent § 2924c(e) may be read as implicitly acknowledging a private right to sue for reinstatement of a defaulted loan, Plaintiff has not alleged a right to reinstatement,” and “a claim challenging an inaccurate notice of default cannot be maintained without alleging both the ability and willingness to secure reinstatement by paying the correct amount.” (Dem., 6:18-26.)

“To successfully challenge a foreclosure sale based on a procedural irregularity, the plaintiff must show both that there was a failure to comply with the procedural requirements for the foreclosure sale and that the irregularity prejudiced the plaintiff.” (Citrus El Dorado, LLC v. Chicago Title Co. (2019) 32 Cal.App.5th 943, 950.) To reinstate, under the tender rule, a debtor must tender the defaulted amount. “The tender rule arose in the context of redemption cases where the plaintiffs sought to set aside the trustee’s sale for irregularities in the foreclosure sale notice or procedure… Thus, the tender rule ‘is based on the theory that one who is relying upon equity in overcoming a voidable sale must show that he is able to perform his obligations under the contract so that equity will not have been employed for an idle purpose.’” (Turner v. Seterus, Inc. (2018) 27 Cal.App.5th 516, 528.)

Defendants conclude that Steen “fails to state a claim without alleging tender of at least the sum of his missed payments. For example, in Turner the debtor satisfied the tender requirement by acknowledging the amount of the default, claiming the ability to pay that amount, and requesting to pay that amount to cure the default. Id. at 521, 531–532. Plaintiff here has not alleged what he believes the correct amount to be, and without alleging how the notice of default is inaccurate, he cannot allege that he has been damaged by that inaccuracy, or that it was material. Plaintiff has not alleged that he has tendered the amount of the default, or signaled that he could or would pursue reinstatement if the notice of default were accurate. Instead, his factual allegations specifically allege that no such offer has been made, and that he has been excused from any obligation of tender.” (Dem., 7:4-12.)

Based on the law and the foregoing assertions made by Defendants, Steen fails to adequately plead his causes of action. Without pleading that Steen had the willingness and ability to tender or pleading that he actually tendered the payment, Steen cannot maintain his causes of action because they are all predicated on Defendants’ decision to move forward with foreclosure proceedings. Without specific allegations regarding Steen’s tender, the causes of action are insufficiently pled. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer as to the first, fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action with leave to amend. The Court sustains the demurrer to the second cause of action without leave to amend because Civil Code sections 2924, 2924c, and 2924f do not provide for a private right of action. (See Lucioni v. Bank of America, N.A., supra, 3 Cal.App.5th at pp. 158-159.)

D.    Third Cause of Action for Breach of Contract

To state a cause of action for breach of contract, a plaintiff must be able to establish “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.) Under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (g), a demurrer is proper when “an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct.”

Defendants assert that “Plaintiff does not plead the existence of any contractual duty requiring Defendants to accept reinstatement under the alleged circumstances, does not identify any payments that were tendered, and does not allege how any such payments were misapplied in violation of a specific contractual term. The Complaint does not identify any term of the note or deed of trust that Defendants violated. Nor does it allege any contractual provision prohibiting Defendants from initiating foreclosure proceedings following Plaintiff’s default. Plaintiff’s conclusory allegations that Defendants ‘refused reinstatement’ and ‘misapplied payments’ are likewise insufficient.” (Dem., 7:24-8:2.)

The Court finds that Steen does not plead the elements for a breach of contract cause of action because he fails to demonstrate what aspect of the contract Defendants breached and how. Steen alleges that “[t]he Deed of Trust and Promissory Note constitute a valid and enforceable contract. [¶] Plaintiff performed or was excused from performing all obligations thereunder. [¶] Defendant breached the contract by refusing reinstatement and misapplying payments.” (Compl., ¶¶ 24-26.) Steen does not direct the Court to language for a contract between the parties, nor does Steen attach the alleged contracts as exhibits. Steen also does not allege ultimate facts stating how Defendants breached the contract and how Steen was excused from performing. Without this, Steen has not sufficiently alleged his breach of contract cause of action. Accordingly, the demurrer to the third cause of action is sustained.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Defendants’ demurrer to the first, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend. Defendants’ demurrer to the second cause of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

Defendants are ordered to give notice of this Order.

PLAINTIFF’S REQUEST FOR DEFAULT JUDGMENT

           

Plaintiff Launch Development Fund LLC (“Plaintiff”) requests default judgment against Defendants ACND LLC and Sjolund Films, Inc. (jointly, “Defendants”), jointly and severally, in the total amount of $2,729,613.21, comprising $2,709,112.50 in damages, $18,253.75 in attorney fees, and $2,246.96 in costs. Plaintiff also seeks injunctive and declaratory relief, for which Plaintiff submitted a memorandum in support.

Plaintiff first asserts that section 9 of the Bridge Loan Agreement between it and Defendants provides for a takeover of the rights to the picture and an assumption of related contracts. Specifically, the agreement provides “[i]n the event that Borrower fails to repay the Repayment Amount by the Maturity Date or fails to cure any Default within three (3) business days of receipt of written notice of any such failure from Lender, Borrower hereby grants to Lender a non-injunctionable (and implementable without requirement of litigation or judgment) right to take over the Picture and to assume all related contracts thereto.” (Daniels Decl., Ex. A, Bridge Loan Agreement, § 9.)

Plaintiff asserts that “[t]he record establishes that: (1) Defendants failed to repay the Repayment Amount by the Maturity Date of July 15, 2024; (2) Launch sent a written notice of default on May 12, 2025; and (3) Defendants failed to cure within three business days or at any time thereafter… All contractual conditions precedent to the takeover right have been satisfied.” (Mot., 2:24-27.) The Court agrees that the evidence provided supports Plaintiff’s valid security interest in the collateral, as defined in the agreement. (See Daniels Decl., ¶¶ 9-13.)

Plaintiff also asserts that it is entitled to injunctive relief to obtain access to Defendants’ bank account even though the bank, Wells Fargo, is not a party to this action. Under the Security Agreement, Plaintiff is granted “a continuing security interest in and to and mortgage of copyright on all of Borrower's right, title and interest, whether now owned or hereafter acquired, in and to the Collateral and all proceeds derived therefrom,” until the repayment amount is paid. (Daniels Decl., Ex. A, Security Agreement, § 1.)

Plaintiff cites the California Uniform Commercial Code section 9203, subdivision (b), which states that “a security interest is enforceable against the debtor and third parties with respect to the collateral only if each of the following conditions is satisfied: (1) Value has been given. (2) The debtor has rights in the collateral or the power to transfer rights in the collateral to a secured party. (3) One of the following conditions is met: (A) The debtor has signed a security agreement that provides a description of the collateral and, if the security interest covers timber to be cut, a description of the land concerned.” Plaintiff next cites California Uniform Commercial Code section 9609, subdivision (a)(1), which provides that “[a]fter default, a secured party may … [t]ake possession of the collateral.” To proceed under subdivision (a), a secured party may do so “[p]ursuant to judicial process.” (Cal. U. Com. Code § 9609, subd. (b)(1).)

The explicit language of the Security Agreement provides that Plaintiff “may resort to its Security Interest in the Collateral hereunder if the Borrower is in Default of the Bridge Agreement or the Note, [and] Lender shall have all rights and remedies of a ‘secured party’ under the Uniform Commercial Code.” (Daniels Decl., Ex. A, Security Agreement, § 3.) Exhibit 1 to the Security Agreement for collateral includes “[a]ny and all accounts, accounts receivable, general intangibles, contract rights, chattel paper, deposit accounts (including, without limitation, any production bank account)…” (Daniels Decl., Ex. A, Security Agreement, Ex. 1, § a, subd. xi.)

Plaintiff asserts that “[t]he Wells Fargo account ending in 2837, held in the name of Sjolund Films, Inc., is [an] account subject to Launch’s security interest. (Daniels Decl. ¶ 12.) A court order directing possession of this account is consistent with Launch’s rights under California Uniform Commercial Code section 9609 and the parties’ agreement.” (Mot., 4:1-4.)

The Court finds that Plaintiff’s new default judgment package corrected the previous defects. Plaintiff’s memorandum provided the Court with a legal basis for its ability to approve the requested injunctive and declaratory relief when third parties are involved. Thus, Plaintiff sufficiently supported its request with the submitted evidence and declarations. Accordingly, the Court grants Plaintiff’s request and will sign the judgment. No appearance at the hearing is necessary.

PLAINTIFF NATHAN REYES’ DEMURRER TO DEFENDANT’S AMENDED ANSWER TO PLAINTIFF’S COMPLAINT

           

Background

On December 30, 2025, Plaintiff Nathan Reyes (“Plaintiff”) filed this lemon law action against Defendant General Motors, LLC (“GM”), alleging three causes of action under the Song-Beverly Act for (1) breach of express warranty, (2) breach of implied warranty, and (3) violation of section 1793.2(b).

Plaintiff alleges that “[o]n March 28, 2023, Plaintiff acquired a new 2023 Chevrolet Traverse” and “[t]he Vehicle was delivered to Plaintiff with serious defects and nonconformities to warranty and developed other serious defects and nonconformities to warranty including, but not limited to defects relating to the Transmission system.” (Compl., ¶¶ 6, 9.)

On February 4, 2026, GM filed an answer to Plaintiff’s complaint. On February 27, 2026, GM filed an amended answer, setting forth twenty-four affirmative defenses.

Plaintiff now demurs to the second through twenty-fourth affirmative defenses in GM’s amended answer. GM opposes. Plaintiff did not reply.

 

Legal Standard

“A party against whom an answer has been filed may object, by demurrer as provided in¿¿Section 430.30¿, to the answer upon any one or more of the following grounds: (a)¿The answer does not state facts sufficient to constitute a defense. (b)¿The answer is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, ‘uncertain’ includes ambiguous and unintelligible. (c)¿Where the answer pleads a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the answer whether the contract is written or oral.” (¿Code Civ. Proc., § 430.20¿.) Every affirmative defense “¿shall be separately stated, and the several defenses shall refer to the causes of action which they are intended to answer, in a manner by which they may be intelligibly distinguished.¿” (¿Code Civ. Proc., § 431.30, subd. (g)¿.)

“¿Demurring to an answer is a commonly recognized practice. [Citations.] Unlike the usual general demurrer to a complaint the inquiry is not into the statement of a cause of action. Instead it is whether the answer raises a defense to the plaintiff’s stated cause of action. [Citations.] A general demurrer raises the objection that the answer does not state facts sufficient to constitute a defense…” (Timberidge Enterprises, Inc. v. City of Santa Rosa (1978) 86 Cal.App.3d 873, 879-880 (internal quotations omitted)¿.) As a general rule, defendants must allege facts in support of affirmative defenses. ((FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 384 (affirmative defenses “proffered in the form of terse legal conclusions, rather than as facts ‘averred as carefully and with as much detail as the facts which constitute the cause of action and are alleged in the complaint’” are not well pled and cannot survive a demurrer).)

“An important difference is that in the case of a demurrer to the answer, as distinguished from a demurrer to the complaint, the defect in question need not appear on the face of the answer. The determination of the sufficiency of the answer requires an examination of the complaint because its adequacy is with reference to the complaint it purports to answer… Another rule, particularly applicable to the case of a demurrer to the answer, is that each so-called defense must be considered separately without regard to any other defense. [Citations.] Accordingly, a ‘separately stated defense or counterclaim which is sufficient in form and substance when viewed in isolation does not become insufficient when, upon looking at the answer as a whole, that defense or counter[]claim appears inconsistent with or repugnant to other parts of the answer.’” ((South Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 733-734.)

Discussion

Plaintiff demurs to every affirmative defense asserted in GM’s amended answer to Plaintiff’s complaint, except for the first.

A.    GM’s General Denial

As a preliminary matter, GM opposes Plaintiff’s demurrer generally, rather than discussing each affirmative defense.

GM asserts that its affirmative defenses may be premised on a general denial because Plaintiff’s complaint is not verified. GM directs the Court to Code of Civil Procedure section 431.30 subdivision (d), which states that “[i]f the complaint … is not verified, a general denial is sufficient but only puts in issue the material allegations of the complaint.” However, general denials operate differently from affirmative defenses. An affirmative defense is a “statement of any new matter constituting a defense” (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.30, subd. (d)), while a general denial is a blanket denial of the whole complaint. ((Walsh v. West Valley Mission Community College Dist. (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1532, 1545 [“The filing of a general denial denies in one sentence all the allegations of the complaint”].) Accordingly, this standard does not apply to GM’s answer.

GM also asserts that the demurrer fails because Plaintiff is on notice of GM’s affirmative defenses. Because pleadings are liberally construed, GM contends “[t]his is sufficient, as this is what is required of defendant.” (Opp., 5:2.) However, as noted above, merely placing a plaintiff on general notice is insufficient; an answer must allege facts in support of affirmative defenses, rather than legal conclusions.

B.    Second to Fourth, Sixth to Seventeenth, and Nineteenth to Twenty-Fourth Affirmative Defenses for Failure to State a Cause of Action

Plaintiff demurs to “Defendant’s Second (2) through Fourth (4), Sixth (6) through Seventeenth (17), and Nineteenth (19) through Twenty-fourth (24) Affirmative Defenses [for] fail[ing] to state facts sufficient to constitute a defense in violation of Code of Civil Procedure, Sections 430.20(a) or 431.30(b), or [for] uncertain[ity] under Code of Civil Procedure, Section 430.20(b).” (Dem., 3:24-27.)

GM opposes Plaintiff’s contentions, stating that “Plaintiff’s counsel seemingly assumes that ‘ultimate facts’ are equivalent to the evidence a party might produce at trial after depositions, written discovery, and any other fact finding tool used in the discovery process.” (Opp., 3:23-25.) GM cites to Doheny Park Terrace Homeowners Assn., Inc. v. Truck Ins Ins. Exchange (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 1076, 1098-1099, where the Court of Appeal stated that “[w]hile it is true that pleading conclusions of law does not fulfill th[e] requirement [to plead facts], it has long been recognized that [t]he distinction between conclusions of law and ultimate facts is not at all clear and involves at most a matter of degree. [Citations.] For example, the courts have permitted allegations which obviously included conclusions of law and have termed them ultimate facts or conclusions of fact. [Citations.] What is important is that the complaint as a whole contain sufficient facts to apprise the defendant of the basis upon which the plaintiff is seeking relief.” (Internal quotations omitted, emphasis in original.) GM asserts that “by misconstruing the meaning of ‘ultimate facts’, Plaintiff’s demurrer advocates that GM’s Answer is subject to a higher pleading standard than what is required by law. Plaintiff’s demurrer does not even attempt to identify how such defenses are purportedly defective beyond the blanket appeal that they lack specificity and fail to plead ultimate facts.” (Opp., 4:5-8.)

GM avers that “Plaintiff’s position is particularly inconsistent because, throughout the demurrer, Plaintiff repeatedly faults GM for allegedly failing to plead causation, failing to explain how certain conduct supports a defense, and failing to provide additional factual detail. In nearly every challenged affirmative defense, Plaintiff asks ‘how’ the alleged conduct constitutes the defense asserted, demands an explanation of causation, or insists on further factual elaboration. Yet Plaintiff’s own Complaint does not come close to satisfying the standard he seeks to impose on GM. The Complaint merely references unspecified ‘transmission defects’ and offers no explanation whatsoever as to what those alleged transmission problems were, how they manifested, when they occurred, what symptoms Plaintiff experienced, or how they allegedly constituted a breach of warranty. Plaintiff cannot simultaneously demand exhaustive factual explanations from GM’s Answer while relying on a Complaint that provides only a conclusory reference to alleged ‘transmission defects.’” (Opp., 4:9-19.)

The Court recognizes that the adequacy of an answer must be read “with reference to the complaint it purports to answer” (see South Shore Land Co. v. Petersen, supra, 226 Cal.App.2d at p. 733.), and, here, Plaintiff’s complaint does not allege specific details about the transmission defect, the repairs Plaintiff’s vehicle underwent, or how often or how many times Plaintiff’s brought the vehicle in for repairs. Accordingly, the amended answer must be analyzed through the lens of the initial complaint’s lack of specificity.

In support of the second affirmative defense (failure to allow a cure), GM alleges “that Plaintiff is barred from obtaining the relief sought in the Unverified Complaint because Plaintiff has failed and refused to allow GM a reasonable opportunity to cure any alleged breach by GM.” (Answer, 2:17-19.) Plaintiff asserts that “[t]his defense is insufficient in that it is simply a legal conclusion and alleges no facts sufficient to constitute a defense.” (Dem., 4:18-19.) GM does not support its assertion with facts. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the second affirmative defense.

In support of the third affirmative defense (mitigation), GM alleges “that the damages alleged by Plaintiff, if any, are the result, in whole or in part, of Plaintiff’s failure to exercise care to reduce or mitigate damages by conduct including Plaintiff’s failure to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects.” (Answer, 2:24-27.) Plaintiff asserts that “[a]lthough Defendant states that Plaintiff failed to timely present the Vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects, Defendant fails to state how this assertion constitutes a failure to mitigate. Defendant also fails to define ‘timely.’” (Dem., 5:6-8.) GM adequately asserts its affirmative defense because it alleges a specific fact, namely that Plaintiff failed to timely present the vehicle for service. Accordingly, the Court overrules the demurrer to the third affirmative defense.

In support of the fourth affirmative defense (unclean hands), GM alleges “Plaintiff is barred in whole or in part by Plaintiff's unclean hands from any and all legal or equitable relief against GM, as requested in the Unverified Complaint or otherwise by conduct including changes or alterations to the vehicle’s software and programming, and by Plaintiff’s failure to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects.” (Answer, 3:3-7.) Plaintiff asserts that “[a]lthough Defendant states that Plaintiff failed to timely present the Vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects, Defendant fails to state how this assertion constitutes Unclean Hands. Defendant also fails to define ‘timely.’” (Dem., 5:6-8.) GM adequately asserts its affirmative defense because GM alleges an ultimate fact, specifically that Plaintiff changed or altered the vehicle and failed to timely present the vehicle for service. Accordingly, the Court overrules the demurrer to the fourth affirmative defense.

In support of the sixth affirmative defense (laches), GM alleges “Plaintiff waited an unreasonable length of time to complain of the alleged acts or omissions at issue in the Unverified Complaint to prejudice GM by conduct including Plaintiff’s failure to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects. The doctrine of laches therefore bars recovery.” (Answer, 3:24-27.) Plaintiff asserts that “[a]lthough Defendant states that Plaintiff failed to timely present the Vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects, Defendant fails to state how this assertion constitutes Laches. Defendant also fails to define ‘timely.’” (Dem., 5:26-28.) GM does not support its assertion because it is unclear how the alleged failure to timely present the vehicle for service connects to the alleged “unreasonable length of time to complain of the alleged acts” at issue in the complaint. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the sixth affirmative defense.

In support of the seventh affirmative defense (failure of performance), GM alleges “that any failure to perform the obligations as described in the Unverified Complaint resulted from Plaintiff's failure to perform as required by the contract and warranty by conduct including Plaintiff’s failure to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects. Such performance by Plaintiff is a condition precedent to the performance of GM’s obligations.” (Answer, 4:3-7.) Plaintiff asserts that “[a]lthough Defendant states that Plaintiff failed to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects, Defendant fails to state how this constitutes a failure of performance. Defendant also fails to define ‘timely.’” (Dem., 6:8-10.) GM provides facts supporting that Plaintiff failed to perform under the contract and warranty by not timely presenting the vehicle for repairs. Accordingly, the Court overrules the demurrer to the seventh affirmative defense.

In support of the eighth affirmative defense (apportionment), GM alleges that “[a]ny and all injuries, if any, and damages, if any, allegedly sustained or suffered by Plaintiff was proximately caused and contributed to by the superseding, intervening acts or omissions of persons other than GM by conduct including changes or alterations to the vehicle’s software and programming, and by Plaintiff’s failure to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects. Those persons, and each of them, were careless and negligent concerning the matters alleged in the Unverified Complaint, and their negligence and carelessness proximately contributed to the loss, injury, damage, or detriment alleged in the Unverified Complaint, with the result that the damages, if any, recoverable by Plaintiff must be diminished in proportion to the fault attributable to those other persons.” (Answer, 4:12-14.) Plaintiff asserts that “[t]his defense offers no factual support as to who purportedly ‘contributed’ to the alleged injuries suffered by Plaintiff, nor how these acts or omissions resulted in the injuries.” (Dem., 6:12-13.) GM fails to discuss or explain the purported intervening cause. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the eighth affirmative defense.

In support of the ninth affirmative defense (misuse of product), GM alleges that “[a]ny and all injuries, if any, and damages, if any, allegedly sustained or suffered by Plaintiff were directly and proximately caused and contributed to by the misuse of and the unreasonable and improper use of GM’s product by conduct including changes or alterations to the vehicle’s software and programming, and by Plaintiff’s failure to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects. The misuse of or failure to use GM’s product properly contributed to the loss, injury, damage, or detriment, if any, alleged in the Unverified Complaint, and the damages, if any, recoverable by Plaintiff must be diminished in proportion to the amount of fault attributable to that misuse or unreasonable or improper use.” (Answer, 4:21-28.) Plaintiff asserts that “[a]lthough GM claims there were changes or alterations to the vehicle's software or programming, and that Plaintiff failed to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects, GM fails to state what these changes or alterations were, what ‘timely present’ means, and how they constitute misuse of the product.” (Dem., 7:1-4.) GM asserts that Plaintiff improperly used the vehicle by making changes or alterations, which alleges a fact. Accordingly, the Court overrules the demurrer to the ninth affirmative defense.

In support of the tenth affirmative defense (alteration of product), GM alleges that “[a]ny damage to the subject vehicle was caused or created by changes or alterations to the vehicle, after GM manufactured and sold the vehicle, by persons other than GM or any of its agents, servants, or employees, barring Plaintiff’s recovery in this action.” (Answer, 5:3-6.) Plaintiff asserts that “[t]his defense is insufficient in that it is simply a legal conclusion and alleges no facts sufficient to constitute a defense.” (Dem., 6:6-7.) GM does not plead facts supporting its assertion that Plaintiff altered his vehicle. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer as to the tenth affirmative defense.

In support of the eleventh affirmative defense (disclaimer), GM alleges “that before and at the time of the alleged acts, omissions, and conduct of GM alleged in the Unverified Complaint, GM had expressly disclaimed, negated, and excluded all warranties of the type alleged by Plaintiff, and of any type, express or implied, whatsoever, in connection with changes or alterations to the vehicle’s software and programming.” (Answer, 5:9-13.) Plaintiff asserts that “[a]lthough GM states that any damage to the vehicle was caused or created by ‘unforeseen changes or alterations to the vehicle’s software and programming’, GM fails to indicate what these changes are and how they constitute an alteration of product. As such, this defense is ambiguous and otherwise uncertain since it is unclear what the effects of alleged alterations are.” (Dem., 7:12-15.) As Plaintiff asserts, GM does not plead facts indicating what the changes are and how they constituted an alteration, making the answer ambiguous. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the eleventh affirmative defense.

In support of the twelfth affirmative defense (lack of causation), GM alleges that “[n]o act or omission of GM was the cause in fact or the proximate cause of the alleged injuries and damages, if any, sustained by Plaintiff and instead caused by conduct including changes or alterations to the vehicle’s software and programming, and by Plaintiff's failure to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects. GM is informed and believes, and therefore alleges, that any breach of warranty of fitness or merchantability, if any, and any other breach of warranty, if any, and any breach of contractual undertakings by GM, if any, were neither the cause in fact nor the proximate cause of Plaintiff's damages. Rather, any alleged breach was only secondary, inconsequential, indirect and in no way contributed to or caused Plaintiff’s alleged damages.” (Answer, 5:16-24.) Plaintiff asserts that “[t]his defense is insufficient in that it is simply a legal conclusion and alleges no facts sufficient to constitute a defense.” (Dem., 7:27-28.) However, GM pleads facts stating that it is due to Plaintiff’s alleged alterations. Accordingly, the Court overrules the demurrer to the twelfth affirmative defense.

In support of the thirteenth affirmative defense (unauthorized use of the product), GM alleges “that any and all damages, if any, allegedly sustained or suffered by Plaintiff was proximately caused and contributed to by Plaintiff’s unreasonable use of the subject vehicle for a purpose to which the subject vehicle was not intended to be used by conduct including changes or alterations to the vehicle's software and programming, and by Plaintiff’s failure to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects. Plaintiff knew or should have known that the use to which Plaintiff put the vehicle was not the use for which the vehicle was manufactured or intended and that the unintended use could cause damage to Plaintiff.” (Answer, 5:27-6:6.) Plaintiff asserts that “[t]his defense is insufficient in that it is simply a legal conclusion and alleges no facts sufficient to constitute a defense.” (Dem., 8:10-11.) GM alleges facts stating that the unauthorized use of the product was due to modifications Plaintiff made to the vehicle. Accordingly, the Court overrules the demurrer to the thirteenth affirmative defense.

In support of the fourteenth affirmative defense (estoppel), GM alleges “that Plaintiff is estopped from obtaining the relief sought in the Unverified Complaint by virtue of Plaintiff’s acts and conduct in connection with the matters alleged in the Unverified Complaint by conduct including changes or alterations to the vehicle’s software and programming, and by Plaintiff’s failure to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects.” (Answer, 6:9-13.) Plaintiff asserts that “GM fails to state what these changes or alterations were, what ‘timely present’ means, and how these assertions constitute estoppel.” (Dem., 9:2-3.) GM pleads facts sufficient to state an affirmative defense, and Plaintiff appears to seek a higher level of specificity than is necessary for the answer when read against the allegations in the complaint. Accordingly, the Court overrules the demurrer to the fourteenth affirmative defense.

In support of the fifteenth affirmative defense (waiver), GM alleges “that Plaintiff has waived Plaintiff’s rights, if any, to obtain the relief sought in the Unverified Complaint by conduct including changes or alterations to the vehicle’s software and programming, and by Plaintiff’s failure to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects.” (Answer, 5:9-10.) Plaintiff asserts that “[t]his defense is insufficient in that it is simply a legal conclusion and alleges no facts sufficient to constitute a defense.” (Dem., 9:5-6.) GM provides facts stating how Plaintiff waived his rights. Accordingly, the Court overrules the demurrer to the fifteenth affirmative defense.

In support of the sixteenth affirmative defense (comparative negligence), GM alleges “that Plaintiff’s damages, if any, were the direct and proximate result of the conduct of Plaintiff or Plaintiff’s agents, employees, and invitees, in that they negligently, carelessly, recklessly, knowingly, and willfully operated, maintained, serviced, directed, and otherwise controlled all operations and maintenance of the subject vehicle by conduct including changes or alterations to the vehicle's software and programming, and by Plaintiff's failure to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects. Plaintiff’s damages, if any, were directly and proximately caused, in whole or in part, or were contributed to or aggravated by the conduct of Plaintiff or Plaintiff's agents, employees, and invitees, when they negligently, carelessly, recklessly, knowingly, and willfully failed to repair the subject vehicle, knowing that the subject vehicle needed service, maintenance, or repair, but instead, proceeded to operate, maintain, navigate, direct, and otherwise make use of the subject vehicle or made improper and inadequate service, maintenance and repairs to it. GM is further informed and believes, and therefore alleges, that the subject vehicle owner knowingly and willfully authorized these actions and knowingly and willfully assumed the known risk that these actions would cause, compound, or aggravate the known problems with the subject vehicle and would proximately cause damage to it.” (Answer, 6:22-7:9.) Plaintiff asserts that “[t]his defense is insufficient in that it is simply a legal conclusion and alleges no facts sufficient to constitute a defense.” (Dem., 9:16-17.) GM provides a longer, more detailed answer, which includes facts that Plaintiff did not timely repair the vehicle and Plaintiff modified it, evidence that Plaintiff could have been comparatively negligent for the vehicle’s problems. Accordingly, the Court overrules the demurrer to the sixteenth affirmative defense.

In support of the seventeenth affirmative defense (assumption of risk), GM alleges that “[t]he risks and dangers in Plaintiff’s conduct were known to Plaintiff, but Plaintiff conducted themselves in such a manner as to expose themselves and remain exposed to such risks and dangers and, by doing so, assumed all the risks attendant to that conduct including changes or alterations to the vehicle’s software and programming, and by Plaintiff’s failure to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects. At the time(s), date(s), and place(s) of the events described in the Unverified Complaint, Plaintiff voluntarily assumed the risks of the activities in which Plaintiff was then and there engaged. Under the circumstances and conditions then and there existing, the resulting injuries, if any, and damages, if any, sustained by Plaintiff were proximately caused by Plaintiff’s own voluntary assumption of risk.” (Answer, 7:12-20.) Plaintiff asserts that “[a]lthough GM claims there were changes or alterations to the vehicle’s software or programming, and that Plaintiff failed to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects, GM fails to state what these changes or alterations were, what ‘timely present’ means, and how these assertions constitute assumption of the risk.” (Dem., 10:5-8.) GM does not take the time to state the dates, times, and places of the events described in Plaintiff’s complaint. Stating “[a]t the time(s), date(s), and place(s) of the events described in the Unverified Complaint…” lacks a basic level of specificity that an answer to a complaint requires. Even if Plaintiff’s complaint lacks specifics, this should be noted, rather than inserting a boilerplate assertion. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the seventeenth affirmative defense.

In support of the nineteenth affirmative defense (good faith belief in legality of actions), GM alleges that “GM acted in good faith and believes its actions to be legal.” (Answer, 8:5-6.) Plaintiff asserts that “this defense is insufficient in that it is simply a legal conclusion and alleges no facts sufficient to constitute a defense.” (Dem., 10:10-12.) GM does not support its assertion with support for why it has a good faith belief that it acted legally. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the nineteenth affirmative defense.

In support of the twentieth affirmative defense (offset for use), GM alleges that it “intends to avail itself of the reduction authorized by Civil Code section 1793.2(d)(2)(C) if GM is found to violate the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act.” (Answer, 8:9-10.) Plaintiff asserts that “this defense is insufficient in that it is simply a legal conclusion and alleges no facts sufficient to constitute a defense.” (Dem., 10:20-21.) GM’s allegations are insufficient because GM does not allege that Plaintiff utilized the vehicle. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the twentieth affirmative defense.

In support of the twenty-first affirmative defense (implied warranty), GM alleges that “[e]ach and every cause of action based on breach of implied warranty is barred by Civil Code § 1791.1(c) to the extent that the subject vehicle’s express warranty had been breached by conduct including changes or alterations to the vehicle’s software and programming, and by Plaintiff’s failure to timely present the vehicle for service or maintenance in connection with the alleged transmission defects.” (Answer, 8:14-17.) Plaintiff asserts that “[t]his defense is insufficient, in that it is simply a legal conclusion and alleges no facts sufficient to constitute a defense.” (Dem., 11:3-4.) GM’s affirmative implied warranty defense appears convoluted and does not allege supporting facts that Plaintiff breached the express warranty, which, as GM contends, bars the implied warranty. GM rests its answer on a legal conclusion, not an ultimate fact. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the twenty-first affirmative defense.

In support of the twenty-second affirmative defense (consent), GM alleges “[t]he repair process to the subject vehicle was appropriate and proper and is believed to have been done with Plaintiff’s consent.” (Answer, 8:20-21.) Plaintiff asserts that “Defendant states that GM repaired Plaintiff’s vehicle appropriately and properly with Plaintiff’s consent, but this defense is insufficient in that it is simply a legal conclusion and alleges no facts sufficient to constitute a defense.” (Dem., 11:10-12.) GM does not support why it believes it had Plaintiff’s consent, nor does it state how the vehicle was appropriately repaired. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the twenty-second affirmative defense.

In support of the twenty-third affirmative defense (failure to provide notice), GM alleges “that Plaintiff failed to provide notice to GM pursuant to Civil Code section 1794(e)(3). Plaintiff is, therefore, barred from asserting the presumptions in Civil Code section 1793.22 and from recovering civil penalties pursuant to Civil Code section 1794(e).” (Answer, 8:24-27.) Plaintiff asserts that “[t]his is simply a legal conclusion and alleges no facts sufficient to constitute a defense.” (Dem., 11:19.) GM does not support its assertion with any facts. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the twenty-third affirmative defense.

In support of the twenty-fourth affirmative defense (failure to state a cause of action for civil penalties), GM alleges the “Complaint fails to state sufficient facts to warrant the imposition of civil penalties if replacement or repurchase of the subject vehicle was not appropriate under the circumstances then known, or if GM offered to repurchase or replace the subject vehicle.” (Answer, 9:3-5.) Plaintiff asserts that “this defense is insufficient in that it is simply a legal conclusion and alleges no facts sufficient to constitute a defense.” (Dem., 11:24-25.) GM does not support its assertion with any facts; it merely states a legal conclusion. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer as to the twenty-fourth affirmative defense.

C.    Fifth Affirmative Defense for Statutes of Limitation

Plaintiff asserts that GM’s fifth affirmative defense is inadequately pled because it does not assert the proper subdivisions for its statute of limitations defense. Plaintiff relies on Davenport v. Stratton (1944) 24 Cal.2d 232, 246-247, where the Court stated that a pleading must specify both the applicable statute and subdivision. ((See Ibid. [“a plea of the statute of limitations, alleging that a cause of action is barred by the Code of Civil Procedure, section 339, is insufficient, since that statute contains several subdivisions”].) Moreover, Code of Civil Procedure section 458 states that “[i]n pleading the Statute of Limitations it is not necessary to state the facts showing the defense, but it may be stated generally that the cause of action is barred by the provisions of Section ____ (giving the number of the section and subdivision thereof, if it is so divided, relied upon) of the Code of Civil Procedure.”

However, GM’s amended answer states various statutes with subdivisions. The amended answer explicitly states that “[b]ecause the Complaint does not provide sufficient facts to determine the timing of each of the type of claims that may be barred under this Chapter, Defendant asserts all these specific statute sections and specific subdivisions as affirmative defenses. Commercial Code § 2725(2) and (3) contain conditions regarding the parties’ conduct that may change how the timing of certain conduct affects the application of the statute.” (Answer 3:14-18.) Accordingly, the Court overrules the demurrer to the fifth affirmative defense.

D.    Eighteenth Affirmative Defense for Qualified Third-Party Dispute Resolution Process

Plaintiff demurs to GM’s eighteenth affirmative defense for not being a legally recognized affirmative defense. In support, Plaintiff merely states “[t]his is not a legally recognized affirmative defense, and therefore improper, and the demurrer to this defense must be sustained.” (Dem., 12:19-20.) Plaintiff provides the Court with no discussion, explanation, or law to support why this is not a legally recognized defense. Plaintiff only demurs to this affirmative defense for not being legally recognized. Accordingly, the Court overrules the demurrer to the eighteenth affirmative defense.

Conclusion

In light of the foregoing, Plaintiff’s demurrer to the third, fourth, fifth seventh, ninth, twelfth, thirteenth, fourteenth, fifteenth, sixteenth, and eighteenth affirmative defense is OVERRULED. Plaintiff’s demurrer as to the second, sixth, eighth, tenth, eleventh, seventeenth, nineteenth, twentieth, twenty-first, twenty-first, twenty-second, twenty-third, and twenty-fourth affirmative defenses is SUSTAINED, with leave to amend.

GM is ordered to file and serve an amended answer, if any, within 20 days of the date of this Order.

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of this Order.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: 

 

JOHN ARCAS AND MARIA ARCAS’ MOTION TO CORRECT CLERICAL ERROR IN APPLICATION FOR AND RENEWAL OF JUDGMENT

           

            Background

On October 31, 2025, Judgment Creditors John Arcas and Maria Arcas (jointly, “Creditors”) filed an “Application for and Renewal of Judgment.” The original judgment was made on January 3, 1996 and was previously renewed on November 10, 2016.

Creditors now move or an order correcting a clerical error in the Application for and Renewal of Judgment filed on October 31, 2025.

            Discussion

Creditors “seek[] to correct the misspelling of Judgment Debtor Stanely Hollis (‘Debtor’) in the Application for and Renewal of Judgment (Form EJ-190) so that the renewal accurately reflects the judgment debtor’s name as it appears in the original judgment entered on January 3, 1996.” (Notice of Mot., 2:7-10.) Creditors make their motion pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d) which states that “[t]he court may, upon motion of the injured party, or its own motion, correct clerical mistakes in its judgment or orders as entered, so as to conform to the judgment or order directed, and may, on motion of either party after notice to the other party, set aside any void judgment or order.”

Creditors’ motion contends that “[b]ecause the underlying judgment is correct and the error appears only in the renewal paperwork, the correction is purely clerical. The Court has authority under Code of Civil Procedure section 473(d) to correct clerical mistakes in its records at any time.” (Mot., 3:9-11.) In support, Creditors’ counsel provides a declaration, which states that “[t]he Court entered judgment in this case on January 2, 1996,” which “correctly identifies the Judgment Debtor as Stanley Hollis.” (Rowell Decl., ¶ 2; Ex. A.) Counsel explains that “[o]n October 31, 2025, I filed an Application for and Renewal of Judgment (Form EJ-190),” but “[d]ue to a clerical and typographical error, the debtor’s first name was misspelled on the renewal form. The original judgment contains the correct spelling.” (Rowell Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) Counsel attests that he “attempted to file an amended renewal to correct the spelling, but the clerk rejected it and advised that a noticed motion and court order are required.” (Rowell Decl., ¶ 5.) Counsel clarifies that “[t]he correction requested does not alter the judgment, and a new party, or change substantive rights. It merely corrects the renewal paperwork so it conforms to the judgment.” (Rowell Decl., ¶ 6.)

The Court reviewed the original judgment and the application for renewal. The Court finds that the application inadvertently listed the incorrect spelling for Judgment Debtor Stanley Hollis as “Stanely Hollis.” Thus, the application should be corrected to reflect the correct name.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Creditors motion to correct the clerical error in the Application for and Renewal of Judgment is GRANTED.

The Application for and Renewal of Judgment filed on October 31, 2025, is corrected to reflect the Judgment Debtor’s name as Stanley Hollis, consistent with the original judgment entered on January 3, 1996. The corrected application is deemed filed on its original filing date.

Creditors are ordered to provide notice of this Order.
