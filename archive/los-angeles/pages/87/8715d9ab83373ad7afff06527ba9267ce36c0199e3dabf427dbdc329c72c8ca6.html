ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On February 5, 2025, Farmers Insurance Exchange (“Farmers Insurance”) was dismissed from both Case Nos. 22STCV24750 and 23STCV03400.

 

On July 16, 2026, default was entered against Defendant Bacon-Up Corporation (“Bacon-Up”).

 

On August 13, 2026, Non-Party Farmers Insurance filed the instant Motion for Leave to Intervene (the “Motion”).

 

On August 17, 2026, Plaintiff Zia Abhari (“Plaintiff”) filed an Opposition.

 

On August 25, 2026, Farmers Insurance filed a Reply.

 

On August 26, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Objection to the Reply.

 

II.              Procedural Issues

As an initial matter, the Court finds the Motion was filed and served one day late pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1005 and 1010.6. However, given the timely Opposition that addresses the Motion on the merits, the Court exercises its discretion to address the Motion on the merits despite Plaintiff’s objection.

 

Additionally, the Court finds there are no specific evidentiary objections for the Court to rule on in Plaintiff’s Objection to the Reply. Rather, the Objection merely reiterates arguments raised in the Opposition.

III.            Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure, section 387, subdivisions (c) and (d) provide:

(c) A nonparty shall petition the court for leave to intervene by noticed motion or ex parte application. The petition shall include a copy of the proposed complaint in intervention or answer in intervention and set forth the grounds upon which intervention rests.

 

(d) (1) The court shall, upon timely application, permit a nonparty to intervene in the action or proceeding if either of the following conditions is satisfied:

 

(A) A provision of law confers an unconditional right to intervene.

 

(B) The person seeking intervention claims an interest relating to the property or transaction that is the subject of the action and that person is so situated that the disposition of the action may impair or impede that person’s ability to protect that interest, unless that person’s interest is adequately represented by one or more of the existing parties.

 

(2) The court may, upon timely application, permit a nonparty to intervene in the action or proceeding if the person has an interest in the matter in litigation, or in the success of either of the parties, or an interest against both.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 387, subds. (c), (d).)

 

Whether the petitioner has an interest in the matter in litigation is a question of fact that must be determined by the court before leave to file is granted.  (See Muller v. Robinson (1959) 174 Cal.App.2d 511, 515.) The burden rests on the one seeking to intervene to show that this is a proper case for intervention. (Id., at p. 515.)   

 

IV.            Discussion

 

Farmers Insurance provides evidence stating that it has a direct interest in the action given that it formerly insured Bacon-Up, and Bacon-Up “is dissolved and therefore … it cannot defend itself.” (Motion, p. 3, Collins Decl., ¶ 3.) “Given that Plaintiff seeks an entry of default against Bacon-Up, [Farmers Insurance] therefore seeks leave of court to intervene and defend both its interests as well as those of Bacon-Up, it’s insured, both of which would be adversely affected if [Farmers Insurance is] not provided leave to intervene.” (Motion, Collins Decl., ¶ 3.)

 

In Opposition, Plaintiff contends Farmers Insurance is attempting to “illegally obstruct the entry of a default judgment that is about to be entered” against Bacon-Up and attempt to prematurely raise coverage defenses against Plaintiff. (See Opp., pp. 5-7.) Plaintiff states that Farmers Insurance must first satisfy the requirement of Revenue and Taxation Code section 19719(b) prior to moving to intervene (Motion, pp. 5-6), and Plaintiff refutes potential challenges and defenses Farmers Insurance will make as to service on Bacon-Up and/or Bacon-Up’s status in this action. (Motion, pp. 6-7.) Finally, Plaintiff states the actions Plaintiff plans to take in the action regarding Bacon-Up. (Motion, pp. 7-8.) These arguments are emphasized in the Objection to the Reply as well.

 

In Reply, Farmers Insurance emphasizes arguments raised in the Motion and refutes Plaintiff’s erroneous assertion that Farmers Insurance is attempting to “exempt itself from criminal penalties pursuant to Revenue and Taxation Code Section 19719(b).” (Reply, pp. 2-3, 5.) Further, Farmers Insurance disputes Plaintiff’s use and interpretation of certain case law in the instant matter. (Reply, pp. 4-5.)

 

Here, Farmers Insurance has an interest in the outcome of this action because it insures Bacon-Up and will face direct liability if it is not permitted to intervene. (See Reliance Ins. Code v. Superior Court (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th 383, 386 [“An insurer’s right to intervene in an action against the insured, for personal injury or property damage, arises as a result of Insurance Code section 11580. Section 11580 provides that a judgment creditor may proceed directly against any liability insurance covering the defendant, and obtain satisfaction of judgment up to the amount of the policy limits”].) Farmers Insurance represents that Bacon-Up is a dissolved entity who is not defending this action, which thus necessitated the instant Motion, and such circumstances are permissible grounds on which to grant a motion to intervene. (See id., at p. 387.) Finally, the Motion is timely as default judgment has not yet been entered against Bacon-Up. (See id., at pp. 386-387 [“[W]here the insurer may be subject to a direct action under Insurance Code section 11580 by a judgment creditor who has or will obtain a default judgment in a third party action against the insured, intervention is appropriate”].)

 

To the extent Plaintiff raises Revenue and Taxation Code section 19719(b) as prohibitive of Farmers Insurance’s intervention, the Court finds this assertion is refuted by the statute itself. Indeed, Revenue and Taxation Code section 19719(b) states that the fine or imprisonment referenced in subdivision (a) does “not apply to any insurer, or to counsel retained by an insurer on behalf of the suspended corporation, who provides a defense for a suspended corporation in a civil action based upon a claim for personal injury, property damage, or economic losses against the suspended corporation, and, in conjunction with this defense, prosecutes subrogation, contribution, or indemnity rights against persons or entities in the name of the suspended corporation.” Here, Farmers Insurance is an insurer providing a defense to dissolved Bacon-Up in a civil action upon a claim for permissible damages by the statute and is moving to intervene on behalf of the dissolved Bacon-Up. Thus, Revenue and Taxation Code section 19719(b) does not require the Court to deny the instant Motion.

 

As to Plaintiff’s contentions that Farmers Insurance’s potential future arguments for and defenses of Bacon-Up warrant denial of the Motion, the Court does not find these arguments to be relevant or persuasive to the determination of the Motion. Plaintiff fails to provide any legal authority mandating the Court determine otherwise.

 

V.              Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Non-Party Farmers Insurance Exchange’s Motion for Leave to Intervene is GRANTED. Moving party is to file a responsive pleading within the next 10 days.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On January 21, 2026, Plaintiff Jamie Merchain (“Plaintiff”) filed a Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) against Defendant Los Angeles Dodgers, LLC (“LA Dodgers”) (“Defendant”) and Does 1-50, alleging causes of action for Premises Liability and Negligence.

 

On March 4, 2026, the Court held an Informal Discovery Conference.

 

On June 1, 2026, Non-Party Kanarek filed the instant Motion to Quash LA Dodgers’ Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records to Kanarek for Privileged and Protected Records and/or Motion for Protective Order (the “Motion”).

 

On July 23, 2026, Defendants filed an Omnibus Opposition to the instant Motion as well as other motions.

 

That same day, the Court continued the hearing on the Motion to September 1, 2026.

 

On August 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Third Amended Complaint (“TAC”) against Defendants Los Angeles Dodgers, LLC and Chavez Ravine Land Company LLC (“Defendants”) and Does 1-50, alleging causes of action for Premise Liability and Negligence. The underlying action involves Plaintiff tripping and falling over an “uplifted portion of raised asphalt, believed to have been caused by an uplifted tree root… positioned at/near the Centerfield entrance gate located in the Right Pavilion between the parking lot and Dodger Stadium on April 20, 2022… The fall caused Plaintiff to suffer severe bodily injuries.” (TAC, p. 2.)

 

On August 6, 2026, Defendants filed an Answer to the TAC.

 

            No Reply to the Motion has been filed.

 

II.              Procedural Issues

 

As an initial matter, the Court notes that the Viselman declaration accompanying the Motion states there are two exhibits attached to the Motion; however, no such exhibits are attached.

 

The Court notes Defendants’ “Omnibus Opposition” is improper given the separate and distinct nature of the Motions. The Court also notes the Omnibus Opposition is just a memorandum of points and authorities and fails to include any evidentiary support.

 

III.            Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.1, subdivision (a) provides, in pertinent part, as follows:

 

If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b), or upon the court’s own motion after giving counsel notice and an opportunity to be heard, may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders. In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)

 

IV.            Discussion

 

Kanarek moves to quash the subpoena directed at it on the grounds the subpoena seeks communications and documents protected by the attorney-client privilege and work product privilege as well as the common interest doctrine, and the subpoena imposes an undue burden on Kanarek. (Motion, pp. 6-8.) Should the Court be disinclined to grant the Motion, Kanarek requests the Court issue a protective order regarding the subpoena. (Motion, p. 10.)

 

In the Omnibus Opposition, Defendants state Kanarek has not met the burden to demonstrate the common interest doctrine applies in this case at all let alone to all the requested documents in the subpoena. (Omnibus Opp., pp. 3-8.) Defendants further state the subpoena is not a fishing expedition, and sanctions should not be awarded as the subpoena was issued in good faith and seek discoverable information directly relevant to the action. (Omnibus Opp., pp. 8-9.)

 

Here, the Court finds the Kanarek subpoena requests are overly broad in scope. (See Motion, Sep. Statement.) While the Court acknowledges there is some relevance to some of the subpoena’s requests as it seeks information from witnesses to the underlying incident in this matter, the subpoenas effectively ask for all communications and documents in Kanarek’s possession relating to the instant litigation or the non-parties’ case with their client Perez. Given the sweeping nature of these requests, the Court finds the Kanarek subpoena is unduly burdensome and thus unenforceable. (See e.g., Calcor Space Facility, Inc. v. Sup. Ct. (Thiem Indus., Inc.) (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 216, 222-223.)

 

Further, the Court finds that to the extent the subpoenas request communications or documents between Kanarek and their client, Perez, such requests violate the attorney-client and work product privileges. (See Evid. Code, § 952.)

 

Insofar as the subpoenas request communications between Kanarek and Plaintiff’s counsel in this matter regarding Plaintiff, Defendants, or this action, the Court proceeds to address the work product privilege and the applicability of the common interest doctrine in an abundance of caution.

 

The ‘joint defense privilege’ and the ‘common interest privilege’ have not been recognized by statute in California. [Citations.] For this reason, we will refer to the joint defense or common interest doctrine, rather than the joint defense or common interest privilege, to avoid suggesting that communications between parties with common interests are protected from disclosure by virtue of a privilege separate from the attorney-client privilege, the work product doctrine, or any other statutorily recognized evidentiary privilege.

…

[T]he party seeking to invoke the doctrine must first establish that the communicated information would otherwise be protected from disclosure by a claim of privilege. For example, the content of the communication may comprise information shared in confidence by a client with his or her attorney, a legal opinion formed and advice given by the lawyer in the course of the attorney-client relationship, or a writing reflecting an attorney's impressions, conclusions, or theories. (See Evid. Code, § 952; Code Civ. Proc., § 2018, subd. (c).) The next step in the analysis is to determine whether disclosing the information to a party outside the attorney-client relationship waived any applicable privileges.

…

‘There is no statutory provision governing waiver of work product protection. [Citation.]’ [Citation.] However, California courts have recognized that the waiver doctrine is applicable to the work product rule as well as the attorney-client privilege. [Citation.] The work product protection may be waived ‘by the attorney's disclosure or consent to disclosure to a person, other than the client, who has no interest in maintaining the confidentiality … of a significant part of the work product.’ [Citation.] Thus, work product protection ‘is not waived except by a disclosure wholly inconsistent with the purpose of the privilege, which is to safeguard the attorney's work product and trial preparation.’

            …

Thus, ‘[f]or the common interest doctrine to attach, most courts seem to insist that the two parties have in common an interest in securing legal advice related to the same matter—and that the communications be made to advance their shared interest in securing legal advice on that common matter.’

 

(OXY Resources California LLC v. Superior Court (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 874, 889-891, as modified.)

 

Applying these principles to the instant circumstances, the Court finds the communications or documents sought by Defendants’ subpoena between Kanarek and Plaintiff’s counsel meet the common interest doctrine requirements.

 

First, the subpoena’s requested communications or documentations are so broadly phrased that they encapsulate protected attorney work product of both Kanarek and Plaintiff – e.g., writings reflecting an attorney’s impressions, conclusions, opinions, legal research, or theories. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.030.)

 

Indeed, the Court finds the requests here are broader than other requests where appellate courts have found a party’s refusal to produce all requested documents to a nonparty law firm unjustified due to overbreadth. (See Getz v. Superior Court (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 637, 657-658.) For example, in Getz, the Third District Court of Appeal found that the common interest doctrine only applied to some of the requested emails from defendant to a nonparty law firm, not all, and thus defendant’s refusal to “produce the text of the 42,852 indexed emails” on grounds of overbreadth was unjustified. (Id., at p. 657.) By contrast, the subpoena request here fails to even specify the kind or manner of communications or documents sought from the subject non-parties and instead make sweeping requests that are impossible to meaningfully and narrowly distinguish or differentiate. As such, the Court finds the first requirement of the common interest doctrine is satisfied.

 

Next, both Plaintiff’s counsel and Kanarek in this matter have in common an interest in securing legal advice related to the same matter of their clients who suffered harm from an identical uplift on Defendants’ property in close temporal proximity, and these communications to each other were made to advance their shared interest in securing legal advice and strategy on the common matter of Defendants’ liability for the uplift. (Motion, pp. 6-7, Kanarek Decl.)

 

Further, Kanarek and Plaintiff’s counsel did not waive the work product protection as each had an interest in maintaining the confidentiality of a significant part of the work product given that they shared communications “regarding matters including preservation of evidence, investigation of the dangerous condition, notice to the defendants, expert-related issues, litigation strategy, and liability analysis, to name a few.” (Motion, pp., 6-7, Kanarek Decl.; OXY Resources California LLC, supra, 115 Cal.App.4th at pp. 889-991.)

 

Accordingly, the Court finds Kanarek has satisfactorily shown the common interest doctrine applies to the communications and documents between Kanarek and Plaintiff’s counsel sought by Defendants’ subpoena.

 

To the extent that Defendants state Kanarek is required to produce at least some requested documents in response to the subpoenas or request an Evidence Code section 915 in-camera hearing with the Court regarding the alleged protected/privileged materials, the Court finds an overly broad and unduly burdensome subpoena does not warrant compliance. Further, the Court may not order disclosure of a communication claimed to be privileged to allow a ruling on the claim of privilege. (See Costco Wholesale Corp. v. Superior Court (2009) 47 Cal.4th 725, 739.)

 

Finally, while a majority of the subpoena requests are overbroad and/or impermissibly infringe on privileges, the Court finds that certain of the subpoena requests warrant a privilege log. Specifically, the Court finds that Request Nos. 1, 8, 13, 14, 17, and 20 in the Kanarek subpoena warrant a privilege log to ascertain the validity of the asserted privilege claims.

 

For all these reasons, the Motion is GRANTED IN PART AND CONTINUED IN PART. The Motion is GRANTED as to all items except for the 1st, 8th, 13th, 14th, 17th, and 20th requests in the subject subpoena.

 

The Motion is CONTINUED as to these six items. Kanarek is to produce a privilege log as to Request Nos. 1, 8, 13, 14, 17, and 20 in the subpoena. Thereafter, the parties are to submit supplemental briefing prior to the next scheduled hearing to address whether any dispute regarding privilege still exists as to these items.

For the foregoing reasons, Non-Party Martin Kanarek’s Motion to Quash LA Dodgers’ Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records to Kanarek for Privileged and Protected Records and/or Motion for Protective Order is GRANTED IN PART and CONTINUED IN PART.

 

The Motion is GRANTED IN PART AND CONTINUED IN PART. The Motion is GRANTED as to all items except for the 1st, 8th, 13th, 14th, 17th, and 20th requests in the subject subpoena.

Thereafter, the parties are to submit supplemental briefing prior to the next scheduled hearing to address whether any dispute regarding privilege still exists as to these items.

Further, the Court finds that to the extent the subpoenas request communications or documents between BD&J and its client, Perez, such requests violate the attorney-client and work product privileges. (See Evid. Code, § 952.)

 

Insofar as the subpoenas request communications between BD&J and Plaintiff’s counsel in this matter regarding Plaintiff, Defendants, or this action, the Court proceeds to address the work product privilege and the applicability of the common interest doctrine in an abundance of caution.

 

The ‘joint defense privilege’ and the ‘common interest privilege’ have not been recognized by statute in California. [Citations.] For this reason, we will refer to the joint defense or common interest doctrine, rather than the joint defense or common interest privilege, to avoid suggesting that communications between parties with common interests are protected from disclosure by virtue of a privilege separate from the attorney-client privilege, the work product doctrine, or any other statutorily recognized evidentiary privilege.

…

[T]he party seeking to invoke the doctrine must first establish that the communicated information would otherwise be protected from disclosure by a claim of privilege. For example, the content of the communication may comprise information shared in confidence by a client with his or her attorney, a legal opinion formed and advice given by the lawyer in the course of the attorney-client relationship, or a writing reflecting an attorney's impressions, conclusions, or theories. (See Evid. Code, § 952; Code Civ. Proc., § 2018, subd. (c).) The next step in the analysis is to determine whether disclosing the information to a party outside the attorney-client relationship waived any applicable privileges.

…

‘There is no statutory provision governing waiver of work product protection. [Citation.]’ [Citation.] However, California courts have recognized that the waiver doctrine is applicable to the work product rule as well as the attorney-client privilege. [Citation.] The work product protection may be waived ‘by the attorney's disclosure or consent to disclosure to a person, other than the client, who has no interest in maintaining the confidentiality … of a significant part of the work product.’ [Citation.] Thus, work product protection ‘is not waived except by a disclosure wholly inconsistent with the purpose of the privilege, which is to safeguard the attorney's work product and trial preparation.’

            …

Thus, ‘[f]or the common interest doctrine to attach, most courts seem to insist that the two parties have in common an interest in securing legal advice related to the same matter—and that the communications be made to advance their shared interest in securing legal advice on that common matter.’

 

(OXY Resources California LLC v. Superior Court (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 874, 889-891, as modified.)

 

Applying these principles to the instant circumstances, the Court finds the communications or documents sought by Defendants’ subpoena between BD&J and Plaintiff’s counsel meet the common interest doctrine requirements.

 

First, the subpoena’s requested communications or documentations are so broadly phrased that they encapsulate protected attorney work product of both BD&J and Plaintiff – e.g., writings reflecting an attorney’s impressions, conclusions, opinions, legal research, or theories. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.030.)

 

Indeed, the Court finds the requests here are broader than other requests where appellate courts have found a party’s refusal to produce all requested documents to a nonparty law firm unjustified due to overbreadth. (See Getz v. Superior Court (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 637, 657-658.) For example, in Getz, the Third District Court of Appeal found that the common interest doctrine only applied to some of the requested emails from defendant to a nonparty law firm, not all, and thus defendant’s refusal to “produce the text of the 42,852 indexed emails” on grounds of overbreadth was unjustified. (Id., at p. 657.) By contrast, the subpoena request here fails to even specify the kind or manner of communications or documents sought from the subject non-parties and instead make sweeping requests that are impossible to meaningfully and narrowly distinguish or differentiate. As such, the Court finds the first requirement of the common interest doctrine is satisfied.

 

Next, both Plaintiff’s counsel and BD&J in this matter have in common an interest in securing legal advice related to the same matter of their clients who suffered harm from an identical uplift on Defendants’ property in close temporal proximity, and these communications to each other were made to advance their shared interest in securing legal advice and strategy on the common matter of Defendants’ liability for the uplift. (See id.; Motion, pp. 6-7, Kanarek Decl.)

 

Further, BD&J and Plaintiff’s counsel did not waive the work product protection as each had an interest in maintaining the confidentiality of a significant part of the work product given that they shared communications “regarding matters including preservation of evidence, investigation of the dangerous condition, notice to the defendants, expert-related issues, litigation strategy, and liability analysis, to name a few.” (Motion, pp., 6-7, Kanarek Decl.; OXY Resources California LLC, supra, 115 Cal.App.4th at pp. 889-991.)

 

Accordingly, the Court finds BD&J has satisfactorily shown the common interest doctrine applies to the communications and documents between BD&J and Plaintiff’s counsel sought by Defendants’ subpoena.

 

To the extent that Defendants state BD&J is required to produce at least some requested documents in response to the subpoenas or request an Evidence Code section 915 in-camera hearing with the Court regarding the alleged protected/privileged materials, the Court finds an overly broad and unduly burdensome subpoena does not warrant compliance. Further, the Court may not order disclosure of a communication claimed to be privileged to allow a ruling on the claim of privilege. (See Costco Wholesale Corp. v. Superior Court (2009) 47 Cal.4th 725, 739.)

 

Finally, while a majority of the subpoena requests are overbroad and/or impermissibly infringe on privileges, the Court finds that certain of the subpoena requests warrant a privilege log. Specifically, the Court finds that Request Nos. 1, 8, 13, 14, 17, and 20 in the BD&J subpoena warrant a privilege log to ascertain the validity of the asserted privilege claims.

 

For all these reasons, the Motion is GRANTED IN PART AND CONTINUED IN PART. The Motion is GRANTED as to all items except for the 1st, 8th, 13th, 14th, 17th, and 20th requests in the subject subpoena.

 

The Motion is CONTINUED as to these six items. BD&J is to produce a privilege log as to Request Nos. 1, 8, 13, 14, 17, and 20 in the subpoena. Thereafter, the parties are to submit supplemental briefing prior to the next scheduled hearing to address whether any dispute regarding privilege still exists as to these items.

 

V.              Conclusion & Order

For the foregoing reasons, Non-Party BD&J’s Motion to Quash LA Dodgers’ Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records to BD&J for Privileged and Protected Records and/or Motion for Protective Order is GRANTED IN PART and CONTINUED IN PART.

 

The Motion is GRANTED IN PART AND CONTINUED IN PART. The Motion is GRANTED as to all items except for the 1st, 8th, 13th, 14th, 17th, and 20th requests in the subject subpoena.

 

The Motion is CONTINUED as to these six items. BD&J is to produce a privilege log as to Request Nos. 1, 8, 13, 14, 17, and 20 in the subpoena. Thereafter, the parties are to submit supplemental briefing prior to the next scheduled hearing to address whether any dispute regarding privilege still exists as to these items.

 

Further, the Court holds that, pursuant to the parties’ oral stipulation at the hearing on the Motion, the definition of “Jaime Merchain” included in the BD&J subpoena is to refer solely to Jaime Merchain and family members.

 

Also pursuant to the parties’ oral stipulation at the August 5, 2026 hearing on the Motions, the definition of “third-party” included in Request Nos. 8 in the BD&J subpoena is to refer solely to Jaime Merchain’s family members.

II.              Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.1, subdivision (a) provides, in pertinent part, as follows:

 

If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b), or upon the court’s own motion after giving counsel notice and an opportunity to be heard, may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders. In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.2, subdivision (a) states: “Except as specified in subdivision (c), in making an order pursuant to motion made under subdivision (c) of Section 1987 or under Section 1987.1, the court may in its discretion award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonable attorney’s fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive.”

 

III.            Discussion

 

Plaintiff moves to quash the two subpoenas directed to non-parties California Correctional Health Care Services (“CCHCS”) and California Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation, Office of Legal Affairs (“CDCR”) on the grounds the subpoenas are unsupported by good cause, seek inadmissible character evidence, are overly broad in scope, violate Plaintiff’s right to privacy, and were untimely issued. (Reply, pp. 1-8.) Finally, Plaintiff reiterates that the Cout should award Plaintiff reasonable expenses under Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.2 for the oppressiveness of the subpoenas. Any and all records related to Jamie Merchain (DOB: 09/14/1972, CDCR# T95686), including any records stored digitally and/or electronically, concerning medical history, treatment, diagnosis, prognoses, including medications, color photographs, evaluations, examinations, physiotherapy, flowsheets, pathology, reports, body scans, lab reports, CT Scans, MRIs, Radiological reports, X-Rays, and all other diagnostic tests, images, and procedures, including but not limited to all records for each and every visit, examination and/or treatment, itemized statements of billing charges including write-offs and balances due, insurance records, lab reports, sign-in sheets, nurses’ notes, doctor’s notes, physical therapy notes, correspondence, insurance information, surgical records, consultation records and/or reports, prescription and pharmacy records, including all dates of service.

 

(Motion, Exh. 1, Attach. 2.)

 

Then, the subpoena to CDCR requests the following: “Any and all records related to Jamie Gonzales Merchain (DOB: 09/14/1972, CDCR# T95686), including any records stored digitally and/or electronically, including but not limited to, inmate history, involvement in fights, incident reports, injuries sustained, insubordination, infractions, disciplinary records, and violation reports.” (Motion, Exh. 2, ¶ 2.)

 

The Court acknowledges there is some relevance to the subpoenas’ requests as they seek information regarding Plaintiff’s injuries sustained prior to the neck and shoulder injuries serving as the basis for this action. (See TAC; Motion; Opp.; Reply.) However, the CCHCR subpoena effectively asks for all medical information about Plaintiff without any limitation based on time or body part, and the CDCR subpoena overreaches into other areas of inquiry beyond Plaintiff’s injuries despite the supporting good cause declaration’s focus on Plaintiff’s injuries and medical history. Given the sweeping nature of these requests, the Court finds the subpoenas are overly broad. (See e.g., Calcor Space Facility, Inc. v. Sup. Ct. (Thiem Indus., Inc.) (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 216, 222-223.)

That said, the request to limit the subpoenas to “body-party only” is too narrow insofar that it does not take into consideration relevant medical history including prior and contributing history (e.g. degenerative conditions or other illnesses) that could be relevant to causation and damages. (See Britt v. Superior Court, (1978) 20 Cal. 3d 844.)

 

Based thereon, the Court finds that the Defendant is entitled to records relating to prior conditions and injuries related to head, neck, shoulder, and hand, as well as degenerative conditions, illness, or other treatment bearing on the same conditions.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On February 29, 2024, Plaintiff Gabriel Banales Lopez (“Plaintiff”) filed the instant action against Defendant Kia America, Inc. (“Defendant”) and Does 1-100.

 

On March 19, 2024, Defendant filed an Answer.

 

On July 2, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Settlement of Entire Case.

 

On August 21, 2025, the Court, pursuant to Plaintiff’s request, dismissed the action without prejudice and retained jurisdiction pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6. (8/21/25 Order.)

 

On August 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion For Attorneys’ Fees, Costs, and Expenses (the “Motion”) and request for judicial notice.

 

That same day, Plaintiff filed a Memorandum of Costs.

 

On August 19, 2026, Defendant filed an Opposition.

 

            On August 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Reply and Evidentiary Objections to the Proudfoot declaration filed in support of the Opposition.

 

II.              Procedural Issues

 

As an initial matter, the Court finds the Motion is untimely filed and served.

 

Plaintiff provides evidence of Defendant’s accepted statutory offer to compromise pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 998, which includes a provision stating the following:

 

Kia offers to permit judgment to be entered solely upon the terms of Paragraph 1, and Plaintiff shall retain the right to petition the Court for an award of reasonably and actually incurred attorney fees and costs recoverable pursuant to California Civil Code Section 1794(d). In ruling on Plaintiff’s fee/cost motion(s), the attorney fees, expenses and costs amount shall be calculated as if Plaintiff was found to have prevailed in this action under section 1794(d) of the California Civil Code. Kia expressly reserves all defenses to Plaintiff’s fee/costs motion(s). Plaintiff may recover for attorney fees and costs reasonably and actually incurred in bringing such a fee/cost motion(s). Kia will pay the attorney fees and cost amounts determined by the Court within 60 days’ written notice of entry of the Court’s ruling awarding those amounts.

 

(Motion, Azimtash Decl., Exh. G, p. 2, ¶ 2.)

 

However, neither the 998 offer nor the Motion, Opposition, or Reply state the parties stipulated to extend the attorneys’ fees motion and costs memorandum deadlines outlined in California Rules of Court, rule 3.1702. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1702, subd. (b)(2).) Further, there has been no request to the Court for an extension of the Rule 3.1702 deadlines, and the Court has not previously found good cause to extend the deadlines outlined by the California Rules of Court. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1702, subd. (d).)

 

As such, the Court is governed by the California Rules of Court for the Motion. (See Hatlevig v. General Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644, 648-649.)  

 

“Rule 3.1702 of the California Rules of Court governs motions for attorney fees in civil cases. [Citations.] A motion for fees incurred up to the time of judgment in the trial court ‘must be served and filed within the time for filing a notice of appeal under rules 8.104 and 8.108 in an unlimited civil case.’ [Citations.] With exceptions …, the time for filing a notice of appeal is the earlier of 60 days after service of notice of entry of judgment or 180 days after entry of judgment. [Citation.] A voluntary dismissal is ‘effectively a “judgment” ’ within the meaning of the rule prescribing the time to appeal.” (Id., at p. 648, citing Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1702.)  

 

“A voluntary dismissal, though not appealable, starts the clock running on the time to move for attorney fees when the dismissal concludes the litigation and triggers a right to fees.” (Id., at p. 649.)

 

California Rule of Court, rule 3.1702, and its predecessor, were “ ‘adopted in order to provide time limits within which all motions for attorney fees in civil cases must be made,’ including cases that end in a voluntary dismissal.” (Ibid., emphasis added.)

 

Here, the case was voluntarily dismissed on August 21, 2025, and thus the clock started running on 8/21/25 when the dismissal concluded litigation and triggered a right to fees. (See ibid.) As such, the deadline to file a motion for attorney’s fees was 180 days after August 21, 2025 – namely, February 17, 2026. (See id., at pp. 648-649.) However, the Motion was inexplicably filed and served on August 5, 2026, nearly a year after the dismissal was ordered and absent appropriate stipulation or a court order. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1702; Motion.) While the Court acknowledges the moving papers and Reply state the delay in filing the Motion was due to informal meet and confer attempts with Defendant, the Court also notes the Opposition contends no meet and confer attempts were performed. (Motion, p. 4, Azimtash Decl., ¶ 16; Opp., p. 3; Reply, p. 4.) The Court finds the Motion is untimely.

 

Thus, the Motion is DENIED.

 

III.            Conclusion & Order

 

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff Gabriel Banales Lopez’s Motion For Attorneys’ Fees, Costs, and Expenses is DENIED.

 

Moving party is ordered to give notice.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On April 8, 2025, Plaintiff Keiana Johnson (“Plaintiff”), proceeding pro se, filed the instant action against Defendant Curtis James Wesley, Jr. (“Defendant”) alleging Breach of Contract and Common Counts causes of action.

 

On April 22, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Pendency of Action. (4/22/25 Notice of Pendency of Action.) On April 28, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Pendency of Action. (4/28/25 Notice of Pendency of Action.) On May 6, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Pendency of Action. (5/6/25 Notice of Pendency of Action.)

 

On July 9, 2025, Defendant filed an Answer.

 

On May 21, 2026, the Court denied Defendant’s Ex Parte Application for Order Shortening Time as “defendant does not have any motion scheduled or filed. Therefore, the Court cannot shorten time on something that has not been filed.” (5/21/26 Order.)

 

On May 21, 2026, Defendant, now proceeding pro se, filed the first Motion to Expunge Lis Pendens, Dismiss Complaint with Prejudice, Award Sanctions, and Refer to District Attorney for Perjury (the “Motion”). On June 26, 2026, the Court took the Motion off calendar due to the parties’ failure to appear. (6/26/26 Order.)

 

On August 4, 2026, Defendant filed the instant second Motion.

 

On August 7, 2026, the Court found the instant case and 26STCP01781 to be related. (8/7/26 Order.)

 

            On August 17, 2026, Plaintiff, now represented by counsel, filed an Opposition to the second Motion and Evidentiary Objections to the Wesley, Jr. declaration filed in support of the second Motion.

 

            On August 24, 2026, Defendant filed a Reply.

 

II.              Evidentiary Objections

 

In support of the Opposition, Plaintiff objects to two portions of the Wesley Jr. declaration filed in support of the second Motion as well as Exhibit L and Exhibit K attached to the Wesley Jr. Declaration. (Opp., Evid. Objections.)

 

Defendant provides arguments against the evidentiary objections in the body of the Reply brief to the second Motion. (Reply, pp. 8-9.)

 

The Court OVERRULES the first and second objections to Wesley Jr.’s declaration supporting the Motion. The Court SUSTAINS the objections to Exhibits L and K attached to the second Motion.

 

III.            Legal Standard

 

“At any time after notice of pendency of action has been recorded, any party, or any nonparty with an interest in the real property affected thereby, may apply to the court in which the action is pending to expunge the notice.… Evidence or declarations may be filed with the motion to expunge the notice.… The claimant shall have the burden of proof under Sections 405.31 and 405.32.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.30.) The “claimant” is the “party to an action who asserts a real property claim and records a notice of the pendency of the action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.1.)

 

The court shall order the notice expunged if (1) the court “finds that the claimant has not established by a preponderance of the evidence the probable validity of the real property claim,” or (2) the Court “finds that the pleading on which the notice is based does not contain a real property claim.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 405.31-405.32.)

 

“In making this determination [for whether a complaint states a real property claim], the court must engage in a ‘ “demurrer-like analysis,” ’ asking whether the claimants have pled facts entitling them to the remedy they seek.” (De Martini v. Superior Court (2024) 98 Cal.App.5th 1269, 1278.) “Review ‘involves only a review of the adequacy of the pleading and normally should not involve evidence from either side, other than possibly that which may be judicially noticed as on a demurrer.’ ” (Kirkeby v. Superior Court (2004) 33 Cal.4th 642, 648.)  

 

IV.           Discussion

 

Defendant moves for various forms of relief in the second Motion, including expunging the lis pendens, dismissing the complaint with prejudice, awarding sanctions, and referring Plaintiff to the District Attorney. Defendant contends the lis pendens must be expunged because Plaintiff cannot establish the probable validity of the real property claim. Further, Defendant contends the Complaint should be dismissed as it is a sham pleading barred by judicial estoppel and the statute of frauds. Defendant requests the Court impose sanctions against Plaintiff under Code of Civil Procedure sections 128.5 and 128.7 for Plaintiff’s actions that constitute harassment and the Complaint’s false factual contentions. Finally, Defendant requests the Court refer Plaintiff to the District Attorney for perjury. In support of the Motion, Defendant attaches a declaration and 15 exhibits. (Motion, Wesley Jr., Exh. List.)

 

In Opposition, Plaintiff contends the Motion should be denied on the following grounds: the Complaint asserts real property claims; the Complaint is not a sham pleading, and Defendant’s evidence does not demonstrate the Complaint is a false or sham pleading; judicial estoppel does not apply here because Plaintiff never took a position in a judicial proceeding or before a tribunal; the statute of frauds does not bar the instant claim for multiple reasons, including the fraud cause of action does not rely on the oral agreement as a contract at all; Plaintiff has cognizable damages; the sanctions requests are procedurally and substantively defective; and there are no grounds to refer Plaintiff to the District Attorney as Defendant mischaracterizes the action and the Complaint. (Opp., pp. 1-18.) Defendant submits a declaration in support of the Opposition. (Opp., Johnson Decl.)

 

In Reply, Defendant contends Plaintiff attempts to recharacterize the Complaint but neither version of the Complaint saves it from lacking probable validity; Plaintiff’s damages theory is contradicted by the documentary record; and Plaintiff’s arguments regarding the procedural defects for the sanctions request is unfounded as Defendant has served the Motion, inclusive of the sanctions request, upon Plaintiff multiple times. Finally, Defendant requests the Court take a “measured approach to the district attorney referral request” but, most importantly, resolve the merits of the Motion.

 

The court must expunge only if the pleading “does not contain a real property claim.” (Code of Civil Procedure section 405.31) A “real property claim” is any cause of action that, if meritorious, would affect title to or the right to possession of specific real property. (Cal. Civ. Proc. Code 405.4.)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 405.32 defines “ ‘probable validity,’ with respect to a real property claim, means that it is more likely than not that the claimant will obtain a judgment against the defendant on the claim.” Code of Civil Procedure section 405.32 states “the court shall order that the notice be expunged if the court finds that the claimant has not established by a preponderance of the evidence the probable validity of the real property claim.”

 

“The party opposing a motion to expunge a lis pendens has the burden to show that a real property claim has been alleged and has probable validity based upon a preponderance of the evidence.” (Nunn v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A.¿(2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 346, 363 citing Code Civ. Proc., §§ 405.32, 405.30.) 

 

Here, Defendant moves to expunge the lis pendens on the grounds that Plaintiff cannot establish the probable validity of the real property claims in the Complaint. The burden of proof is thus on Plaintiff. (Ibid.)

 

At the outset, the Court notes that Defendant mischaracterizes the claims brought in the Complaint. Specifically, Plaintiff does not claim forgery of the operative deeds; she claims fraudulent inducement based on false misrepresentations made by the Defendant. In support of her Opposition, Plaintiff submits a declaration that states Defendant misrepresented a financing arrangement with Plaintiff and, due to Plaintiff’s reliance on Defendant’s misrepresentations, Plaintiff conveyed title to real property 515 W. 160th Street, Gardena, California 90248 (the “Subject Property”) to Defendant; however, Plaintiff did not receive adequate loan proceeds and/or title back to the Subject Property in return as the parties previously arranged and agreed to. As a result, the property was sold in non-judicial foreclosure sale. (See Opp., Johnson Decl.)

 

In review of the Complaint and its multiple exhibits and the Opposition’s declaration in conjunction with the evidence filed in support of the Motion, the Court finds that Plaintiff has demonstrated the probable validity of a real property claim based upon a preponderance of the evidence.  (See Nunn, supra, 64 Cal.App.5th at p. 363; Code Civ. Proc., § 405.32.) Consistent with Kirkeby v. Superior Court holding, where a claim seeking to undo a title transfer (there, a fraudulent conveyance) “affects title to or the right to possession of specific real property”, as in here, the claim seeking to undo a title transfer supports a lis pendens.  (Kirkeby v. Superior Ct., (2004) 33 Cal. 4th 642.) Thus, Plaintiff’s fraudulent-inducement claim, which seeks to restore her interest in the property, is title-affecting in the same way.

 

To the extent that the Reply brief riases additional arguments, such as the back and forth on the authenticity as to Exhibit E - “Without Collection” document - these are matters that are evaluated for credibility, which at this juncture is insufficient for a finding against the Plaintiff in the context of a motion to expunge a lis pendens.

 

As such, based on the totality of the circumstances and the evidence presented in conjunction with the Complaint, the Motion is DENIED as to the request to expunge lis pendens.

 

Furthermore, the Court does not find that the Complaint is a sham pleading, barred by judicial estoppel, or barred by the statute of frauds, or warrants referral to the District Attorney. The Court finds these requests in the Motion are either unpersuasive, meritless, or not supported by sufficient evidence. Further, the Court finds the Motion mischaracterizes the nature of all claims raised in the Complaint to attempt to support these requests.

 

As to Defendant’s requests for sanctions pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 128.5 and 128.7, Defendant fails to raise these claims in a separate motion as is statutorily required. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 128.5, (f)(1)(A), 128.7, subd. (c)(1).)

 

As such, the Motion is DENIED as to the requests for dismissal, sanctions, and referral to the District Attorney.

 

V.             Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant Curtis James Wesley, Jr.’s Motion to Expunge Lis Pendens, Dismiss Complaint with Prejudice, Award Sanctions, and Refer to District Attorney for Perjury is DENIED.
