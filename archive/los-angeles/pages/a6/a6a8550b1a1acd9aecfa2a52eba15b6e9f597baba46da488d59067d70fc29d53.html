DEPARTMENT M LAW AND MOTION RULINGS



Case Number: 24SMCV00651    Hearing Date: July 1, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Shen, et al., v. Li, et al.

CASE NO.:                24SMCV00651

MOTION:                  Motion to Compel Compliance

HEARING DATE:   7/1/2026

 

 

Legal Standard

Under Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 1987.1(a), “If a subpoena requires … the production of … documents, [or] electronically stored information …, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person [including a party] … may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders.” The court may also “make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.” (CCP § 1987.1(a).)

 

Sanctions are available under both CCP § 1987.2 and CCP § 2025.480(j). CCP § 1987.2 states: “[T]he court may in its discretion award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonable attorney’s fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification . . .” CCP § 2025.480(j) states: “The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel an answer or production, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” CCP § 2020.240, “[a] deponent who disobeys a deposition subpoena in any manner described in¿subdivision (c) of Section 2020.220¿may be punished for contempt under Chapter 7 (commencing with¿Section 2023.010) without the necessity of a prior order of court directing compliance by the witness. The deponent is also subject to the forfeiture and the payment of damages set forth in¿Section 1992.” 

  

ANALYSIS 

 

Plaintiffs/Cross-Defendants Jinwan Shen and Da Bai Niu move to compel production of records held by Cathay Bank, a third-party custodian of records, of nonparty Byang LLC pursuant to a lawfully served Third-Party Business Records. Plaintiffs further request sanctions against the objectors, Byang LLC in the sum of $4,560.00.

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice is GRANTED.

 

Subject Subpoena

 

The Cathay Subpoena seeks the following categories of documents:

 

RE: Byang LLC, a California Limited Liability Company with State of California Entity No. 202565813539, from January 1, 2025, to the present. All Documents as defined under the Evidence Code 250, relating to, including but not limited to:

 

1. all account opening statements, signature cards, account statements, canceled checks, deposit slips, withdrawal slips, wire transfers, wire memos, and debit memos, loans, financing, and lines of credit, query result reports;

2. all credit card records, records of payments made by a credit card, credit card details listing all cardholders, account holders, credit card users, co-signors and/or guarantors, credit card account statements, credit card fees, credit card purchase history, credit card purchase information, credit card application(s), credit card report(s), guarantee agreement(s), background investigation(s) conducted, and bank teller journals, bank teller ledgers, correspondence, and fraud monitoring records;

3. all fiat and cryptocurrency transaction histories; all blockchain transaction hashes and associated metadata; all internal wallet addresses; all external wallet addresses used by the customer; all fiat deposit and withdrawal records including ACH, wire, debit card, and credit card transactions; all information relating to linked bank accounts including routing numbers, account numbers, and any ACH return codes; all IP logs, device logs, browser fingerprints, login and logout timestamps; all records of account access events, and other security-related events; and

4. all electronically stored information (ESI) including but not limited to emails, and other forms of electronically stored information.

 

In summary, the Subpoena seeks Cathay Bank records concerning third-party Byang LLC from January 1, 2025 to the present, including transfers totaling $1.3M from Defendant Digital Horizon Ventures Inc. to Byang LLC, via Cathay Bank. Plaintiffs show good cause for ordering production by demonstrating that the banking records will evidence how Defendants dissipated funds pursuant to their scheme to defraud.

 

Here, Plaintiffs allege that Defendants Lingxiao Li, Yaojun Kou, and Defendant Kou’s entities, including Primepeak Investments Inc. and Digital Horizon, diverted Plaintiffs’ assets and proceeds from the Milliken Ave. Warehouse (1890 S. Milliken Ave., Ontario, CA 91761) sale, a property purchased with Plaintiffs’ funds. Plaintiffs put forth evidence that Primepeak purchased and flipped the Milliken Ave. Warehouse, and received $4,504,770.06 in sale proceeds, of which at least $2.0M of those funds were transferred to Digital Horizon on or about April 30, 2024. (SAC ¶¶ 166, 533–534.) Digital Horizon then used $862,554.53 of that $2.0M to purchase and flip the 7710 Bluebell Avenue property in Los Angeles on or about May 30, 2024, and January 7, 2025, respectively. Thereafter, Digital Horizon transferred $1,300,000.00 in funds to Byang LLC through Cathay Bank. (Reddy Decl., ¶¶ 11-13, 16, Ex. 6, pp. 1, 3, 7; Ex. 7, pp. 1, 4; Ex. 8, p. 1; Ex. 11, pp. 1–2, 7.)

 

On April 6, 2026, Plaintiffs issued the subject subpoena to Cathay Bank, seeking third-party Byang LLC’s bank records from January 1, 2025, to the present in order to trace Defendant Digital Horizon’s March 2025 transfers of sales proceeds from the 7710 Bluebell Avenue property. Plaintiffs simultaneously served a Notice to Consumer or Employee on Byang LLC, and thereafter personally served the Subpoena on Cathay Bank’s Custodian of Records on April 20, 2026. (Reddy Decl., ¶ 5; Ex. 1.) Responses were due on May 6, 2026.

 

Byang LLC objects to the subpoena on several grounds.  For instance, Byang argues that the subpoena is moot based upon its claim has a borrower/lender relationship with Digital Horizon and the funds were part of a repayment.  Byang’s claim, however, does not negate Plaintiffs ability to test that theory with its subpoena.  Otherwise, the significant relevance of the banking records overrides Byang’s corporate privacy interest. Plaintiffs therefore do not need to establish direct relevance, essential need, or that they explored less intrusive forms of discovery. (See Hecht, Solberg, Robinson, Goldberg & Bagley v. Superior Court (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 579, 594-595 [because the corporate privacy right is not constitutionally protected, the issue presented in determining whether the requests for production infringe that right is resolved by a simple balancing test].)  Moreover, there is an existing protective order and the production can be made pursuant to that order’s terms.

 

As to privilege and work product, Byan fails to provide sufficient evidence supporting those objections and failed. (CCP § 2031.240(c)(1).) Byang LLC has provided no factual information, no privilege log, and no identification of any specific privileged communication or document to support its blanket assertion that the Subpoena seeks privileged material. (Reddy Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. 2, p. 3:1–7.) Similarly, as to trade secrets, proprietary information, and business confidentiality, Byang also fails to meet their burden of establishing their existence so that the burden would be shifted to Plaintiffs.  Therefore, the objections are without merit.

 

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED. Sanctions are imposed in the reduced total amount of $1,800.00 against Byang. Sanctions are to be paid to Plaintiffs’ counsel of record within 30 days, and documents are to be produced within 45 days.

 


Case Number: 25SMCV00959    Hearing Date: July 1, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Medoff v. LA Housing Outreach LLC, et al.

CASE NO.:                25SMCV00959

MOTION:                  Special Motion to Strike

HEARING DATE:   7/1/2026

 

 

Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 425.16 permits the Court to strike causes of action arising from an act in furtherance of the defendant's right of free speech or petition, unless the plaintiff establishes that there is a probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim. “The anti-SLAPP procedures are designed to shield a defendant’s constitutionally protected conduct from the undue burden of frivolous litigation.” (Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 393.) “The anti-SLAPP statute does not insulate defendants from any liability for claims arising from the protected rights of petition or speech. It only provides a procedure for weeding out, at an early stage, meritless claims arising from protected activity.” (Id. at 384.) 

 

“Resolution of an anti-SLAPP motion involves two steps. First, the defendant must establish that the challenged claim arises from activity protected by section 425.16. If the defendant makes the required showing, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to demonstrate the merit of the claim by establishing a probability of success.” (Baral, supra, 1 Cal.5th at 384, citation omitted.) The California Supreme Court has “described this second step as a ‘summary-judgment-like procedure.’ The court does not weigh evidence or resolve conflicting factual claims. Its inquiry is limited to whether the plaintiff has stated a legally sufficient claim and made a prima facie factual showing sufficient to sustain a favorable judgment. It accepts the plaintiff’s evidence as true, and evaluates the defendant’s showing only to determine if it defeats the plaintiff’s claim as a matter of law. ‘[C]laims with the requisite minimal merit may proceed.’” (Id. at 384-385 [citations omitted].) 

 

A moving party must move to strike under this section within 60 days of service of the complaint. (CCP §425.16(f).)

 

CCP section 425.16(e) defines protected acts as the following: 1) any written or oral statement or writing made before a legislative, executive, or judicial proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law; 2) any written or oral statement or writing made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a legislative, executive, or judicial body, or any other official proceeding authorized by law; 3) any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest; or 4) any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest. 

 

“[T]he critical consideration is whether the cause of action is based on the defendant's protected free speech or petitioning activity.” (Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82, 89.) A claim is subject to an anti-SLAPP motion only if the conduct constituting the protected activity “itself is the wrong complained of.” (Park v. Board of Trustees of Calif. State Univ. (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1060.) In other words, “[a] claim arises from protected activity when that activity underlies or forms the basis for the claim.”¿(Id. at 1062.) “‘[T]he mere fact that an action was filed after protected activity took place does not mean the action arose from that activity for the purposes of the anti-SLAPP statute.’”¿(Navellier, supra, 29 Cal.4th at 89.)¿ “Instead, the focus is on determining what ‘the defendant’s activity [is] that gives rise to his or her asserted liability—and whether that activity constitutes protected speech or petitioning.’”¿(Id. at 92.)¿ “‘The only means specified in section 425.16 by which a moving defendant can satisfy that [‘arising from’] requirement is to demonstrate that the defendant’s conduct by which plaintiff claims to have been injured falls within one of the four categories described in subdivision (e)…” (Equilon Enterprises v. Consumer Cause, Inc. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 53, 66.) “In short, in ruling on an anti-SLAPP motion, courts should consider the elements of the challenged claim and what actions by defendant supply those elements and consequently form the basis for liability.”¿(Id.)¿¿ 

 

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

 

Defendants’ request for judicial notice is GRANTED. (Evid. Code § 452(d).)

 

ANALYSIS

 

Defendants LA Housing Outreach, LLC, Jonathan Sedaghat, and Starkwood, Inc., move to strike each cause of action of the Complaint. Defendants argue that their activity in filing an underlying action and obtaining a judgment against Medoff is protected activity covered by the anti-SLAPP statute. Defendants further argue that the action is meritless because the gravamen of the action has been litigated previously and decided against Plaintiff.

 

Initially, the Court in Department 205 held that Defendants did not meet their burden on the initial prong. The court found that the causes of action were “mixed” claims involving both protected and unprotected activity. (Baral, supra, 1 Cal.5th at 396.) The court noted that “[i]f a cause of action contains multiple claims and a moving party fails to identify how the speech or conduct underlying some of those claims is protected activity, it will not carry its first-step burden as to those claims.” (Bonni v. St. Joseph Health System (2021) 11 Cal.5th 995, 1011). “The nonmovant is not faced with the burden of having to make the moving party's case for it.” (Id.) The Court concurs that Defendants failed to identify specific speech or conduct which underlies any particular claim in the Complaint in the moving papers.

 

Defendants have since filed a supplemental briefing consistent with the court’s leave, identifying several allegations and a cause of action subject to their motion. Therefore, the Court examines whether Defendants appropriately identify allegations of protected conduct supporting each cause of action.

 

As to the first cause of action, Defendants assert that “LARSO, including, specifically, Section 151.09 was within LA Housing's right to petition.” “LARSO” not a cognizable protected activity. Thus, the burden is not met as to this cause of action.

 

As to the second cause of action, Defendants identify the allegation that "Defendants [sic] filed eviction against Plaintiff illegally in September 2023, and therefore violated the Los Angeles Rent Stabilization Ordinance (LAMC § 151.09)." (Compl., ¶ 48.) Litigation-related activities, such as the filing of a lawsuit or unlawful detainer action, reflect the exercise of a person’s constitutionally guaranteed right to petition the government for grievances. (Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 84, 90; see Feldman v. 1100 Park Lane Assocs. (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 1467, 1480 [finding that an unlawful detainer action, service of notice to quit and threats to evict tenants constitute protected activities].) Thus, that portion of the cause of action is subject to anti-SLAPP protections.

 

As to the sixth cause of action, Defendants identify paragraph 90, which states “Another such example of negligence is that Defendants and/or agents of Defendants lawfully refused Plaintiff’s rent and attempted to evict Plaintiff despite Plaintiff having legitimate tenancy rights as is specified in the Los Angeles Tenant Anti-Harassment Ordinance and LARSO.” Since protected activity underlies this portion of the negligence claim, that allegation supporting the negligence claim is subject to anti-SLAPP protections.

 

Defendants argue that paragraphs 124 and 125 of the Complaint claims the premises were the subject of LARSO. Again, a property being subject to LARSO is not protected petitioning activity. These identified allegations are not protected. Defendants claim that at paragraph 134, Medoff was allegedly harmed by being "unlawfully evicted." Indeed, this is protected activity. Defendants also identify the twelfth cause of action for retaliatory eviction, which is also premised on protected activity.

 

Otherwise, the gravamen of the remaining causes of action do not arise from Defendants’ eviction notices and filings. Alternatively, Defendants do not meet their burden to show which allegations of protected activity supply an element of the respective cause of action. For instance, Defendants do not demonstrate how certain allegations, such as portions of ¶¶ 36 (re: threatening and taking action to evict Plaintiff), 64, 84, 94, 103-104, 109-110, 114, 118, 129, and 133, supply an element of the respective cause of action.

 

Accordingly, Defendants meet their burden on the first prong as to paragraphs 48, 90, 134, and the entirety of the twelfth cause of action (¶¶ 136-145). Plaintiff has the burden to substantiate these allegations.

 

Second Prong – Res Judicata

 

            Defendants demonstrate that, as a matter of law, the claims underlying these identified allegations fail due to the application of res judicata principles. The doctrine of res judicata generally precludes parties from relitigating a cause of action determined with finality in a prior proceeding. (Smith v. ExxonMobil Oil Corp. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1407, 1413-14; Wright v. Ripley (1998) 65 Cal.App.4th 1189, 1193.) The doctrine has two aspects: claim preclusion, which applies to a previously litigated cause of action, and issue preclusion, which applies to issues necessarily decided in a prior action. (Vandenberg v. Superior Court (1999) 21 Cal.4th 815, 828.) “[R]es judicata does not merely bar relitigation of identical claims or causes of action. Instead, in its collateral estoppel aspect, the doctrine may also preclude a party to prior litigation from redisputing issues therein decided against him, even when those issues bear on different claims raised in a later case. Moreover, because the estoppel need not be mutual, it is not necessary that the earlier and later proceedings involve the identical parties or their privies. Only the party against whom the doctrine is invoked must be bound by the prior proceeding. [Citations.]” (Ibid.)¿Issue preclusion will apply if the party to be bound agreed expressly or impliedly to submit an issue to prior adjudication and had a full and fair opportunity to litigate under circumstances affording due process protections. (Ayala v. Dawson¿(2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 1319, 1327.)

 

Defendants demonstrate that the above allegations attempt to re-litigate already decided issues in the related eviction action, LA Housing Outreach v. Medoff (LASC Case No. 23SMCV04542). That action broaches the same issues presented in the identified allegations. In the underlying action, when Medoff refused to vacate her unit, LA Housing filed an action against her for breach of written lease, ejectment, and fraud, based on its position that Medoff was an unapproved subtenant. The Court found for LA Housing and against Medoff. Plaintiff argues that the issues of Defendants’ capability as a landlord and compliance with applicable laws were not appropriate to be litigated in the prior action. However, the preclusive effect of res judicata applies to affirmative defenses that would preclude removal of the tenant in an eviction action. (Needelman v. DeWolf Realty Co., (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 750, 759.) The alleged LARSO violations and retaliatory eviction would have been such a defense to the underlying proceeding and could have been fairly raised. (Civ. Code § 1942.5; Kriz v. Taylor (1979) 92 Cal.App.3d 302, 312-314; see Barela v. Superior Court (1981) 30 Cal.3d 244, 249-250 [re: common law retaliatory eviction defense]; see also CCP § 1174.2 & Green v. Superior Court (1974) 10 Cal.3d 616, 631 [breach of the warranty of habitability is an affirmative defense to an unlawful detainer].) Further, consent is a defense to trespass/ejectment. (Cobb v. City of Stockton (2011) 192 Cal.App.4th 65, 72 [a trespass requires that the entry be without permission]; Baugh v. Consumers Associates, Limited (1966) 241 Cal.App.2d 672, 675 [ejectment requires proof of ownership disclosing a right to possession].) Plaintiff, in fact, attempted to raise this as a defense but was excluded from doing so pursuant to a motion in limine due her own failure to raise the defense in the answer. Plaintiff had a full opportunity to litigate the issue, and did so at the trial court level and on appeal.

 

The Court continued the hearing on this motion because judgment in the eviction action was not yet final. CCP section 1049 provides that "[a]n action is deemed to be pending from the time of its commencement until its final determination upon appeal, or until the time for appeal has passed, unless the judgment is sooner satisfied". The remittitur in the underlying action was issued on June 10, 2026.  (RJN Exh. 5.) As such, res judicata provides a complete defense to the subject allegations.

 

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED in part. The court strikes paragraphs 48, 90, and 134, and the twelfth cause of action.

 

 


Case Number: 25SMCV06277    Hearing Date: July 1, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Lawrence Gilson v. Exeter 207 McDowell LLC, et al.

CASE NO.:                25SMCV06277

MOTION:                  Motion to Dismiss Forum non Conveniens

HEARING DATE:   7/1/2026

 

Legal Standard

 

Forum Non Conveniens

 

“Forum non conveniens is an equitable doctrine invoking the discretionary power of a court to decline to exercise the jurisdiction it has over a transitory cause of action when it believes that the action may be more appropriately and justly tried elsewhere. [Citations].” (Stangvik v. Shiley, Inc. (1991) 54 Cal.3d 744, 751.) The doctrine has been codified in Code of Civil Procedure section 410.30. “The inquiry is whether ‘in the interest of substantial justice an action should be heard in a forum outside this state.’ ” (Animal Film, LLC v. D.E.J. Productions, Inc. (2011) 193 Cal.App.4th 466, 471.)

 

Where plaintiff is a California resident for purposes of forum non conveniens, there is a strong presumption in favor of plaintiff's choice of forum. (Stangvik, supra, 54 Cal.3d at 754.) A nonresident plaintiff's choice of forum is entitled to “due deference” under all circumstances, but not a strong presumption of appropriateness. (National Football League v. Fireman's Fund Ins. Co. (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 902, 929-930.)  

 

In a contract dispute where the contract contains a forum selection clause “a threshold question in a forum non conveniens motion is whether a forum selection clause is mandatory or permissive.” (Animal Film, Inc., supra, 193 Cal.App.4th at 471.) Because California favors enforcement of mandatory forum selection clauses, they will be given effect absent a showing by the party resisting the motion that enforcement of such clauses would be unreasonable. (Quanta Computer Inc. v. Japan Communications Inc.¿(2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 438, 445.) “The factors that apply generally to a forum non conveniens motion do not control in a case involving a mandatory forum selection clause. Where there is a mandatory forum selection clause, ‘the test is simply whether application of the clause is unfair or unreasonable, and the clause is usually given effect. Claims that the previously chosen forum is unfair or inconvenient are generally rejected. A court will usually honor a mandatory forum selection clause without extensive analysis of factors relating to convenience.” (Ibid. [citations and internal quotation marks omitted].) 

 

ANALYSIS

 

Specially appearing Defendants Exeter 207 McDowell LLC, Herbert W. Tousley, IV, Thomas M. Nelson and Robert Stolpestad (“Exeter” or “Defendants”) move for an order dismissing this action on grounds of forum non conveniens based upon a forum selection clause contained in an Intercreditor and Subordination Agreement (“IAC”) with another lender, Bridgewater Bank, a Minnesota chartered state bank (“Bridgewater”). Defendants’ motion asserts that the IAC contains a mandatory Minnesota forum selection clause governing disputes concerning the IAC and that Plaintiff’s declaratory relief claim falls within its scope. Plaintiff opposes on the grounds that Defendants are not parties or third-party beneficiaries to the ICA, that they lack standing to invoke Section 26 of the ICA, and that traditional forum non conveniens factors favor California if the clause is not enforceable. In reply, Defendants contend that (1) the Note incorporates the ICA and makes it controlling in any conflict, (2) Defendants executed the ICA Acknowledgments agreeing to be bound, and (c) the action squarely seeks interpretations of the ICA.

 

According to the Complaint, Defendant Exeter is a Delaware limited liability company based in Saint Paul, Minnesota. (Complaint, ¶ 5.) Exeter sought to develop a commercial real estate project located in Phoenix, Arizona at 207 E. McDowell Road (the “Property”). (Complaint, ¶¶ 11-13.) As part of this development, Exeter obtained loans from multiple parties including Plaintiff Lawrence Gilson, in his capacity as Trustee of the Gilson Living Trust, dated April 2, 1991. (Complaint, ¶ 1.) Exeter also obtained a loan from Bridgewater in the amount of $3,282,500 that is secured by the Property. (Complaint, ¶ 14.) To memorialize the Note, Exeter, Herbert W. Tousley, IV, Thomas M. Nelson and Robert Stolpestad executed a Promissory Note, dated October 12, 2022, in favor of Plaintiff (the “Unsecured Note”) (Complaint, Exhibit A.) .

 

The Unsecured Note between Exeter and Plaintiff does not have a forum selection clause, although it does state it will be governed and construed under the laws of California. (Complaint, Ex. A, Unsecured Note, ¶ 13.) The Unsecured Note, however, expressly provides it is subject to the terms of IAC, which are controlling. Paragraph 15(f) of the Unsecured Note provides:

 

Notwithstanding anything herein to the contrary, this Note and the indebtedness evidenced hereby, all liens and security interests securing such indebtedness, and the exercise of any rights or remedies in respect of such indebtedness, any collateral and the payment, receipt and acceptance of certain payments on this Note are subject to the provisions of the Intercreditor and Subordination Agreement, dated as of October 12, 2022 (as amended, restated, supplemented or otherwise modified from time to time in accordance with the terms thereof, the “Subordination Agreement”), between Bridgewater Bank, a Minnesota state banking corporation, and Gilson Living Trust, Dtd 4/2/1991, a California Trust. In the event of any conflict between the terms of the Subordination Agreement and this Note, the terms of the Subordination Agreement shall govern and control. (emphasis added).

 

Plaintiff alleges the Unsecured Note is in default. (Complaint, ¶ 2.) However, Plaintiff’s sole cause of action is for declaratory relief where Plaintiff seeks relief necessarily involving the IAC and its interpretation. (Complaint, ¶ ¶ 24-28). For instance, Plaintiff affirmatively asserts that he seeks “a declaration interpreting the parties’ rights and obligations, particularly the effect of Section 18 of the ICA, so it may understand what actions, if any, are permitted under the Note and ICA” (Complaint, ¶ 39). Plaintiff further alleges “judicial clarification is needed to reconcile Section 18 with the ICA’s other clauses ‘to the extent and in the manner set forth herein.’” (Complaint, ¶ 45). Plaintiff further “seeks a declaration that the ICA does not expressly or by implication prevent Defendants from satisfying the Note in whole or in part.” (Complaint, ¶ 46.) Plaintiff’s three prayers for declaratory relief directly reference or arise from the IAC, including: 1) That “the Note is enforceable and not subject to any defenses or offsets even if the Bridgewater Loan remains unsatisfied;” 2) That “the ICA does not expressly or by implication prevent Defendants from satisfying the Note in whole or in part;” and 3) That “even if Exeter were prevented from satisfying the GLT Loan, the ICA does not expressly or by implication prevent the Individual Defendants from paying the Note in whole or in part.”. (Complaint, Prayer at a(i-iii).)

 

Here, the ICA states it “shall be governed by, and construed in accordance with, the laws of the State of Minnesota,” and the Subordinate Lender “irrevocably consents to the exclusive jurisdiction of any state or federal court” in Minnesota.  The ICA further provides the parties agree that the specified venue is the most convenient, and the Subordinate Lender waives objections to venue and more convenient forum. As stated, the Unsecured Note contains no forum selection clause, is governed by California law, and expressly states it and the indebtedness evidenced thereby are “subject to the provisions of the Intercreditor and Subordination Agreement,” and “[i]n the event of any conflict between the terms of the Subordination Agreement and this Note, the terms of the Subordination Agreement shall govern and control.” Defendants executed the ICA “Obligors’ Acknowledgment,” approving the ICA, agreeing and consenting to its terms, and agreeing “to be bound by such Agreement,” which necessarily includes Section 26’s forum provision .

As a result, the Court concludes that the ICA’s Minnesota forum selection clause is mandatory and encompasses claims requiring interpretation, construction, or application of the ICA. The Unsecured Note’s subordination to the ICA confirms the parties’ intent that disputes over the ICA’s effect on payments under the Unsecured Note be resolved in the designated Minnesota forum. Plaintiff and Defendants all signed the IAC, and with respect to the Unsecured Note between the parties, further agreed to be bound by the IAC.  In the IAC, Defendants are specifically defined as “Obligors.” Defendants also signed the IAC as an “Obligors’ Acknowledgment.” (Aikin Decl., Ex. 2, pp. 29-31.) The Obligors’ Acknowledgment provides that as to the IAC each Obligor, and thus each Defendant, “hereby approve[s] of such Agreement, and agree[s] and consent[s] to the terms thereof.” (Aikin Decl., Ex. 2., page 29.) The Obligor’s Acknowledgement further provides that each Obligor, and thus each Defendant “agree[s] to be bound by such Agreement.” (Id.) As such, they are parties to the IAC.  The IAC states that it is governed by the laws of Minnesota and Plaintiff expressly consented to Minnesota Courts (State or Federal) having exclusive jurisdiction over the IAC. Defendants signed the IAC and consented to its terms, including the Minnesota forum selection clause.  The Unsecured Note contains no forum selection clause and expressly states it is subject to the provisions of the IAC, and that those provisions are controlling.  Thus, the motion to dismiss would be granted.

Furthermore, a forum selection clause may be enforced against a plaintiff who is not a party to the contract in question if the plaintiff is “closely related to the contractual relationship.” (Net2Phone, supra, 109 Cal.App.4th at 589.) To determine whether a nonsignatory is closely related to the contractual relationship, the court must consider whether the evidence demonstrates one of the following factors: (1) the nonsignatory agreed to be bound by the terms of the agreement, (2) the contracting parties intended the nonsignatory to benefit from the agreement; or (3) there was a “defined and intertwining business relationship with a contracting party”. (Bancomer, S. A. v. Superior Court (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 1450, 1461.) “The key to the closely related test is whether the nonsignatories were close to the contractual relationship, not whether they were close to the third party signator. This makes sense because the forum selection clause is part of the underlying contract, and it is the contractual relationship gone awry that presumably spawns litigation and activates the clause. Giving standing to all closely related entities honors general principles of judicial economy by making all parties closely allied to the contractual relationship accountable in the same forum, thereby abating a proliferation of actions and inconsistent rulings.” (Bugna v. Fike, (2000) 80 Cal.App.4th 229, 235.) The mere fact that a contract would be relevant evidence at trial, or would need to be construed to evaluate a plaintiff's claim, does not trigger application of a forum selection clause in that contract. (Berclain America Latina v. Baan Co., (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 401, 408.) The intertwined business relationship between the contracting party and the nonsignatory must exist at the time that the agreement containing the forum selection clause was signed. (Id.)

 

Here, Defendants are “closely related” to the IAC.  As stated, they agreed to the terms of the IAC, signed the Obligors’ Acknowledgment, and agreed to the terms in Paragraph 15(f) of the Unsecured Note. In addition, there is a defined and intertwining business relationship between the parties.  As argued by Defendants, the Complaint identifies a broad set of agreements between Plaintiff, Bridgewater, and Defendants that are bound together by the ICA. The Complaint seeks to determine rights as among multiple agreements, including the Note and the ICA. Furthermore, there is no evidence that enforcing the forum selection clause would be unreasonable.

 

For these reasons, the motion is GRANTED and the matter is DISMISSED.
