Case Number: 23STCV26453    Hearing Date: June 24, 2026    Dept: B

Tentative Ruling

Nething v. University of Southern California, Case no. 23STCV26453

Hearing date June 24, 2026

Defendant CSC’s Motion for Summary Judgment

Defendant USC’s Motion for Summary Judgment

Plaintiffs Rebecca and Robert Nething sue defendants University of Southern California (“USC”), Contemporary Services Corp. (“CSC”) for premises liability. Defendants USC and CSC move for summary judgment. Plaintiffs failed to file timely opposition; the court granted their request to continue the hearing and grant leave to file opposition. The opposition contained no substantive argument, only request for relief per Code Civ. Proc. §473, et seq.

Defendants request judicial notice of ESPN statistics for the 11/8/22 USC v. Washington State football game showing attendance of 63,204. GRANTED, per Evid. Code §452(h).

A motion for summary judgement must be granted if there is no triable issue of material fact as to each cause of action. Code Civ. Proc. §437c(a), (c); Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843. To obtain summary judgment, moving party must prove each element of the cause of action. Harris v. Rudin, Richman & Appel (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 1332, as modified Mar. 5, 2002. A defendant can do so by showing plaintiff cannot establish one or more elements of the claim or there is a complete defense to that claim. Aguilar, supra, at 853. Once moving party meets this burden, the burden shifts to responding party to show a triable issue of material fact as to that cause of action or a defense thereto. Harris, supra, at 1337.

Plaintiffs’ Opposition and Request for Relief under Code Civ. Proc. §473 et seq.

Defendants’ motions were filed 11/17/25; defendants filed notices of non-opposition on 5/26/26. Plaintiffs moved ex parte to continue hearing, citing a calendaring error for their failure to oppose. 5/27/26 Ex Parte App. The court continued the hearing to allow plaintiffs time to substantively oppose, with an opposition deadline of 6/15/26. Plaintiffs’ opposition filed 6/15/26 contained no substantive argument, citing Code Civ. Proc. §437c(h) and stating: (1) plaintiffs failed to seek discovery as to duty; (2) plaintiffs failed to identify, locate or depose percipient witnesses as to foreseeability; and (3) seeking 90 days to conduct discovery. See generally, Opp.

This case was filed on 10/30/23. CSC answered 10/22/24; USC answered 10/25/24. Trial was set for 4/28/25 but continued once by stipulation and once by motion. 3/19/25 Stip.; 1/22/26 Min. Order. These summary judgment motions were filed 11/17/25 and set for hearing 2/25/26. On 1/22/26 the hearings were continued to allow plaintiffs to conduct discovery per Code Civ. Proc. §437c(h). Id. Plaintiffs have had almost two years to conduct discovery, including 7 months since these motions were filed, plus a 90-day continuance per Code Civ. Proc. §437c(h).

Plaintiffs argue relief is mandatory under Code Civ. Proc. §473(b) as lead counsel Garcia was undergoing medical treatment from March-May 2026. Opp., 1:12; Decl. Garcia para. 3. Counsel asserts the same calendaring error which caused them to miss the initial opposition deadline caused them to overlook the need to conduct discovery prior to filing opposition. Id. at para. 6. Counsel substituted into this matter on 12/4/25 and was unaware prior counsel failed to conduct significant discovery. Id. at paras. 7-8. Plaintiffs admit “virtually no discovery necessary to defeat the defendants’ motions” has occurred (Opp., 2:23-24) and request more time and further relief under Code Civ. Proc. §473c(h).

The court granted relief pursuant to plaintiffs’ prior section 473c(h) request. See 12/18/25 Resp.; see also 1/22/26 Min. Order. Plaintiffs are not entitled to multiple section 473c(h) continuances. There is no indication that plaintiffs made any attempts at discovery since the prior relief was granted. That plaintiffs failed to conduct discovery in the 7 months since these motions were filed is dilatory conduct and a lack of diligence, neither of which entitles plaintiffs to further relief under section 473c(h).

Nor are plaintiffs entitled to relief under Code Civ. Proc. §473(b); counsel came into this matter on 12/4/25 and was present when the court granted the request to continue hearing on the motions for summary judgment to allow for discovery. See 1/22/26 Min. Order. Counsel’s health issues did not arise until March 2026, months after he came into this matter and more than a month after the court granted additional time to conduct further discovery. That counsel failed to investigate the status of discovery before substituting in to the case or in the months prior to his health issues does not demonstrate excusable neglect nor inadvertence, but a lack of diligence; that no discovery was served after the court granted the prior request to continue also shows a lack of diligence. Further, the court granted plaintiffs’ request for section 473(b) relief by continuing the hearing again to allow plaintiffs to file opposition after their failure to do so. See 5/29/26 Minute Order.

Plaintiffs exhausted avenues for relief under Code Civ. Proc. §473 et seq. While the court favors resolving issues on their merits, this does not entitle plaintiffs to multiple continuances where there is a continuing lack of diligence. Counsel’s response on 12/18/25 identifies then-outstanding discovery issues. See 12/18/25 Resp. Those seem to be identical to the discovery plaintiffs assert still needs to be completed, including discovering the identity of and deposing percipient witnesses. See Opp. p. 7. Plaintiffs’ counsel had three months between coming into this matter and developing health issues, and at least a month prior to those concerns when counsel had knowledge of outstanding discovery that was sought prior to filing oppositions to these motions. Failure to conduct any discovery seems to be a lack of diligence. There is no basis for further relief under either section 473(b) or section 473c(h).

Defendant CSC’s Motion for Summary Judgment

Plaintiffs bring causes of action for negligence and negligent hiring against defendant CSC, which moves for summary judgment on both claims. To prove negligence, plaintiff must “show[] that the defendant owed the plaintiff a legal duty, that the defendant breached the duty, and that the breach was a proximate or legal cause of injuries suffered by the plaintiff.” Ann M. v. Pacific Plaza Shopping Center (1993) 6 Cal.4th 666, 673. Generally, there is no duty to protect a party against a third party; however, where a special relationship exists, an affirmative duty to protect may arise. See Brown v. USA Taekwondo (2021) 11 Cal. 5th 204, 209. Absent facts showing a special relationship, no such duty arises. Id. at 220-21.

USC leased and operated the Coliseum on 10/8/22. SSMF 1. The Coliseum hosted over 63,000 for the football game on 10/8/22. SSMF 2; RJN 1. CSC is a crowd management vendor retained by USC to provide guest services on 10/8/22. SSMF 3. The incident occurred during halftime when plaintiff exited the women’s restroom and was knocked to the ground when Doe collided with her from behind. SSMF 6. The incident was captured on surveillance footage, which shows an unidentified, non-party individual pushing Doe, who then collided with plaintiff from behind, causing her to fall to the ground. SSMF 7. The incident occurred over 1-2 seconds. Id. Neither CSC nor USC received complaints regarding issues in the area where the incident occurred, nor about any involved individuals. SSMF 11. Plaintiffs had no interaction with USC or CSC staff or law enforcement regarding the incident. SSMF 12. Defendants learned of the incident after the fact. Id.

Plaintiffs failed to offer evidence of a prior relationship, or special relationship, between defendants, plaintiffs and/or unidentified third parties. SSMF 11-19. Plaintiffs failed to provide evidence that defendants had advance knowledge of Doe, or the unidentified man who pushed him, prior to the alleged incident. Id. Plaintiffs failed to proffer evidence of any measures defendants could have taken to prevent the incident. Id.

A party moving for summary judgment may point to a responding party’s factually devoid discovery responses. See Code Civ. Proc. §437(o)(2); see also Union Bank v. Super. Ct. (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 573, 590. Plaintiffs’ responses to defendants’ discovery requests are factually devoid and provide no evidence or facts in support of the causes of action against defendants. SSMF 20. Plaintiffs reiterate allegations of the complaint. Id. The failure to proffer evidence of existence of a special relationship, and therefore duty, as well as evidence of causation, is sufficient to shift the burden to plaintiffs to show a triable issue of material fact.

Plaintiffs’ opposition offers no disputed or additional facts; it admits no discovery has been done, and plaintiffs have no evidence to create a triable issue of fact. As plaintiffs are not entitled to further section 473 relief, there is no way to raise a triable issue of fact. The court has no choice but to GRANT summary judgment in CSC’s favor.

Defendant USC’s Motion for Summary Judgment

Plaintiffs bring causes of action against USC for premises liability, negligence and negligent hiring. The facts and law presented by USC on the second and third claims are identical to those presented by CSC. Plaintiff’s omnibus opposition is likewise identical. The court adopts its ruling above and GRANTS summary judgment in USC’s favor on the second and third claims for negligence and negligent hiring and solely addresses the first claim for premises liability.

Premises liability is rooted in negligence law and based in the same factors. California courts have held the duty owed to patrons at a sporting event does not extend to controlling misconduct of third persons that defendant has no reason to anticipate and no reasonable opportunity to prevent. See Rogers v. Jones (1976) 56 Cal.App.3d 348, 351. The undisputed facts show no special relationship between plaintiffs, defendants and Doe. See SSMF 1-19. The undisputed facts show the incident occurred over 1-2 seconds and without warning. SSMF 7. This shifts the burden to plaintiffs to show a duty via a special relationship, as well as foreseeability and causation. As above, plaintiffs failed to produce evidence to show a triable issue of fact. The court must GRANT summary judgment in USC’s favor on all issues.


Case Number: 23VECV02011    Hearing Date: June 24, 2026    Dept: B

Tentative Ruling

Brentwood Communications Int’l. v. Fully Torqued Productions, Case no. 23VECV02011

Hearing date June 24, 2026

Plaintiff BCII’s Motion for Sanctions

Plaintiff BCII’s Motion for Leave to Amend Complaint

Plaintiff BCII’s Motion for Leave to Amend Answer to the Cross-Complaint - UNOPPOSED

Plaintiff Brentwood Communications Int’l., Inc., (“BCII”) sues defendant Fully Torqued Productions for breach of contract. Defendants Pazmany and Fully Torqued Industries, LLC were named as Doe defendants on 3/26/26. Plaintiff alleges defendants agreed to pay plaintiff $868,584 for production/post-production services for defendant’s series “Fully Torqued.” Plaintiff alleges defendant failed to pay for services, with a balance owing of $529,031.

The cross-complaint alleges plaintiff made fraudulent misrepresentations to induce defendant to entering into production agreements for seasons one and two of “Fully Torqued.” Defendants allege plaintiff breached the agreement, including revising the production budget of season one without prior approval and failure to timely deliver program episodes.

Plaintiff moves for sanctions, alleging discovery abuses. Defendants oppose. Plaintiff moves for leave to amend the complaint to add two new causes of action; defendants oppose. Plaintiff moves for leave to amend the answer to the cross-complaint; the motion answer is unopposed.

Defendants filed the declaration of Pazmany. Plaintiff objects. Objections 1-22 OVERRULED.

Plaintiff BCII’s Motion for Sanctions

Plaintiff offers the declaration of Poyourow. Defendants object. Objections 1-12 OVERRULED.

Plaintiff requests evidentiary, issue and monetary sanctions of $20,825, alleging discovery abuses. “The power to impose discovery sanctions is a broad discretion subject to reversal only for arbitrary, capricious, or whimsical action. Only two factors are absolutely prerequisite to imposition of the sanctions: (1) there must be failure to comply . . . and (2) the failure must be willful.” Calvert Fire Ins. Co. v. Cropper (1983) 141 Cal.App.3d 901, 904.

The court may impose monetary or other sanctions for misuse of the discovery process. Code Civ. Proc. §2023.030. Such misuses include: …(b) using a discovery method in a manner that does not comply with specified procedures; (c) employing discovery to an extent that causes annoyance or undue burden and expense; (d) failing to respond or to submit to authorized discovery; (e) making, without justification, an unmeritorious discovery objection; (f) making an evasive response to discovery; and (g) disobeying a court order to provide discovery. Code Civ. Proc. §2023.010.

Plaintiff asserts defendants “intentionally and wrongfully delayed the timely and prompt production of documents.” Mtn., p. 4, fn. 1. Plaintiff moved to compel further production of financial documents. Decl. Poyourow, para. 11. At the informal discovery conference, defense counsel represented no further responsive documents existed. Id. at para. 12. On 8/22/25 the court ordered defendants to: (1) produce code compliant, original, verified supplemental responses to RFPS, set one, nos. 145-147, 150-152, 157-162, 164-167, 171-174, 176, 191-192 and 207 within 20 days; (2) produce complete, un-redacted documents in response to RFPs nos. 13-60, 62-142 and 163; and (3) pay sanctions of $9,599.99. Id. at para. 13, exh. 1.

Plaintiff asserts defendants failed to serve timely responses, responding 33 days late, in violation of court order. See Decl. Poyourow, para. 16, exhs. 2-3. Plaintiff asserts the tardy responses included objections previously found unavailing by the court and denied possessing responsive financial documents. Id. at para. 17, exhs. 2.

Plaintiff asserts defendants continue to withhold responsive documents, including communications with nonparty Foster, on the basis of privilege. Decl. Poyourow, para. 22, exhs. 6-7. Plaintiff asserts defendants continue to withhold responsive financial documents relating to nonparty Summit Racing, which produced responses pursurant to subpoena showing these documents do exist. Id. at para. 23.

Defendants argue they complied with all discovery obligations and orders. Notwithstanding, defendants assert they served further supplemental responses on 6/9/26. Decl. Hilvert, para. 7. Defendants assert these documents were not produced previously, resulting from oversight due to the volume of the production. Decl. Pazmany, para. 6.

Defendants argue the remaining financial documents concern Pazmany’s personal bank account and Fully Torqued Industries, LLC, which defendants contend were not subject to the 8/22/25 order. Defendants reassert that all responsive, relevant non-privileged documents in their possession have been produced and argue no sanctions are warranted.

Pazmany and Fully Torqued Industries were not subject to the court’s 8/22/25 order, as neither was named until 3/26/26. See 3/26/26 Amendments to Compl. The proper remedy as to these defendants is a motion to compel or motion to compel further. However, Fully Torqued Production previously produced a history of financial transactions that were run through Full Torqued Industries, LLC’s bank account related to the series’ profits and expenses. See Decl. Poyourow, exh. 4. These records were subject to the 8/22/25 order; failure to produce unredacted records violates the order.

The court finds defendant Fully Torqued Productions and counsel violated the order with regards to the further documentation produced on 6/9/26. Defendants state the failure to produce was due to oversight; mere oversight does not justify a delay of over a year. Further, defendants produced redacted responses, in violation of the order which stated: “[defendant]’s heavily redacted production of financial documents omits key information necessary for plaintiff to make sense of those documents for purposes of this action.” 8/22/25 Min. Order.

Fully Torqued Productions and counsel violated the order, necessitating sanctions. The court will continue the issue of evidentiary sanctions; if defendants fail to produce full, code-compliant and unredacted responses within 30 days, the court will consider granting issue and evidentiary sanctions.

Plaintiff requests $20,825 in attorney’s fees at $425/hour. See Decl. Poyourow, paras. 35-41. Plaintiff requests 49 hours: 10 hours for meet and confer, 6 hours preparing for IDC, 25 hours for drafting moving papers, plus 6 hours for reply and 2 hours for hearing. Id. at paras. 35, 41.

While the court may award sanctions (Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 639), fees for meet and confer or IDC efforts are not awarded, as these fees would typically be incurred. 25 hours for drafting is unreasonable; the court awards 5 hours for drafting, 2 hours for reply and 1 hour for hearing. The requested hourly rate is reasonable. The court awards 8 hours at $425/hour, totaling $3,400.

GRANTED as to monetary sanctions, joint and several by defendant Fully Torqued Productions and counsel Lowe & Associates, P.C. within 30 days. The motion as to issue and evidentiary sanctions is continued; if defendants fail to produce full, code-compliant, unredacted responses as of that date, the court will consider such.

Plaintiff BCII’s Motion for Leave to Amend Complaint

Plaintiff offers the declaration of counsel Poyourow. Defendants object. Objections 1-21 OVERRULED.

“A party may amend its pleading once without leave of the court at any time before the answer, demurrer, or motion to strike is filed…” Code Civ. Proc. §472(a). Otherwise, leave of court is required to file an amended pleading. Hodges v. Cnty. of Placer (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 537, 544. Amendments may be appropriate at various stages of litigation, including late in the proceedings, where the amendment is justified and does not unfairly prejudice the opposing party. Honig v. Financial Corp. of Am. (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 960, 965. The key consideration is not when amendment is sought, but whether amendment is justified under the circumstances. Id.

CRC 3.1324 requires that papers supporting a motion for leave to amend: (1) include a copy of the proposed amended pleading, numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings; (2) state what allegations in the previous pleading are to be deleted and where those allegations are located; and (3) state what allegations are proposed to be added and where those allegations are located. CRC rule 3.1324(a).

Plaintiff seeks to add causes of action against FTP, FTI, and Pazmany to impose alter ego liability and pierce the corporate veil. The causes of action are breach of contract - fraud, accounting and declaratory relief. See Decl. Poyourow, para. 25, exh. 6.

Defendants oppose, arguing the statute of limitations on the proposed claims expired. This is to be raised on demurrer or motion for judgment on the pleadings. The court will not consider the merits of the proposed claims now, only whether the proposed amendment complies with CRC 3.1324 and Honig, supra.

Defendants argue there is no basis to name Pazmany or FTI, as Pazmany should have been named previously and FTI is not a proper party because it was not party to the underlying contract. The proposed amendment turns on an alter-ego theory of liability and contains allegations permitting the naming of Pazmany and FTI. Notwithstanding, a motion to quash or demurrer is the appropriate remedy for arguments as to misjoinder.

Defendants argue plaintiff has been dilatory in seeking to discover facts giving rise to proposed alter-ego allegations. Defendants argue permitting amendment now would be prejudicial. Poyourow’s declaration asserts that evidence first produced after the 8/12/25 IDC and 8/22/25 court order form the basis of the proposed amendments. Decl. Poyourow, para. 25 at 10:11. Defendants failed to produce timely responses to that order and only recently produced further responses. Defendants cannot avail themselves of a delay argument, much of which they caused.

Plaintiff gave notice multiple times between November 2025 and February 2026 that it would seek to amend the complaint in light of newly discovered evidence. See Decl. Poyourow, para. 22, exh. 4; para. 23, exh. 5; para. 24. This matter remains in discovery, and no trial date has been set. There is no substantial prejudice as to the timing of the amendment.

Defendants argue permitting the addition of Pazmany and FTI would impermissibly expand the scope of the litigation. This is unavailing. When an amended complaint rests on the same general facts as the original complaint, the change in legal theory or addition of new claims based on those facts does not render the amendment improper. Smeltzley v. Nicholson Mfg. Co. (1977) 18 Cal.3d 932, 939. The question is whether a proposed amendment rests on the same general set of facts as the original; here, it does.

California has a policy favoring liberal amendment of pleadings up to, and including, the eve of trial. Here, trial has not been set; defendants cannot show substantial prejudice as to the timeliness of the amendment. The proposed amendment arises from the same general facts and is permitted under Smeltzley, supra. GRANTED.

Plaintiff BCII’s Motion for Leave to Amend Answer to the Cross-Complaint

California favors liberally permitting amendments. See Atkinson v. Elk Corp. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 739, 760. The right to file an amended answer “as of course” is limited to the time within which plaintiff may demur to the original answer, whether such demurrer is actually filed or not. Milstein v. Sartain (1943) 56 Cal.App.2d 924, 933; Manha v. Union Fertilizer Co. (1907) 151 Cal. 581, 583. After this time has elapsed, a party may seek leave to amend via motion. See Hodges v. Cnty. of Placer (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 537, 544. Procedural requirements are stated in CRC rule 3.1324, as above.

The proposed amended answer is attached See Decl. Poyourow, exh. 4. Plaintiff argues good cause to for leave exists, as: (1) allegedly new evidence necessitating amendment only became available via discovery after cross-defendants filed the initial answer; (2) newly discovered evidence shows there may have been a unilateral or mutual mistake on which version of the “Season One Production Agreement” and “Production Budget” were effective, a material issue in this case; (3) the motion for leave to amend is timely; and (4) cross-complainants are not likely to suffer prejudice from the proposed amendments. The motion to amend the answer is unopposed. The proposed amendment is compliant with CRC rule 3.1324. GRANTED.
