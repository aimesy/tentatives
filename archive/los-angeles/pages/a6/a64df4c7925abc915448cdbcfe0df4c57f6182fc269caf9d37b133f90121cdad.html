    PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION FOR TRIAL PREFERENCE

     

 

 

I.                Background

 

On November 13, 2025, Plaintiffs Dickie Ray and Brucie Jane Cagle filed their complaint for personal injury alleging Mr. Cagle developed pleural disease and lung cancer from occupational exposure to asbestos and asbestos-containing products. On May 28, 2026, Plaintiffs filed this motion for trial preference under Code of Civil Procedure section 36 subdivisions (a) and (e). Plaintiffs argue that Mr. Cagle is over the age of 70 and his health is such that preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing Plaintiffs’ interest in the litigation. Plaintiffs request a trial be set 120 days from the date of the hearing on this matter.

 

On June 11, 2026, Defendants Mechanical Drives & Belting, DCO LLC, Pneumo Abex LLC, and Deere & Company filed oppositions. On the same day, Defendants Daimler Truck North America LLC, Kaiser Gypsum Company, Inc., Mack Trucks, Inc., Lear Siegler Diversified Holdings Corp., and Caterpillar Inc. filed joinders. On June 16, 2026, Defendant Mechanical Drives filed a notice of withdrawal.

 

In the joint opposition by Pneumo Abex and DCO (collectively, “DCO”), the parties do not dispute that Mr. Cagle is above the age of 70 or that he has a substantial interest in the litigation. Instead, DCO contend that Plaintiffs have not presented sufficient evidence, competent or current medical evidence, that Mr. Cagle’s health is such that preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing Plaintiffs’ interest in the litigation. DCO also ask the court to impose conditions on discovery and pre-trial motions to protect Defendants’ due process rights.

 

Deere argues in its opposition that preference is not mandatory and a grant would not serve the interest of justice. Alternatively, Deere argues that if the court were to grant preference, specific conditions should be imposed, such as for trial date timing and discovery considerations.  

 

On June 17, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their reply. Plaintiffs maintain that they satisfied their initial burden on this motion. They further argue that due process concerns do not give rise to denial.

 

Plaintiffs presented sufficient evidence to show that Mr. Cagle’s health is such that preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing Plaintiffs’ interest in the litigation. Therefore, Plaintiffs’ motion for trial preference is granted. Trial is set for Monday, October 19, 2026. The Final Status conference is set for Monday, October 5, 2026. A status conference for the proposed trial setting order is set for Thursday, July 16, 2026 at 9 a.m.

 

II.               Discussion

 

A.    Legal Standards

 

A party who is over 70 years old may petition the court for a preference for trial date, which the court shall grant if it makes both of the following findings: (1) the party has a substantial interest in the action as a whole; and (2) the health of the party is such that a preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing the party’s interest in the litigation. (Code Civ. Proc., § 36, subd. (a).) An affidavit submitted in support of a motion for preference under this subdivision may be signed by the attorney for the party seeking preference based upon information and belief as to the medical diagnosis and prognosis of any party. (Id., § 36.5.)

Moreover, the court may also grant a motion for preference in its discretion supported by a showing that satisfies the court that the interests of justice will be served by granting the preference.¿ (Id., § 36, subd. (e).)

 

“Upon the granting of such a motion for preference, the court shall set the matter for trial not more than 120 days from that date and there shall be no continuance beyond 120 days from the granting of the motion for preference except for physical disability of a party or a party’s attorney, or upon a showing of good cause stated in the record.”  (Id., § 36, subd. (f).)  “Any continuance shall be for no more than 15 days and no more than one continuance for physical disability may be granted to any party.” (Ibid.)

 

B.    Analysis

 

Plaintiff Dickie Ray Cagle (“Cagle”) is seventy-four years old and was diagnosed with Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia in October 2020. (Gold Decl., ¶¶ 7, 8(a), Exhs. A-C.) On August 3, 2021, a biopsy of Mr. Cagle’s left lung nodule resulted in a lung cancer diagnosis. (Id., ¶ 8(b), Exh. I.) A PET Scan on February 22, 2024, showed “mediastinal lymph nodes (measuring up to 1.1 cm), intense sigmoid colon FDG focus, left lung volume loss, scarring, pleural thickening and scattered areas of reticulation/fibrosis in both lungs, and subpleural bullous features in the lower lobe of his right lung.” (Id., ¶ 8(f), Exh. G.) Biopsied tissue from October 2025 “identified a well to moderately differentiated leukemia-related lung adenocarcinoma.” (Id., ¶ 8(g).) On March 4, 2026, Mr. Cagle was admitted to the hospital with “acute respiratory failure and lung abscess” and was treated for pneumonia. (Id., ¶ 8(j).) He was admitted to the hospital again four days later for “severe shortness of breath, tachycardia, fever, chills, weakness and productive cough.” (Id., ¶ 8(k), Exh. J.) A CT-Scan taken on the same day revealed chest abnormalities and “multiple prominent and enlarged bilateral inguinal, iliac chain, retroperitoneal, periportal and distal para-esophageal lymph nodes,” which had worsened since his 2023 PET scan. (Id.) Mr. Cagle has undergone chemotherapy multiple times over this period of time. Mr. Cagle continues to be treated for leukemia, lung cancer, and possible colon cancer. (Id., ¶ 8(m), Exh. G.) He has also been advised by his physicians that there is no cure for either his leukemia or his lung cancer. (Id., Exh. A; D. Cagle Decl., ¶ 3.)

Plaintiffs’ attorney of record Cecil B. Crain also described their observations of Mr. Cagle’s health during four separate visits spanning several months and lasting approximately two hours each:

“[d]uring each meeting, Mr. Cagle was weak and struggled to form complete sentences and maintain a conversation. I noticed that his ability to speak and maintain a conversation was getting worse each visit. He also experienced constant coughing and difficulty breathing, that kept getting worse every time I visited him. After my second visit, Mrs. Cagle called to let me know that Mr. Cagle had been admitted to the hospital with pneumonia. On my last visit with him on April 22nd, I noticed that his coughing and ability to speak was getting worse. I also noticed that his fatigue was much worse than my previous visits. Following my last visit on April 22, Mrs. Cagle let me know that Mr. Cagle had been diagnosed with tuberculosis.”

 (Crain Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) Counsel also states that Mr. Cagle’s “overall health declined with each successive visit,” and that he has not sat for a deposition yet due to his hospitalizations and health challenges. (Id., ¶ 5.)

As noted, only DCO and Deere substantively oppose Plaintiffs’ motion. In their joint opposition, DCO argue:

“Plaintiffs have not provided sufficient evidence that his health condition justifies a preferential trial setting. The only evidence submitted in support of the motion is a declaration from Plaintiffs’ counsel (Declaration of Cecil B. Crain). That declaration is anecdotal, speculative, and not based on competent medical evidence. The declaration references subjective impressions about Mr. Cagle’s speech patterns and breathing difficulties. Counsel also attaches medical records, which say nothing about Mr. Cagle’s declining health or his inability to participate in litigation. Also, no declaration from a treating physician or medical provider was submitted…It would not be an undue burden for Plaintiffs to submit current medical records or a declaration from Mr. Cagle’s treating physician to support the motion. The absence of such evidence is telling.”

(DCO Opp., at p. 3.)

 

Deere does not argue in its opposition that Plaintiffs have not provided sufficient evidence of Mr. Cagle’s health but rather argues that preference is not mandatory in asbestos litigation because it is a coordinated proceeding in Los Angeles County and Code of Civil Procedure section 404 applies. (Deere Opp., at pgs. 1-4 [The Isaak court, on the other hand, found that ‘[w]hen the two statutes are read together, they mean that the Judicial Council was authorized to enact rule 3.504, which grants trial courts the ability to proceed without strictly following laws governing civil actions, including section 36.’ (Isaak v. Superior Court (2002) 73 Cal.App.5th 792, 800 [emphasis added].) Preference is therefore not mandatory under any provision in coordinated proceedings where ‘“the prescribed manner of proceeding cannot, with reasonable diligence, be followed in a particular coordination proceeding”’. (Id. at 797-798 [quoting Cal. Rule of Court, Rule. 3.504(c)].) Instead, ‘“the assigned judge may prescribe any suitable manner of proceeding that appears most consistent with those statutes and rules.”’ (Isaak, supra, 73 Cal.App.5th at 797-798 [quoting Cal. Rule of Court, Rule. 3.504(c)].) The LAOSD Asbestos Cases coordinated proceeding abounds with similar provisions. (See General Asbestos Litigation Case Management Order entered in LAOSD Asbestos Cases JCCP No. 4674 on July 8, 2022 [‘LAOSD General Asbestos CMO’].)].) Deere argues that because preference is not mandatory here, the court should deny this motion because of due process concerns, at least until Mr. Cagle’s deposition is completed. (Id., at pgs. 4-5.)

 

In reply, Plaintiffs maintain that they satisfied their burden on this motion. First, Plaintiffs claim that DCOs’ assertion that Plaintiffs only submitted Crain’s declaration as evidence of Mr. Cagle’s condition is false, because Plaintiffs also submitted Mr. Gold’s, Mr. Cagle’s, and Mrs. Cagle’s declarations as well as Mr. Cagle’s medical records. (Reply at p. 1.) Plaintiffs also argue that Mr. Gold’s and Mr. Crain’s declarations meet the requirements under Code of Civil Procedure section 36.5, subdivision (a). (Id.) Second, Plaintiffs argue that DCO “ignores the fact” that Mr. Cagle “is suffering from terminal lung cancer along with a myriad of other conditions including leukemia, severe neuropathy, stage III chronic kidney disease, a pre-cancerous colon condition, and most recently pneumonia, and tuberculosis, all of which that have resulted in a rapid decline in his health since late 2025.” (Reply at p. 2.) Plaintiffs contend that Mr. Cagle is “in constant pain, overwhelmingly fatigued, short of breath, even at rest, weak, unable to sleep well, without appetite, and increasingly confined to bed for much of each day;” “he is now largely homebound, dependent on a walker or other support to move around, unable to safely go out alone, and no longer capable of participating in the daily activities that he enjoyed.” (Id.)

 

As to Deere’s Isaak argument, Plaintiffs argue the case is distinguishable from the facts here. (Isaak, supra, 73 Cal.App.5th at p. 792.) Plaintiffs contend that the “crucial difference” is that “Discovery is well underway. The issues of causation in asbestos exposure have been well litigated. This is not a new area that would require extensive discovery or witness preparation. Furthermore, Plaintiffs are willing to make concessions such as shortened time to respond to discovery, and to file Motions for Summary Judgment. As such, Isaak is not applicable because this is not a case where there is no possibility the case could not be tried in 120 days.” (Reply at pgs. 2-3.)

 

The evidence before the court is sufficient to establish that Plaintiffs are entitled to a trial preference under Code of Civil Procedure section 36, subdivision (a). There is no dispute that Mr. Cagle’s age is above the threshold required under the statute, or that he has a substantial interest in the litigation. The only issue in dispute is whether his health is such that Plaintiffs’ interest in the litigation is likely to be prejudiced in the absence of trial preference. The court finds that the evidence presented is sufficient to satisfy this burden. The declarations of Mr. Crain, Mr. Gold, and attached documentation are sufficient to show that Mr. Cagle’s current symptoms and the likely progression of his symptoms given the absence of viable treatment will impact his testimonial capacities to his prejudice. For instance, Mr. Crain describes Mr. Cagle’s current symptoms as significantly affecting his ability to maintain a conversation. (Crain Decl. ¶ 3 [“During each meeting, Mr. Cagle was weak and struggled to form complete sentences and maintain a conversation. I noticed that his ability to speak and maintain a conversation was getting worse each visit. He also experienced constant coughing and difficulty breathing, that kept getting worse every time I visited him.”].) This, paired with the evidence that Mr. Cagle lacks viable treatment options and his other serious medical conditions, is sufficient to persuade the court that his health is such that Plaintiffs’ interest in the litigation will be prejudiced without trial preference.  

 

Moreover, as Plaintiffs note, under the required standard for Code of Civil Procedure section 36, subdivision (a), Plaintiffs were not required to present a declaration from a physician regarding Mr. Cagle’s health, let alone a declaration from a treating physician. (Code Civ. Proc., § 36.5 [“An affidavit submitted in support of a motion for preference under subdivision (a) of Section 36 may be signed by the attorney for the party seeking preference based upon information and belief as to the medical diagnosis and prognosis of any party. The affidavit is not admissible for any purpose other than a motion for preference under subdivision (a) of Section 36.”].) Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion for trial preference is granted.

 

Both substantive oppositions also request that the court impose certain conditions as part of the trial setting order including, but not limited to, shortening the motion for summary judgment and/or adjudication notice period, ordering Plaintiffs’ and third-party witnesses’ immediate availability for depositions, and ordering Plaintiffs to provide any outstanding authorizations. Defendants do not cite any authority for the proposition that the court can impose such conditions upon the parties as part of an order granting trial setting preference. The court expects the parties to meet and confer regarding the appropriate conditions for a proposed trial setting order. In reply, Plaintiffs indicate that they are willing to agree to certain stipulations and will meet and confer with Defendants on a date for Mr. Cagle’s deposition once he is physically able. (Reply at p. 3.) If there are disputes as to the appropriate conditions, the court will rule on these disputes at the status conference regarding the trial setting order.

 

 

III.           Conclusion

 

Plaintiffs’ motion for trial preference is granted. The parties are to meet and confer regarding appropriate conditions for a trial setting order. The court sets trial for Monday, October 19, 2026, at 9:00 A.M. The Final Status conference is set for Monday, October 5, 2026 at 9:00 A.M. A status conference for the proposed trial setting order is set for Thursday, July 16, 2026 at 9 a.m. Plaintiffs shall confer with Defendants and give notice of this ruling.

    SPECIALLY APPEARING DEFENDANT BASHAS’ INC.’S MOTION TO QUASH SERVICE OF SUMMONS AND COMPLAINT FOR LACK OF PERSONAL JURISDICTION

     

 

 

I.                Background

 

On April 30, 2026, Plaintiff Janet Larkly filed her first amended complaint for personal injury alleging she developed mesothelioma from familial and personal use of asbestos-containing talcum powder products throughout her lifetime, including personal use of cosmetic products starting in high school, and her mother’s use of powder products on her brother and herself starting from infancy. Plaintiff also alleges she was exposed to asbestos-containing materials while at jobsites due to their presence and use by other contractors. As against specially appearing Defendant Bashas’ Inc. (“Bashas’ ” or “Defendant”), Plaintiff alleges Bashas’ is joined “for retailing talc products” containing asbestos that she was exposed to in various residences throughout California from 1950-2008, her residence in Texas from 2008 to 2015, and various residences throughout Arizona from 2015 to the present.

 

On March 27, 2026, Defendant Bashas’ filed its motion to quash arguing Plaintiff failed to show evidence supporting the exercise of jurisdiction over it. Defendant argues it is an out of state corporation with only one previous retail grocery store location in Needles, California, which operated between 1989 and May 2014, and is therefore not subject to general jurisdiction in California. Defendant further argues that Plaintiff failed to present evidence that her claims arise from or relate to Defendant’s California contacts, especially because Plaintiff alleges she was exposed to asbestos by talc powder sales in Los Angeles County and Needles is in San Bernardino County.  

 

On April 11, 2026, Plaintiff opposed, arguing the court may exercise special jurisdiction over Defendant because it was registered to do business in California in 1988 and 1989 and Defendant conceded that it operated the Needles location at the time of registration and for almost 25 years. Plaintiff also argues that she satisfies the purposeful availment prong because her underlying allegations that she purchased asbestos-containing talc body powder and cosmetics and Defendant’s retail of such products are enough at this stage. As to the reasonableness prong, Plaintiff argues that she has sufficiently demonstrated that the court has specific jurisdiction here and so the burden moves to Defendant, who submitted no evidence of unfairness or unreasonableness. In the alternative, Plaintiff argues she is entitled to jurisdictional discovery to present evidence to support her contention that Defendant was a retailer of asbestos-containing talc body and cosmetic powders. Plaintiff relies primarily on Defendant’s Statement and Designation by Foreign Corporation filed on August 26, 1988, and Defendant’s Certificate of Surrender of Right to Transact Intrastate Business filed on July 26, 1989.

 

The court finds Plaintiff failed to present sufficient evidence to show that Plaintiff’s claims arise from or relate to Bashas’ California contacts. Plaintiff likewise fails to present sufficient evidence to show she is entitled to a continuance to conduct jurisdictional discovery. Therefore, Defendant’s motion to quash is granted.

 

II.               Discussion

 

A.    Legal Standards

 

A defendant may move to quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over them. (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a)(1).) The court may dismiss without prejudice the complaint in whole, or as to that defendant, when dismissal is made pursuant to Section 418.10. (Code Civ. Proc., § 581, subd. (h).)¿ 

 

"A court of this state may exercise jurisdiction on any basis not inconsistent with the Constitution of this state or of the United States." (Code Civ. Proc., § 410.10.) "The Due Process Clause protects an individual's liberty interest in not being subject to the binding judgments of a forum with which he has established no meaningful 'contacts, ties, or relations."' (Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz (1985) 471 U.S. 462, 471 – 472.) A state court may not exercise personal jurisdiction over a party under circumstances that would offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." (Asahi Metal Industry Co., Ltd., v. Superior Court of California, Solano County (1987) 480 U.S. 102, 113.)¿ 

 

When a defendant moves to quash service of process on jurisdictional grounds, the plaintiff has the initial burden of demonstrating facts justifying the exercise of jurisdiction. (Jayone Foods, Inc. v. Aekyung Industrial Co. Ltd. (2019) 31 Cal.App.5th 543, 553.) Once facts showing minimum contacts with the forum state are established, the defendant has the burden to demonstrate the exercise of jurisdiction would be unreasonable. (Ibid.) "The plaintiff must provide specific evidentiary facts, through affidavits and other authenticated documents, sufficient to allow the court to independently conclude whether jurisdiction is appropriate. [Citation.] The plaintiff cannot rely on allegations in an unverified complaint or vague and conclusory assertions of ultimate facts. [Citation.]" (Strasner, supra, 5 Cal.App.5th at p. 222.)¿ 

 

A defendant is subject to a state's general jurisdiction if its contacts "are so continuous and systematic as to render [it] essentially at home in the forum State." (Daimler AG v. Bauman (2014) 571 U.S. 117, 127.) A nonresident defendant may be subject to the specific jurisdiction of the forum "if the defendant has purposefully availed himself or herself of forum benefits [citation], and the 'controversy is related to or "arises out of' a defendant's contacts with the forum.' [Citations.]" (Vons Companies, Inc. v. Seabest Foods, Inc. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 434, 446.) This test does not require a "causal relationship between the defendant's in-state activity and the litigation." (Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court (2021) 592 U.S. 351, 362) The "arise out" of standard "asks about causation," but "relate to" does not. (Ibid.) "[W]hen a corporation has 'continuously and deliberately exploited [a State's] market, it must reasonably anticipate being haled into [that State's] court[s]' to defend actions 'based on' products causing injury there." (Id. at p. 364.)

 

B.    General Jurisdiction

 

General jurisdiction is not at issue. Plaintiff does not argue that Bashas’ is subject to general jurisdiction in California. Accordingly, the court analyzes whether Plaintiff presents evidence that Defendant is subject to specific jurisdiction in California.

 

C.    Specific Jurisdiction

 

A court may exercise specific jurisdiction over a plaintiff when three elements are satisfied: “(1) ‘the defendant has purposefully availed himself or herself of forum benefits’ [citation]; (2) ‘the controversy is related to or “arises out of” [the] defendant's contacts with the forum’ [citation]; and (3) “‘“the assertion of personal jurisdiction would comport with ‘fair play and substantial justice’” [citation.]”  (Pavlovich v. Superior Court (2002) 29 Cal.4th 262, 269.)

 

Here, Bashas’ argues that there is no factual basis to establish specific jurisdiction because Plaintiff has not established a connection between non-resident Defendant and California. Plaintiff has not shown or provided evidence that she purchased and used asbestos-containing talc powders from Defendant’s Needles location between 1989 and 2014. (Motion at pgs. 8-9 [To exercise specific jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant, there must be an “affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy, principally [an] activity or an occurrence that takes place in the forum State.” (Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of California (2017) 582 U.S. 255, 282 (Bristol-Myers), citing Goodyear, supra, 564 U.S. at p. 931.) “When there is no such connection, specific jurisdiction is lacking regardless of the extent of a defendant’s unconnected activities in the State. (Ibid., citing Goodyear, supra, 564 U.S. at p. 93.) The only connection between Bashas’ and the State of California was that Bashas’ had a retail grocery location in Needles, California, between 1989 and 2014. (Exhibit “A,” ¶ 3.) To establish a claim for specific jurisdiction over Bashas’ therefore, Plaintiff would have to submit an affidavit or authenticated documentation demonstrating that she purchased and used talc powder containing asbestos from Bashas’ Needles location during that time period. (See Auto. Antitrust, supra, 135 Cal.App. 4th at 110–11; Via View, Inc. v. Retzlaff (2016) 1 Cal.App.5th 198, 210; Paneno v. Centres for Academic Programmes Abroad Ltd. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 1447, 1454.)].)

 

1.     Purposeful Availment

Purposeful availment “focuses on the defendant's intentionality. This prong is satisfied when the defendant purposefully and voluntarily directs his activities toward the forum so that he should expect, by virtue of the benefit he receives, to be subject to the court's jurisdiction based on his contacts with the forum.” (Pavlovich, supra, 29 Cal.4th at p. 269.)  

            Here, Defendant argues that the only connection between California and itself was the retail grocery location in Needles between 1989 and 2014. (Motion at p. 8.) The court notes that while Defendant references Exhibit “A,” which it states is the declaration of David C. Basha, no Exhibits have been attached to the motion, nor has the Declaration been filed separately.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that this prong is not in dispute because Defendant was registered to do business in California in 1988 and 1989 and conceded that it operated the Needles grocery store from May 18, 1989 to May 2014. (Opp. at p. 3; Halbany Decl., Exh. 1.) Bashas’ surrendered its right to transact intrastate business on July 3, 1989, which was filed with the Secretary of State of the State of California on July 26, 1989. (Id., Exh. 2.)

 

The court agrees with Plaintiff that this prong is not in dispute because Defendant’s motion as to specific jurisdiction solely focuses on the connection between California and itself and the underlying controversy. Accordingly, the court turns to the relatedness prong.

 

2.     Relatedness

A plaintiff may present sufficient evidence of relatedness where there is evidence the defendant “systematically served a market in [the forum state] for the very [products]that the plaintiffs allege malfunctioned and injured them in those States.” (Ford, supra, 592 U.S. at p. 366.) The inquiry must be focused on determining whether there is “a connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue.” (Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of California, San Francisco County (2017) 582 U.S. 255, 265.) Accordingly, the court looks to determine whether Plaintiff presented evidence that Defendant served the California market for the same product Plaintiff alleges injured her.  ¿ 

            Plaintiff fails to present sufficient evidence to support a finding of relatedness. Plaintiff fails to present evidence of what products she alleges harmed her and where she obtained them. In her complaint, Plaintiff merely states Defendant retailed “talc products.” (Compl. ¶ 25.) In opposition, Plaintiff only references “asbestos-containing talc body powder and cosmetics, and alleges that Bashas was a retailer of these products.” (Opp. at p. 3.) Plaintiff does not present any admissible evidence to support her exposure to these general categories of asbestos-containing talc body powder and cosmetics or that she purchased those products from Defendant’s grocery store in Needles. On this motion, Plaintiff must present admissible evidence to support her theory of jurisdiction. (Strasner, supra, 5 Cal.App.5th at p. 222 [“The plaintiff must provide specific evidentiary facts, through affidavits and other authenticated documents, sufficient to allow the court to independently conclude whether jurisdiction is appropriate. [Citation.] The plaintiff cannot rely on allegations in an unverified complaint or vague and conclusory assertions of ultimate facts. [Citation.]”]) Accordingly, in the absence of any admissible evidence regarding what products Plaintiff used or where Plaintiff purchased them, there is no evidence from which the court could make the determination that Plaintiff’s claims arose from or related to Defendant’s forum contacts.

 

Plaintiff’s contention appears to be that she was not required to present any admissible evidence of her exposure to a product for which Defendant was responsible to satisfy her burden on relatedness. (Opposition at p. 3 [“in Jayone Foods, the Court of Appeal upheld California’s specific personal jurisdiction over a distributor’s cross-complaint for indemnity against a foreign manufacturer, because the allegations of the underlying tort complaint were based in California.”].) Plaintiff’s opposition therefore relies exclusively on the allegations in her complaint as to support her theory of relatedness. (Id. at pp. 3-4.) 

 

Plaintiff’s interpretation of the Jayone case as permitting her to show she satisfied the relatedness prong without admissible evidence of the product at issue is unpersuasive. The Jayone case arose from a cross complaint filed by the defendant and cross complainant Jayone against another Defendant. (Jayone, supra, 31 Cal.App.5th at pp. 548-549 [“Plaintiffs allege that An's death was caused by her long-term and frequent use of the Aekyung Humidifier Cleaning Agent, and on that basis, assert claims for products liability and negligence. [¶] On December 2, 2015, Jayone filed a cross-complaint against Aekyung (a Korean corporation), Kim's Home Center (a California corporation), and¿Woosung America Corporation (Woosung) (a California corporation).¿The cross-complaint alleges claims for equitable indemnity, contribution, and declaratory relief with respect to any judgment rendered against Jayone and in favor of Plaintiffs.”]) Accordingly, unlike in this case, the evidence supporting the underlying exposure to the allegedly hazardous product in the Jayone case was not in the possession of the party opposing the motion to quash. Instead, the Jayone cross-complainant was able to rely on the plaintiff’s allegations in the underlying complaint as an admission of the plaintiff of their theory of the case.

 

Second, in Jayone the evidence presented by the cross-complainant specified the product at issue in the case. There, the cross-complainant’s opposition included a declaration and deposition testimony directly identified the product at issue in the plaintiff’s underlying complaint and discussed the time period during which these products were imported by cross complainant from the cross defendant. (Jayone, supra, 31 Cal.App.5th at p. 550-552.) This latter evidence was key to supporting a finding of relatedness in the case. (Id. at p. 560 [“In opposing Aekyung's motion to quash, Jayone presented evidence that it directly purchased from Aekyung 200 boxes of the Humidifier Mate in April 2006, and another 100 boxes of the Humidifier Mate in January 2007. All products were shipped to Jayone at the Port of Los Angeles. Jayone also presented evidence that it sold 55 boxes of the Humidifier Mate to Kim's Home Center in Los Angeles between 2006 and 2007. Those sales occurred in December 2006, October 2007, and November 2007. The Humidifier Mate products that Jayone sold to Kim's Home Center included product that Jayone purchased from Aekyung in April 2006 and January 2007. Accordingly, the record shows that, between 2006 and 2007, (1) An¿allegedly purchased the Aekyung Humidifier Mate directly from Kim's Home Center; (2) Kim's Home Center purchased 660 bottles of the Humidifier Mate directly from Jayone; and (3) Jayone purchased 3,600 bottles of the Humidifier Mate directly from Aekyung. These California sales involving the Aekyung Humidifier Mate substantially connect Plaintiffs' claims to Aekyung's contacts with the state.”] 

 

Here, in contrast, Plaintiff is the only party who possesses the relevant evidence that would show what products she was exposed to and nevertheless failed to identify the products at issue in this case. The court recognizes that Plaintiffs are under no obligation to prove that Defendant caused Plaintiff’s injuries at the jurisdictional stage. (Bader v. Avon Products, Inc. (2020) 55 Cal.App.5th 186, 198 [“In sum, neither Bristol-Myers nor any other authority cited by Avon requires proof of a product defect at the jurisdictional phase, and the trial court erred by requiring Bader to establish that the Avon perfumed talc powder used by Schmitz contained asbestos at this juncture”].)  However, Plaintiff is required to present evidence that the products she alleges injured her were connected to Defendant’s intentional contacts with California. (Id. at p. 200 [“This evidence establishes that Avon used sales representatives to market and sell the products at issue directly to Schmitz in California. This evidence establishes a sufficient ‘“affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy, principally, [an] activity or an occurrence that takes place in the forum State and is therefore subject to the State's regulation.”’”] Accordingly, while Plaintiff did not need to present evidence that any specific product to which she was exposed contained asbestos at this stage, Plaintiff was required to provide some evidence that Bashas’ purposefully availed itself of the California market by intentionally selling products to California and that the sales of these products were related to the injury that Plaintiff suffered. In the absence of admissible evidence regarding what product or products Plaintiff alleged caused her injuries, the court has no way to make either determination. 

 

Therefore, Plaintiff failed to present sufficient evidence to support a finding of relatedness. 

 

D.    Jurisdictional Discovery

 

A plaintiff attempting to assert jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant is entitled to an opportunity to conduct discovery of the jurisdictional facts necessary to sustain its burden of proof[.]” (Hardell v. Vanzyl (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 960, 975-976; Goehring v. Superior Court (Bernier) (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 894.) The court has “discretion to continue the hearing on a motion to quash service of summons for lack of personal jurisdiction to allow the plaintiff to conduct discovery on jurisdictional issues.” (HealthMarkets, Inc. v. Superior Court (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1160, 1173.) However, “In order to prevail on a motion for a continuance for jurisdictional discovery, the plaintiff should demonstrate that discovery is likely to lead to the production of evidence of facts establishing jurisdiction.” (In re Automobile Antitrust Cases I & II (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 100, 127.)

 

Plaintiff argues that limited jurisdictional discovery is appropriate here without presenting sufficient evidence to entitle her to conduct jurisdictional discovery. Plaintiff states discovery would be directed to Defendant’s purposeful availment, including the foundation for David Basha’s declaration statements, which the court noted above has not been filed, and “all names under which any retail grocery stores were operated, and the dates and locations of all retail stores operated, owned, or controlled by Bashas during the years that Plaintiff lived in California.” (Opp. at page 4.)

 

As noted above, Plaintiff’s opposition failed to present evidence regarding the products Plaintiff alleges Defendant was responsible for retailing which gave rise to Plaintiff’s injuries. Because of this missing evidence, the court finds that Plaintiff has not presented evidence showing that it is likely that further discovery will lead to the production of facts supporting jurisdiction over Defendant. In the absence of evidence that Defendant is subject to general jurisdiction in California, there must be a relationship between Plaintiff’s causes of action and Defendant’s California contacts to justify the exercise of specific personal jurisdiction over Defendant. Plaintiff need not prove her exposure to asbestos at this stage.

 

Because Defendant is a retailer of final products, Plaintiff was required to submit evidence that she purchased and used asbestos-containing talc products from Defendant’s Needles store location during the relevant time period. Plaintiff did not submit an affidavit, declaration, or authenticated documentation demonstrating purchase of products from Defendant’s store or identification of products. The absence of admissible evidence supporting Plaintiff’s threshold exposure to any identified product does not merit a continuance to conduct jurisdictional discovery into facts which should already be in Plaintiff’s possession. Accordingly, the court finds that Plaintiffs failed to present sufficient evidence to merit a continuance to conduct jurisdictional discovery. Any jurisdictional discovery would not cure the absence of evidence regarding the identity of the products at issue and Plaintiff’s exposure to those products.  The court also noes that the preference trial date is less than three weeks from today’s hearing.

 

III.           Conclusion

Plaintiff failed to present sufficient evidence to show that Bashas’ is subject to jurisdiction in California. Plaintiff also failed to present sufficient evidence to entitle her to a continuance to conduct jurisdictional discovery because she presented no admissible evidence regarding what talc powder products she used and bought from Defendant’s Needles store location which she alleges contained asbestos. Therefore, Defendant’s motion to quash service of summons and complaint for lack of personal jurisdiction is granted. The court dismisses the complaint without prejudice pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 581, subdivision (h).

Moving Defendant Bashas’ Inc. is ordered to give notice.
