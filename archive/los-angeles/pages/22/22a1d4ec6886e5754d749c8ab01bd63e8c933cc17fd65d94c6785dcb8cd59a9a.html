BACKGROUND

 

            On March 28, 2022, Plaintiff Pacific Coast Co. Ltd. d/b/a Brincfi filed the Complaint against Daniel Choi, alleging causes of action for conversion, intentional fraud, breach of contract, breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and unjust enrichment.

            No opposition has been filed.

 

           

LEGAL STANDARD

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437 subdivision (c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67, citing FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal. App. 3d 367, 381-382.)  

 

As to each cause of action, as framed by the complaint, the defendant moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to show “that one or more elements of the cause of action ... cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(p)(2); see also Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)  

 

Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto. To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)  

 

DISCUSSION

 

           

Defendant moves for summary judgment on the FAC.

 

Token Ownership

 

Defendant moves for summary judgment on the ground that every cause of action in Plaintiff's First Amended Complaint depends on Plaintiff’s ownership of the subject tokens, and that Plaintiff cannot make the necessary showing of ownership. Defendant contends that Plaintiff's verified discovery responses abandoned any possession-based theory and that its person most knowledgeable admitted the company did not own the tokens at the time of the alleged theft. Defendant characterizes Plaintiff's later claim program as a settlement offer letting the true token owners take ten percent of value in exchange for waiving claims against Plaintiff, not a transfer of ownership.

 

Defendant further argues that Plaintiff neither pleaded nor can prove an assignment. A plaintiff cannot defeat summary judgment on a theory absent from the operative pleading, and the First Amended Complaint never mentions an assignment. (Laabs v. City of Victorville (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1258.) In any event, an assignment requires a clear manifestation of intent to transfer the specific right, and the claim program document shows only a settlement offer. (California Ins. Guarantee Assn. v. Workers' Comp. Appeals Bd. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 1328, 1335.)

 

No opposition has been filed.

 

Here, Defendant has shown that Plaintiff did not own the tokens at the time of alleged theft. (UMF 3.) Thus, as all causes of action are based on Plaintiff’s theft of the tokens, the Court finds that Defendant has met his initial burden.

 

As Plaintiff has not filed an opposition, Plaintiff has failed to show any triable issues of material fact.

 

Therefore, the Court grants the Motion for Summary Judgment.

HEARING: Motion to Set Aside Dismissal

 

            The Court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers.

 

RULING

 

            The Court denies the Motion to Set Aside Dismissal.

           

BACKGROUND

 

            On December 31, 2024, Plaintiff Miach Herd filed the Complaint against Defendant Ford Motor Company alleging causes of action for violations of the Song-Beverly Act.

 

            On October 27, 2025, after Plaintiff failed to appear at the hearing on the Motion to Compel, the Court dismissed the case without prejudice.

 

            On March 25, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Motion to Set Aside Dismissal.

 

            On April 20, 2026, the Court granted the Motion to Set Aside Dismissal.

 

            On June 8, 2026, the parties stipulated to vacate the prior order, as Plaintiff failed to serve Defendant with the prior motion and had Plaintiff refile the Motion to Set Aside Dismissal.

 

            On June 26, 2026, Plaintiff refiled the instant motion.

 

            On July 31, 2026, Defendant filed the Opposition.

 

            On August 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Reply.

                       

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect…¿ [The application]¿shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.”¿ (Code Civ. Proc., §¿473 subd. (b).) “‘[W]hen relief under section 473¿is¿available, there is a strong¿public¿policy¿in¿favor¿of granting relief and allowing the requesting party his or her day in court…[Citation.]” (Rappleyea v. Campbell¿(1994) 8 Cal. 4th 975, 981-82.) 

¿ 

Although a trial court has the discretion to vacate the entry of a default or subsequent judgment, this discretion may be exercised only after the party seeking relief has shown that there is a proper ground for relief and that the party has raised that ground in a procedurally proper manner, within any applicable time limits.”¿(Cruz v.¿Fagor¿America, Inc. (2007) 146 Cal.App.4th 488, 495.) “The defendant must … demonstrate a satisfactory excuse for not responding to the original action in a timely manner.”¿(Id.¿at 504.) Moving parties have the initial burden to prove excusable neglect by a preponderance of competent evidence.¿(Kendall v. Barker¿(1988) 197 Cal.App.3d 619, 624.)

¿ 

Code Civ. Proc.,¿§ 473 subd. (d) provides in relevant part that “[t]he court may,¿upon motion of the injured party…set aside any void judgment or order.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473 subd. (d.))¿ 

 

Code Civ. Proc., section 473.5 permits the Court to set aside a default and default judgment when the service of a summons has not resulted in actual notice to a party in time to defend the action. Code Civ. Proc., section 473.5 requires the motion to be accompanied by an affidavit showing under oath that the party's lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by the party's avoidance of service or inexcusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5.)¿The notice of motion shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of the following:¿ 

¿ 

1) two years after entry of a default judgment against him or her; or¿¿ 

2) 180 days after service on him or her of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered. ¿¿ 

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5.)

 

DISCUSSION

 

            Timeliness

 

            An application for discretionary relief must be made within a reasonable time, not exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken. (Arambula v. Union Carbide Corp. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 333, 340.) The six-month limit is mandatory; a court has no authority to grant relief under section 473, subdivision (b), unless an application is made within the six-month period. (Ibid.)

 

            In Arambula, the Court found that a later filed motion cannot relate back to an earlier defective motion to satisfy the six-month deadline found in section 473(b). (Id. at 341-342.) Here, the facts are extremely similar to Arambula; Plaintiff filed a timely Motion to Set Aside. But failed to serve it, followed by an untimely but served Motion to Set Aside. (See Ibid.) Thus, the Court must find that the instant motion is untimely. Plaintiff argues that the parties' stipulation waived the timeliness issues and that denying the instant motion after granting the previous one would give Defendant an unearned windfall. However, the Court had no power to grant the original motion as no service was completed, and the Court cannot consider any public policy or fairness issues because the six-month time limit is mandatory and jurisdictional.  (Id at 340.)

 

            Therefore, the Court denies the Motion to Set Aside Dismissal.

LEGAL STANDARD

 

The Federal Arbitration Act (the “FAA”) applies in both federal and state courts to contracts evidencing a transaction involving interstate commerce. (9 U.S.C. §§ 1-2; Southland Corp. v. Keating (1984) 465 U.S. 1, 12.) The FAA preempts conflicting state law. (Preston v. Ferrer (2008) 552 U.S. 346, 353.) The party that contends the FAA applies bears the burden to demonstrate that the arbitration agreement is in a “contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce.” (Woolls v. Super. Ct. (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 197, 211.) 

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2, “[o]n petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party thereto refuses to arbitrate such controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists unless it determines that: (a) The right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or (b)¿Grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement.”¿ 

 

DISCUSSION

Existence of Valid Arbitration Agreement and Claims Covered by Arbitration Clause¿ 

 

“[T]he petitioner bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence…” (Giuliano v. Inland Empire Personnel, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1276, 1284.) “In determining whether an arbitration agreement applies to a specific dispute, the court may examine only the agreement itself and the complaint filed by the party refusing arbitration [citation]. The court should attempt to give effect to the parties’ intentions, in light of the usual and ordinary meaning of the contractual language and the circumstances under which the agreement was made.” (Weeks v. Crow (1980) 113 Cal.App.3d 350, 353.) “To determine whether a contractual arbitration clause requires arbitration of a particular controversy, the controversy is first identified, and the issue is whether that controversy is within the scope of the contractual arbitration clause.” (Titolo v. Cano (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 310, 316.) “Doubts as to whether an arbitration clause applies to a particular dispute are to be resolved in favor of sending the parties to arbitration. The court should order them to arbitrate unless it is clear that the arbitration clause cannot be interpreted to cover the dispute.” (California Correctional Peace Officers Ass’n v. State (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 198, 205.) 

 

“[A] party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense. [Citation.] In these summary proceedings, the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Giuliano, supra, 149 Cal.App.4th at p. 1284.) 

 

“The burden of persuasion is always on the moving party to prove the existence of an arbitration agreement with the opposing party by a preponderance of the evidence: Because the existence of the agreement is a statutory prerequisite to granting the [motion or] petition, the [party seeking arbitration] bears the burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence.” (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.) However, the burden is a three-step framework. “First, the moving party bears the burden of producing prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy. The moving party can meet its initial burden by attaching to the [motion or] petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party's] signature. Alternatively, the moving party can meet its burden by setting forth the agreement's provisions in the motion.” (Ibid.) “For this step, it is not necessary to follow the normal procedures of document authentication.” (Ibid.)

 

“If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then in the second step, the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement.” (Ibid.) “The opposing party can do this in several ways. For example, the opposing party may testify under oath or declare under penalty of perjury that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement.” (Ibid.)

 

Last, “if the opposing party meets its burden of producing evidence, then in the third step, the moving party must establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties.” (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff argues that no valid arbitration agreement exists and that Defendant, which bears the burden of proving a valid, written agreement to arbitrate by a preponderance of the evidence, cannot carry that burden. Because the existence of an agreement to arbitrate is governed by state contract law, the Federal Arbitration Act does not compel enforcement absent a valid agreement under California law. (First Options of Chicago, Inc. v. Kaplan (1995) 514 U.S. 938, 944.)

 

Plaintiff first contends that neither provision binds him because both lack consent. Contract formation requires mutual assent, measured objectively by the parties' outward manifestations. (Harris v. TAP Worldwide, LLC (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 373, 381.) That standard does not apply, however, where the offeree does not know a proposal has been made, so an offeree is not bound by inconspicuous provisions of which it was unaware in a document whose contractual nature is not obvious, a principle that applies with particular force to arbitration. (Knutson v. Sirius XM Satellite Radio, Inc. (9th Cir. 2014) 771 F.3d 559, 566.) On that basis, courts have refused to enforce arbitration provisions buried in purchase forms, rental agreements, subscription arrangements, and warranty booklets of which the party was unaware. (Domestic Linen Supply Co., Inc. v. LJT Flores, Inc. (2020) 58 Cal.App.5th 180, 185.) Plaintiff asserts the same facts here, contending that South Bay Hyundai never explained that the Owner's Handbook and Warranty Information booklet contained an arbitration provision, that he never acknowledged receipt of the booklet, and that it was placed in the Vehicle before the lease and has remained there since. Because he neither read nor was shown the provision, Plaintiff argues he did not consent, and either provision fails for lack of mutual assent.

 

Plaintiff next argues that the warranty, though quasi-contractual, is not a contract capable of binding him. Where a party did not know it was entering a contract, both the contract and its arbitration clause are void for lack of mutual assent. (Esparza v. Sand & Sea, Inc. (2016) 2 Cal.App.5th 781, 789-790.) California law does not treat manufacturer warranties as part of the sales contract and never has. (Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 1324, 1334-1336.) A warranty binds the seller as an express representation of what it agreed to sell but imposes no binding obligation on the buyer beyond allowing a reasonable opportunity to repair. (Norcia v. Samsung Telecommunications America, LLC (9th Cir. 2017) 845 F.3d 1279, 1288.) Because the warranty here does not bind Plaintiff, he argues that, as in Esparza, there is no underlying contract and the arbitration provision is void for lack of mutual assent.

 

In reply, Defendant argues that it has established the existence of an agreement to arbitrate and that the Warranty's provision is enforceable. On a petition to compel arbitration, the petitioner need not authenticate the agreement or introduce it into evidence, because the court makes only a preliminary finding of the agreement's existence, and once the petitioner alleges the agreement exists, the burden shifts to the respondent to prove its falsity. (Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 218-219.) Defendant contends it alleged the agreement in its moving papers and that Plaintiff's declaration confirms the Vehicle came with a copy of the Warranty. (Valenzuela Decl., ¶ 8.)

 

Defendant contends that Plaintiff's own Complaint establishes mutual assent, because Plaintiff sued for breach of the express written warranty and relied on the Warranty as the foundation of the action. A party is estopped from contradicting its own pleadings and may not use a contract when it is advantageous and then avoid its arbitration clause. (Sholly v. Annan (9th Cir. 1971) 450 F.2d 74, 76.) A Song-Beverly claim exists only because the manufacturer issued an express warranty, so Plaintiff cannot invoke the Warranty and Defendant's obligations under it while denying assent to its terms. (Cummins, Inc. v. Superior Court (2005) 36 Cal.4th 478, 484.) Defendant adds that the Warranty states in bold that a California lessee agrees to the binding arbitration provision by using the Vehicle or accepting warranty benefits.

 

Defendant also argues the absence of a signature is no bar because a party is estopped from relying on it while enforcing other terms of the same contract, and a nonsignatory who receives a direct benefit is estopped from refusing to arbitrate. (Boucher v. Alliance Title Co., Inc. (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 262, 269.) Voluntary acceptance of a transaction's benefit is consent to its obligations, and an offeree who knows an offer has been made may accept its terms through conduct.  (Windsor Mills, Inc. v. Collins & Aikman Corp. (1972) 25 Cal.App.3d 987, 992.) The controlling equitable estoppel inquiry is whether the plaintiff seeks to enforce beneficial contractual terms while avoiding its own agreement to arbitrate. (Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2025) No. S279969.) Whether Plaintiff read the arbitration terms is irrelevant, and Defendant disputes that the provision is buried, noting it opens the Hyundai Warranty Information section and appears in the table of contents.

 

Finally, Defendant argues Norcia is inapposite because that plaintiff brought no warranty claims, so contract law rather than warranty law governed, and the court noted a warranty like this one could require arbitration. (Norcia, supra, 845 F.3d 1279, 1284, 1288, fn. 3.) Because Plaintiff's claims rest on the Warranty, Defendant contends mutual assent is conceded, and district courts have compelled arbitration on the same facts.

 

In Kostandian v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 872, the Court found that the defendant met its initial burden by presenting a copy of the warranty booklet, reciting its terms verbatim, and providing the necessary allegations, which shifted the burden to the plaintiff, who then produced no evidence. (Id. at 882.) While the Court mentioned the signed delivery sheet, it was not the basis for its finding that the defendant had met its initial burden. (Ibid.)

 

            Here, Defendant has provided the warranty booklet included with the car that included the arbitration agreement. Defendant can meet its initial burden by merely reciting the terms of the arbitration agreement. (Gamboa, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.) Thus, while there was a signed delivery sheet in Kostandian, one is not required for Defendant to pass its initial burden. (Kostandian, supra, 120 Cal.App.5th 872, 882.) Thus, the Court finds that Defendant has met its initial burden. In response, Plaintiff has provided a declaration stating that he was never told about the arbitration agreement and never signed any agreement. (Valenzuela Decl. ¶ 5-7.) However, the Declaration states that the warranty booklet was delivered with the car in its glovebox. (Valenzuela Decl. ¶ 8.) Furthermore, the Court has found that warranties in manuals become part of the bargain when packaged with the product, even if not read. (Weinstat v. Dentsply Internat., Inc. (2010) 180 Cal.App.4th 1213, 1228-1229.) Plaintiff's reliance on Norcia does not change the result. Norcia involved a nonwarranty dispute and expressly reserved whether a plaintiff who sues on the warranty may be compelled to arbitrate claims arising out of that warranty. (Norcia, supra, 845 F.3d 1279, 1287-1288.) Here, Plaintiff sues for breach of the express written warranty contained in the same booklet as the arbitration provision, the situation Norcia left open and Kostandian resolved in favor of arbitration. (Kostandian, 120 Cal.App.5th 872, 883-884.) Thus, because Plaintiff received the booklet with the vehicle and now sues to enforce the warranty it contains, Plaintiff has not met his burden of showing a lack of assent.

 

Unconscionability

 

            “The burden of proving unconscionability rests upon the party asserting it.” (OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho (2019) 8 Cal.5th 111, 126.) “‘[U]nconscionability has both a “procedural” and a “substantive” element,’ the former focusing on ‘oppression’ or ‘surprise’ due to unequal bargaining power, the latter on ‘overly harsh’ or ‘one-sided’ results.” (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th at p. 114 [citations omitted].) “As a matter of general contract law, California courts require both procedural and substantive unconscionability to invalidate a contract.” (Torrecillas v. Fitness International, LLC (2020) 52 Cal.App.5th 485, 492.) California courts “apply a sliding scale, meaning if one of these elements is present to only a lesser degree, then more evidence of the other element is required to establish overall unconscionability. In other words, if there is little of one, there must be a lot of the other.” (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff also argues that the arbitration agreement is unconscionable. However, the Court has reviewed the portions of the contract Plaintiff points to as unconscionable and finds that they are not so unfair as to shock the conscience.

 

Therefore, the Court grants the Motion to Compel Arbitration.
