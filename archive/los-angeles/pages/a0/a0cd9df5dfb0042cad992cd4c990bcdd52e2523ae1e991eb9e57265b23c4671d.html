[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING MOTION TO VACATE JUDGMENT

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

July 9, 2026

 

On June 1, 2026, Larry J. Caldwell filed a motion to vacate the judgment against him on the cross-complaint “on the grounds that the Court lacked jurisdiction to render a Judgment based on the fact that there was no statutorily authorized Cross-Complaint before the Court.  The purported Cross-Complaint did not comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 428.10 because it did not contain at least one cause of action by at least one of the Defendants.”

BASIC PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On August 17, 2020, Paula Green filed this action for breach of lease based on habitability issues.  Ultimately, she amended her allegations and filed a Third Amended Complaint (“TAC”) on November 29, 2021.

On January 24, 2024, Douglas Emmett Management LLC (“Douglas Emmett”), Barrington Pacific LLC (“Barrington Pacific”), and ESIS Inc. (“ESIS”) filed a cross-complaint against Paula Green and Larry J. Caldwell based on breach of a tentative settlement agreement for the TAC and Green’s and Caldwell’s wrongful retention of the $275,000 settlement payment.  This was eventually amended to a Second Amended Cross-Complaint (“SACC”) on September 23, 2024, alleging (1) declaratory relief; (2) conversion; (3) unjust enrichment; (4) fraudulent transfer; (5) constructive fraudulent transfer; (6) breach of fiduciary duty; and (7) money had and received.

During trial, on June 30, 2025, the Court granted a motion for directed verdict against Caldwell on the SACC “in connection to the principal amount of $275,000.00 and any pre-judgment interest from the date the funds were transferred into Mr. Caldwell’s business account.”  The Court also granted an oral motion to add Indemnity Insurance Company of America (“Indemnity Insurance”) as a cross-complainant as the underlying insurance policy holder.

On July 3, 2025, a jury returned a verdict awarding an additional $175,000.00 in consequential damages to ESIS and Indemnity Insurance.  The parties stipulated to $300,000.00 in punitive damages.

The Court entered judgment on September 16, 2025: “Indemnity Insurance of North America is entitled to $275,000.00 + interest from September 2022 at the legal rate of 10%; $175,000.00 in consequential damages for attorney’s fees and costs from September 2022 to the present; and $300,000.00 for punitive damages as against Larry J. Caldwell, for a total monetary judgment of $750,000.00 + interest.”  On November 12, 2025, the Court entered an amended judgment to correct the name of Indemnity Insurance.

Caldwell appealed, and on February 2, 2026, the Court dismissed his appeals because he was in default.

DISCUSSION

Caldwell brings his motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d), arguing that the judgment against him is void.

A.        The Motion Is Untimely Because the Judgment is Not Void on Its Face.

“A trial court has no statutory power under section 473, subdivision (d) to set aside a judgment that is not void: Once six months have elapsed since the entry of a judgment, ‘a trial court may grant a motion to set aside that judgment as void only if the judgment is void on its face.’”  (Cruz v. Fagor America, Inc. (2007) 146 Cal.App.4th 488, 495-496.)

A judgment is void on its face when the invalidity is apparent upon an inspection of the judgment-roll or the court record without consideration of extrinsic evidence.  (People v. The North River Ins. Co. (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 226, 233; [quoting Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1441; Pittman v. Beck Park Apartments Ltd. (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1009, 1021].)  “A judgment is ‘void’ only when the court entering that judgment ‘lack[ed] jurisdiction in a fundamental sense’ due to the ‘“‘entire absence of power to hear or determine the case’”’ resulting from the ‘“‘absence of authority over the subject matter or the parties.’”’  [Citations.]”  (Ibid.)

Caldwell argues that this Court lacked jurisdiction because “there was no statutorily authorized Cross-Complaint before the Court.”  (Motion at p. 2.)  The Court rejects this argument, as explained below.

Because this Court did have jurisdiction to grant relief and enter judgment, the judgment is not void on its face.  Accordingly, Caldwell’s motion—filed more than six months after entry of the judgment and amended judgment—is untimely, and the Court has no power to grant the requested relief under section 473.

B.        The Judgment On the SACC Is Not Void.

Caldwell contends that only Barrington Pacific LLC and Douglas Emmett Management LLC, as Defendants on the TAC, are the only parties who had a right to bring a cross-complaint.  (Motion at p. 11.)  ESIS and Indemnity Insurance “did not have the right to bring a Cross-Complaint because they were not a ‘a party against whom a cause of action has been asserted in a complaint or cross-complaint.’”  (Id. at p. 11.)

Caldwell further summarizes his arguments as follows: “The fatal defect in the Cross-Complaint was that the only real party in interest with an actual cause of action against Paula Green or Larry Caldwell was not a Defendant.  This fatal defect could not be fixed by substituting Indemnity Insurance in.  [¶]  That means there never was a Cross-Complaint legally before the Court at any time during the case, and in particular, when the Court rendered Judgment.  [¶]  The absence of an authorized Cross-Complaint meant that the relief sought against Larry Caldwell was not contained in a pleading authorized by the Code of Civil Procedure.  [¶]  Since the relief was being sought outside of an authorized pleading, the Court did not have jurisdiction to grant any relief in the Cross-Complaint, and the Judgment granting that relief is void.”  (Motion at p. 18.)

The Court has already addressed a variation of this argument.  In his demurrer to the First Amended Cross-Complaint, Caldwell argued that ESIS lacked statutory authorization to bring the FACC because they were previously a non-party.  The Court overruled the demurrer on this ground by citing Code of Civil Procedure sections 428.10 and 428.20, and stating: “Douglas Emmett and Barrington Plaza (the original Defendants) filed the FACC against Green (the original Plaintiff) and Caldwell (previously a non-party), which arises from the same controversy.  [Citation.]  They joined ESIS as a Cross-Complainant under Code of Civil Procedure §428.20 because joinder is permitted under Section 378.  [Citation.]”  (08/22/2024 Minute Order; see also Opposition at pp. 8-9.)

According to Caldwell, “following the Court’s Order partially sustaining Green and Caldwell’s First Amended Cross-Complaint, the only cause of action that Defendants [Barrington Pacific and Douglas Emmett] alleged in the Second Amended Cross-Complaint was their purported cause of action for declaratory relief.”  (Motion at p. 12; see Reply at pp. 2-3.)  In fact, when the Court sustained in part Caldwell’s demurrer to the First Amended Cross-Complaint, it granted Douglas Emmett and Barrington Plaza leave to amend the second through seventh causes of action.  Thereafter, Caldwell demurred to only the sixth cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty in the SACC, and the Court overruled that demurrer on May 5, 2025.  Consequently, all of the SACC’s claims—not just declaratory relief—by all Cross-Complainants survived pleading challenges and proceeded to trial.  And at trial, the Court granted a directed verdict in favor of ESIS and Indemnity Insurance.

Caldwell’s remedy for this Court’s perceived mistakes was to appeal and seek a reversal of the demurrer orders, directed verdict, or other orders.  That appeal was dismissed.  In sum, the Court agrees that Caldwell’s motion is “a woefully untimely motion for reconsideration” and another attempt to get relief after his failed appeal.  (Opposition at pp. 7-8.)

CONCLUSION

The motion is DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING COMBINED DEMURRER AND MOTION TO STRIKE; GRANTING MOTION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

July 9, 2026

 

On December 30, 2021, Plaintiff Autumn Yancey-Estime filed this action.

On February 25, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Amendment to Complaint identifying Brianne Watson as Doe 3.

On May 5, 2026, Watson filed a combined demurrer and motion to strike.  “Motions to strike and demurrers should be filed as separate documents.”  (Weil & Brown, Cal. Prac. Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group June 2026 Update) ¶ 7:162.1.)

On June 1, 2026, Watson filed a motion for protective order, or in the alternative, a motion to quash Plaintiff’s deposition notice.

DEMURRER AND MOTION TO STRIKE

Only the first (discrimination), second (harassment), third (retaliation), and sixth (intentional infliction of emotional distress) causes of action are alleged against “Defendants” and not specific individual defendants.  According to Watson’s counsel, “Plaintiff’s counsel has clarified in written communications that Plaintiff only seeks to name Ms. Watson under the Sixth Cause of Action for IIED and intends to dismiss her from the other Causes of Action.”  (Motion at p. 5, fn. 1.)

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true.  (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.)

The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subds. (a)-(b).)

A.        The Sixth Cause of Action Fails to State a Claim.

“‘[T]o state a cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress [“IIED”] a plaintiff must show: (1) outrageous conduct by the defendant; (2) the defendant’s intention of causing or reckless disregard of the probability of causing emotional distress; (3) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (4) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct.’  [Citation.]  ‘Conduct, to be ‘outrageous’ must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized society.’  [Citation.]”  (Huntingdon Life Sciences, Inc. v. Stop Huntingdon Animal Cruelty USA, Inc. (2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 1228, 1259.)  “While the outrageousness of a defendant’s conduct normally presents an issue of fact to be determined by the trier of fact [citation], the court may determine in the first instance, whether the defendant’s conduct may reasonably be regarded as so extreme and outrageous as to permit recovery.”  (Trerice v. Blue Cross of California (1989) 209 Cal.App.3d 878, 883.)  When a plaintiff does not suffer physical injury, the conduct must involve “extreme and outrageous intentional invasions of one’s mental and emotional tranquility.”  (Alcorn v. Anbro Engineering, Inc. (1970) 2 Cal.3d 493, 498.)

 Plaintiff alleges that “Defendants repeatedly discriminated and harassed Autumn based on her race and gender over the course of two and a half years” and retaliated against her.  (Complaint ¶ 82.)  Specifically, her employer’s President and Vice President “harassed [Plaintiff], discriminated against her, and intentionally inflicted emotional distress on her”; “sexually harassed [Plaintiff] by, among other things, touching and massaging Autumn’s shoulders without her permission, commenting on [Plaintiff]’s physical body appearance, calling [Plaintiff] ‘beautiful’, commenting on [Plaintiff]’s ‘backside’, and referring to other women in conversations with [Plaintiff] as ‘hot’”; and “did nothing to stop [the President] and provide a safe work environment for [Plaintiff] and others similarly situated at Vanguard.”  (Complaint ¶¶ 1-3.)  Plaintiff informed Watson when the President made an overtly racist statement, and Watson told Plaintiff that she would talk to the President about it.  (Complaint ¶ 37.)  Watson said “she supported whatever decision [Plaintiff] made, that she should not be made to feel uncomfortable at work, and that she was free to take a couple of days off to consider what she wanted to do next.”  (Complaint ¶ 37; see Complaint ¶ 38.)

Although Plaintiff notes her allegation that “each of the Defendants named herein acted as the employee, agent, servant, partner, alter-ego and/or joint venture of one or more of the other Defendants” (Complaint ¶ 19), she also highlights that her factual allegations involve only acts by the President and Vice President, not Watson.  (Opposition at pp. 5-6.)

The demurrer is sustained on this ground.

B.        The Entire Doe Amendment is Barred.

Watson argues that the Doe amendment should be stricken because the IIED claim is time-barred and does not relate back to the filing on the complaint.  (Demurrer at pp. 11-12.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 474 permits a plaintiff who is ignorant of the name of a defendant to name a Doe in a complaint, and “when his true name is discovered, the pleading or proceeding must be amended accordingly.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 474.)  To do so, the plaintiff must have actually been ignorant of the defendant’s identity when he or she filed the complaint.  (Optical Surplus, Inc. v. Superior Court (1991) 228 Cal. App. 3d 776, 783-782.)  “Ignorance of the true name of the defendant should not be feigned.”  (Scherer v. Mark (1976) 64 Cal. App. 3d 834, 840.)  “[S]ection 474 includes an implicit requirement that a plaintiff may not ‘unreasonably delay’ his or her filing of a Doe amendment after learning a defendant’s identity.”  (A.N. v. County of Los Angeles (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1058, 1066-1067.)

Plaintiff argues that “Defendant Watson’s argument that she was only expressly referenced in two paragraphs that are insufficient to state an IIED claim against her only reinforces the fact that Plaintiff could not have alleged the facts that led to the Doe Amendment in December 2021 because she was not aware of them.”  (Opposition at p. 7.)  According to Plaintiff, she learned of facts to support liability against Watson only after discovery in August and September 2025.  (Ibid.)

Regardless whether Plaintiff should have known facts about Watson’s involvement when she initially filed this action, the Court strikes the amendment for failure to timely serve Watson.

“A plaintiff must serve ‘a defendant within three years after the action is commenced against the defendant.’  ([Code Civ. Proc.,] § 583.210, subd. (a).)  ‘[A]n action is commenced at the time the complaint is filed.’  (Ibid.)  Dismissal is mandatory where a plaintiff fails to serve a defendant within the statutory time limits.  (§ 583.250, subd. (b).)  The three-year rule applies where the defendant seeking dismissal was served as a Doe defendant named in the original complaint, later amended to show his or her true name.  [Citations.]  In short, a plaintiff has three years from the date of filing the complaint to identify and serve a Doe defendant.”  (Inversiones Papaluchi S.A.S. v. Superior Court (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1055, 1061.)  “Service requirements ‘are mandatory and are not subject to extension, excuse, or exception except as expressly provide by statute.’  (§ 583.250, subd. (b).)  The Legislature has articulated four conditions that toll the time for service.  (§ 583.240, subd. (a).)  The conditions ‘must be construed strictly against the plaintiff.’  [Citation.]”  (Id. at pp. 1061-1062.)  The exceptions include “[t]he defendant was not amenable to the process of the court,” and “[s]ervice, for any other reason, was impossible, impracticable, or futile due to causes beyond the plaintiff’s control.  Failure to discover relevant facts or evidence is not a cause beyond the plaintiff’s control for the purpose of this subdivision.”  (Code of Civ. Proc., § 583.240, subd. (a), (d).)

Plaintiff filed this action on December 30, 2021.  She did not name Watson as a Doe defendant until February 25, 2026—over four years later.  Because Plaintiff did not serve Watson within three years of initiating this action, dismissal is mandatory.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 583.250.)

Accordingly, the Court strikes the inclusion of Watson as Doe 3.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).)

C.        Conclusion

The motion is GRANTED.

Brianne Watson is DISMISSED WITH PREJUDICE.

MOTION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER

In light of Watson’s dismissal as a party, the motion for protective order or to quash the Notice of Deposition is GRANTED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING MOTION FOR JUDGMENT NOTWITHSTANDING THE VERDICT; DENYING MOTION FOR NEW TRIAL

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

July 9, 2026

 

On March 24, 2026, a jury returned a verdict in favor of Plaintiffs Saerom Ha and Jason Ha and against Defendant BMW of North America LLC.  The Court entered judgment on April 23, 2026.

On May 22, 2026, Defendant filed a combined motion for judgment notwithstanding the verdict, or in the alternative, for a new trial.

DISCUSSION

The court must render judgment notwithstanding the verdict whenever a motion for a directed verdict for the aggrieved party should have been granted.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 629.)  “A motion for judgment notwithstanding the verdict may be granted only if it appears from the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the party securing the verdict, that there is no substantial evidence in support.”  (Sweatman v. Department of Veterans Affairs (2001) 25 Cal.4th 62, 68.)  “If the evidence is conflicting or if several reasonable inferences may be drawn, the motion for judgment notwithstanding the verdict should be denied.”  (Hauter v. Zogarts (1975) 14 Cal.3d 104, 110 (Hauter).)

A verdict may be vacated and a new trial ordered due to irregularity in the proceedings that prevented a party from having a fair trial, misconduct of the jury, accident or surprise, newly discovered evidence, excessive or inadequate damages, insufficiency of the evidence to justify the verdict, or error in law.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 657.)

Generally, Defendant contends that the vehicle’s noisy breaks did not constitute a breach of express warranty, and Plaintiffs “failed to submit competent, admissible evidence to support this theory.”  (Motion at pp. 9-10.)

Defendant argues that Plaintiffs failed to show that such a defect was covered under the express warranty.  (Motion at p. 13.)  In fact, the warranty provides coverage for “defects in material or workmanship” in “brake pads/linings” and brake disks.  (Ibid.; Opposition at p. 6.) 

Defendant also relies on its expert, who “testified extensively that, because these brakes need to have the capability to stop this very powerful and very fast vehicle, they are designed differently than the brakes on a standard vehicle and that noise coming from the brakes, especially when used at low speeds, is normal and not a defect.”  (Motion at p. 10.)  Defendant points to a Service Information Bulletin “that affirms the brakes used on the subject vehicle are ‘high performance brake systems, which are designed for sporty dynamic driving styles.  As a side effect of the highest possible break performance, customers are likely to hear noises due to the extremely resilient materials.’”  (Ibid.)  According to Defendant, “[t]he only evidence in support of Plaintiffs’ argument that the brake noise constituted a ‘defect’ or a nonconformity to the express warranty was Plaintiffs’ testimony that the brakes were loud,” which is not evidence that they were defective.  (Id. at p. 11.)  Yet, the noises described in the owner’s manual  are “only described as operational noises, not the screeching noise Plaintiffs experienced.”  (Opposition at p. 6.)  Plaintiff Mr. Ha “described the noise as nails against a chalkboard,” and he “provided objective evidence of the noise as he recorded a video of the noise which was played several times before the jury.”  (Ibid.)

Defendant further argues that a design defect cannot form the basis of an express warranty cause of action under Song-Beverly.  (Motion at pp. 14-17.)  According to Defendant, “substantial testimony demonstrated that the brakes on the subject vehicle worked as designed.”  (Id. at p. 16.)  Defendant again relies on its expert, multiple dealership employees, a Service Information Bulletin, and the owner’s manual.  (Id. at pp. 16-17.)  But Plaintiff Mr. Ha “confirmed that he only experienced the noise defect 3,000 to 4,000 miles after he leased the car which was well beyond the break in period of 300 miles.”  (Opposition at p. 8.)

Defendant argues that there was no substantial evidence of willfulness to support double civil penalties.  (Motion at pp. 17-18.)  Defendant acknowledges that this requires a finding that the vehicle had a warrantable defect and that Defendant intentionally declined to follow its legal obligations under Song-Beverly.  (Id. at p. 18.)  Plaintiffs requested a repurchase, and the only documentation of Defendant’s evaluation of the merits of Plaintiffs’ request was “Declined.”  (Opposition at p. 9.)  Additionally, Defendant’s Person Most Qualified confirmed that he made no notes during his evaluation, and Defendant has no written policies and procedures to help facilitate the review of the merits of a customer’s repurchase request.  (Id. at pp. 9-10.)

Finally, Defendant argues that the implied warranty verdict must also be set aside.  (Motion at pp. 19-20.)  Defendant observes that the jury recognized that the subject vehicle was fit for the ordinary purpose for which vehicles are used, but the jury “found for Plaintiffs on implied warranty because it determined the subject vehicle was not ‘of the same quality as those generally acceptable in the trade.’”  (Id. at p. 20.)  According to Defendant, “[h]ad the verdict form accurately reflected the language in Song-Beverly, it is apparent that Plaintiffs did not meet [their] burden.”  (Ibid.)  But as Plaintiffs highlight, “Defendant stipulated to this language on the verdict form, and it cannot claim foul now.”  (Opposition at p. 11.)

The jury was instructed with CACI 107, which includes, “If you believe it is true, the testimony of a single witness is enough to prove a fact.”  The jury was also instructed with CACI 219, which provides that in determining whether to believe an expert’s testimony, the jury should consider “[t]he facts the expert relied upon” and “[t]he reasons for the expert’s opinion.”  Following these instructions, the jury apparently believed Plaintiff Mr. Ha’s testimony and considered the bases and weaknesses of the experts’ opinions.

The Court determines that when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the opposing party, several reasonable inferences may be drawn from the evidence to support the verdict.  (See Hauter, supra, 14 Cal.3d at p. 110.)  The motion for judgment notwithstanding the verdict is DENIED.

Similarly, after weighing the evidence, the Court is not convinced that the jury clearly should have reached a different verdict or decision.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 657.)  The motion for new trial is DENIED.

CONCLUSION

The Motion is DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING DEMURRER TO FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT; GRANTING MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

July 9, 2026

 

On August 4, 2025, Plaintiffs Jeanette C. Johnson (by and through her Successor in Interest, Kari L. Maya), Kari L. Maya, Teri M. Montgomery, and Ladonna A. Lopez filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”).  The FAC alleges (1) elder abuse/neglect, (2) negligence, (3) violation of residents’ rights, and (4) wrongful death.

On October 3, 2025, Defendant Priority Care Professional Home Health Inc. filed a demurrer to the first and third causes of action and a motion to strike.  Plaintiffs later dismissed the third cause of action against Defendant.

DEMURRER

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true.  (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.)

To plead elder abuse, the plaintiff must allege “facts establishing that the defendant: (1) had responsibility for meeting the basic needs of the elder or dependent adult, such as nutrition, hydration, hygiene or medical care [citations]; (2) knew of conditions that made the elder or dependent adult unable to provide for his or her own basic needs [citations]; and (3) denied or withheld goods or services necessary to meet the elder or dependent adult’s basic needs, either with knowledge that injury was substantially certain to befall the elder or dependent adult (if the plaintiff alleges oppression, fraud or malice) or with conscious disregard of the high probability of such injury (if the plaintiff alleges recklessness) [citations].”  (Carter v. Prime Healthcare Paradise Valley LLC (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 396, 406-407 (Carter).)  “The plaintiff must also allege . . . that the neglect caused the elder or dependent adult to suffer physical harm, pain or mental suffering.”  (Id. at p. 407.)  There must be an allegation of authorization or ratification on the part of a managing agent in order to recover damages for dependent adult abuse against corporate defendants.  (Civ. Code, § 3294; Welf. & Inst. Code, § 15657, subd. (c).)

“‘[N]eglect’ within the meaning of Welfare and Institutions Code section 15610.57 covers an area of misconduct distinct from ‘professional negligence.’”  (Covenant Care, Inc. v. Superior Court (2004) 32 Cal.4th 771, 783.)  “As used in the [Elder Adult and Dependent Adult Civil Protection] Act, neglect refers not to the substandard performance of medical services but, rather, to the ‘failure of those responsible for attending to the basic needs and comforts of elderly or dependent adults, regardless of their professional standing, to carry out their custodial obligations.’”  (Ibid.)  To distinguish dependent adult abuse from professional negligence, there must be a showing of recklessness, fraud, malice, or oppression.  (Ibid.)

Defendant argues that the FAC “is bereft of specific facts.”  (Demurrer at p. 5.)  Plaintiffs allege that after almost a month of care in two other facilities, Defendant “was contracted to provide Home Health services” to the decedent, but it “failed to provide services” to the decedent during those four days.  (FAC ¶¶ 45, 47-50.)  On the fourth day, the sacrum “was staged by [Defendant] PRIORITYCARE as Stage II (photographs show a stage IV wound),” and “[s]everal other wounds were noted including unstageable left heel, unstageable right ankle, and stage II right gluteus wound.”  (FAC ¶ 46.)  Defendant “neglected to provide medical care for JOHNSON’s physical and mental health needs by failing to take all the necessary steps to properly care for her . . . failed to adequately inform JOHNSON’s physician of the nature and extent of her injuries and failed to carry out doctor’s orders adequately and completely for their treatment and failed to adequately and appropriately document JOHNSON’s plan of care.”  (FAC ¶ 78.)  Defendant also ratified the mistreatment when it “failed to terminate, discipline, reprimand, or otherwise repudiate the acts and omissions of any employee due to or based upon the care, treatment, monitoring or supervision, or lack thereof.”  (FAC ¶ 81.)  These allegations lack the required particularity.

Plaintiffs argue that they “need only set forth the essential facts of the case with reasonable precision and particularity sufficient to acquaint the defendant with the nature of the claim,” and l recklessness, oppression, fraud, or malice need to be proven only at trial, not alleged in the pleadings.  (Opposition at pp. 3-4 [formatting omitted].)  Not so.  Plaintiffs’ cited case does not involve elder abuse.  For elder abuse, “the facts constituting the neglect and establishing the causal link between the neglect and the injury ‘must be pleaded with particularity,’ in accordance with the pleading rules governing statutory claims.”  (Carter, supra, 198 Cal.App.4th at p. 407.) 

The demurrer is sustained.

MOTION TO STRIKE

The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subds. (a)-(b).)

Defendant moves to strike allegations related to punitive damages and attorney fees under Welfare & Institutions Code section 15657.  A plaintiff can recover punitive damages in tort cases where “the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.”  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).)  “The mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages.  [Citation.]  Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim.  [Citation.]”  (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166, fn. omitted.) 

For the reasons discussed with the demurrer, the motion to strike is granted.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING DEFENDANT’S DEMURRER

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

July 9, 2026

 

On June 27, 2025, Plaintiff Marychris Davis filed this action against Defendant General Motors LLC arising from Plaintiff’s purchase of an allegedly defective vehicle.

On October 2, 2025, Defendant filed a demurrer to the fourth and fifth causes of action.

DISCUSSION

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true.  (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.)

A.        Plaintiff’s Fourth Cause of Action is Time-Barred.

Defendant argues that the fourth cause of action for breach of implied warranty is time-barred.  (Demurrer at pp. 8-9.)

An implied warranty claim is governed by the four-year statute of limitations period of California’s Uniform Commercial Code section 2725.  (Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1297, 1305-1306 (Mexia).)  “A cause of action accrues when the breach occurs, regardless of the aggrieved party’s lack of knowledge of the breach.  A breach of warranty occurs when tender of delivery is made, except that where a warranty explicitly extends to future performance of the goods and discovery of the breach must await the time of such performance the cause of action accrues when the breach is or should have been discovered.”  (Comm. Code, § 2725, subd. (2).)  In contrast to an express warranty, “an implied warranty . . . is not a warranty that ‘explicitly extends to future performance of the goods’  [Citations.]”  (Cardinal Health 301, Inc. v. Tyco Electronics Corp. (2008) 169 Cal.App.4th 116, 134 (Cardinal Health 301).)  “The implied warranty of merchantability may be breached by a latent defect undiscoverable at the time of sale.”  (Mexia, supra, 174 Cal.App.4th at p. 1304.)  “In the case of a latent defect, a product is rendered unmerchantable, and the warranty of merchantability is breached, by the existence of the unseen defect, not by its subsequent discovery. . . . Thus, although a defect may not be discovered for months or years after a sale, merchantability is evaluated as if the defect were known”  (Id. at p. 1305.)

Defendant argues that any breach of the implied warranty occurred at the time of purchase on March 14, 2021, and the statute of limitations ran four years later, on March 14, 2025.  (Demurrer at p. 9.)  Plaintiff filed this action over three months later, on June 27, 2025.

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that a claim can be tolled by delayed discovery or fraudulent concealment.  (Opposition at pp. 4-7.)  But Plaintiff cites cases that are either nonbinding federal orders or do not involve the statute of limitations for implied warranties.  (Ibid.)  Plaintiff does not refute that the cause of action accrued when the breach occurred (when tender of delivery was made), and that an implied warranty does not explicitly extend to future performance of the goods.  (See Comm. Code, § 2725, subd. (2); Cardinal Health 301, supra, 169 Cal.App.4th at p. 134.)

The demurrer to the fourth cause of action is sustained.

B.        Plaintiff’s Fifth Cause of Action Is Not Facially Time-Barred.

Defendant argues that the fifth cause of action is barred by the three-year statute of limitations.  (Demurrer at pp. 9-10; see Code Civ. Proc., § 338, subd. (d).)

To sustain a demurrer on the statute of limitations, the running of the statute must appear clearly and affirmatively on the face of the complaint.  (Geneva Towers Ltd. Partnership v. City of San Francisco (2003) 29 Cal.4th 769, 781.)  “‘[I]t is not enough that the complaint might be time-barred.  [Citation.]’”  (Ibid.)

Plaintiff alleges that “the running of the limitation periods have been tolled by, inter alia, the following doctrines or rules: equitable tolling, the discovery rule, equitable estoppel, the repair rule, and/or class action tolling (e.g., the American Pipe rule).”  (Complaint ¶ 23.)

“The general rule is that the statute of limitations begins to run when the wrongful act is done, even though the plaintiff is ignorant of his or her cause of action or of the identity of the wrongdoer.”  (Snow v. A. H. Robins Co. (1985) 165 Cal.App.3d 120, 127.)  The running of the statute of limitations may be postponed on certain causes of action until the time of discovery of the facts.  (Ibid.)  “With respect to actions based on fraud, the statute of limitations is tolled whenever plaintiff is able to show the defendant fraudulently concealed facts which would have led him to discover his potential cause of action.”  (Id. at pp. 127-128.)

Plaintiff alleges that the parties entered into a warranty contract on March 14, 2021.  (Complaint ¶ 6.)  Before Plaintiff purchased the vehicle, Defendant knew about the Engine Defect.  (E.g., Complaint ¶ 51.)  “Plaintiff discovered Defendant’s wrongful conduct alleged herein shortly before the filing of the complaint” on June 27, 2025.  (Complaint ¶ 24.)

The Complaint does not facially show that the statute of limitations bars this cause of action.  It is not clear from the face of the Complaint when Plaintiff should have discovered that Defendant concealed information about the vehicle’s defects.

The demurrer is overruled on this ground.

C.        Plaintiff’s Fifth Cause of Action Fails to Plead Fraud With Specificity.

Defendant argues that the fifth cause of action for Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment does not allege facts with the requisite specificity.  (Demurrer at pp. 10-16.)

Fraud based on concealment requires that “(1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.”  (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 310-311 (Bigler-Engler).)  “Even under the strict rules of common law pleading, one of the canons was that less particularity is required when the facts lie more in the knowledge of the opposite party.”  (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn., Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1256, 1384.)

An essential element of intentional concealment includes the duty to disclose, which must be based upon a transaction, or a special relationship, between plaintiff and defendant.  (Id. at p. 314.)  “There are ‘four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.’”  (Id. at p. 311.)  “[O]ther than the first instance, in which there must be a fiduciary relationship between the parties, ‘the other three circumstances in which nondisclosure may be actionable presuppose[ ] the existence of some other relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise. . . . “[W]here material facts are known to one party and not to the other, failure to disclose them is not actionable fraud unless there is some relationship between the parties which gives rise to a duty to disclose such known facts.”  [Citation.]’  [Citation.]”  (Hoffman v. 162 North Wolfe LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 1178, 1187 (Hoffman).)  “Such a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.”  (Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at p. 312.)

Plaintiff alleges that Defendant knew about the Engine Defect before the sale of the vehicle.  (Complaint ¶ 51; see Complaint ¶¶ 47, 49-50 [listing defects].)  Plaintiff did not know about these defects and problems, and Defendant, who had superior knowledge of the defect, did not disclose the defects when Plaintiff purchased the vehicle.  (E.g., Complaint ¶¶ 51, 53.)  These allegations are specific enough to allege the information that was concealed and the danger posed.  (See Jones v. ConocoPhillips Co. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1187, 1199-1200.)

However, Plaintiff does not allege facts showing the existence of a relationship between the parties that gives rise to a duty to disclose facts.  (See Hoffman, supra, 228 Cal.App.4th at p. 1187.)  Plaintiff alleges that Defendant “knew, or should have known, about the Engine Defect through its exclusive knowledge of non-public, internal data about the Engine Defect.”  (Complaint ¶ 51.)  There are no facts showing that Defendant’s knowledge was exclusive or that Defendant intended to defraud.  Indeed, Plaintiff alleges that there were prior customer complaints, suggesting that the knowledge was in fact public.  (Complaint ¶¶ 51, 53.)

Moreover, in a transaction that does not involve fiduciary or confidential relations, the “transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.”  (Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at pp. 311-312.)  Plaintiff alleges no such direct transaction with and direct concealment by Defendant.

The demurrer is sustained on this ground.

D.        Plaintiff’s Fifth Cause of Action is Barred by the Economic Loss Rule.

Defendant argues that the fifth cause of action is barred by the economic loss rule.  (Demurrer at pp. 16-18.)

Under the economic loss rule, “[w]here a purchaser’s expectations in a sale are frustrated because the product he bought is not working properly, his remedy is said to be in contract alone, for he has suffered only ‘economic’ losses.”  (Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 988 (Robinson), quotation marks omitted.)  However, tort damages may be permitted when the breach of contract is accompanied by a tort such as fraud.  (Id. at pp. 989-990.)  To plead around the economic loss rule, a party must plead the existence of a duty that arises independent of any contractual duty and independent injury, other than economic loss, that arises from the breach of that duty.  (Id. at pp. 988-991.)

“[U]nder California law, the economic loss rule does not bar [a claim] for fraudulent inducement by concealment.  Fraudulent inducement claims fall within an exception to the economic loss rule recognized by our Supreme Court” in Robinson, and such claims allege fraudulent conduct that is independent of the alleged warranty breaches.  (Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 843.)  The Supreme Court has confirmed that “the economic loss rule does not apply to limit recovery for intentional tort claims like fraud.”  (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 38.)  “A plaintiff may assert a tort claim for fraudulent concealment based on conduct occurring in the course of a contractual relationship, if the elements of the cause of action can be established independently of the parties’ contractual rights and obligations and the tortious conduct exposes the plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond the reasonable contemplation of the parties when they entered into the agreement.”  (Ibid.)

Accordingly, a properly pleaded claim for fraudulent inducement is not barred by the economic loss rule.  Here, however, Plaintiff does not establish all elements of fraudulent concealment independently of the parties’ contractual rights and obligations.

Additionally, Plaintiff fails to allege any independent duty or harm beyond the terms of the contractual relationship, and only economic damages from breach of warranty are alleged.  Plaintiff “was harmed and suffered actual damages in that the Subject Vehicle's Engine is substantially certain to fail before its expected useful life has run.”  (Complaint ¶ 55.)  This harm is purely economic from the purchase of the vehicle.  Plaintiff’s allegation that the defects “substantially increase the likelihood that the engine will fail, lose power, and/or cut off during operation, thereby resulting in accidents involving property damage, personal injury and even death” (Complaint ¶ 49) does not state that Plaintiff in fact was damaged.  Unknown risk that does not result in liability or injury by definition is not actual damage.  (Small v. Fritz Companies, Inc. (2003) 30 Cal.4th 167, 202 [“Misrepresentation, even maliciously committed, does not support a cause of action unless the plaintiff suffered consequential damages,” concurring opinion]; Boschma v. Home Loan Center, Inc. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 230, 248 [“as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage”].) 

Plaintiff’s only alleged harm is the economic harm from the purchase of the vehicle, which was within the reasonable contemplation of the parties when they entered into the agreement.  (See Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 38.)  Accordingly, this cause of action is barred by the economic loss rule.

The demurrer to the fifth cause of action is sustained.

CONCLUSION

The demurrer is SUSTAINED.

At the hearing, Plaintiff should be prepared to explain what additional facts will be pleaded and exactly how any amendments can remedy the deficiencies of the Complaint without making it a sham pleading.  The Court will decide at the hearing whether to grant leave to file a First Amended Complaint.
