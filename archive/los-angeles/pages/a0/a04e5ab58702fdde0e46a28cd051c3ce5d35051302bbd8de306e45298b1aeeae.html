BACKGROUND

 

Plaintiff Kevin Adonay Quijada Molina sues Defendant CCN Framing, Inc. for alleged wage and hour violations. Plaintiff seeks to represent a class of Defendant’s current and former non-exempt employees.

              On March 29, 2022, Plaintiff commenced the Action by filing a Complaint alleging causes of action against Defendant for failure to pay minimum and straight time wages, failure to pay overtime wages, failure to provide meal periods, failure to authorize and permit rest periods, failure to timely pay final wages at termination, failure to provide accurate itemized wage statements, failure to indemnify necessary business expenditures, and unfair business practices.

On October 28, 2022, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint in the Action containing an additional cause of action against Defendant for Violation of PAGA.

On August 14, 2024, the Parties participated in an all-day mediation presided over by Mark LeHocky, Esq., which ultimately resulted in settlement. The terms of settlement were finalized in the long-form Class Action and PAGA Settlement Agreement (“Settlement Agreement”), a copy of which was filed with the Court on June 18, 2025.

On February 2, 2026, after the parties filed further briefing and a revised Settlement Agreement to address concerns raised by the Court, preliminary approval of the settlement was granted. All references below are to the agreement attached to the Declaration of Lucy Nguyen filed January 30, 2026.

Notice was given to the Class Members as ordered. (See Declaration of Beth Paris (“Paris Decl.”).) Now before the Court is the Motion for Final Approval of the Settlement.

ANALYSIS OF SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT

 

A.     Does a presumption of fairness exist? 

The Court preliminarily found in its Order of February 2, 2026 that the presumption of fairness should be applied.  No facts have come to the Court’s attention that would alter that preliminary conclusion.  Accordingly, the settlement is entitled to a presumption of fairness as set forth in the preliminary approval order.

B.      Is the settlement fair, adequate, and reasonable?

The settlement was preliminarily found to be fair, adequate and reasonable.  Notice has now been given to the Class. 

  Reaction of the class members to the proposed settlement.

Number of Class Members: 1,805 (Paris Decl., ¶¶5, 10.)   

Number of notice packets mailed: 1,805 (Id. at ¶¶7, 10.)         

Number of undeliverable notices: 198 (Id. at ¶9.)

Number of opt-outs: 0 (Id. at ¶12.)

Number of objections: 0 (Id. at ¶13.)

Average individual payment: $189.45 (Id. at ¶16.)

The Court finds that the notice was given as directed and conforms to due process requirements.  Given the reactions of the Class Members to the proposed settlement and for the reasons set for in the Preliminary Approval order, the settlement is found to be fair, adequate, and reasonable.

 

C.      Attorney Fees and Costs

              Class Counsel requests an award of $216,666.67 in fees and $25,378.28 in costs. (Memo ISO Final at 7:26-28, 17:16-18.) The Settlement Agreement provides for up to $216,666.67 (33 1/3%) for attorney fees and $28,000 in costs (¶3.2.2).

“Courts recognize two methods for calculating attorney fees in civil class actions: the lodestar/multiplier method and the percentage of recovery method.”  (Wershba v. Apple Computer, Inc. (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 224, 254.)  Here, class counsel request attorney fees using the percentage method, as crosschecked by lodestar. (Memo ISO Final at pp. 12-17.)

In common fund cases, the Court may employ a percentage of the benefit method, as cross-checked against the lodestar. (Laffitte v. Robert Half Int’l, Inc. (2016) 1 Cal.5th 480, 503.) The fee request represents one-third of the gross settlement amount, which is the average generally awarded in class actions.  (See In re Consumer Privacy Cases (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 545, 558, fn. 13 [“Empirical studies show that, regardless whether the percentage method or the lodestar method is used, fee awards in class actions average around one-third of the recovery.”].)

Here, the $216,666.67 fee request represents a reasonable percentage of the total funds paid by Defendant. Notice of the fee request was provided to class members in the notice packet, and no one objected. (Paris Decl., ¶12, Exhibit A.)

 

              As for costs, Class Counsel is requesting a cost amount of $25,378.28. This is less than the $28,000 cap provided for at preliminary approval, which was disclosed to Class Members in the Notice and not objected to. (Paris Decl., ¶12, Exhibit A.) Based on the above, the recommendation is to award $216,666.67 in fees and $25,378.28 in costs.

D.     Incentive Award

The class representative, Kevin Adonay Quijada Molina, seeks an enhancement payment of $10,000 for his contributions to the action. (Memo ISO Final at 18:20-22.)   

In connection with the final fairness hearing, named Plaintiffs must submit declarations attesting to why they should be entitled to an enhancement award in the proposed amount.  The named Plaintiffs must explain why they “should be compensated for the expense or risk he has incurred in conferring a benefit on other members of the class.”  (Clark v. American Residential Services LLC (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 785, 806.)  Trial courts should not sanction enhancement awards of thousands of dollars with “nothing more than pro forma claims as to ‘countless’ hours expended, ‘potential stigma’ and ‘potential risk.’ Significantly more specificity, in the form of quantification of time and effort expended on the litigation, and in the form of reasoned explanation of financial or other risks incurred by the named plaintiffs, is required in order for the trial court to conclude that an enhancement was ‘necessary to induce [the named plaintiff] to participate in the suit . . . .’”  (Id. at 806-807, italics and ellipsis in original.)

Plaintiff represents that his contributions to this litigation include: discussing the case and mediation with his counsel by phone, searching for and reviewing documents, and reviewing the settlement. He estimates spending 70 hours on the case. (Declaration of Kevin Adonay Quijada Molina ISO Prelim, ¶¶6-8.)

Based on the above, as well as the benefits obtained on behalf of the class, the court grants enhancement payment in the amount of $7,500 to Plaintiff.

 

E.      Settlement Administration Costs

              The settlement administrator, Apex Class Action, is requesting $16,000 for the costs of settlement administration. (Paris Decl., ¶20.) This is less than the estimated cost of $20,000 provided for in the settlement agreement (¶3.2.3) and disclosed to class members in the Notice, to which there were no objections. (Paris Decl., ¶12, Exhibit A.) Based on the above, the recommendation is to award costs in the requested amount of $16,000.

TENTATIVE RULING

 

Duke Choi’s petition for approval of minor’s compromise is granted.

 

During oral arguments, the Court will ask the attorney to address the annuity payment schedule, particularly, future Court approval of the scheduled payments.

 

BACKGROUND

 

This case concerns alleged exposures to toxins at a residential complex.

 

Here, Duke Choi moves, on behalf of his minor child, Ellis Jia Choi, for approval of Ellis’s settlement with Defendants Fifteen Group Capital LLC, Art Colony Property, LLC, The Center for Toxicology and Environmental Health, LLC, FPI Management, Inc., Rogelio Navar, and Marks Sanders.

 

APPLICABLE LAW

 

“An enforceable settlement of a minor's claim or that of a person lacking the capacity to make decisions or a person for whom a conservator has been appointed can only be consummated with court approval.”  (Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civ. Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶ 12:572.)

 

“[A] petition for approval must be presented to the court.  Until it is granted (signed), there is no final settlement, and any settlement agreement therefore is voidable.”  (Ibid., emphasis in original.)

 

“The petition must be executed by counsel and must be verified by the petitioner under penalty of perjury.”  (Id. at ¶ 12:574.5.)  It “must contain a full disclosure of all information that has ‘any bearing on the reasonableness’ of the compromise or the covenant not to sue.”  (Ibid.)  And it “must be prepared on mandatory Judicial Council form MC-350[.]”  (Ibid.)

 

If the petitioner has been represented or assisted by an attorney in preparing the petition to compromise the claim or in any other respect with regard to the claim, the petition must disclose the following information:

 

• The attorney's name, state bar number, law firm, if any, and business address;

 

• Whether the attorney became involved with the petition, directly or indirectly, at the instance of any party against whom the claim is asserted or of any party's insurance carrier;

 

• Whether the attorney represents or is employed by any other party or any insurance carrier in the matter;

 

• Whether the attorney has received any attorney fees or other compensation for services provided in connection with the claim giving rise to the petition or with preparation of the petition, and, if so, the amounts and the identity of the person who paid the fees or other compensation;

 

• If the attorney has not received any attorney fees or other compensation for services provided in connection with the claim giving rise to the petition or with preparation of the petition, whether the attorney expects to receive any fees or other compensation for these services, and, if so, the amounts and identity of the person who is expected to pay the fees or other compensation; and

 

• The terms of any agreement between the petitioner and the attorney.

 

(Id. at ¶ 12:574.10, citation omitted.)

 

Unless the court has approved the attorney's fee agreement in advance, the court must determine a reasonable fee based on all the circumstances of the case and may consider the following nonexclusive factors:

 

• The circumstances of the minor or disabled person;

 

• The amount of the fee in proportion to the value of the services;

 

• The novelty and difficulty of the issues involved and the skill required by the attorney;

 

• The amount involved and the results obtained;

 

• The time limits or other constraints imposed under the circumstances;

 

• The nature and length of the professional relationship between the attorney and the representative of the minor or disabled person;

 

• The experience, reputation and ability of the attorneys;

 

• The time and work required;

 

• The informed consent of the representative of the minor or disabled person to the fee;

 

• The relative sophistication of the attorney and the client;

 

• The likelihood that the attorney's acceptance of the case would preclude other employment;

 

• Whether the fee is fixed, hourly or contingent;

 

• If a contingent fee, the risk borne by the attorney, the costs advanced by the attorney, and the delay in payment of fees and reimbursement of costs;

 

• Any statutory requirements for representation agreements that may be applicable.

 

(Id. at ¶ 12:576, citation omitted.)

 

DISCUSSION

 

Settlement Terms

 

* Gross settlement = $97,000.00.

 

* Attorney fees to be paid from the gross settlement = $24,250.00 (25% of the gross settlement).

 

* Attorney costs to be paid from the gross settlement = $0.00.

 

* Medical expenses to be paid from the gross settlement = $0.00.

 

* Net settlement = $72,750.00.

 

* The net settlement will be invested in a single-premium deferred annuity operated by Pacific Life & Annuity Services, Inc.  Five payments will be made to Ellis pursuant to an agreed-to schedule.

 

Analysis

 

With one potential caveat, the Court finds that the petition should be granted because:

 

* This is a complex toxic-exposure case.

 

* The case involved novel and difficult issues.

 

* There are 25 Plaintiffs total.  (See Petition, Attachment 11(b)(3).)

 

* The global settlement amount is $2,425,000.00.  (See id. at Attachment 11(b)(6).)

 

* Ellis is receiving an equal 1/25th share of the global settlement ($2,425,000.00 ÷ 25 = $97,000.00).  (See ibid.)

 

* Currently, Ellis does not have any medical expenses.  (See id. at § 12.)

 

* “There are no statutory or contractual liens for payment of [Ellis’s] medical expenses.”  (Id. at § 12(5)(a)(i).)

 

* Anticipated future medical expenses are merely based on a fear of future harms.  (See id. at § 8.c.)

 

* Plaintiffs’ counsel “did not become concerned with this matter, directly or indirectly, at the instance of a party against whom the claim is asserted or a party’s insurance carrier.”  (Id. at § 17.d.)

 

* The retainer agreement calls for Plaintiffs’ counsel to receive attorney fees totaling 25% of the gross settlement.  (See id. at Attachments 13a, 17a.)

 

* The 25% rate is a highly reasonable rate for complex cases in Los Angeles County and for the services provided by Plaintiffs’ counsel.

 

* Although Plaintiffs’ counsel is representing the other Plaintiffs and will be receiving attorney fees from their settlements, the Court’s understanding is that those fees will be based on the same reasonable 25% rate that Ellis is being charged.

 

* The parties agreed to the annuity payment schedule.  (See id. at Attachment 18b(3).)

 

The potential caveat concerns Court approval of the annuity payments.  It is unclear whether the parties intend to come to the Court for review prior to each scheduled payment. Attachment 18b(3) provides for five installments and does not mention court approval.  At the hearing, the Court will ask the attorneys to clarify the parties’ expectation on this matter.

 

The petition is otherwise granted.

TENTATIVE RULING

 

The hearing on Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is granted.

 

BACKGROUND

 

Plaintiff used to work for Honeybee.  She alleges that Honeybee subjected her and other current and former employees to numerous wage-and-hour violations.

 

Here, Honeybee moves to compel Plaintiff’s individual claims to arbitration.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Existence and Consent

 

“[W]hen a petition to compel arbitration is filed and accompanied by prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy, the court itself must determine whether the agreement exists and . . . whether it is enforceable.”  (Rosenthal v. Great Western Financial Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413.)  “[T]he threshold question . . . is whether there is an agreement to arbitrate.’”  (Cruise v. Kroger Co. (2015) 233 Cal.App.4th 390, 396, emphasis in original.)

 

Fundamentally, the burden process is a three-step process.  “First, the moving party bears the burden of producing ‘prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy.’”  (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.)  “The moving party ‘can meet its initial burden by attaching to the [motion or] petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party's] signature.’”  (Ibid.)  In the alternative, “the moving party can meet its burden by setting forth the agreement's provisions in the motion.”  (Ibid.) 

 

“If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then in the second step, the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement.”  (Ibid.)  “The opposing party can do this in several ways.”  (Ibid.)  “For example, the opposing party may testify under oath or declare under penalty of perjury that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement.”  (Ibid.)

 

“If the opposing party meets its burden of producing evidence, then in the third step, the moving party must establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties.”  (Ibid.)  “The burden of proving the agreement by a preponderance of the evidence remains with the moving party.”  (Id. at 165-166.)

 

Defendant passes the first step.  A copy of the Honeybee arbitration agreement is physically included with the moving papers.  (See Paderogo Decl., Ex. A; see also Gamboa, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th at 165.)

 

For the second and third steps, Plaintiff argues:

 

. . . The Agreement to Arbitrate Disputes identifies only a single entity—“Honeybee Foods Corporation dba Jollibee”—as “the Company,” and it was not signed by Honeybee or by anyone on its behalf. The only indication of assent anywhere on the document is an electronic notation stating that it was e-signed by Plaintiff, together with a date; there is no signature of any Defendant. A contract that is signed by only one side, and that reflects no assent by the party seeking to enforce it, does not establish the mutual assent California law requires. [Banner Entertainment, Inc. v. Superior Court (1998) 62 Cal. App. 4th 348, 357–58 (“Banner Entertainment”).]

 

The defect is compounded by the parties named in this action. Plaintiff has sued two corporate entities—Jollibee Foods Corporation and Honeybee Foods Corporation dba Jollibee Group North America—and an individual, Florence Cohen. Yet none of Jollibee Foods Corporation, Jollibee Group North America, or Florence Cohen is named anywhere in the Agreement, and none signed it. The Agreement contains no terms expressly extending its reach to the Company’s parents, affiliates, or subsidiaries, and no language including the Company’s owners, officers, directors, or agents within the definition of “the Company.” A non-party to the Agreement cannot invoke it to compel arbitration, and no Defendant may bootstrap itself into an agreement it never entered. [Pagarigan v. Libby Care Center, Inc. (2002) 99 Cal. App. 4th 298, 301.]

 

(Opposition, p. 4.)

 

The first argument is unavailing.  An arbitration agreement does not have to be signed by the defendant to be a viable contract.  Instead, the rule is this: “When it is clear, both from a provision that the proposed written contract would become operative only when signed by the parties as well as from any other evidence presented by the parties that both parties contemplated that acceptance of the contract’s terms would be signified by signing it, the failure to sign the agreement means no binding contract was created.”  (Banner Entertainment, supra, 62 Cal.App.4th at 358, emphasis in original.)  Plaintiff fails to cite such a “provision” or “other evidence[.]”  (Ibid.; see also Opposition, pp. 3-5; cf. Paderogo Decl., Ex. A, p. 3 [supplying no signature line for either side].)

 

This brings the Court to Plaintiff’s second argument.  Defendant insists that Plaintiff sued Defendant under the wrong name – “Honeybee Foods Corporation dba Jollibee Group North America[.]”  (Notice of Motion, p. 1.)  Defendant represents that “Honeybee Foods Corporation dba Jollibee” – i.e., the “Company” in the Honeybee agreement – is the correct entity.  (Ibid.)  Honeybee Foods Corporation is named in the arbitration agreement and is the Defendant here. The dba is irrelevant to who the proper party is.

 

Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”)

 

Defendant contends the FAA governs.  (See Motion, pp. 14-15.)

 

This issue is unopposed.  (See Opposition, pp. 3-11 [failing to address the FAA].)

 

As a matter of guidance, the Court agrees with Defendant.  The FAA applies if the plain language says it applies or if “the underlying contract facilitates interstate commercial transactions or directly or indirectly affects” interstate commerce.  (Knight, Cal. Practice Guide: Alternative Dispute Resolution (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶ 5:50.2, emphasis in original.)  The first paragraph of the Honeybee agreement states that “Employee and the Company have agreed to submit to binding arbitration pursuant to the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA) any dispute, controversy or claim arising out of or relating to Employees relationship with the Company and/or the termination thereof[.]”  (Paderogo Decl., Ex. A, p. 1, bolding in original, italicizing added.)  The wording renders the FAA applicable.

 

Unconscionability and Enforcement

 

Unconscionability is a contract defense that can be utilized to “invalidate [an] arbitration agreement[].”  (Torrecillas v. Fitness International, LLC (2020) 52 Cal.App.5th 485, 492.)  Courts apply state law to test whether the agreement is unconscionable.  (See, e.g., Lagatree v. Luce, Forward, Hamilton & Scripps (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 1105, 1119.)

 

“[U]nconscionability has both a procedural and a substantive element, the former focusing on oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power, the latter on overly harsh or one-sided results.”  (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114, internal quotation marks omitted.)  “The prevailing view is that [procedural and substantive unconscionability] must both be present in order for a court to exercise its discretion to refuse to enforce a contract or clause under the doctrine of unconscionability.”  (Ibid.)  “But they need not be present in the same degree.”  (Ibid.)  “Essentially a sliding scale is invoked which disregards the regularity of the procedural process of the contract formation, that creates the terms, in proportion to the greater harshness or unreasonableness of the substantive terms themselves.”  (Ibid.)  “In other words, the more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.”  (Ibid.)

 

The burden belongs to the party opposing arbitration.  (See Ajamian v. CantorCO2e, L.P. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 771, 795.)

 

Procedural element: Plaintiff contends the Honeybee agreement constitutes a contract of adhesion.  (See Opposition, p. 6.)

 

The Court disagrees for three reasons.  One, “a predispute arbitration agreement is not invalid merely because it is imposed as a condition of employment.”  (Lagatree, supra, 74 Cal.App.4th at 1122.)  Two, the plain language undercuts Plaintiff’s argument:

 

Employee certifies that (s)he is aware that by entering into this Agreement, (s)he is waiving his/her right to have his/her Arbitrable Claims against the Company, if any, tried before a jury and that (s)he is waiving the right to appeal the arbitrators ruling on the merits other than to the extent necessary for judicial confirmation of the award under 9 U.S.C. § 9. Employee further certifies that (s)he has had the opportunity to consult with legal counsel prior to executing this Agreement.

 

(Paderogo Decl., Ex. A, p. 3, emphasis added.)  Three, there is no statement under penalty of perjury stating that Plaintiff requested extra time to read the onboarding documents or assistance from an attorney or other consultant to explain the meaning of the arbitration terms or that Defendant denied the requests, assuming they were made.

 

Substantive element: Plaintiff maintains that (1) “the agreement provides no mechanism to compel third-party discovery” (Opposition, p. 7 [bolding, capitalizing, and underlining deleted), (2) the JAMS rules “permit only one deposition per side, contrary to Code of Civil Procedure section 1283.05” (ibid., bolding and underlining deleted; see also id. at p. 8), (3) “the agreement unlawfully compels arbitration of sexual harassment claims in violation of state and federal law and public policy” (id. at p. 8, bolding and capitalizing deleted; see also id. at p. 9), and (4) “the agreement lacks any semblance of mutuality[.]”  (Id. at p. 9, bolding and capitalizing deleted; see also id. at p. 10.)  

 

Point (1) is overstated.  Plaintiff cites Aixtron, Inc. v. Veeco Instruments Inc. (2020) 52 Cal. App.5th 360 yet fails to mention that there is a split in the case law.  Under the FAA, “[a]rbitrators are authorized to subpoena nonparties to appear before them and to produce documents or other evidence.”  (Knight, supra, at ¶ 5:387.11, emphasis in original.)  Some decisions “restrict the subpoena power to hearings in the physical presence of the arbitrator[.]”  (Id. at ¶ 5:387.11a [citing cases], emphasis in original.)  Others “state that an arbitration panel might be able to subpoena a nonparty for prehearing discovery ‘under unusual circumstances’ and ‘upon a showing of special need or hardship.’”  (Id. at ¶ 5:387.11b [citing case].)  “A few cases hold that ‘implicit’ in the arbitrator's power to subpoena relevant documents for production at a hearing ‘is the power to order the production of relevant documents for review by a party prior to the hearing.’”  (Id. at ¶ 5:387.11c [citing case].)  And at least “[o]ne case holds that arbitrators may compel nonparties to testify and produce evidence before the panel prior to any scheduled hearing on the merits.”  (Id. at ¶ 5:387.11d [citing case].)  The Rutter Guide advises that “[o]ne lesson to draw from these cases is that document discovery from third parties may be obtained prehearing if the arbitrators issue a subpoena to the third party to appear and produce documents before at least one of the arbitrators at a ‘hearing’ with the intention that this ‘hearing’ will be adjourned once the documents are secured to the merits hearing.”  (Id. At ¶ 5:387.11e.)  “This is often referred to as a ‘work-around’ the limitations of” FAA section 7.  (Ibid.)  “No court has disapproved this practice, so cases that state prehearing deposition discovery is not permitted by the FAA should not be read to prevent the ‘work-around.’”  (Ibid.)  In short, third-party discovery can be compelled.

 

Point (2) is incorrect.  The JAMS rules allow “[e]ach Party [to] take at least one deposition of an opposing Party or an individual under the control of the opposing Party” and give the arbitrator power “to grant a request for additional depositions, based upon the reasonable need for the requested information, the availability of other discovery and the burdensomeness of the request on the opposing Parties and the witness.”  (JAMS Employment Arbitration Rules & Procedures, rule 17(b), emphasis added.)

 

Point (3) fails.  Plaintiff claims the agreement is unconscionable since it fails to carve out sexual-harassment claims.  (See Opposition, pp. 8-9.)  Notably, though, the complaint does not allege sexual harassment.  Moreover, the agreement broadly exempts “claims that may not be arbitrated as a matter of law[.]”  (Paderogo Decl., Ex. A, p. 1, emphasis added.)  A non-arbitrable sexual-harassment claim does not become arbitrable simply by not being listed in the agreement’s non-exhaustive list of exempted claims.  (See ibid.)

 

Point (4) also fails.  Plaintiff asserts that the class waiver “strips Plaintiff of valuable procedural mechanisms she would otherwise possess under California law while imposing no comparable burden on Defendant.”  (Opposition, p. 10.)  However, because the FAA applies, the class waiver is enforceable.  (See, e.g., Knight, supra, at ¶ 5:49.4c [discussing AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion (2011) 563 U.S. 333]; see also Iskanian v. CLS Transportation Los Angeles, LLC (2014) 59 Cal.4th 348, 359-360 [finding that the FAA preempts “a state’s refusal to enforce [] a [class] waiver on grounds of public policy or unconscionability”].)    

 

Consequently, as a matter of guidance, the level of procedural unconscionability is low, the level of substantive unconscionability is low, the agreement can be enforced, and the class claims can be stricken.
