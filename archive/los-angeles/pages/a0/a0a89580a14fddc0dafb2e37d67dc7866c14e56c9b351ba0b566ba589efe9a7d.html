CLIFFTON DACUNO LLEGO,

Plaintiff,

vs.

LOS ANGELES COUNTY METROPOLITAN TRANSPORTATION AUTHORITY,

Case No.:

Hearing Date:

September 18, 2026

ORDER DENYING MOTION FOR LEAVE TO AMEND COMPLAINT

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Cliffton Dacuno Llego (“Llego”) allegedly slipped and fell while riding Defendant Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority (“Metro”), resulting in injury. Llego sued Defendants for general negligence and premises liability.

On February 3, 2026, Llego filed the instant motion for leave to amend the complaint. Metro filed an opposition. Llego replied.

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (a)(1),[1] “[t]he court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect; and may, upon like terms, enlarge the time for answer or demurrer. The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.” 

A motion to amend a pleading before trial must 1) include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading; 2) state what allegations in the previous pleading are to be deleted and where, by page, paragraph and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and 3) state what allegations are proposed to be added, if any, and where, by page, paragraph and line number, the allegations are located. (Cal. Rules of Court rule 3.1324, subd. (a).) It must also include a support declaration specifying 1) the effect of the amendment; 2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; 3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and 4) the reason why the request for amendment was not made earlier. (Id. at subd. (b).)

 

III.       DISCUSSION

Llego’s motion for leave to amend the complaint fails to comply with the requirements of California Rules of Court rule 3.1324, as it did not attach a copy of the proposed complaint, does not specifically outline the allegations to be deleted and/or added by page, paragraph, and line number, and does not contain a  declaration testifying to the effects of the amendment, why the amendment is necessary and proper, when the facts were discovered, or why the request was not made earlier.

California Rules of Court rule 3.1324, subdivision (a), requires any motion to amend a pleading include a copy of the proposed amendment and state what allegations are to be added and deleted, identified by page, paragraph, and line number. Llego’s motion does not include a copy of the proposed First Amended Complaint, nor it does not identify the allegations to be added or deleted beyond generally describing the statutory basis for his claims. This does not satisfy the requirements of California Rules of Court rule 3.1324, subdivision (a)(1)-(3).

Llego also failed to submit a declaration addressing any of the issues required by California Rules of Court rule 3.1324, subdivision (b). The attached declaration only provides a summary of events leading up to this request; essential information is excluded.

Llego has failed to comply with California Rules of Court rule 3.1324’s requirements. Therefore, the Court denies the motion.

IV.        CONCLUSION

Llego’s motion for leave to amend is DENIED.

Metro is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 24STCV27602    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: 730

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

Department 730

 

MASON & ASSOCIATES INSURANCE SERVICES, LLC,

Plaintiff,

vs.

GENERAL MOTORS, LLC,

Defendant.

Case No.: 

24STCV27602

Hearing Date: 

September 18, 2026

ORDER GRANTING MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Mason & Associates Insurance Services, LLC (“Mason”) entered into a warranty contract with Defendant General Motors, LLC (“GM”) regarding a 2023 Cadillac Escalade ESV (“subject vehicle”). The subject vehicle allegedly has defects and nonconformities making the car effectively worthless. GM allegedly failed to reasonably repair or reimburse Mason for the vehicle. Mason sued GM for four causes of action, including breach of the implied warranty.

On April 3, 2026, Mason filed a conditional notice of settlement of the entire case.

On April 6, 2026, Mason filed the instant motion for attorney fees. GM filed an opposition. Mason filed a reply brief.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

Attorneys’ fees are allowed as costs when authorized by contract, statute, or law. (Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10)(B).[1]) Civil Code section 1717 states that “[i]n any action on a contract, where the contract specifically provides that attorney’s fees and costs, which are incurred to enforce that contract, shall be awarded either to one of the parties or to the prevailing party, then the party who is determined to be the party prevailing on the contract, whether he or she is the party specified in the contract or not, shall be entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees in addition to other costs.  (Civ. Code § 1717, subd. (a).)  The term “on a contract” is liberally construed to include any action that “involves” a contact.  (Blickman Turkus, LP v. MF Downtown Sunnyvale, LLC (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 858, 894.)  “To determine whether an action is on the contract, we look to the complaint and focus on the basis of the cause of action.”  (Brown Bark III, L.P. v. Haver (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 809, 821.)  “[F]ees are properly awarded under section 1717 ‘to the extent that the action in fact is an action to enforce—or avoid enforcement of—the specific contract.’”  (Turner v. Schultz (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 974, 980.)  The prevailing party on the contract is “the party who recovered greater relief in the action on the contract.”  (Civ. Code, § 1717, subd. (b)(1).) 

 The attorney bears the burden of proof as to “reasonableness” of any fee claim. (§ 1033.5(c)(5).) This burden requires competent evidence as to the nature and value of the services rendered. (Martino v. Denevi (1986) 182 Cal.App.3d 553, 559.) “Testimony of an attorney as to the number of hours worked on a particular case is sufficient evidence to support an award of attorney fees, even in the absence of detailed time records.” (Ibid.)

A party's verified billing invoices are prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses, and services listed were necessarily incurred. (See Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682.) “In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence. General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated to not suffice.” (Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 459, 488, quoting Premier Med. Mgmt. Sys., Inc. v. California Ins. Guarantee Ass’n (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 564.) The Court has discretion to reduce fees that result from inefficient or duplicative use of time. (Horsford v. Bd. of Trustees of California State Univ. (2005) 132 Cal. App. 4th 359, 395.)

In determining a reasonable attorney fee, the trial court begins with the lodestar, i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate. (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 36.) The lodestar may then be adjusted based on factors specific to the case in order to fix the fee at the fair market value of the legal services provided. (Ibid.) These facts include (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award. (Ibid.)

 

IV.        DISCUSSION

Mason moves for an award of attorney fees and costs in the amount of $66,325.78, of which $3,915.28 is attributed to costs and $62,410.50 is attributed to attorneys’ fees.

This motion was filed within the timeframe required by California Rules of Court rule 3.1702, subdivision (b)(1).

A.         Entitlement to Attorney Fees

Mason seeks an award of attorney’s fees pursuant to the section 998 compromise offered by GM. (Mot., Barry Decl. ¶ 9, Ex. 1.) On November 7, 2025, Mason’s counsel, on behalf of Mason, accepted the section 998 compromise with the following provision: “Plaintiffs’ attorney's fees, expenses, and costs that have been reasonably incurred pursuant to California Civil Code § 1794(d) may be determined by the Court via noticed motion. For purposes of any such motion, GM will agree that Plaintiffs are the prevailing party.” (Ibid.)

As the settlement agreement explicitly provides Mason is the prevailing party, for purposes of determining attorney’s fees, and is entitled to said fees, Mason is entitled to attorney fees under Civil Code section 1717, subdivision (a).

B.         Reasonableness of Fees

1.          Reasonable Hourly Rate

In assessing the reasonableness of hourly billing rates, “the court may rely on its own knowledge and familiarity with the legal market, as well as the experience, skill, and reputation of the attorney requesting fees [citation], the difficulty or complexity of the litigation to which that skill was applied [citations], and affidavits from other attorneys regarding prevailing fees in the community and rate determinations in other cases.”  (569 East County Boulevard LLC v. Backcountry Against the Dump, Inc. (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 426, 437; see Mountjoy v. Bank of America, N.A. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 266, 272 [“a reasonable hourly rate is the product of a multiplicity of factors . . . [including] the level of skill necessary, time limitations, the amount to be obtained in the litigation, the attorney’s reputation, and the undesirability of the case”].)  “The reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work.” (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.) “The experienced trial judge is the best judge of the value of professional services rendered in [her] court.” (Ibid.)

Mason seeks to recover attorneys’ fees for 20.9 hours at a rate of $695.00 per hour for the work of David N. Barry (“Barry”). (Id. at ¶ 13.) Barry argues his rate is reasonable, as he has 24 years of legal experience, 21 of which has been for warranty related cases. (Id. at ¶ 21.) Barry also stated his firm’s rates have been routinely upheld in other cases but did not attach any evidence or request the Court take judicial notice of the subject cases for the Court to verify the rates; therefore, the Court will not consider these. However, based upon Barry’s experience, this rate appears facially reasonable to the Court.

Mason also seeks to recover attorneys’ fees for 66.9 hours at a rate of $675.00 per hour for the work of Elizabeth Quinn (“Quinn”). The Court notes Quinn is noted as billing at $675.00 per hour in the moving papers, and $600.00 per hour in Barry’s declaration. (Id. at ¶ 13.) Based on the attached exhibit 2, it appears Quinn routinely bills at $675.00 per hour, and the $600.00 per hour is a mistake. (Mot., Quinn Decl. ¶ 3, Ex. 2.) Quinn’s rate is based upon 23 years of trial practice, and approximately 8 years of that relating to warranty work. (Id. at ¶¶ 8, 12.) The Court finds this rate also facially reasonable, based upon Quinn's experience.

Mason also requests attorneys’ fees for 4.7 hours at a rate of $525.00 per hour for the work of Debora Rabieian, 0.6 hours at a rate of $375.00 per hour for the work of C. Richard Lara, and 0.1 hours at a rate of $350.00 per hour for Christopher Derhartounian. Rabieian has worked as an attorney for 6 years. (Mot., Rabieian Decl. ¶¶ 4-5.) Lara has worked as an attorney for three years. (Mot., Lara Decl. ¶ 6.) The Court finds both rates facially reasonable. However, no evidence was provided as to Derhartounian’s experience; the Court cannot assess if the assigned rate was fair. Therefore, the Court strikes the $35.00 attributable to Derhartounian.

GM does not argue the billed rates are too high. Therefore, the Court will not reduce counsel’s requested rates.

2.          Reasonable Hours Incurred

“A trial court assessing attorney fees begins with a touchstone or lodestar figure, based on the ‘careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney ... involved in the presentation of the case.” (Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1321.) “The reasonableness of attorney fees is within the discretion of the trial court, to be determined from a consideration of such factors as the nature of the litigation, the complexity of the issues, the experience and expertise of counsel and the amount of time involved. The court may also consider whether the amount requested is based upon unnecessary or duplicative work.” (Wilkerson v. Sullivan (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.)

A prevailing party’s verified billing invoices are prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses, and services listed were necessarily incurred.  (Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682.) “In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence.”  (Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 240 Cal.App.4th 459, 488.) 

Mason seeks to recover attorney fees for a collective 93.2 hours litigating this matter. Counsel did not submit verified billing invoices in support of the expended time, instead presenting an exhibit with time allocations for each task. (Mot., Barry Decl. ¶ 14, Ex. 2.) Therefore, Mason has not presented prima facie evidence that the services listed were necessarily incurred. The remaining tasks are facially reasonable based upon the papers submitted in this action. Therefore, the Court strikes $17,241.50 in attorney fees from the requested base fees of $62,410.50, making the new base total $45,169.00.

C.          Costs

Allowable costs “shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(2).) Any items not specifically mentioned by statute “may be allowed or denied in the court's discretion.” (Id., subd. (c)(4).) The Beverly-Song Act allows a successful plaintiff to recover both “costs” and “expenses.” (See Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)

'“If the items appear to be proper charges the verified memorandum is prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses and services therein listed were necessarily incurred by the defendant [citations], and the burden of showing that an item is not properly chargeable or is unreasonable is upon the [objecting party].' [Citation.]” (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131.)

Mason requests a total of $3,915.28 in costs and expenses, pursuant to the memorandum of costs. (See 4/6/26 ‘Memorandum of Costs’.) GM argues certain portions of Mason’s costs are unrecoverable; however, as noted in GM’s opposition, GM did not file any motion to tax costs. Therefore, the Court will not consider GM’s opposition on the issue of costs specifically. The Court instead evaluates the costs on its own merits.

The costs appear to be facially reasonably and allowable under section 1033.5. Therefore, the Court grants the request for costs in full.

 

V.         CONCLUSION

Plaintiff Mason & Associates Insurance Services, LLC’s motion for attorney fees is GRANTED, in part. The Court awards attorney fees of $45,169.00 and costs of $3,915.28.

Mason is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 25STCV37848    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: 730

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

Department 730

 

LA THEIA BLACK

Plaintiff,

vs.

CITY OF RANCHO CUCAMONGA, et al.

Defendants.

Case No.:

25STCV37848

Hearing Date:

September 18, 2026

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING DEFENDANTS’ DEMURRER

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Defendant City of Rancho Cucamonga (“City”) allegedly improperly transferred Plaintiff La Theia Black’s (“Black”) business to Defendant Anacani Debaca (“Debaca”), without ever obtaining Black’s signature. Defendants Rancho Plaza Holding, LLC (“RPH”) and RPH’s owner Gavriel Sfaee (“Sfaee”), allegedly allowed Debaca to operate in Black’s former commercial space under Black’s permits, approvals, and goodwill. Black sued Defendants for conversion, appropriation of name or likeness, negligence, intentional infliction of emotional distress, defamation, violation of federal civil rights, and liability for policy or custom.

On September 18, 2026, RPH and Sfaee (“Moving Defendants”) filed a demurrer as to Black’s second amended complaint (“SAC”), in its entirety. Black filed an opposition. Moving Defendants filed a reply.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10[1] states: “The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) The person who filed the pleading does not have the legal capacity to sue; (c) There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) There is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) The pleading is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, uncertain includes ambiguous and unintelligible; and (g) In an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct.”

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.  (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.”  (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.)  As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations.  (Ibid.)  The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.”  (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

Under Government Code section 911.2, subdivision (a), any claim relating to a cause of action for personal injury shall be presented no later than six months after accrual of the action. A claim relating to any other cause of action shall be presented no later than one year after accrual of the cause of action. (Ibid.) A claim must be submitted via personal delivery, mail or electronic submission to a proper authority. (Gov. Code § 915, subd. (a).)

“Except as provided in [Government Code] Sections 946.4 and 946.6, no suit for money or damages may be brought against a public entity on a cause of action for which a claim is required to be presented in accordance with Chapter 1 (commencing with Section 900) and Chapter 2 (commencing with Section 910) of Part 3 of this division until a written claim therefor has been presented to the public entity and has been acted upon by the board, or has been deemed to have been rejected by the board, in accordance with Chapters 1 and 2 of Part 3 of this division.” (Gov. Code § 945.4.) Failure to present a claim for money or damages to a public entity bars a party from filing suit against that entity. (State of California v. Superior Court (2004) 32 Cal.4th 1234, 1239.)

III.       DISCUSSION

A.         Allegations of the Complaint

Black alleges that, since January 17, 2016, Black has solely owned and operated her business, the Original Muffin Message (“OMM”), with all necessary permits and licenses. (SAC ¶ 10.) Starting March 18, 2020, Black leased a commercial space for OMM at 9612 Foothill Boulevard, Suite 125, in Rancho Cucamonga (“subject location”). (SAC ¶ 11.) Although Black allegedly never sold, assigned, or transferred ownership in OMM, she later learned Defendant County of San Bernadino’s (“County”) records showed OMM had been transferred to Debaca ss of December 2022. (SAC ¶¶ 12, 14.) Black was allegedly never notified of any transfer prior to February 1, 2024, when she applied for a home baking permit. (SAC ¶¶ 13, 15.)

Black alleges Debaca told a County employee Debaca received OMM via transfer of ownership in 2022. (SAC ¶ 16.) A County employee stated the County processed the transfer without written authorization from Black, as it was not required by the County. (SAC ¶¶ 17, 19.)

Black specifically alleges that Sfaee and RPH knowingly allowed Debaca to operate in Black’s former business location under Black’s business permits, without verifying lawful ownership, resulting, in part, in Black’s personal information being submitted to the IRS as part of a potentially fraudulent tax return. (SAC ¶¶ 20-21.) Sfaee and RPH also allegedly knowingly and intentionally took possession of Black’s property, prevented Black from having access to her personal property, and/or destroyed Black’s property. (SAC ¶ 26.)

Black alleges Sfaee and RPH are liable for conversion, appropriation of name or likeness, negligence, intentional infliction of emotional distress, and defamation.

B.          Scope of Leave to Amend

The Court first notes that multiple causes of action alleged in Black’s SAC are outside the scope of leave to amend granted by the Court when ruling on the previous demurrer. “Generally, where a court grants leave to amend after sustaining a demurrer, the scope of permissible amendment is limited to the cause(s) of action to which the demurrer has been sustained . . . .” (Edmon & Karnow, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2016) ¶ 6:635.5; see Harris v. Wachovia Mortgage, FSB (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1018, 1023.) The general rule does not apply if the new cause of action directly responds to the court’s reason for sustaining the earlier demurrer. (See Patrick v. Alacer Corp. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 995, 1015.)

When ruling on the previous demurrer, the Court sustained the City’s demurrer, in full, based upon failure to allege timely presentation of a claim to the City, as required by the Tort Claims Act. (See ‘6/9/26 Minute Order’.) The scope of leave to amend, therefore, only allowed Black to amend the complaint to address the issue of timely presentation of a claim to government entities and issues/causes of action already alleged in the FAC.

The FAC makes no mention of appropriation of likeness, identity, or name; therefore, the SAC’s second cause of action “Appropriate of Name or Likeness” is stricken, without leave to amend, as Black did not have a right to add this cause of action.

The fifth cause of action for “Defamation” is similarly stricken, without leave to amend. While the FAC does contain allegations of Debaca making “false representations,” regarding transfer of Black’s business ownership, it makes no reference to inspection reports posted by Debaca, RPH, or Sfaee containing “express statements to the public that Debaca's business was the same as Plaintiffs, which is false,” which are the sole basis for defamation in the SAC. (FAC ¶ 49, SAC ¶ 47.) The cause of action for defamation improperly alleges new facts that are outside the scope of leave and therefore is stricken.

C.          Conversion

Moving Defendants first request the Court sustain the demurrer as to the cause of action for conversion as it is uncertain and improperly pled, as it neither identifies the property alleged converted nor any wrongful act by Moving Defendants. “The elements of a conversion claim are: (1) the plaintiff’s ownership or right to possession of the property; (2) the defendant’s conversion by a wrongful act or disposition of property rights; and (3) damages.” (Lee v. Hanley (2015) 61 Cal.4th 1225, 1240.) It does not require bad faith, knowledge, or even negligence; it requires only that the defendant have intentionally done the act depriving the plaintiff of his or her rightful possession.” (Voris v. Lampert (2019) 7 Cal.5th 1141, 1158.)

The Court agrees with Moving Defendants. It appears the ‘personal property’ in question, in regard to the conversion claim, is Black’s “goodwill, intellectual property, and business identity,” although Black also refers to permits issued by the County, making it unclear what property is specifically at issue. (SAC ¶¶ 25-26.) There are also no factual allegations of Moving Defendants engaging in a wrongful act or disposition with intent to deprive Black of her personal property. The cause of action for conversion only discusses Debaca’s application for transfer of ownership, unrelated to Moving Defendants. (SAC ¶ 27.) The general allegations assert Moving Defendants allowed Debaca to operate on the subject property under Black’s permits and goodwill without verifying lawful ownership, which is not indicative of any intent to deprive Black of her personal property; at most, it is indicative of negligence. (SAC ¶ 21.)

The SAC is insufficiently pled as it is both ambiguous and does not clearly assert any intent by Moving Defendants to deprive Black of property. Therefore, the Court sustains the demurrer as to this cause of action.

D.         Negligence

Moving Defendants request the Court sustain the demurrer as to the cause of action for negligence as Black does not allege facts supporting a duty, breach, or proximate causation attributable to Moving Defendants. “The elements of a cause of action for negligence are well established. They are '(a) a legal duty to use due care; (b) a breach of such legal duty; [and] (c) the breach as the proximate or legal cause of the resulting injury.’” (Ladd v. County of San Mateo (1996) 12 Cal.4th 913, 917.)

The Court agrees there is no clear duty attributable to Moving Defendants within the SAC; the SAC only states “Defendants and each of them owed a duty of due care to Plaintiff,” but does not explain what duty Moving Defendants, as landlords, had to Black, as a tenant, that were violated under these allegations. (SAC ¶ 38.) A landlord does not have a duty to ensure the validity of governmental permitting decisions outside the landlord’s control. (Day v. Lupo Vine Street, L.P. (2018) 22 Cal.App.5th 62, 73-74.) A landlord also is not liable for the negligent acts of tenants, meaning Debaca’s alleged negligence is not attributable to Moving Defendants. (St. John v. Schaeffler (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 1146.)

In opposition, Black asserts Moving Defendants do have a basic duty of care to not engage in conversion, defamation, and appropriation. However, as discussed previously, claims for defamation and appropriation are stricken as outside the scope of leave to amend, and conversion is subject to demurrer due to insufficient pleadings. Even assuming the Court considered these allegations, simply ‘allowing’ harm to occur is not enough to satisfy proximate cause pleading requirements. Therefore, the Court sustains the demurrer as to this cause of action.

E.          Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress (“IIED”)

Moving Defendants finally request the Court sustain the demurrer as to the IIED claim as the facts do not allege 1) extreme and outrageous conduct; 2) intent to cause distress or reckless disregard; 3) extreme emotional distress. A cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress requires: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff's suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant's outrageous conduct. (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1050-1051 [citations and quotations omitted].) A defendant’s conduct is “outrageous” when it is so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community. (Ibid.) The defendant must either intend his or her conduct to inflict injury or have engaged in it with the realization that injury will result. Liability for intentional infliction of emotional distress does not extend to mere insults, indignities, threats, annoyances, petty oppressions, or other trivialities. (Ibid.) 

The Court agrees. There is no alleged extreme or outrageous conduct attributable to Moving Defendants. At most, Moving Defendants failed to ensure Debaca’s business permits were proper. This does not constitute outrageous conduct outside all possible bounds of decency. Additionally, outside of one conclusory statement, Black has no allegations addressing Moving Defendants’ intent to cause Black emotional distress. (SAC ¶ 43.) Black also fails to allege any facts regarding severe emotional distress, merely asserting she “suffered severe emotional distress from the threat of identity theft and the' real impact of audits and investigations.” (FAC ¶ 44.)

As Black failed to plead facts to support any element of an IIED cause of action, the Court sustains the demurrer as to this cause of action.

E.          Leave to Amend

The burden is on a plaintiff to show in what manner he can amend the complaint, and how that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349; Hendy v. Losse (1991) 54 Cal.3d 723, 742.) Black requests leave to amend, arguing she can add in additional detail to cure the lack of specificity. As at least, in part, the demurrer was sustained due to insufficient facts, the Court grants leave to amend in the interest of resolving this issue on the merits and allowing Black another opportunity to present an actionable complaint.

IV.        CONCLUSION

Defendant Rancho Plaza Holding, LLC and Gavriel Sfaee’s demurrer to Black’s Second Amended Complaint is SUSTAINED, with 30 days leave to amend, as to the first, third, and fourth causes of action.

The causes of action for defamation and appropriation of name or likeness are stricken, without leave to amend.

Moving Defendants are to give notice.
