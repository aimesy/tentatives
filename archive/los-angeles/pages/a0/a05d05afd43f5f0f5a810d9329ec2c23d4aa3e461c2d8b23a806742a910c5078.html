Case Number: 24STCP01897    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: 26

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Respondent Daybreak Game Company, LLC’s Motion to Tax Costs is granted to correct the language of the proposed judgment filed on 09/19/2025 as follows.

 

No. 3 is corrected from “Pre-judgment interest is awarded at a rate of ten percent (10%) per annum, calculated from July 4, 2024, through the date of entry of this judgment” to “Pre-judgment interest is awarded at a rate of ten percent (10%) per annum, calculated from July 4, 2024 to the date of final payment of the total arbitration award.”

 

No. 4 is corrected from “Costs of suit in the amount of $1,955.40, as set forth in the filed Memorandum of Costs (MC-010 and MC-011), are included as part of this judgment” to “Costs of suit in the amount of $695.40 are included as part of this judgment.”

 

No. 5 is deleted in its entirety.

 

 

Analysis:

 

On June 12, 2024, Petitioner Kurtis Wilson (“Petitioner”) filed a Petition to Confirm Arbitration Award (the “Petition”) against Respondent Daybreak Game Company, LLC (“Respondent”). The Petition initially came for hearing on October 16, 2024, at which time the Court continued the matter to allow Petitioner to demonstrate service of the Petition and notice of the hearing on Respondent. (Minute Order, 10/16/24.)

 

On January 15, 2025, Petitioner filed the Petition to Correct and Confirm Arbitration Award, to which Respondent filed a response on February 10, 2025. At the hearing on March 18, 2025, the Court did not grant the Petition to Correct Arbitration Award and instead, gave Petitioner 60 days’ leave to file a petition to confirm arbitration award. (Minute Order, 03/18/25.)

 

Petitioner filed a Petition to Confirm Arbitration Award on May 13, 2025. Following full briefing, the Court granted the Petition and ordered Petitioner to file a proposed judgment within 30 days. (Minute Order, 08/18/25.)

 

Petitioner submitted a proposed judgment on September 16, 2025 that attached a memorandum of costs. Respondent filed a “response” to the proposed judgment on September 24, 2025, objecting to certain costs and the calculation of interest. On February 11, 2026, in light of the response to the memorandum of costs being filed and served within the time period required for a motion to tax or strike costs, the Court found that Respondent had preserved its objections pursuant to Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(b)(1).) (Minute Order, 02/11/26.) However, the Court ordered Respondent to file a noticed motion to tax costs within ten days, with the briefing schedule to be set per the Code of Civil Procedure. (Ibid.)

 

Respondent timely filed the instant Motion to Tax Costs on February 20, 2026. Respondent served Petitioner with the Motion by first-class mail and electronic mail on the same day. On May 13, 2026, the Court granted Petitioner’s ex parte application to continue the hearing date to allow for an opposition. (Minute Order, 05/13/26.) The Court specifically ordered Respondent to file and serve supplemental papers demonstrating the date of final payment of the total arbitration award by June 18, 2026. (Ibid.) The Court further ordered that opposition and reply papers must be filed and served pursuant to the Code of Civil Procedure. (Ibid.)

 

Respondent filed and served a supplemental declaration on June 11, 2026. Petitioner filed an untimely opposition on July 13, 2026, only five court days prior to the hearing date. It was also untimely served on Respondent on July 10, 2026, just six days before the hearing. Opposition papers must be filed and served at least nine court days before the hearing. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1005, subd. (b).)

 

Discussion

 

Petitioner’s proposed judgment included the following statements to which Respondent objects: (1) “Pre-judgment interest is awarded at a rate of ten percent (10%) per annum, calculated from July 4, 2024, through the date of entry of this judgment;” (2) “Costs of suit in the amount of $1,955.40, as set forth in the filed Memorandum of Costs (MC-010 and MC-011), are included as part of this judgment.” (Proposed Judgment, filed 09/19/25, p. 1.); and (3) “The total judgment amount, including the principal, pre-judgment interest, and costs, shall bear post-judgment interest at the legal rate from the date of entry of this judgment until paid in full.”

 

 

 

Objection to Pre-Judgment Interest Language in the Proposed Judgment

 

Regarding the award of pre-judgment interest in the proposed judgment, Respondent argues that it goes beyond the Court’s award of pre-judgment interest to the date of final payment of the total arbitration award, and that Petitioner did not set forth the applicable interest calculation as ordered, thereby waiving the interest award.

 

Respondent is correct that the language of the proposed judgment does not reflect the Court’s ruling. The interest award in connection with the Petition was for “interest (if any) in the amount of ten percent per annum from July 4, 2024 to the date of final payment of the total arbitration award.” (Minute Order, 08/18/25, p. 1, emphasis added.) Although Respondent argues that by failing to include the interest calculation as ordered Petitioner has waived the right to pre-judgment interest, the Court declines to make such a finding. Respondent cites no authority for the waiver argument. (Response to Proposed Judgment, filed 09/24/25, p. 2:20-22; Motion to Tax Costs, pp. 3:27-4:1.)

 

Regarding evidence of Respondent’s payment of the judgment, including interest, its supplemental declaration demonstrates that it issued Petitioner a check on August 15, 2024 in the amount of $22,250.00. (Supp. Decl., filed 06/11/26, ¶4.) In light of the arbitration award, which the Court reiterated in its ruling dated August 18, 2025, Petitioner was entitled to the principal amount of $21,985.00 plus ten percent interest from July 4, 2024, to the date of the final payment. Ten percent interest on $21,895.00 from July 4, 2024 to August 15, 2024 (42 days) amounted to $6.02 per day, or total interest of $22,237.98. This is attested to in Respondent’s supplemental declaration as the basis of its payment in the amount of $22,250.00, which exceeded the amount owed to Petitioner by $12.02. (Id. at ¶¶2-3.) Accordingly, the proposed judgment will be corrected to reflect the language of the arbitration award and the Court’s ruling regarding the dates pre-judgment interest is owed.

 

Objection to Costs for Video Editing

 

Respondent first moves to tax the entire memorandum of costs on the grounds that Petitioner filed it prematurely, prior to the entry of any judgment in this action. As with its argument regarding waiver, Respondent cites no authority that this is grounds for relief. (Motion to Tax Costs, p. 1:2-4.) In fact, absent a showing of prejudice, “courts treat prematurely filed cost bills as being timely filed.” (Haley v. Casa Del Rey Homeowners Assn. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 863, 880.)

 

Respondent also seeks to strike $1,260.00 from the memorandum of costs for video editing. Respondent argues that video editing expenses are not an allowable cost under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, and are not reasonable for a single-day evidentiary hearing before an arbitrator. To the extent that the video editing costs were incurred with respect to the arbitration proceeding, and not the instant action to confirm the arbitration award, they are not recoverable as costs in this proceeding. The Court’s award of costs to Petitioner was made pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1293.2, which pertains to costs for the judicial proceeding, not the arbitration. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1293.2.) Indeed, Petitioner’s late opposition makes clear that the video editing costs were incurred during the arbitration proceeding, and not as part of the instant action. (Opp., p. 2:7-15.) Therefore, the request to strike Item 13 of the Memorandum of Costs is granted in the amount of $1,260.00. The cost bill is reduced to $695.40, and the proposed judgment will be corrected accordingly.

 

Objection to Post-Judgment Interest

 

Respondent contends that the language adding post-judgment interest to the judgment is improper because it goes beyond the Court’s ruling confirming the arbitration award. The language awarding Petitioner post-judgment interest on the principal, pre-judgment interest, and costs is indeed beyond the scope of the Court’s ruling, and the arbitration award. The Court strikes that language in its entirety.

 

Conclusion

 

Respondent Daybreak Game Company, LLC’s Motion to Tax Costs is granted to correct the language of the proposed judgment filed on 09/19/2025 as follows.

 

No. 3 is corrected from “Pre-judgment interest is awarded at a rate of ten percent (10%) per annum, calculated from July 4, 2024, through the date of entry of this judgment” to “Pre-judgment interest is awarded at a rate of ten percent (10%) per annum, calculated from July 4, 2024 to the date of final payment of the total arbitration award.”

 

No. 4 is corrected from “Costs of suit in the amount of $1,955.40, as set forth in the filed Memorandum of Costs (MC-010 and MC-011), are included as part of this judgment” to “Costs of suit in the amount of $695.40 are included as part of this judgment.”

 

No. 5 is deleted in its entirety.

Case Number: 24STLC05308    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: 26

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Plaintiff ZB, NA dba California Bank & Trust’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees is denied.

 

 

Analysis:

           

On July 25 2024, Plaintiff ZB, NA dba California Bank & Trust (“Plaintiff”) filed the instant action for breach of contract and declaratory relief against Defendant Ben B. Safyari (“Defendant”). Defendant filed an Answer on October 3, 2024.

 

Plaintiff filed a Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the alternative, Summary Adjudication, which the Court granted on December 23, 2025. (Minute Order, 12/23/25.) Pursuant to that order, the Court entered judgment in Plaintiff’s favor on March 13, 2026. Notice of the entry of judgment was served on both parties by the clerk on the same day. (Notice of Entry of Judgment, 03/13/26.)

 

On March 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed a memorandum of costs and the instant Motion for Attorney’s Fees. The Motion was served on Defendant by first-class mail on the same day.

 

At the initial hearing on June 11, 2026, the Court continued the matter to allow Plaintiff to file and serve supplemental briefing and any necessary declaration regarding the propriety of recovering the attorney’s fees incurred with respect to the Small Claims cases. (Minute Order, 06/11/26.) The Court ordered Plaintiff to file and serve the supplemental papers by June 18, 2026. However, no supplemental papers have been filed to date.

 

On June 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Continued Hearing on Motion, which it served on Defendant on the same day. No opposition has been filed to date.

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiff moves for an award of attorney’s fees in the amount of $27,072.00 and $2,406.78 in costs pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 1032 and 1033.5, and Civil Code section 1717. A prevailing party is entitled to recover costs, including attorney’s fees when authorized by contract. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (a)(4); § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10)(A).) Civil Code section 1717 provides that reasonable attorney’s fees and costs shall be awarded to the prevailing party in an action on a contract, where the contract specifically provides for attorney’s fees and costs. (Civ. Code, § 1717, subd. (a).)

 

A motion for attorney’s fees must be filed and served within the time for filing a notice of appeal under Cal. Rules of Court Rule 8.822. (Cal. Rules of Court Rule 3.1702(a).) Cal. Rules of Court Rule 8.822 states that an appeal must be filed within either (1) 30 days after service of the notice of entry of the judgment; or (2) 90 days after entry of the judgment. (Cal. Rules of Court 8.822(1).) The instant Motion was timely filed 11 days after notice of entry of the judgment on both parties.

 

It is undisputed that Plaintiff is the prevailing party in this action, as the party in whose favor the Court granted summary judgment was granted and judgment has been entered. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (a)(4).) Accordingly, it is entitled to recover its costs under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a). It is also entitled to recover its attorney’s fees if such recovery is provided for in the contract at issue. The parties’ contract provides for recovery of collection costs, including attorney’s fees and legal expenses. (Motion, Exhibit List filed 10/21/25, Exh 1, p. 4 at “Collection Costs”.) Therefore, Plaintiff is entitled to recover its attorney’s fees in this action as the prevailing party.

 

Plaintiff’s counsel billed its attorney’s work at $330.00 per hour, which is a very reasonable billing rate for attorneys in Southern California. (Motion, Schneider Decl., ¶4.) According to its billing records, Plaintiff’s attorneys billed 78.4 hours on this action. (Id. at ¶4 and Exh. 1.) However, the Court finds the amount billed includes items that were not part of this action. For example, on July 24, 2024, Plaintiff’s counsel billed $726.00 for 2.20 hours attending a hearing on small claims in Los Angeles Superior Court. (Motion, Schneider Decl., Exh. 1, p. 2.) A review of the 38 pages of billing records attached in support of the Motion shows that they include many references to time spent on the Small Claims cases filed by Defendants. Although the Small Claims cases were referenced by the parties in Plaintiff’s Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the alternative, Summary Adjudication, the instant Motion for Attorney’s Fees provides no briefing as to why attorney’s fees incurred in the Small Claims cases are recoverable as attorney’s fees in the instant action.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 116.540 states that “a corporation may appear and participate in a small claims action only through a regular employee, or a duly appointed or elected officer or director, who is employed, appointed, or elected for purposes other than solely representing the corporation in small claims court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 116.540, subd. (b).) Furthermore, section 116.530 specifically provides that “no attorney may take part in the conduct or defense of a small claims action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 116.530, subd. (a).) This rule is subject to certain exceptions. (Code Civ. Proc., § 116.530, subds. (b), (c).)

 

Despite being provided with an opportunity to explain through supplemental briefing its entitlement to fees incurred on the Small Claims cases, Plaintiff has not filed any additional papers that would allow the Court to award fees for that time. Given the ubiquity of the billing entries for the Small Claims cases, the Court is unable to determine the amount of fees Plaintiff reasonably incurred in this action.

 

Conclusion

 

Therefore, Plaintiff ZB, NA dba California Bank & Trust’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees is denied.

Case Number: 24STLC06456    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: 26

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Defendant Jose Antonio Chavez’s Motion for Terminating Sanctions and Request for Monetary Sanctions is granted in part and denied in part. The Court dismisses Plaintiff’s Complaint filed on 09/09/2024 with prejudice but does not award Defendant monetary sanctions.

 

Defendant is to file and serve a proposed judgment in accordance with this ruling within 20 days.

 

 

Analysis:

 

Plaintiff Angel De Jesus Aguilar Calderon (“Plaintiff”), in propria persona, filed the instant action for motor vehicle negligence against Defendant Jose Antonio Chavez (“Defendant”) on September 9, 2024. Defendant filed an Answer on June 9, 2025.

 

On March 4, 2026, the Court granted Defendant’s (1) Motion to Compel Responses to Form Interrogatories; Request for Monetary Sanctions; and (2) Motion to Deem Requests for Admission Admitted; Request for Monetary Sanctions. (Minute Order, 03/04/26.) The following day, the Court granted Defendant’s (1) Motion to Compel Responses to Special Interrogatories; Request for Monetary Sanctions; and (2) Motion to Compel Responses to Request for Production of Documents; Request for Sanctions, as to the request to compel responses. (Minute Order, 03/05/26.) The Court ordered Plaintiff to serve verified responses to the interrogatories and requests for production without objections, and to pay monetary sanctions of $470.00, within 20 days’ notice of the orders. (Minute Order, 03/04/26 and 03/05/26.) Notices of the rulings were filed with the Court on March 5 and 6, 2026. (Notices of Ruling, filed 03/05/26 and 03/06/26.)   

 

On April 14, 2026, Defendant filed the instant Motion for Terminating Sanctions and Request for Monetary Sanctions. The Motion was served on Plaintiff by first-class mail on the same day. No opposition has been filed to date.

 

Discussion

 

Defendant seeks an order dismissing Plaintiff’s Complaint under Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030, pursuant to which the court may impose a terminating sanction by one of the following orders:

 

(1)   An order striking out the pleadings or parts of the pleadings of any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process.

(2)   An order staying further proceedings by that party until an order for discovery is obeyed.

(3)   An order dismissing the action, or any part of the action, of that party.

(4)   An order rendering a judgment by default against that party.”

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (d).) Where a party willfully disobeys a discovery order, courts have the discretion to impose terminating, issue, evidence or monetary sanctions. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2023.010, subds. (d), (g); R.S. Creative, Inc. v. Creative Cotton, Ltd. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 486, 495.) The court should look at the totality of the circumstances in determining whether terminating sanctions are appropriate. (Lang v. Hochman (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1225, 1246.) Ultimate discovery sanctions are justified where there is a willful discovery order violation, a history of abuse, and evidence showing that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with discovery rules. (Van Sickle v. Gilbert (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1495, 1516.) “[A] penalty as severe as dismissal or default is not authorized where noncompliance with discovery is caused by an inability to comply rather than willfulness or bad faith.” (Brown v. Sup. Ct. (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 701, 707.)

 

On March 4, 2026, the Court ordered Plaintiff to serve responses to Form Interrogatories, and to pay monetary sanctions of $470.00 to defense counsel. (Motion, McKeon Decl., ¶7 and Exh. A.) On March 5, 2026, the Court ordered Plaintiff to serve responses to Special Interrogatories and Requests for Production. (Id. at ¶7 and Exh. B.) The responses were to be served and the sanctions paid within 20 days’ notice of the rulings. (Id. at ¶7 and Exhs. A-B.) Defendant served notice of the rulings on Plaintiff on March 5 and 6, 2026. (Ibid.) As of the filing of the instant Motion, however Plaintiff has not served responses nor paid sanctions as ordered. (Id. at ¶11.)

 

Under the above authority, terminating sanctions are warranted for Plaintiff’s non-compliance with the discovery orders. Despite notice of the discovery motions, service of the Court’s orders, and service of the instant Motion for sanctions, Plaintiff has not served responses, paid sanctions, nor filed any opposition. This demonstrates that Plaintiff’s compliance with the Court’s orders cannot be achieved through lesser sanctions. Although terminating sanctions are a harsh penalty, “[t]he court [is] not required to allow a pattern of abuse to continue ad infinitum.” (Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsystem (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 262, 280.)

 

Plaintiff also moves for monetary sanctions under subdivision (a) of the moving statute, on account of Defendants’ misuse of the discovery process. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (a).)  Failure to respond to an authorized method of discovery, especially in light of the Court’s orders, is a misuse under Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.010. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subd. (d).)  However, the Court finds terminating sanctions appropriate, and sufficient, to address the abuse.   Plaintiff’s request for another order that Defendant pay sanctions of $470.00 in connection with the Motion to Deem Requests for Admissions, Set One, as Admitted, is unnecessary. The prior order stands, regardless of Plaintiff’s non-compliance; re-issuing the order is of no effect.

 

Conclusion

 

Defendant Jose Antonio Chavez’s Motion for Terminating Sanctions and Request for Monetary Sanctions is granted in part and denied in part. The Court dismisses Plaintiff’s Complaint filed on 09/09/2024 with prejudice but does not award Defendant monetary sanctions.

 

Defendant is to file and serve a proposed judgment in accordance with this ruling within 20 days.

Based on Defendant’s failure to respond to the propounded discovery, Plaintiff is entitled to an order compelling her to serve verified responses to the discovery requests without objections. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300.)

 

Conclusion

 

Plaintiff Deere & Company’s Motion to Compel Responses to Requests for Production of Documents, Set One, is Granted.

 

Defendant Violeta S. Salinas is to serve verified responses to the requests, without objections, within 20 days’ service of this order.

Case Number: 26STLC02251    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: 26

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Defendant City of Monterey Park’s Demurrer to the Complaint is sustained without leave to amend.

 

 

Analysis:

 

Plaintiff Shawn Ivy (“Plaintiff”) filed the instant action against Defendant City of Monterey Park (erroneously sued as “Monterey Park Police Department”) (“Defendant”) on April 2, 2026. On June 9, 2026, Defendant filed and served the instant Demurrer to the Complaint. No opposition has been filed to date.

 

 

Discussion

 

Allegations in the Complaint

 

The Complaint states on the face page that this is an action for (1) negligence; and (2) issuance of subpoena duces tecum. Plaintiff asks the Court to compel Defendant “to release certain sealed records pertaining to a 1995 Grand Theft Auto charge identified in Plaintiff’s FBI background report.” (Compl., ¶1.) Plaintiff alleges that the FBI report contains an error because the date of birth for the arrest does not match Plaintiff’s birth year. (Ibid.) Plaintiff attempted to get the report from Defendant and was informed that the records in question are sealed and require a court-issued subpoena to access. (Ibid.) Plaintiff seeks to identify the potential mix-up and correct the erroneous charge on his record, and asks the Court to issue a subpoena duces tecum compelling Defendant “to release all records related to the arrest and case file concerning the 1995 Grand Theft Auto charge, including any fingerprint match information.” (Ibid.) Plaintiff also asks for $30,000.00 in damages for Defendant’s egregious conduct and continuing neglect with respect to this matter. (Id. at ¶¶1, 4.) The request for a subpoena is brought pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 1985.3, 2020.410, and 1060, Penal Code section 1230.4, Evidence Code section 1040, and Article I, section 13 of the California Constitution. (Id. at ¶3.)

 

Demurrer to Complaint

 

The Demurrer is accompanied by a meet and confer declaration as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41. (Demurrer, Ross Decl., ¶2.) Defendant demurs to the Complaint for failure to allege sufficient facts, citing Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e).

 

Defendant first demurs on the basis that the Complaint is barred by failure to allege compliance with the Government Claims Act. No suit for money or damages may be brought against a public entity, until a written claim therefor has been presented to the entity and acted upon or deemed rejected. (Gov. Code, § 945.4; Rason v. Santa Barbara City Housing (1988) 201 Cal.App.3d 817, 822.) A claim for personal injury or death must be submitted no later than six months after the cause of action accrued, and as to any other cause of action no later than one year after accrual. (Gov. Code, § 911.2, subd. (a).) The Complaint must allege the plaintiff’s compliance with the requirements for claims submission. (State of Calif. v. Sup. Ct. (Bodde) (2004) 32 Cal.4th 1234, 1243-1244.)

 

The Complaint does not address timely presentation of any claim.  Plaintiff alleges that Plaintiff requested a background check from the FBI on September 7, 2024, and that on December 18, 2025, the Monterey Park Police Department received a certified letter from Plaintiff apparently notifying them of the alleged error in Plaintiff’s FBI report.  No other pertinent dates are alleged.  Even assuming that a cause of action may have accrued when Plaintiff requested a background check and that Plaintiff’s certified letter is a written claim therefor, Plaintiff has not pled compliance with the Government Claims Act because more than a year passed between those two events.  This pleading defect requires the Court to sustain the Demurrer.  

Additionally, government liability is entirely statutory and cannot be based on common law. (Govt. Code, § 815.2; Munoz v. City of Palmdale (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 367, 369.) The Complaint does not identify any statute that provides for a negligence cause of action against Defendant. (Compl., ¶3 (“Legal Basis for Complaint”).)

 

Defendant also demurs as to Count 2 on the basis that no cause of action exists for “issuance of subpoena duces tecum.” Defendant is correct that no language in Code of Civil Procedure section 1985.3 allows a party to file a cause of action for issuance of a subpoena, nor does Code of Civil Procedure section 2020.410 create a cause of action.  Count 2 is not a valid cause of action.  To the extent Plaintiff asks the Court to issue a subpoena duces tecum for the arrest reports under § 1985.3, that section does not authorize access to another person’s criminal records and it requires compliance with notice procedures not established here.  (See Code Civ. Proc., §1985.3(a)(1), (b).)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Demurrer to the Complaint is sustained for failure to allege compliance with the mandatory requirements of the Tort Claims Act, and for failure to allege a cause of action for negligence or issuance of a subpoena duces tecum. Leave to amend is not granted. Plaintiff bears the burden to demonstrate that leave to amend should be granted, which must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) This burden is unmet in light of Plaintiff’s non-opposition to the Demurrer.

 

Conclusion

 

Defendant City of Monterey Park’s Demurrer to the Complaint is sustained without leave to amend.

Case Number: 26STLC02864    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: 26

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Defendants WBLM 14 Le Count Owner, LLC and GREP Atlantic, LLC’s Motion to Quash Service is granted. Moving Defendants are dismissed.

 

 

Analysis:

 

Plaintiffs Codell Hall and Annita Taylor (“Plaintiffs”) filed the instant action for violations of the Investigative Consumer Reporting Agencies Act (“the ICRAA”) against Defendants WBLM 14 Le Count Owner, LLC (Defendant WBLM 14”) and GREP Atlantic, LLC (“Defendant Greystar”) on April 27, 2026. On June 18, 2026, Defendants filed the instant Motion to Quash Service of Summons. Plaintiffs filed an opposition on July 2, 2026 and Defendants replied on July 9, 2026.

 

Discussion

 

Allegations in the Complaint

 

Plaintiffs applied to rent an apartment in a building that Defendants own and manage in New York called “Stella Apartments.” (Compl., ¶¶1-4.) Defendant WBLM 14 is a New York limited liability company headquartered in the same state, and Defendant GREP Atlantic is a Delaware limited liability company headquartered in Texas. (Id. at ¶¶3-4.) Unnamed defendants include other persons or entities “who procured [or furnished] any investigative consumer report about any Plaintiff in connection with their housing applications.” (Id. at ¶6.) Defendants engaged a company called RentCafe, LLC, which is based in Santa Barbara, California, to build its application system. (Id. at ¶12.) The application system was built in California. (Id. at ¶13.) Defendant procured the investigative reports from RentGrow, Inc., whose officers and directors are located in Santa Barbara, California. (Id. at ¶14.) Defendants marketed their properties within California. (Id. at ¶16.)

 

Basis of Motion to Quash

 

Defendants move to quash service of the Summons and Complaint in this action pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 418.10, which provides: “A defendant, on or before the last day of his or her time to plead or within any further time that the court may for good cause allow, may serve and file a notice of motion for one or more of the following purposes: To quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over him or her.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a)(1), emphasis added.) A defendant has 30 days after the service of the summons to file a responsive pleading.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 412.20, subd. (a)(3).)

 

Defendants argue that they lack sufficient minimum contacts with California for the state to exercise personal jurisdiction over them. (Citing Pavlovich v. Superior Court (2002) 29 Cal.4th 262, 268.) They contend that they are based outside of California and the rental application is an online tool that can be accessed anywhere. Once a defendant moves to quash, the plaintiff bears the burden to prove that sufficient minimum contacts with the forum state exist. (In re Automobile Antitrust Cases I and II (2007) 135 Cal.App.4th 100, 110.) If the plaintiff meets that burden, the defendant must show that the exercise of jurisdiction would be unreasonable. (HealthMarkets, Inc. v. Superior Court (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1160, 1168.)

 

Specific Jurisdiction

 

Plaintiff’s opposition relies on specific, as opposed to general, jurisdiction to support this Court’s exercise of jurisdiction over Defendants. Specific jurisdiction exists if: (1) the defendant purposefully availed himself of forum benefits; (2) the controversy is directly related to or arises out of the defendant’s contacts with the forum; and (3) the assertion of jurisdiction over the defendant is reasonable, i.e., comports with fair play and substantial justice. (Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz (1985) 471 U.S. 462, 472-73, 477-78.) Purposeful availment means the defendant has purposefully directed its activities at the forum state so it can expect to be subject to jurisdiction there in light of the benefits received. Plaintiffs point to two different activities by Defendants to demonstrate purposeful availment: the contracts Defendants maintained with California companies, and that they obtained consumer reports about California residents.

 

Regarding the second basis—obtaining consumer reports of California residents—Plaintiffs point to federal court cases where jurisdiction was established over companies that procured credit reports of the forum’s residents. Defendants reply that the cases are either non-binding or factually distinguishable. The Court agrees that the federal district court cases are, at most, persuasive. (See People v. Koury (1989) 214 Cal.App.3d 676, 686.)

 

The Court is not convinced that obtaining consumer reports of California residents is “purposeful availment.”  In Myers v. The Bennett Law Offices (9th Cir. 2000), 238 F.3d 1068, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals ruled that a Utah corporation that obtained credit reports of Nevada residents was subject to jurisdiction there. (Myers v. The Bennett Law Offices (9th Cir. 2000), 238 F.3d 1068, 1087-1075.) In Walden v. Fiore (2014) 134 S.Ct. 1115, however, the United States Supreme Court ruled that a plaintiff cannot be the only connection between a defendant and a forum, thereby undermining the reasoning of Myers.  As summarized by one District Court, “The Supreme Court and the Ninth Circuit have emphasized that a plaintiff cannot be the only connection between a defendant and a forum, and merely because the plaintiff may experience harm in the forum does not show ‘express aiming.’ ” (Deveroux v. TT Mktg., Inc., No. 1:18-CV-487 AWI SAB, 2018 WL 3968249, at *5 (E.D. Cal. Aug. 16, 2018) (citing Walden, 134 U.S. 1124-25; Axiom Foods, 874 F.3d at 1069-70; Picot, 780 F.3d at 1214-15.)  There must be contacts by the defendant with the forum, not merely with a forum resident. (See id.)  The defendant’s contractual relationships, for example, must envision “continuing and wide-reaching contacts” in the forum State. (Walden, supra, 571 U.S. at 285 (citing Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz (1985) 471 U.S. 462, 479-480).)

 

Plaintiffs attempt to paint Defendants’ relationships with RentCafe, LLC and RentGrow, Inc., and Yardi Systems, Inc. as of this continuous and wide-reaching nature.  The Court disagrees.   First, Yardi Systems, Inc is RentCafe, LLC’s and RentGrow, Inc.’s parent company, but other than that, Plaintiffs do not explain what it does or the nature of Defendants’ relationship to it.  Its purported location in California is irrelevant to jurisdiction.

 

Second, both RentCafe, LLC and RentGrow, Inc. were incorporated in Delaware, and only RentCafe, LLC lists its principal address and mailing address in Santa Barbara, California. (Opp., Ollinger Decl., Exh. 3; Reply, RJN, Exh. 1.) The Court takes judicial notice of RentGrow, Inc.’s Statement of Corporate Information, which shows that, aside from being incorporated in Delaware, RentGrow, Inc. has its principal address and mailing address in Maryland, not California. (Reply, RJN, Exh. 1; Opp., Ollinger Decl., Exh. 5.)  Contacts with Rent Grow, Inc. therefore do not show continuous and wide-reaching contacts in the forum state. 

 

Nor does RentCafe, LLC’s presence in California support the requisite contacts.  RentCafe LLLC’s involvement in this action is limited to building the application system used by prospective renters such as Plaintiffs. Plaintiffs make no allegation directly connecting the application system to the consumer reports. (Compl., ¶¶21-24.) Plaintiff characterizes the consumer reports Defendants allegedly procured regarding prospective renters as “from California” without explaining what that means.  Plaintiff’s own evidence is that the reports are procured by RentGrow, Inc., which is not “from California.” (See Opp., Ollinger Decl., ¶3; Reply, RJN, Exh. 1.)

 

The opposition also fails to articulate what forum benefits Defendants obtain by entering into a contract with the California corporation, RentCafe, LLC. For example, Plaintiffs do not contend that contracting with RentCafe, LLC gave Defendants particular access to California residents to encourage their rental applications to Stella Apartments. Plaintiffs characterize advertisements they saw on social media as “in California” but do not demonstrate how those advertisements were purposefully directed at this state.  

 

The Court does not find persuasive the decision in Ratliff v. Venture Express, Inc. (N.D. Ill. 2019) 2019 WL 1125820, on which Plaintiffs rely.  In Ratliff, the Tennessee defendant challenging personal jurisdiction as to a suit filed in Illinois was determined to have “purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting business in Illinois by setting up a website through which Illinois residents could submit job applications; receiving Ratliff’s application through the website; seeking and obtaining his signature on consent and disclosure forms, one of which presumably authorized it to obtain his background report, see 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(2)(B)(ii); moving forward with the hiring process by obtaining the HireRight report; and ultimately deciding not to hire him based in part on that report, thus triggering an obligation under the FCRA to notify him that it had not hired him based on the report and that he had rights under the FCRA to obtain a copy of the report and dispute its accuracy and completeness with HireRight.” (Ratliff, 2019 WL 1125820, at *8.)  Significantly, the HireRight report which was the basis for the application rejection was prepared by an Illinois company; here, the report at issue was prepared by RentGrow, Inc., a Maryland company.  The unpublished Third Circuit case. Rogers v. Smith Volkswagen, LTD, No. CV 19-2567, 2020 WL 1676400, (E.D. Pa. Apr. 6, 2020) is distinguishable for the same reason, as is the slip opinion in Smith v. Ed Overcash (E.D. Pa. Oct. 17, 2014) Case No. 14-cv-4368.

 

The other cases on which Plaintiffs rely also do not support the exercise of jurisdiction in this action. Bernard v. Atlas Score, LLC (D. Or., Mar. 12, 2013) 2013 WL 997083 was decided in the year prior to Walden and relies heavily on Myers. (Bernard, 2013 WL 997083, *2-3.) The district court cited Myers for the proposition that “a defendant’s conduct is expressly aimed at the forum state when it requests a credit inquiry on a plaintiff that it knows is a resident of the forum.” (Ibid.) This reasoning was discredited in Walden and in Axiom Foods, Inc. v. Acerchem Int’l, Inc. (9th Cir. 2017) 874 F.3d 1064, 1069-70), as discussed above.  In Echols v. SafeRent Solutions LLC (D. Ariz., Oct. 4, 2022) 2022 WL 4970312, the plaintiffs sued the consumer reporting agency that prepared their background checks, and the background checks were then provided to landlords in the forum state. (Echols v. SafeRent Solutions LLC (D. Ariz., Oct. 4, 2022) 2022 WL 4970312, *8-10.).  The facts are not comparable.  The Court fails to see how the two pages cited in Stacker v. Intellisource, LLC, No. 20-2581-JWB, 2021 WL 2646444, (D. Kan. June 28, 2021) support Plaintiff’s position.  The finding of personal jurisdiction cited by Plaintiff is at page three; the facts recited there are very different than here in that the Kansas plaintiff’s Kansas employer obtained plaintiff’s consumer report from an out-of-defendant. (Id. at *3.)  The corollary entity to defendant in this case – the employer – was not sued and was not out of state.  More generally, the cases Plaintiffs cites are overwhelmingly unpublished, and unpersuasive for that additional reason.

 

 

For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiffs fail to demonstrate that the first prong of the minimum contacts analysis is satisfied.

 

Conclusion

 

Defendants WBLM 14 Le Count Owner, LLC and GREP Atlantic, LLC’s Motion to Quash Service is granted. Moving Defendants are dismissed.
