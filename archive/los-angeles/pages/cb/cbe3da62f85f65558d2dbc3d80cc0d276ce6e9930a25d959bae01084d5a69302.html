    DEFENDANT SANOFI-AVENTIS U.S. LLC’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT OR, IN THE ALTERNATIVE, SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

 

I.                Background

 

On August 12, 2025, Plaintiffs Terrence and Sarah Moore filed their complaint for personal injury alleging Mr. Moore developed mesothelioma from direct exposure to asbestos contaminated consumer talc products. As against Sanofi-Aventis, Plaintiffs allege that Mr. Moore was exposed to asbestos via its asbestos-containing Gold Bond and Desenex products she used between 1980 and 2010. (Compl. at ¶7.)

 

            On April 2, 2026, Defendant Sanofi-Aventis U.S., LLC (“Sanofi-Aventis” or “Defendant”) moved for summary judgment or adjudication on the grounds that it has no connection to the Gold Bond or Desenex products that Plaintiffs attribute to it.

            On June 5, 2026, Plaintiffs opposed, arguing Sanofi-Aventis’s motion relies on affidavits that fail to comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 2015.5 and lack foundation. Plaintiffs also argue that the motion is procedurally improper because it does not dispose of an entire cause of action.

On June 15, 2026, Sanofi-Aventis replied, arguing its supporting affidavits are admissible because they comply with the requirements of Code of Civil Procedure section 2015.5 and have foundation. Sanofi-Aventis also argues that Plaintiffs’ procedural arguments fail because the motion is directed at all of Plaintiffs’ claims against it.

Sanofi-Aventis failed to meet its burden to show that no triable issues of material fact remain over whether Mr. Moore was exposed to an asbestos-containing product for which it was responsible. The facts stated in the affidavits Sanofi-Aventis submitted in support of its motion are inadmissible for lack of personal knowledge. The burden does not shift to Plaintiffs. For the same reasons, Sanofi-Aventis’s motion for summary adjudication is denied.

 

II.             Discussion

 

A.    Legal Standard

A defendant seeking summary judgment must “conclusively negate[] a necessary element of the plaintiff’s case, or . . . demonstrate[] that under no hypothesis is there a material issue of fact that requires the process of trial.”  (Guz v. Bechtel Nat. Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 334.)  To show that a plaintiff cannot establish an element of a cause of action, a defendant must make the initial showing “that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854.)  “The defendant may, but need not, present evidence that conclusively negates an element of the plaintiff’s cause of action. The defendant may also present evidence that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence – as through admissions by the plaintiff following extensive discovery to the effect that he has discovered nothing.”  (Id. at p. 855.) “ ‘If plaintiffs respond to comprehensive interrogatories seeking all known facts with boilerplate answers that restate their allegations, or simply provide laundry lists of people and/or documents, the burden of production will almost certainly be shifted to them once defendants move for summary judgment and properly present plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses.’ ”  (Weber v. John Crane, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1433, 1440.) 

B.    Analysis

            Sanofi-Aventis moves for summary judgment on the grounds that has no connection to any Gold Bond or Desenex products. Sanofi-Aventis relies exclusively on two affidavits by Sanofi-Aventis’s contract corporate secretary, Stacy Apgar, to support this contention. (Degen Decl., Exhs. B-C.) Plaintiffs argue in opposition that the affidavits are  inadmissible because they fail to comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 2015.5[1] and because the affidavits lack foundation and personal knowledge.

            In reply, Sanofi-Aventis argues that the affidavits are properly made “on personal knowledge” and “sets forth admissible facts, show[ing] the affiant is competent to testify to the maters stated therein.” (Reply at p.4.)

            Ms. Apgar’s affidavits state that Sanofi Aventis’s three US subsidiaries are Gold Bond Co LLC (“GBC”), Sanofi-Aventis U.S., LLC (“SAUL”), and Sanofi U.S. Services, Inc. (“SUSI”).  (Degen Decl., Exh. B at ¶3, Exh. C at ¶3.) Ms. Apgar served as the assistant secretary of SAUL and SUSI. (Id.) Ms. Apgar was responsible for maintaining the corporate books and records for SAUL and SUSI. (Id., Exh. B at ¶2, Exh. C at ¶2.) Ms. Apgar testifies that SAUL and SUSI have never owned the Gold Bond or Desenex brands. (Id., Exh. B at ¶6, Exh. C at ¶8.) The facts stated in the affidavits are based on Ms. Apgar’s personal knowledge and her review of records kept in the normal course of business by Sanofi-Aventis. (Id.)

            Unless a witness is giving expert testimony, the “testimony of a witness concerning a particular matter is inadmissible unless he has personal knowledge of the matter. Against the objection of a party, such personal knowledge must be shown before the witness may testify concerning the matter. (Evid. Code, § 702, subd. (a).)  Corporate representatives are also subject to this personal knowledge rule and are limited to testimony reflecting their personal knowledge. (LAOSD Asbestos Cases (Ramirez v. Avon Products)¿(2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 939 (“Ramirez”).) For example, in Ramirez, a corporate representative’s testimony was inadmissible because the events described took place before the representative began working for the corporation and the representative failed to identify a source for her information. (Id. at p.951.) Even if the representative was “channeling” information from other individuals with personal knowledge, the trial court had no way of evaluating the reliability of the representative’s information obtained from these individuals. (Id.) The documents attached to the representative’s declaration likewise failed to cure the defects with the representative’s declaration because the documents were themselves inadmissible hearsay. (Id.)

            Here, Ms. Apgar fails to identify the source of her information that SAUL and SUSI have never owned the Gold Bond or Desenex brands. Additionally, there is no evidence of when Ms. Apgar began working for Sanofi-Aventis. There is thus no evidence that Ms. Apgar has the personal knowledge to testify that SAUL and SUSI did not own these brands during the alleged exposure period between 1980 and 2010. Although Ms. Apgar states her testimony is based in part on her review of corporate records, the documents she reviewed are not included with the moving papers. Like the evidence described in Ramirez, there is no way for the court here to verify the reliability of Ms. Apgar’s information. Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ objections for lack of foundation and personal knowledge are sustained. There being no other evidence of whether Sanofi-Aventis is liable for Gold Bond or Desenex products, Sanofi-Aventis fails to meet its burden on summary judgment. The burden does not shift to Plaintiffs. Sanofi-Aventis’s motion for summary judgment is denied. The motion for summary adjudication is denied for the same reasons.

 

III.           Conclusion

Sanofi-Aventis failed to meet its burden to show that no triable issues of material fact remain over whether Mr. Moore was exposed to an asbestos-containing product for which it was responsible. The facts stated in the affidavits Sanofi-Aventis submitted in support of its motion are inadmissible for lack of personal knowledge. The burden does not shift to Plaintiffs. Sanofi Aventis’s motion for summary adjudication is denied for the same reasons.

Plaintiffs to give notice.


[1]              Plaintiffs argue that Ms. Apgar’s declaration fails to comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 2015.5, which requires declarations supporting a matter to be sworn under penalty of perjury under California law. However, section 2015.5 states where a matter is required or permitted to be supported by “sworn statement, declaration, verification, certificate, oath, or affidavit,” the matter may be supported by “unsworn statement, declaration, verification, or certificate” sworn under penalty of perjury. In other words, Code of Civil Procedure section 2015.5 applies to unsworn statements, declarations, verifications, or certificates submitted in lieu of affidavits. Here, Sanofi-Aventis submitted affidavits in support of its motion. Accordingly, section 2015.5 does not exclude Ms. Apgar’s affidavits.

II.             Discussion

 

A defendant seeking summary judgment must “conclusively negate[] a necessary element of the plaintiff’s case, or . . . demonstrate[] that under no hypothesis is there a material issue of fact that requires the process of trial.”  (Guz v. Bechtel Nat. Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 334.)  To show that a plaintiff cannot establish an element of a cause of action, a defendant must make the initial showing “that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854.)  “The defendant may, but need not, present evidence that conclusively negates an element of the plaintiff’s cause of action.  The defendant may also present evidence that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence – as through admissions by the plaintiff following extensive discovery to the effect that he has discovered nothing.”  (Id. at p. 855.) “ ‘If plaintiffs respond to comprehensive interrogatories seeking all known facts with boilerplate answers that restate their allegations, or simply provide laundry lists of people and/or documents, the burden of production will almost certainly be shifted to them once defendants move for summary judgment and properly present plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses.’ ”  (Weber v. John Crane, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1433, 1440.) 

 

A.    Fraud

False representation under Restatement of Torts section 402B establishes “liability for injuries caused by justifiable reliance on false advertising.” (Westlye v. Look Sports, Inc. (1993) 17 Cal.App.4th 1715, 1750.) Under this section “ ‘[o]ne engaged in the business of selling chattels who, by advertising, labels, or otherwise, makes to the public a misrepresentation of a material fact concerning the character or quality of a chattel sold by him is subject to liability for physical harm to a consumer of the chattel caused by justifiable reliance upon the misrepresentation, even though [¶] (a) it is not made fraudulently or negligently, and [¶] (b) the consumer has not bought the chattel from or entered into any contractual relation with the seller.’ [Citation.]” (Id. at pp. 1750-1751.) “The rule ‘is one of strict liability for physical harm to the consumer, resulting from a misrepresentation of the character or quality of the chattel sold, even though the misrepresentation is an innocent one, and not made fraudulently or negligently.’ [Citation.]” (Hauter v. Zogarts (1975) 14 Cal.3d 104, 114.) Under section 402-B, the “‘reliance need not necessarily be that of the consumer who is injured. It may be that of the ultimate purchaser of the chattel . . . who because of such reliance passes it on to the consumer who is in fact injured, but is ignorant of the misrepresentation.’ [Citation.]” (Westlye, supra, 17 Cal.App.4th at p. 1751.) 

The elements of a cause of action for fraudulent concealment or fraud by non-disclosure are: “(1) the defendant concealed or suppressed a material fact; (2) the defendant was under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff; (3) the defendant intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would not have acted as they did if they had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff sustained damage.” (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 310-311, citing Kaldenbach v. Mutual of Omaha Life Ins. Co. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 830, 850.) 

Element two, sometimes referred to as the special relationship requirement, means that there are four circumstances which can give rise to actionable fraud by non-disclosure or concealment: “ ‘(1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts. [Citation.]’ ” (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336.) In the absence of a fiduciary relationship between a plaintiff and a defendant, “As a matter of common sense, such a relationship can only come into being as a result of some sort of transaction between the parties.” (Id. at p. 337.) Unlike products liability, where there is a “general principle that a manufacturer has a duty to warn consumers of a product's hazards and faults,” a duty to disclose material facts arises in the fraud context “only where there is already a sufficient relationship or transaction between the parties.” (Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at p.312.)

1.     Defendant’s Burdan: Factually Devoid

(Motion at pp.7-8 [“In the instant action, Plaintiff’s theory against Abex, and all defendants, is that they misrepresented the safety of their products by failing to include a warning about the possibility of asbestos in their products. As such, any actionable fraud is premised on the non-disclosure of information rather than any affirmative misrepresentation of fact. Plaintiff presents no specific facts of any affirmative misrepresentation made by Abex, including through advertising… Plaintiff’s discovery responses are also factually devoid as to specific facts showing that Ms. Marschik actually relied upon any affirmative misrepresentations of fact made by Abex…Summary adjudication cannot be avoided by any claim that Abex made false statements to third parties.”].)

Pneumo Abex moves for summary adjudication of the cause of action for fraudulent concealment on the grounds that Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that a transactional relationship existed between Ms. Marschik and Pneumo Abex giving rise to a duty to disclose. (Motion at p.14 [“To provide factually rich discovery responses, Ms. Marschik was required to present evidence of a special relationship between Abex and her. Additionally, Pneumo Abex argues that there is no evidence that it had actual knowledge of the health hazards associated with exposure to asbestos from the specific products at issue in this case. (Motion at p.15 [“Plaintiff’s discovery responses also fail to provide any specific evidence of such knowledge that any of Abex’s officers, directors, or managing agents actually knew of any alleged human health hazards associated with exposure to asbestos from specific products at or prior to the time of Ms. Marschik’s alleged exposure to asbestos-containing products manufactured, sold or supplied by Abex.”].)

Plaintiffs’ interrogatory responses fail to state that Pneumo Abex made any affirmative representations to Ms. Marschik or that it misrepresented the safety of its products. Plaintiffs’ discovery responses give rise to the inference that Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their contention that Pneumo Abex misrepresented the safety of its products to Ms. Marschik.

            As for the cause of action for fraudulent concealment, Plaintiffs’ discovery responses fail to establish that a direct transactional relationship existed between Ms. Marschik and Pneumo Abex. Although Plaintiffs allege that Ms. Marschik operated automotive repair stations with her then-husband and worked near others using Pneumo Abex products, there are no facts that Ms. Marschik transacted with Pneumo Abex. (Lu Decl., Exh. C at pp.3-35.) The discovery responses are thus devoid of facts supporting Plaintiffs’ contention that Pneumo Abex owed Ms. Marschik a duty to disclose defects in its products.

Plaintiffs’ interrogatory responses establish that Pneumo Abex knew of the hazards of asbestos and knew of the health hazards asbestos posed to its employees. ((Lu Decl., Exh. C at pp.3-35, Exh. E at pp. 53-56.) However, the responses fail to state the factual basis for Plaintiffs’ contention that Pneumo Abex knew customers using Pneumo Abex brake linings and blocks would be exposed to asbestos and be at increased risk for asbestos-related disease. Rather, the interrogatories state that it was foreseeable that customers handling these products would use them in a manner that exposed them to asbestos. (Id., Exh. C at p.15.) The fact that exposure to asbestos was foreseeable fails to establish that Pneumo Abex knew of the hazards posed by their products and intentionally concealed them. Plaintiffs’ discovery responses are thus devoid of facts supporting Plaintiffs’ contention that Pneumo Abex knew of the hazards posed by its products, owed Ms. Marschik a duty to disclose those hazards, and intentionally concealed them.

Plaintiffs’ discovery responses give rise to the inference that Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their claims for false representation and fraudulent concealment. Accordingly, Pneumo Abex meets its burden on summary judgment. The burden shifts to Plaintiffs.

2.     Plaintiffs’ Burden

Plaintiffs argue in opposition that summary adjudication is not appropriate here because their discovery responses contained detailed facts showing Pneumo Abex was aware of the hazards of asbestos before Ms. Marschik began operating the automotive repair stations. Plaintiffs advance additional excerpts from Ms. Marschik’s deposition, directing the court’s attention to portions of the testimony containing background information about the history of the repair stations Ms. Marschik operated with her then-husband. (PUMF Nos. 1-16.) Ms. Marschik testified that her husband and other mechanics used after market parts in her presence, including Pneumo Abex products. (Halbany Exh. E at 85:8-86:9.) Ms. Marschik testified that her husband and other mechanics used Pneumo Abex products hundreds of times throughout the years. (Id. at 92:14-93:5.) Plaintiffs direct the court to portions of Ms. Marschik’s testimony describing her exposure to Pneumo Abex products and how often they were used at her workplace. This testimony fails to establish that Pneumo Abex made misrepresentations to Ms. Marschik or that it intentionally concealed the hazards of its products.

Plaintiffs also advance excerpts from the deposition of Pneumo Abex’s corporate representative, Albert Indelicato, taken in another matter[1]. (Halbany Decl., Exh. I.) Plaintiffs direct the court’s attention to portions of the transcript showing Pneumo Abex also sold brake linings under the name Rayloc. (UMF Nos. 22-23; Halbany Decl,. Exh. I at 72:11-74:10, 79:3-8, 22:12-23:3.) Plaintiffs also advance the deposition testimony of Byron Frantz for the same purpose. (UMF Nos. 22-23; Halbany Decl., Exh. H at 33:18 34:16, 35:4-19, Exh. L at 17-18; 65-66.) Plaintiffs advance the deposition testimony of another corporate representative, William Bucella, to show that Pneumo Abex knew of the link between asbestos exposure and cancer as early as the 1940s. (Halbany Decl., Exh. L at 17-18; 65-66.) Plaintiffs’ remaining evidence consists of a publication from the Ford Motor Company regarding asbestos exposure during blow out of brake and clutch assemblies, studies and conferences about the release of asbestos dust during brake replacement, and Pneumo Abex’s responses to interrogatories from another matter showing that the company placed a warning on its brake linings in the 1970s stating that asbestos dust may cause serious bodily harm. (Halbany Decl., Exhs. M-T.)

This evidence likewise fails to establish that Pneumo Abex made misrepresentations to Ms. Marschik or that it intentionally concealed the hazards of its products. As discussed, even if there is evidence that Pneumo Abex knew of the hazards of asbestos, the evidence fails to establish that it knew customers using its products would be exposed to asbestos such that they were at increased risk of asbestos-related disease. Although Plaintiff provides publications warning of asbestos dust exposure during brake replacement, there is no evidence that Pneumo Abex was aware of those studies and knew that customers using their products would be exposed to asbestos dust and an increased risk of asbestos-related disease. These documents likewise fail to establish that Pneumo Abex had a duty to disclose these hazards to Ms. Marschik or that it intentionally concealed these hazards from her.

Plaintiffs fail to advance affirmative evidence supporting their causes of action for false representation and fraudulent concealment. Accordingly, Pneumo Abex’s motion for summary adjudication of the causes of action for false representation and fraudulent concealment is granted.

III.           Discussion

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

A defendant seeking summary judgment must “conclusively negate[] a necessary element of the plaintiff’s case, or . . . demonstrate[] that under no hypothesis is there a material issue of fact that requires the process of trial.”  (Guz v. Bechtel Nat. Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 334.)  To show that a plaintiff cannot establish an element of a cause of action, a defendant must make the initial showing “that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854.)  “The defendant may, but need not, present evidence that conclusively negates an element of the plaintiff’s cause of action.  The defendant may also present evidence that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence – as through admissions by the plaintiff following extensive discovery to the effect that he has discovered nothing.”  (Id. at p. 855.) “ ‘If plaintiffs respond to comprehensive interrogatories seeking all known facts with boilerplate answers that restate their allegations, or simply provide laundry lists of people and/or documents, the burden of production will almost certainly be shifted to them once defendants move for summary judgment and properly present plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses.’ ”  (Weber v. John Crane, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1433, 1440.) 

B.    Causation

In asbestos litigation, a plaintiff is required to show causation. A plaintiff must show “some threshold exposure” to asbestos-containing products and must further establish to a “reasonable medical probability” that a particular exposure or series of exposures was a “legal cause” of his injury, i.e., a substantial factor in bringing about the injury. (Rutherford v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (1997) 16 Cal.4th 953, 982.) “[T]he proper analysis is to ask whether the plaintiff has proven exposure to a defendant's product, of whatever duration, so that exposure is a possible factor in causing the disease and then to evaluate whether the exposure was a substantial factor.” (Lineaweaver v. Plant Insulation Co. (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1416 (Lineweaver).)   

Where a plaintiff’s allegations pertain to asbestos as an intended ingredient of a product the plaintiff was exposed to occupationally, the plaintiff is required to present evidence “defendant's product was definitely at his work site and that it was sufficiently prevalent to warrant an inference that plaintiff was exposed to it…” (Turley v. Familian Corp. (2017) 18 Cal.App.5th 969, 980 [Citing Lineaweaver, supra, 31 Cal.App.4th at p. 1420.].) Where a defendant manufactures similar products, some of which contained asbestos and some of which did not, a plaintiff must present evidence from which the court can reasonably infer that it was more likely than not that the plaintiff was exposed to an asbestos-containing variety of the defendant’s¿product. (Collin v. CalPortland Co.¿(2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 582, 593.)

A.    Legal Standard

 

A defendant seeking summary judgment must “conclusively negate[] a necessary element of the plaintiff’s case, or . . . demonstrate[] that under no hypothesis is there a material issue of fact that requires the process of trial.”  (Guz v. Bechtel Nat. Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 334.)  To show that a plaintiff cannot establish an element of a cause of action, a defendant must make the initial showing “that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854.)  “The defendant may, but need not, present evidence that conclusively negates an element of the plaintiff’s cause of action.  The defendant may also present evidence that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence – as through admissions by the plaintiff following extensive discovery to the effect that he has discovered nothing.”  (Id. at p. 855.) “ ‘If plaintiffs respond to comprehensive interrogatories seeking all known facts with boilerplate answers that restate their allegations, or simply provide laundry lists of people and/or documents, the burden of production will almost certainly be shifted to them once defendants move for summary judgment and properly present plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses.’ ”  (Weber v. John Crane, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1433, 1440.) 

B.    Causation

In asbestos litigation, a plaintiff is required to show causation. A plaintiff must show “some threshold exposure” to asbestos-containing products and must further establish to a “reasonable medical probability” that a particular exposure or series of exposures was a “legal cause” of his injury, i.e., a substantial factor in bringing about the injury. (Rutherford v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (1997) 16 Cal.4th 953, 982.) “[T]he proper analysis is to ask whether the plaintiff has proven exposure to a defendant's product, of whatever duration, so that exposure is a possible factor in causing the disease and then to evaluate whether the exposure was a substantial factor.” (Lineaweaver v. Plant Insulation Co. (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1416 (Lineweaver).)   

Where a plaintiff’s allegations pertain to asbestos as an intended ingredient of a product the plaintiff was exposed to occupationally, the plaintiff is required to present evidence “defendant's product was definitely at his work site and that it was sufficiently prevalent to warrant an inference that plaintiff was exposed to it…” (Turley v. Familian Corp. (2017) 18 Cal.App.5th 969, 980 [Citing Lineaweaver, supra, 31 Cal.App.4th at p. 1420.].) Where a defendant manufactures similar products, some of which contained asbestos and some of which did not, a plaintiff must present evidence from which the court can reasonably infer that it was more likely than not that the plaintiff was exposed to an asbestos-containing variety of the defendant’s¿product. (Collin v. CalPortland Co.¿(2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 582, 593.)

Plaintiff’s discovery responses contained sufficient facts to support her claim that she was exposed to asbestos by Eaton brake products. Accordingly, Eaton fails to meet its initial burden on summary judgment. The motion for summary judgment is denied.

C.    Fraud

Eaton moves for summary adjudication of the fraud-based causes of action for (1) false representation and (2) intentional tort/intentional failure to warn/concealment (hereinafter “fraudulent concealment”) on the grounds that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that Eaton misrepresented the safety of its products or that it had a duty to disclose the hazards of its products to her.

False representation under Restatement of Torts section 402B establishes “liability for injuries caused by justifiable reliance on false advertising.” (Westlye v. Look Sports, Inc. (1993) 17 Cal.App.4th 1715, 1750.) Under this section “ ‘[o]ne engaged in the business of selling chattels who, by advertising, labels, or otherwise, makes to the public a misrepresentation of a material fact concerning the character or quality of a chattel sold by him is subject to liability for physical harm to a consumer of the chattel caused by justifiable reliance upon the misrepresentation, even though [¶] (a) it is not made fraudulently or negligently, and [¶] (b) the consumer has not bought the chattel from or entered into any contractual relation with the seller.’ [Citation.]” (Id. at pp. 1750-1751.) “The rule ‘is one of strict liability for physical harm to the consumer, resulting from a misrepresentation of the character or quality of the chattel sold, even though the misrepresentation is an innocent one, and not made fraudulently or negligently.’ [Citation.]” (Hauter v. Zogarts (1975) 14 Cal.3d 104, 114.) Under section 402-B, the “‘reliance need not necessarily be that of the consumer who is injured. It may be that of the ultimate purchaser of the chattel . . . who because of such reliance passes it on to the consumer who is in fact injured, but is ignorant of the misrepresentation.’ [Citation.]” (Westlye, supra, 17 Cal.App.4th at p. 1751.) 

The elements of a cause of action for fraudulent concealment or fraud by non-disclosure are: “(1) the defendant concealed or suppressed a material fact; (2) the defendant was under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff; (3) the defendant intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would not have acted as they did if they had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff sustained damage.” (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 310-311, citing Kaldenbach v. Mutual of Omaha Life Ins. Co. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 830, 850.) 

Element two, sometimes referred to as the special relationship requirement, means that there are four circumstances which can give rise to actionable fraud by non-disclosure or concealment: “ ‘(1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts. [Citation.]’ ” (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336.) In the absence of a fiduciary relationship between a plaintiff and a defendant, “As a matter of common sense, such a relationship can only come into being as a result of some sort of transaction between the parties.” (Id. at p. 337.) Unlike products liability, where there is a “general principle that a manufacturer has a duty to warn consumers of a product's hazards and faults,” a duty to disclose material facts arises in the fraud context “only where there is already a sufficient relationship or transaction between the parties.” (Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at p.312.)

i.                 Defendant’s Burden: Factually Devoid

Eaton argues that summary adjudication of the fraud-based causes of action is appropriate because Ms. Marschik “has no evidence, nor can she reasonably obtain any evidence, that Eaton ever made any misrepresentation with the intent to harm her. There is no evidence Ms. Marschik had any communications with Eaton whatsoever and no witness possesses any information regarding any representations made by Eaton.” (Motion at pdf p.15.) Additionally, Eaton argues there is no evidence of “a legally cognizable relationship between Eaton and Ms. Marschik such that it had a duty to disclose information. While Plaintiff broadly allege all of the defendants, not just Eaton, ‘owed [Ms. Marschik] a duty,’…has provided no evidence of any relationship giving rise to a necessary fiduciary or transactional relationship.” (Id. at pdf p.16.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that “Eaton’s actions in failing to disclose the deadly hazards created by its products while knowing of such hazards is the type of nondisclosure that constitutes false representation. Eaton is not entitled to summary adjudication of this issue.” (Opp. at p.11.)

When Eaton asked Plaintiff to state all facts supporting the causes of action for false representation and fraudulent concealment, Plaintiff responded as follows:

“On or before 1930, and thereafter, EATON was aware or should have been aware that members of the general public and other “exposed persons”, who would come in contact with their asbestos-containing products had no knowledge or information indicating that asbestos or asbestos-containing products could cause injury. EATON knew that members of the general public and other “exposed persons” who came in contact with its asbestos-containing products, would assume, and in fact did assume, that exposure to their asbestos-containing products was safe, when in fact said exposure was extremely hazardous to health and human life.

On or before 1932, and thereafter, EATON knew or should have known of the dangers associated with breathing asbestos-containing dust and knew or should have known that users of asbestos and asbestos products, as well as members of the general public who would be exposed to asbestos and asbestos containing products, had no knowledge or information indicating that asbestos could cause injury. EATON also knew or should have known that the users of asbestos and asbestos-containing products, as well as members of the general public who were exposed to asbestos and asbestos-containing products, would assume, and in fact did assume, that exposure to asbestos and asbestos containing products was safe, when in fact said exposure was extremely hazardous.

It was known and foreseeable to EATON that the above-identified asbestos-containing products were used, installed, removed, handled and otherwise disturbed in the manner described above when the above-identified asbestos-containing products left EATON’s possession, particularly given its long standing relationship and knowledge of use of these products; yet, EATON provided no warnings to Plaintiff Bobbette Marschik or any other end user and bystander about the dangers of the above-identified asbestos-containing products. (Wyatt Decl., Exh. E at pp.7-13.)

Ms. Marschik testified at her deposition that she had never seen any catalogs, advertisements, or manuals from Eaton. (Wyatt Decl., Exh. G at 469:21-470:6.) Ms. Marschik had never received communications from anyone employed by Eaton. (Id., at 470:7-14.) (Id., at 468:14-22.)

Eaton satisfies its initial burden to prove that Ms. Marschik lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support her fraud-based causes of action. For the false representation cause of action, Ms. Marschik’s deposition testimony establishes that she had never communicated with Eaton employees and had never seen Eaton advertisements. This evidence establishes that Eaton could not have made misrepresentations about its’ products’ safety to Ms. Marschik because she admits she had no communication with Eaton. As for the cause of action for fraudulent concealment, Ms. Marschik also testified that she had never personally ordered brakes from Eaton. (Wyatt Decl., Exh. G at 468:14-22.) This evidence establishes that no transactional relationship giving rise to a duty to disclose existed between Ms. Marschik and Eaton.

Plaintiff’s discovery responses therefore give rise to an inference that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support her fraud-based causes of action. Accordingly, the burden shifts to Plaintiff.

ii.               Plaintiff’s burden

Plaintiff argues in opposition that Eaton is not entitled to summary adjudication of the false representation and intentional tort claims because Eaton failed to disclose the deadly hazards of its products. Plaintiff presents no argument or affirmative evidence addressing the issues raised in Eaton’s moving papers. As discussed, the discovery record establishes that Eaton never communicated with Ms. Marschik and no transactional relationship existed between Eaton and Ms. Marschik. Plaintiff therefore fails to present affirmative evidence to support her fraud-based causes of action against Eaton. Accordingly, Eaton’s motions for summary adjudication of Plaintiff’s fraud-based causes of action are granted.

D.    Punitive Damages

When a motion for summary adjudication targets a request for punitive damages, a higher standard of proof is at play. “Although the clear and convincing evidentiary standard is a stringent one, ‘it does not impose on a plaintiff the obligation to “prove” a case for punitive damages at summary judgment [or summary adjudication.’  [Citations.]  Even so, ‘where the plaintiff’s ultimate burden of proof will be by clear and convincing evidence, the higher standard of proof must be taken into account in ruling on a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication, since if a plaintiff is to prevail on a claim for punitive damages, it will be necessary that the evidence presented meet the higher evidentiary standard.’  [Citation.]”  (Butte Fire Cases (2018) 24 Cal.App.5th 1150, 11581159.) For a corporate defendant, the oppression, fraud or malice “must be on the part of an officer, director, or managing agent of the corporation.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (b).) That requirement can be satisfied “ ‘if the evidence permits a clear and convincing inference that within the corporate hierarchy authorized persons acted despicably in “willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” ’  [Citation.]” (Morgan v. J-M Manufacturing Company, Inc. (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 1078, 1090 (“Morgan”).) A plaintiff also “can satisfy the ‘managing agent’ requirement ‘through evidence showing the information in the possession of the corporation and the structure of management decision making that permits an inference that the information in fact moved upward to a point where corporate policy was formulated.’  [Citation.]”  (Id. at p. 1091.)  

“ ‘[I]ntentionally marketing a defective product knowing that it might cause injury and death is ‘highly reprehensible.’  [Citation.]”  (Bankhead v. ArvinMeritor, Inc. (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 68, 85.)  Punitive damages may be available when a defendant knows the dangers of asbestos, took action to protect its own employees from the hazard, knew that its products were likely to pose a danger to users, and did not warn them.  (Pfeifer v. John Crane, Inc. (2013) 220 Cal.Ap.4th 1270, 1300.)  Such evidence “was sufficient to show malice, that is, despicable conduct coupled with conscious disregard for the safety of others.”  (Id. at pp. 1300-1301.) 

i.                 Defendant’s Burden: Factually Devoid

Defendant moves for summary adjudication of the demand for punitive damages on the grounds that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence establishing by clear and convincing evidence that Eaton acted with malice, fraud, or oppression. (Motion at pdf pp.17-18 [“…in response to Eaton’s Special Interrogatory expressly seeking all facts upon which he bases her punitive damages claim, Plaintiff does not provide a single reference to Eaton’s alleged conduct towards Plaintiff and, instead, provides a broad, conclusory statement, without any supporting facts, that Eaton failed to provide any warning to her regarding the hazards of working with asbestos containing brakes and that Eaton Defendant knew this created dust, that this dust could contain asbestos and this dust released during use and replacement.”].)

In opposition, Plaintiff, citing Pfeiffer v. John Crane, Inc. (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 1270 (“Pfeifer”), argues that Eaton “knew that its products contained asbestos, knew that they were meant to be used for decades, and knew that asbestos dust was created from working with them. Yet Eaton continued to sell products without any warning, sold replacement parts and never sent out any recall notices for its products. These asbestos-containing products were never tested to determine if their use created asbestos dust in concentrations above regulatory limits. This was a conscious disregard of health and safety and, therefore, sufficient to raise triable issues as to culpability for punitive damages.” (Opp. at p.13.)

In Pfeiffer, evidence was sufficient to establish by clear and convincing evidence that a manufacturer of gaskets, JCI, knew that asbestos was hazardous by the 1970s when the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (“OSAH”) promulgated regulations requiring manufacturers of asbestos-based products to monitor concentrations of asbestos fibers in the air in its factories. (Pfeifer, supra, 220 Cal.App.4th at p.1300.) JCI also prepared safety data sheets for its sheet gaskets warning its employees that exposure to asbestos could cause asbestosis and cancer. (Id.) OSHA regulations also required manufacturers to place warning labels on products unless the fibers had been modified by a bonding agent that limited the asbestos released during foreseeable use of the gaskets. (Id.) JCI knew asbestos dust was released when its users replaced fragmented old. (Id.) Additionally, JCI knew products did not fully encapsulate the asbestos fibers and that removal methods resulted in concentrations of asbestos fibers that greatly exceeded ordinary background levels. (Id.) The evidence supported a finding that JCI acted with a conscious disregard for the safety of others because it understood that asbestos dust endangered workers and continued to sell a product that generated considerable asbestos dust without warnings. (Id. at pp.1300-1301.)

Here, Plaintiff’s interrogatory responses state in relevant part:

“It was known and foreseeable to EATON that the above-identified asbestos-containing products were used, installed, removed, handled and otherwise disturbed in the manner described above when the above-identified asbestos-containing products left EATON’s possession, particularly given its long standing relationship and knowledge of use of these products; yet, EATON provided no warnings to Plaintiff Bobbette Marschik or any other end user and bystander about the dangers of the above-identified asbestos-containing products. EATON had a duty to warn users of its products of the dangers of asbestos materials, but failed to do so. As a result, asbestos materials used, installed, removed, handled and otherwise disturbed in the manner described above without any precautions being taken and Plaintiff Bobbette Marschik suffered heavy exposure to asbestos. Such exposure to asbestos contributed to Plaintiff Bobbette Marschik developing mesothelioma.”

(Wyatt Decl., Exh. E at pp.8-9.)

Additionally, Plaintiff’s responses to requests for production direct Eaton to OSHA standards relating to asbestos to show that Eaton was on notice of the hazards of asbestos in its products:

“Plaintiff also identifies OSHA standards relating to asbestos, found at Code of Federal Regulations, Title 29, Chapter 17, Section 1910, et seq.; and Title 8, Section 5208 of the California OSHA regulations pertaining to asbestos exposure. Defendant was subject to these regulations, which thus but (sic) Defendant on constructive if not actual notice of harms associated with asbestos exposure long before Plaintiff was exposed to asbestos from Defendant’s products.”

(Wyatt Decl., Exh. F at p. 5.)

            Eaton satisfies its burden to show that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support her demand for punitive damages. Although Plaintiff’s discovery responses state that it was foreseeable based on OSHA standards and known uses of its products that end users of its brakes would handle their products in a manner that released asbestos dust, there are no facts that Eaton was on notice that handling the brakes would expose others not directly working with the brakes, such as Ms. Marschik, to asbestos dust. In Pfeiffer, a manufacturer knew of the exposure risks to end users because it knew removing its gaskets resulted in dust exceeding levels permitted by OSHA. Here, unlike in Pfeiffer, there is no evidence that Eaton knew end users handling brakes would expose others not directly working with the brakes to hazardous levels of asbestos. There is no evidence in the discovery record that Eaton knew of the hazards its products posed to bystanders like Ms. Marschik and continued to sell them without warning. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s discovery responses are devoid of facts supporting the demand for punitive damages.

ii.               Plaintiff’s Burden

Plaintiff argues that summary adjudication of the demand for punitive damages is not appropriate because Eaton knew its products contained asbestos, knew their products created asbestos dust, and failed to test the products to determine if their use created asbestos dust in concentrations above regulatory limits in conscious disregard for the health and safety of others. (Opp. at p.13.) However, this assertion is made in the opposition memorandum without evidentiary support.

Plaintiff provides the deposition testimony of Eaton’s corporate representative, Roger Hobbie, to show that Eaton sold asbestos-containing brakes until 1986 and knew of the hazards of asbestos by the 1960s. (PUMF No. 45, Halbany Decl., Exhs. E-H, Q-R.) Plaintiff also advances several research papers on the risk of asbestos exposure in brake maintenance work. (Halbany Decl., Exhs. I-P.) Mr. Hobbie’s deposition testimony at most establishes that Eaton made asbestos brakes until 1986 and knew of the hazards of asbestos. However, the testimony fails to establish that Eaton knew of the exposure risks to others not working directly with the brakes such as Ms. Marschik. As for the remaining evidence, there is no evidence that Eaton was aware of these research papers.

Plaintiff fails to present affirmative evidence supporting her demand for punitive damages. Accordingly, the motion for summary adjudication of the demand for punitive damages is granted.

 

E.    Breach of Implied Warranty

Eaton moves for summary adjudication of the cause of action for breach of implied warranty on the grounds that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that Eaton was in privity with Ms. Marschik regarding the sale of any Eaton products. (Motion at pdf pp.16-17.) Eaton also argues that Plaintiff failed to identify a specific Eaton product that contributed to her injuries. (Id.)

Eaton’s motion for summary judgment was denied because it failed to meet its burden that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that she was exposed to an asbestos-containing product for which Eaton was responsible. Accordingly, Eaton’s argument pertaining to product identification fails. The motion for summary adjudication to the cause of action for breach of implied warranty thus turns on whether Ms. Marschik was in privity with Eaton.

“Vertical privity is a prerequisite in California for recovery on a theory of breach of the implied warranties of fitness and merchantability.” (United States Roofing, Inc. v. Credit Alliance Corp. (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 1431, 1441, internal citations omitted.) However, it is well-settled that “employe[e]s should be considered a member of the industrial ‘family’ of the employer—whether corporate or private— and to thus stand in such privity to the manufacturer as to permit the employe[e]s to be covered by warranties made to the purchaser-employer.” (Peterson v. Lamb Rubber Co. (1960) 54 Cal.2d 339, 347.) 

            Here, there is no dispute that Ms. Marschik was an employee of the service centers she operated with her then-husband. As an employee of the service centers that purchased the products at issue, Ms. Marschik stands in privity with Eaton and is covered by the warranties made to those service centers. Eaton’s argument with respect to privity fails. The motion for summary adjudication of the cause of action for breach of implied warranty is denied.

A.    Continuance Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure 437c subdivision (h).

Plaintiff requests a continuance pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (h) which states “If it appears from the affidavits submitted in opposition to a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication, or both, that facts essential to justify opposition may exist but cannot, for reasons stated, be presented, the court shall deny the motion, order a continuance to permit affidavits to be obtained or discovery to be had, or make any other order as may be just. The application to continue the motion to obtain necessary discovery may also be made by ex parte motion at any time on or before the date the opposition response to the motion is due.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(h).) “A declaration in support of a request for continuance under section 437c, subdivision (h) must show: ‘(1) the facts to be obtained are essential to opposing the motion; (2) there is reason to believe such facts may exist; and (3) the reasons why additional time is needed to obtain these facts. [Citations.]’ ” (Cooksey v. Alexakis (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 246, 254.)

Plaintiff requests a continuance under Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (h), arguing “Plaintiff has not yet had the opportunity to take the deposition of Eaton through its PMQ, which will include examination and document production, including but not limited to its manufacture and sale of asbestos-containing brakes and gaskets to heavy-duty truck and mobile home manufacturers as well as Eaton’s knowledge of the hazards of asbestos.” (Opp. at p.15.)

Plaintiff fails to present sufficient evidence to show she is entitled to a continuance of this motion. Specifically, Plaintiff fails to establish that the deposition of the corporate representative would provide facts essential to opposing the motion. The motion for summary judgment was denied. The motion for summary adjudication to the cause of action for breach of implied warranty was also denied. With respect to the fraud causes of action, deposing Eaton’s PMQ would not reveal information about whether Ms. Marschik communicated with Eaton or directly transacted with Eaton because her own testimony establishes that she did neither. With respect to the demand for punitive damages, Plaintiff’s counsel failed to explain how deposing Eaton’s PMQ would reveal whether it knew of the risk posed to bystanders not working directly with Eaton’s brake products such as Ms. Marschik. Plaintiff therefore fails to establish why she believe additional facts exist to support her opposition. The request for a continuance is denied.

III.           Conclusion

Eaton fails to meet its initial burden to prove that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that she was exposed to an asbestos-containing product for which it is responsible. The burden does not shift to Plaintiff. Accordingly, the motion for summary judgment is denied. Eaton’s motion for summary adjudication to the cause of action for breach of implied warranty is also denied for the same reasons as the motion for summary judgment. Additionally, Eaton’s argument with respect to privity is without merit.

Eaton satisfies its initial burden to prove that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that Eaton engaged in conduct amounting to malice, fraud, or oppression. Eaton also satisfies its initial burden to prove that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that Eaton misrepresented the safety of its product or intentionally concealed the hazards of its products. Plaintiff failed to present affirmative evidence supporting her fraud-based causes of action and her demand for punitive damages. Eaton’s motion for summary adjudication of the fraud-based causes of action and the demand for punitive damages is granted.
