Case Number: 24NNCV06446    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION FOR RELIEF FROM DEEMED ADMISSIONS

I.         INTRODUCTION

Plaintiffs Gohar Bosnuyan, Eric Vivanco-Perez and David Bosnuyan, a minor, by Gohar Bosnuyan as his guardian ad litem (“Plaintiffs”), bring this action against Defendants Hyland Inn Near Pasadena Civic Center; RVN Inc. and Vimal Patel (“Defendants”), alleging causes of action for (1) battery, (2) negligence, (3) intentional infliction of emotional distress, (4) fraudulent concealment, (5) private nuisance, (6) public nuisance, and (7) breach of contract. Plaintiffs allege they were knowingly assigned to a hotel room infested with bedbugs, which caused physical and emotional injuries, property damage, and other harm.

On June 2, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion for relief from deemed admissions. On June 12, 2026, Defendants filed an opposition. On July 7, 2026, after hearing from counsel, the Court withdrew its tentative ruling denying the motion and permitted the parties to submit supplemental declarations addressing their respective positions and the outstanding issues.

On August 17, 2026, Plaintiffs’ counsel submitted a supplemental declaration addressing the outstanding issues. On September 14, 2026, Defendants filed an opposition.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

CCP section 2033.300 provides that “[a] party may withdraw or amend an admission made in response to a request for admission only on leave of court granted after notice to all parties.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.300(a).)  “The court may permit withdrawal or amendment of an admission only if it determines that the admission was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect, and that the party who obtained the admission will not be substantially prejudiced in maintaining that party’s action or defense on the merits.”  (Id., § 2033.300(b).)  “The court may impose conditions on the granting of the motion that are just, including, but not limited to, the following: [¶] (1) [a]n order that the party who obtained the admission be permitted to pursue additional discovery related to the matter involved in the withdrawn or amended admission[;] [¶] (2) [a]n order that the costs of any additional discovery be borne in whole or in part by the party withdrawing or amending the admission.”  (Id., § 2033.300(c).).

III.      ANALYSIS

            On July 7, 2026, the Court withdrew its tentative ruling denying Plaintiffs’ motion for relief from deemed admissions and permitted the parties to submit supplemental declarations addressing their respective positions and the outstanding issues.

CCP section 2033.300 provides that “[a] party may withdraw or amend an admission made in response to a request for admission only on leave of court granted after notice to all parties.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.300(a).)  “The court may permit withdrawal or amendment of an admission only if it determines that the admission was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect, and that the party who obtained the admission will not be substantially prejudiced in maintaining that party’s action or defense on the merits.”  (Id., § 2033.300(b).) 

In the supplemental declaration, Plaintiffs’ counsel states that Plaintiff served substantive responses to Defendants RFA, Set One on January 27, 2026, but acknowledges that the responses were not accompanied by executed verifications. (Janfaza Decl., ¶¶ 5-10.) Counsel states that verifications for Plaintiffs Gohar Bosnuyan and David Bosnuyan were executed and transmitted to Defendants on February 19, 2026. (Id., ¶¶ 16-19.) Plaintiff Eric Vivanco-Perez subsequently provided his verification on August 14, 2026, with counsel explaining that obtaining his verification was delayed due to his incarceration and transfer between facilities. (Id., ¶ 57.)

Although untimely, Plaintiffs’ supplemental declaration establishes that substantive responses were provided and that the responses have now been verified. Defendants have not shown that withdrawal of the deemed admissions would substantially prejudice their ability to defend the action on the merits. While Defendants point to Plaintiffs’ delay and repeated extensions, the record reflects that Defendants have had Plaintiffs’ substantive RFA responses since January 27, 2026. Further, the matter remains set for trial on January 12, 2027.

In light of the service of verified responses, the absence of substantial prejudice to Defendants, and the policy favoring resolution of cases on their merits, the Court finds relief warranted.

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s motion for relief from deemed admissions

IV.       ORDER

            The Court GRANTS Plaintiffs motion for relief from deemed admissions.

Plaintiffs are to give notice.

Case Number: 25NNCV00322    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING IN PART DEFENDANTS’ UBER TECHNOLOGIES, INC., RASIER, LLC AND RASIER-CA, LLC’S MOTION TO COMPEL NEUROPSYCH IME OF PLAINTIFF PATRIA VALDEZ

I.         INTRODUCTION

This action arises out of a motor vehicle collision that occurred on August 31, 2024, near the intersection of Vineland Avenue and Victory Boulevard in North Hollywood. Plaintiffs Patria Diaz and her minor son, Cedric Valdez (“Plaintiffs”), allege they suffered severe injuries when Defendant Daryoush Mehrabani, while driving for Uber, crossed into oncoming traffic and caused a head-on collision. Plaintiffs filed this action on January 16, 2025, asserting claims against Mehrabani, Uber Technologies, Inc., Rasier, LLC, and Rasier-CA, LLC.

On August 24, 2026, Defendants Uber Technologies, Inc., Rasier, LLC, and Rasier-CA, LLC.(“Defendants”) filed the instant motion to compel a neuropsychological independent medical examination (“IME”) of Plaintiff Patria Diaz. On September 9, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On September 17, 2026, Defendants filed a reply.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

“Any party may obtain discovery . . . by means of a physical or mental examination of (1) a party to the action, (2) an agent of any party, or (3) a natural person in the custody or under the legal control of a party, in any action in which the mental or physical condition … of that party or other person is in controversy in the action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.020, subd. (a).) 

In a personal injury action, the defendant may demand one physical examination of plaintiff as of right, without advance leave of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.220.) 

If a defendant seeks a further physical examination of plaintiff, or a mental examination, the defendant must first file a motion and “obtain leave of court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.310, subd. (a).) Such a motion must “specify the time, place, manner, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination, as well as the identity and the specialty, if any, of the person or persons who will perform the examination.” (Id., subd. (b).) The court may grant such a motion “only for good cause shown.” (Id., § 2032.320, subd. (a).) A showing of good cause generally requires “that the party produce specific facts justifying discovery and that the inquiry be relevant to the subject matter of the action or reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” (Vinson v. Super. Ct. (1987) 43 Cal.3d 833, 840.) 

The moving party must support the motion with a meet and confer declaration. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.310, subd. (b).) A meet and confer declaration must state facts “showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.) 

The examination will be limited to whatever condition is “in controversy” in the action.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.020, subd. (a).) “An order granting a physical or mental examination shall specify the person or persons who may perform the examination, as well as the time, place, manner, diagnostic tests and procedures, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.320, subd. (d).) “The court is to describe¿in detail¿who will conduct the examination, where and when it will be conducted, the conditions, scope and nature of the examination, and the diagnostic tests and procedures to be employed. The way to describe these ‘diagnostic tests and procedures’—fully and in detail—is to list them by name.” (Carpenter v. Super. Ct. (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 249, 260.).

III.      ANALYSIS

As a preliminary matter, the Court notes that Plaintiff filed objections to the new evidence and arguments submitted with Defendants’ reply. Plaintiff requests that the Court strike  or disregard the challenged materials, or alternatively, permit Plaintiff to file a sur-reply. The Court declines to do so. 

Defendants move for an order compelling Plaintiff to submit to a neuropsychological examination with neuropsychologist Dr. Kyle Boone, Ph.D. Defendants contend the examination is warranted because Plaintiff alleges she suffers and continues to suffer from cognitive deficits and injuries as a result of the accident. (Motion, pp. 1-2.) In opposition, Plaintiff argues the motion should be denied on procedural grounds because Defendants failed to adequately meet and confer, failed to file a separate statement, and failed to specify a date for the examination. (Opposition, pp. 6-7.)

As to the procedural objections, the Court declines to deny the motion on these grounds. The parties’ respective positions and the conditions in dispute are sufficiently presented in the papers, and, in the interest of judicial economy, the Court elects to reach the merits of the motion.

As to the merits, there is no dispute that Plaintiff’s neuropsychological condition is in controversy or that good cause exists for an examination. Plaintiff alleges she suffered a traumatic brain injury resulting in continuing cognitive symptoms and expressly states in opposition that she does not oppose a neuropsychological examination by Dr. Boone. (Opposition, p.4.) 

Thus, the issue before the Court is not whether the examination should occur, but the manner and conditions under which the examination will proceed. The Court addresses the disputed conditions as follows:

Tests administered

Plaintiff argues that any order must identify the specific tests Dr. Boone is permitted to administer. Defendants respond that their notice identified each test from which Dr. Boone may select. (Reply, pp. 5-6.) The Court finds that Dr. Boone may administer some or all of the tests identified in Defendants’ notice as clinically appropriate. The examination shall be limited to the identified tests.

Audio recordings and raw data

Plaintiffs argue the entire examination must be audio recorded. Plaintiffs request that the Court require Dr. Boone to make a continuous audio recording of the entire examination including testing and provide Plaintiff’s counsel an unedited copy. (Opposition, pp.8-9.) The Court finds Plaintiffs’ requests reasonable. Thus, the entire mental examination, including the testing portion, shall be audio recorded.

As to raw data, Plaintiff requests that the raw testing data be produced directly to Plaintiff’s counsel subject to a protective order. (Opposition, pp. 9-11.) Defendants oppose direct disclosure to counsel and contend that the raw testing materials should be disclosed only to a licensed psychologist or neuropsychologist retained by Plaintiff due to concerns regarding test security. (Reply, p. 11.)

The Court finds that the raw testing data and audio recording may be produced directly to Plaintiff's counsel subject to an appropriate protective order. As recognized in Randy's Trucking, Inc. v. Superior Court (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 818, the Court has discretion to order disclosure of neuropsychological testing materials directly to counsel while imposing appropriate safeguards to protect the confidentiality and integrity of the testing materials.

Thus, Dr. Boone shall produce the raw testing data and audio recording to Plaintiff’s counsel subject to a protective order.

History 

Plaintiff argues that Dr. Boone should be permitted to obtain clinically relevant history necessary to conduct the neuropsychological examination but should not be permitted to use the examination as an unrestricted or duplicative deposition. (Opposition, pp. 11-12.) The Court finds that Dr. Boone may inquire into Plaintiff’s history to the extent reasonably related to Plaintiff’s claimed injuries and the conditions in controversy, excluding any inquiry into attorney-client communications or litigation strategy.

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS Defendants’ motion in part, subject to the conditions set forth above. The parties are ordered to meet and confer regarding a mutually agreeable date for the examination.

IV.       CONCLUSION AND ORDER

The Court GRANTS Defendants’ motion in part subject to the conditions set forth above. The Parties are ordered to meet and confer regarding a mutually agreeable date for the examination.

Defendants are to give notice.

Case Number: 25NNCV07850    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING DEFENDANTS’ DEMURRER AND GRANTING MOTION TO STRIKE

I.         INTRODUCTION

            Plaintiffs Lincoln Chan and Linda Thi Nguyen (collectively, "Plaintiffs") bring this action against Defendants Donna Milosavljevic, individually and as Trustee of the Milosavljevic Trust, and David Milosavljevic (collectively, "Defendants") arising out of a residential tenancy at property owned and/or managed by Defendants. The Complaint asserts causes of action for (1) Private Nuisance, (2) Breach of Warranty of Habitability, (3) Negligence, (4) Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing, (5) Breach of the Implied Covenant of Quiet Enjoyment, (6) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress, (7) Negligent Infliction of Emotional Distress, (8) Violation of Civil Code section 1946.2, (9) Violation of AB 1482, (10) Violation of Civil Code section 1950.5, (11) Unjust Enrichment, and (12) Breach of Contract.

On June 18, 2026, the Court sustained Defendants’ demurrer in part, including as to Plaintiffs’ causes of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress (“IIED”) and negligent infliction of emotional distress (“NIED”), with leave to amend.

On July 20, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”). On August 19, 2026, Defendants filed the instant demurrer to the FAC and motion to strike. Plaintiffs filed an opposition on September 10, 2026, and Defendants filed a reply on September 18, 2026.

Meet and confer

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a), the demurring party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading at least five days before the responsive pleading is due. This must occur in person, telephone or by video conference with the aim of resolving any objections raised in the demurrer. Additionally, the demurring party must file and serve a declaration detailing the meet and confer efforts. It should be noted, however, that the failure to meet and confer is not itself grounds for sustaining or overruling a demurrer or granting or denying a motion to strike. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a)(4), 435.5, subd. (a)(4).)

On July 20, 2026, the parties engaged in a telephonic meet and confer regarding the issues raised in the demurrer but were unable to reach a resolution. (Weissman Decl., ¶ 3.) Thus, meet and confer requirements are met.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.) 

CCP section 430.10(f) provides that a pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous and unintelligible. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(f).) “A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) “A demurrer for uncertainty will be sustained only where the complaint is so bad that defendant cannot reasonably respond—i.e., he or she cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him or her.” (Weil & Brown, Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group) § 7:85 (emphasis in original).) “The objection of uncertainty does not go to the failure to allege sufficient facts.” (Brea v. McGlashan (1934) 3 Cal.App.2d 454, 459.) “It goes to the doubt as to what the pleader means by the facts alleged.” (Id.) “Such a demurrer should not be sustained where the allegations of the complaint are sufficiently clear to apprise the defendant of the issues which he is to meet.” (People v. Lim (1941) 18 Cal.2d 872, 882.)  

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

Further, the court may, upon motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false, or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.) 

Civ. Code §3294: 

(a) In an action for the breach of an obligation not arising from contract, where it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice, the plaintiff, in addition to the actual damages, may recover damages for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant. 

(c) As used in this section, the following definitions shall apply: 

(1) “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others. 

(2) “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights. 

(3) “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.

III.      ANALYSIS

Fifth and Sixth causes of action: IIED and NIED

Defendants demur to these causes of actions for IIED and NIED, arguing that Plaintiffs fail to allege extreme and outrageous conduct sufficient to support their emotional distress claims. (Demurrer, pp. 6-7.)

As to NIED, Defendants additionally argue that this cause of action is predicated largely on Plaintiff’s allegation of a “special relationship” between Plaintiffs and Defendants giving rise to a heightened duty of care. (FAC ¶ 154.) Defendants contend that the landlord-tenant relationship does not create such a heightened duty. (Id.)

“The elements of a prima facie case for the tort of intentional infliction of emotional distress are: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct. Conduct to be outrageous must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.” (Wilson v. Hynek (2012) 207 Cal.App.4th 999, 1009, citation and ellipses omitted.) 

The law of negligent infliction of emotional distress in California is typically analyzed by reference to two theories of recovery: the ‘bystander’ theory and the ‘direct victim’ theory. The negligent causing of emotional distress is not an independent tort, but the tort of negligence. The traditional elements of duty, breach of duty, causation, and damages apply. Whether a defendant owes a duty of care is a question of law.” (Spates v. Dameron Hosp. Ass’n (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 208, 213, ellipses, quotation marks, brackets, and paragraph breaks omitted.) 

The Court previously sustained Defendants’ demurrer to these causes of action with leave to amend. In the FAC, Plaintiffs now allege that Defendants wrongfully terminated their tenancy during their children’s school year and improperly withheld their security deposit. (FAC, ¶¶ 129-130, 141.) Specifically, Plaintiffs allege Defendants stated that the property was being sold, but shortly after Plaintiffs moved out, Defendants instead rented the property at a higher rate. (FAC, ¶¶ 131-140.) Plaintiffs further allege Defendants improperly withheld portions of their security deposit. (FAC, ¶¶ 141-142.)

As to IIED, the Court finds these additional allegations insufficient to constitute extreme and outrageous conduct. Even accepting as true that Defendants falsely represented that they intended to sell the property, terminated Plaintiffs tenancy, rented the property at a higher rate, failed to provide relocation assistance, and improperly withhold portions of the security deposit, the alleged conduct is not so extreme as to exceed all bounds usually tolerated in a civilized community. (Wilson, supra, 207 Cal.App.4th at p. 1009.)

As for NIED, Plaintiffs allege that Defendants owed them a heightened duty of care based upon a “special relationship” arising during their tenancy. (FAC¶154.) Plaintiffs allege Defendants knew or should have known that wrongfully terminating their tenancy and withholding their security deposit would cause severe emotional distress. (FAC, ¶¶ 155–178.)

The Court likewise finds that these allegations are insufficient. NIED is not an independent tort, but rather a form of negligence requiring the traditional elements of duty, breach, causation, and damages. (Spates, supra, 114 Cal.App.4th at p. 213.) In Gonzales v. Personal Storage, Inc. (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 464, the court held that a landlord-tenant relationship did not itself give rise to a duty to protect the tenant from emotional distress. (Id. at pp. 474-475.) Thus, Plaintiffs fail to sufficiently allege the duty necessary to support their NIED claim.

Given that Plaintiffs expressly state in opposition that they “do not have any additional facts to plead” (Opposition., p. 2), the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer to the fifth and sixth causes of action without leave to amend.

Motion to Strike

Defendants moves to strike the old plumbing/cockroach allegations in FAC ¶¶ 5, 6, 18, and 40-59; treble/punitive damages language from ¶¶ 88 and 103; the last sentence of ¶ 104; ¶ 118; the last sentence of ¶ 154 in the NIED cause of action; and paragraphs 7, 8, and 12 of the prayer.  (Notice, p.2.)

Given that the Court sustains the demurrer as to the fifth and sixth causes of action without leave, the request to strike the last sentence of ¶ 154 is now MOOT.

As to the remaining portions of the motion, the Court finds as follows. Cockroach/plumbing allegations

Defendants move to strike paragraphs 5, 6, 18, and 40-59 arguing these allegations concern the plumbing and cockroach conditions underlying claims that Plaintiffs previously abandoned are therefore no longer relevant. The Court agrees. As the Court previously found, Plaintiffs expressly abandoned their claims arising from the alleged cockroach infestation and plumbing conditions. Thus, these allegations are no longer relevant to Plaintiffs’ claims and are properly stricken.

Thus, the motion to strike references to the cockroach infestation and plumbing conditions in paragraphs 5, 6, 18, and 40-59 is GRANTED.

Punitive/treble damages

Defendants next move to strike Plaintiffs allegations and prayer for treble and punitive damages, arguing the FAC fails to allege sufficient facts showing malice, oppression or fraud. (Motion, pp. 4-8.)

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that Civil Code section 1946.2, subdivision (h), expressly permits an award of treble and punitive damages where the statutory requirements are satisfied. Specifically, section 1946.2, subdivision (h)(1)(C) permits recovery of up to three times actual damages “[u]pon a showing that the owner has acted willfully or with oppression, fraud, or malice.”

Here, the Court previously found that the original Complaint did not allege sufficient facts showing malice, oppression, or fraud. The FAC does not sufficiently cure this deficiency. Although Plaintiffs now allege that Defendants willfully terminated Plaintiffs’ tenancy under the stated ground that the property was being sold despite having no intention of selling the property, and thereafter re-rented the property at a higher rate (FAC, ¶¶ 99-104), the underlying conduct is substantially the same as previously alleged. The original Complaint already alleged that Defendants misrepresented their reason for terminating the tenancy, re-rented the property at a higher rate, and wrongfully withheld portions of Plaintiffs’ security deposit.

Thus, the Court finds the additional allegations insufficient to support Plaintiffs’ claims for treble or punitive damages. The motion to strike is GRANTED.

IV.       ORDER

            The Court SUSTAINS the demurrer to the Sixth and Seventh Causes of Action without leave to amend.

            The Court further GRANTS the motion to strike.

Defendant is to give notice.
