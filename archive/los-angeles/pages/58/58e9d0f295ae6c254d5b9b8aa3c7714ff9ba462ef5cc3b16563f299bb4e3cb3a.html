Background

 

This is a breach of contract action arising out of plaintiffs Anna Choung’s (“Choung”) and ACPC Clayton, Inc.’s (“ACPC”) (together, “Plaintiffs”) claim to a 50-percent ownership and co-management interest in an accounting enterprise operated under the “Avano” brand, which Plaintiffs allege they jointly established with defendant Jesse Lin (“Lin”) and his management entity, defendant Palmerston, LLC (“Palmerston”) (together, the “Lin Defendants”), through two California limited liability companies, defendant Avano, LLC and defendant Avano Tax, LLC (collectively, “Avano”).  Plaintiffs allege that, on May 22, 2025, Lin delivered an ultimatum demanding Choung accept a restructuring of the partnership on terms that would strip her of management rights and reduce her economic interest below the agreed 50-50 split. Plaintiffs allege Choung rejected the proposal, and Lin Defendants thereafter unilaterally locked her out of all Avano platforms and systems. 

On May 29, 2025, Plaintiffs filed their complaint, alleging  causes of action for (1) breach of oral contract (against the Lin Defendants), (2) breach of implied contract (against the Lin Defendants), (3) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing (against the Lin Defendants), (4) breach of fiduciary duty (against the Lin Defendants), (5) conversion (against the Lin Defendants), (6) dissolution of general partnership (against all Defendants), (7) dissolution of limited liability companies (against all Defendants), (8) accounting (against the Lin Defendants), (9) declaratory relief (against the Lin Defendants) and (10) injunctive relief (against the Lin Defendants).

On July 2, 2025, the Lin Defendants filed a cross-complaint, alleging causes of action against Plaintiffs, Avano and Roes 1-20 for (1) breach of oral contract, (2) breach of implied contract, (3) breach of fiduciary duty, (4) breach of covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (5) conversion, (6) civil theft, (7) restitution, (8) declaratory relief and (9) injunctive relief.

Choung moves to compel Lin’s further responses to Requests for Admission, Set One, Requests for Production, Set One, Special Interrogatories, Set One, and Form Interrogatories, Set One.  Lin opposes the motion.

LEGAL STANDARDS 

A.    Requests for Admission

California law requires a responding party to respond to each propounded request for admission with either a substantive answer or an objection to the particular request. (Code Civ. Proc. §2033.210, subd. (b).) Each substantive answer must: “(1) Admit so much of the matter involved in the request as is true, either as expressed in the request itself or as reasonably and clearly qualified by the responding party; (2) Deny so much of the matter involved in the request as is untrue; (3) Specify so much of the matter involved in the request as to the truth of which the responding party lacks sufficient information or knowledge.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2033.220, subd. (b).) “If a responding party gives lack of information or knowledge as a reason for a failure to admit all or part of a request for admission, that party shall state in the answer that a reasonable inquiry concerning the matter in the particular request has been made, and that the information known or readily obtainable is insufficient to enable that party to admit the matter.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2033.220, subd. (c).) 

A propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses to requests for admission if any of the following apply: “(1) An answer to a particular request is evasive or incomplete; or (2) An objection to a particular request is without merit or too general.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2033.290, subd. (a).) 

B.    Requests for Production

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.310, the Court may order a responding party to serve a further response to a request for production when the Court finds that (1) a statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete; (2) a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive; or (3) an objection in the response is without merit or too general.¿   

To prevail, the party moving for the order must first offer specific facts demonstrating “good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) This burden “is met simply by a fact-specific showing of relevance.” (TBG Ins. Servs. Corp. v. Superior Court (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.)  If “good cause” is shown by the moving party, the burden shifts to the responding party to justify any objections made to document disclosure. (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98.)¿ “A representation of inability to comply with the particular demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand. This statement shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of the responding party. The statement shall set forth the name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.230.)¿¿ 

C.    Interrogatories

A party to whom interrogatories are served must respond in writing and under oath by: (1) an answer containing the information sought to be discovered; (2) an exercise of the party’s option to produce writings; or (3) an objection to the particular interrogatory. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.210, subds. (a)(1)-(3).) “Each answer in response to interrogatories shall be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220, subd. (a).) “If an interrogatory cannot be answered completely, it shall be answered to the extent possible.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220, subd. (b).) “If the responding party does not have personal knowledge sufficient to respond fully to the interrogatory, that party shall so state, but shall make a reasonable and good faith effort to obtain the information by inquiry to other natural persons or organizations, except where the information is equally available to the propounding party.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220, subd. (c).)¿¿¿   

A party may move for an order compelling a further response to interrogatories where: (1) an answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete; (2) an exercise of the option to produce documents under Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.230 is unwarranted or the required specification of those documents is inadequate; or (3) an objection is without merit or is too general. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.300, subds. (a)(1)-(3).) 

MOTION TO Compel further responses

 

I.                Objections

 

Choung submits two evidentiary objections to the Declaration of Shauna W. Avrith filed in support of Lin’s Opposition to Motion to Compel Further Responses to Requests for Admission, Set One.  Plaintiff’s objections are overruled.

 

Choung submits two evidentiary objections to the Declaration of Shauna W. Avrith filed in support of Lin’s Opposition to Motion to Compel Further Responses to Form Interrogatories, Set One.  Plaintiff’s objections are overruled.

 

II.             Discussion

 

Choung moves the Court for an order compelling Lin to serve further verified, Code-compliant responses to Choung’s Form Interrogatories, Set One (“FROGs”), Special Interrogatories, Set One (“SROGs”), Requests for Production, Set One (“RPDs”), and Requests for Admission, Set One (“RFAs”).  Additionally, Choung requests monetary sanctions in the total amount of $20,212.40 against Lin and his counsel of record, jointly and severally, per motion.

 

A.              Requests for Admission

 

Choung argues Lin’s responses to RFAs Nos. 9, 11, 12, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 27, 28, 30, 32, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 65, 70, 73, 76, 79, 82, 83, 84, 85, 86, 87, 88, 89, 90, 91, 92, 93, 94, 95, 96, 101, 102, 113, 114, 115, 116, 117, 118, 119, 120, 121, 122, 123, 124, and 137, are evasive and incomplete, and that the objections are without merit.  

 

In her reply, Choung withdraws the motion as to Nos. 12, 43, 65, 70, 73, 76, 79, 101, 102, 117, and 137 (Rep., p. 2.); as such, the RFAs remaining in dispute are Nos. 9, 11, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 27, 28, 30, 32, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 44, 82, 83, 84, 85, 86, 87, 88, 89, 90, 91, 92, 93, 94, 95, 96, 113, 114, 115, 116, 118, 119, 120, 121, 122, 123, and 124.

 

i.                 RFAs Nos. 9, 11, 19- 23, 27, 28, 30, 32, 37-42, 44, 82-96, 113-116, 118-124

 

Lin objects to these requests on the grounds that “Avano Enterprise” is argumentative and contrived for purposes of litigation, and that the request is vague, ambiguous, and unintelligible. Lin further responds that he is unable to admit or deny each request as framed. Additionally, Lin objects on the grounds the requests assume facts and calls for a legal conclusion. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Lin’s responses are insufficient and his objections are meritless. A party who asserts lack of information or knowledge as a reason for failing to admit must state in the answer “that a reasonable inquiry concerning the matter in the particular request has been made, and that the information known or readily obtainable is insufficient to enable that party to admit the matter.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2033.220, subd. (c).)  Lin’s responses fail to include a reasonable inquiry statement, and his supplemental responses do not cure the defect. 

 

Further, Lin’s objection that the term “Avano Enterprise” is argumentative and contrived cannot be reconciled with the fact that Lin admitted or denied dozens of other requests using the identical term.  Additionally, Lin’s objections on the grounds of vague and ambiguous are meritless.  A party responding to a discovery request is obligated to attempt a good-faith response even where the request contains some ambiguity.  (Deyo v. Kilbourne (1975) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 783.) 

 

Finally, objections on the grounds the requests call for legal conclusions are inapplicable.  “Any party may obtain discovery … by a written request that any other party to the action admit the genuineness of specified documents, or the truth of specified matters of fact, opinion relating to fact, or application of law to fact. A request for admission may relate to a matter that is in controversy between the parties." (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.010.)  These requests are properly related to the duties the parties owed and the propriety of dissolution.

 

Accordingly, Choung’s motion to compel further responses is granted as to RFA Nos. 9, 11, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 27, 28, 30, 32, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 44, 82, 83, 84, 85, 86, 87, 88, 89, 90, 91, 92, 93, 94, 95, 96, 113, 114, 115, 116, 118, 119, 120, 121, 122, 123, and 124.  Lin must provide verified Code-compliant responses to these RFAs. 

 

ii.               Sanctions

 

Choung seeks sanctions in the amount of $6,072.10 against Lin and his counsel of record, jointly and severally, consisting of 5.90 hours of attorney time at an hourly rate of $1,019 per hour and a $60.00 filing fee. (Lau Decl., ¶ 21.)  The Court finds the hourly rate unreasonable. While Choung’s counsel cites the Laffey Matrix for attorneys with 11-19 years of experience having a rate of $1,019, counsel does not state his experience in his declaration, and also fails to explain why an attorney with less experience and a lower hourly rate could not have prepared the motions.  Further, the hours requested are unreasonable, as there are four motions that make similar arguments.

 

Based on the Court’s experience, the Court awards monetary sanctions in the reduced amount of $2,460.00, consisting of four hours of attorney time at an hourly rate of $600.00, and the $60.00 filing fee, against Lin and his counsel jointly and severally. Lin’s request for sanctions is denied. 

 

 

B.              Requests for Production

 

Choung argues Lin’s responses to RPD Nos. 8, 9, 14, 16, 17, 18, 19, 22, and 23, are evasive and incomplete, and that the objections are meritless.

 

i.       RPD Nos. 8, 9, 14, 16, 19, 23

 

Lin objects to these requests on the grounds that the phrase “Avano Enterprise” is argumentative and contrived, and the request is vague, ambiguous, and unintelligible as framed.  Lin further objects on the grounds the requests prematurely call for disclosure of expert opinion and calls for production of documents in Choung’s possession. (Separate Statement, pp. 4-7, 11-12.) 

 

Lin’s supplemental responses now supply Code-compliant answers for Nos. 8, 16, 19, and 23, with representations of Lin’s inability to comply and affirmation that a diligent search and reasonable inquiry have been made. (Avrith Decl., ¶ 10, Ex. H.)  For Nos. 9 and 14, Lin’s supplemental responses include statements of compliance identifying responsive documents by Bates number. (Avrith Decl., ¶ 10, Ex. H.)  Therefore, no further responses are required.

 

Accordingly, Choung’s motion to compel further is denied as to RPDs Nos. 8, 9, 14, 16, 19, and 23.

 

ii.     RPD No. 22

 

Lin objects on the grounds the request is vague, ambiguous, unintelligible, and compound. (Separate Statement, p. 12.)

 

Here, Lin’s response is insufficient.  Choung has shown good cause.  Each demand is relevant to the lockout, the ownership dispute, the alleged diversion of funds through related entities, and the dissolution claim. If “good cause” is shown by the moving party, the burden shifts to the responding party to justify any objections made to document disclosure. (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98.)¿ Lin has not done so here.

 

Additionally, Lin fails to affirm a diligent search and reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with Choung’s request.  Further, Lin’s objections on the grounds of vagueness and ambiguity are meritless.  A party responding to a discovery request is obligated to attempt a good-faith response even where the request contains some ambiguity.  (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 783.)  Further, the request is not compound, as it seeks documents related to a single subject.

 

Accordingly, Choung’s motion to compel further is granted as to RPD No. 22. 

 

iii.             RPD Nos. 17, 18

 

Lin objects on the grounds the phrase “Avano Enterprise” is argumentative and contrived for purposes of litigation, that the request prematurely calls for disclosure of expert opinion and calls for production of documents in Choung’s possession. Lin further responds he has no documents in his possession, custody, or control responsive to this request.  (Separate Statement, pp. 8-10.)

 

Lin’s supplemental responses are Code-compliant and agree to produce all documents responsive to these demands. (Avrith Decl., ¶ 10, Ex. H.)  However, Lin’s response reflects a future promise and not completed production.  To the extent production has not been completed, Lin shall complete production and serve a verified statement of compliance confirming production is complete within 30 days of this Ruling.

 

Accordingly, Choung’s motion to compel further is granted as to RPDs Nos. 17 and 18.

 

iv.             Sanctions

 

Choung seeks sanctions in the amount of $4,339.80 against Lin and his counsel of record, jointly and severally, consisting of 4.20 hours of attorney time at an hourly rate of $1,019 per hour and a $60.00 filing fee. (Lau Decl., ¶ 21.)  The Court finds the hourly rate unreasonable. While Choung’s counsel cites the Laffey Matrix for attorneys with 11-19 years of experience having a rate of $1,019, counsel does not state his experience in his declaration or explain why an attorney with a lower hourly rate could not have prepared the motion papers.  Further, the hours requested are unreasonable, as there are four motions that make similar arguments.

 

Based on the Court’s experience, the Court awards monetary sanctions in the reduced amount of $1,860.00, consisting of three hours of attorney time at an hourly rate of $600.00, and the $60.00 filing fee, against Lin and his counsel jointly and severally. Lin’s request for sanctions is denied. 

 

C.              Form Interrogatories

 

Choung argues Lin’s responses to FROGs Nos. 2.11, 2.12, 12.6, 14.2, 15.1, 17.1 are evasive and incomplete, and that Defendant’s objections are meritless.  In reply, Plaintiff withdrew the motion as to No. 12.6. (Rep., p. 2.)

 

i.                 FROGs Nos. 2.11, 2.12, 14.2

 

Lin objected to each interrogatory on the grounds it was vague, ambiguous, and unintelligible as to the term “INCIDENT”. Lin further objects on the grounds the interrogatories call for information that is neither relevant or reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence and was overly broad. (Separate Statement, pp. 4-6.)

 

Lin’s vague and ambiguous objections are meritless. A party responding to a discovery request is obligated to attempt a good-faith response even where there is some ambiguity. (See Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 783.)  Requests relating to the “incident” are not so vague and ambiguous that Lin is unable to attempt a good-faith response.  

 

Accordingly, Choung’s motion to compel further is granted as to FROGs Nos. 2.11, 2.12, and 14.2.

 

ii.               FROGs No. 15.1

 

Lin objects to this interrogatory on the grounds it is overbroad, unduly burdensome, and harassing, and premature given discovery has just begun.  (Separate Statement, p. 7.)

 

Lin’s objections are meritless and his response is insufficient.  The Court finds the interrogatory is not overbroad.  Further, Lin’s answer to subpart (b) is answered only by cross-reference, and subpart (c), as well as Lin’s supplemental response, fails to answer the interrogatory in its entirety. 

 

Accordingly, Choung’s motion to compel further is granted as to FROGs No. 15.1.

 

 

iii.             FROGs No. 17.1

 

Lin objects to this interrogatory as overbroad, unduly burdensome, oppressive, and a misuse of the discovery process, based on the propounded 140 RFAs. (Separate Statement, p. 10.)  

 

On their face, Lin’s responses are materially incomplete.  This interrogatory seeks information regarding unqualified admissions in response to Choung’s requests for admission.  Lin gave non-admission responses to roughly 100 requests but addressed only about 30 in his response. (See Separate Statement, pp. 10-17.)

 

Lin’s objection on the ground of undue burden is not well taken.  An objection grounded in burden must be “sustained by evidence showing the quantum of work required,” and the objecting party must supply that evidence. (See Williams v. Superior Court (2027) 3 Cal. 5th 531, 549.) Lin did not submit evidence showing the work required to respond to Choung’s interrogatories. 

 

Accordingly, Choung’s motion to compel further is granted as to FROGs No. 17.1.

 

iv.             Sanctions

 

Choung seeks sanctions in the amount of $5,868.30 against Lin and his counsel of record, jointly and severally, consisting of 5.70 hours of attorney time at an hourly rate of $1,019 per hour and a $60.00 filing fee. (Lau Decl., ¶ 21.)  The Court finds the hourly rate unreasonable. While Choung’s counsel cites the Laffey Matrix for attorneys with 11-19 years of experience having a rate of $1,019, counsel does not state his experience in his declaration or explain why an attorney with a lower hourly rate could not prepare the motion papers.  Further, the hours requested are unreasonable, as there are four motions that make similar arguments.

 

Based on the Court’s experience, the Court awards monetary sanctions in the reduced amount of $1,860.00, consisting of three hours of attorney time at an hourly rate of $600.00, and the $60.00 filing fee, against Lin and his counsel jointly and severally. Lin’s request for sanctions is denied. 

 

 

D.              Special Interrogatories

 

Choung argues Lin’s responses to SROGs Nos. 30, 34, 48, 49, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54, and 55, are evasive and incomplete, and that Defendant’s objections are meritless.  

 

i.                 SROGs Nos. 30, 34

 

Lin objects to these interrogatories on the grounds the phrase “Avano Enterprise” is argumentative and contrived, and that it calls for information within Choung’s knowledge. (Separate Statement, pp. 3-4.)  Lin further responded he will produce all non-privileged documents in his possession, custody, or control responsive to the request. (Separate Statement, pp. 4, 5.)

 

Lin’s response is evasive and incomplete. First, as discussed above, Lin’s objection that “Avano Enterprise” is argumentative and contrived is not reconciled with the fact Lin has responded to other discovery requests including this term. Further, Lin did not identify any responsive documents, only responding he would produce documents. A promise to produce documents is not the identification the interrogatory requires. 

 

Accordingly, Choung’s motion to compel further responses is granted as to SROGS Nos. 30 and 34.

 

ii.               SROGs Nos. 48-51

 

Lin objects to No. 48 for prematurely calling for disclosure of expert opinion. Lin further responds damages estimates will be disclosed after Lin has had adequate time to conduct discovery and investigation. Lin incorporates No. 48 into his responses of Nos. 49-51. (Separate Statement, pp. 5-8.)

 

A party may not defer entirely to a future calculation; it must answer to the extent of the information reasonably available and cannot plead ignorance to information obtainable from sources under its control. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.220, subds. (a)–(c); Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 782.)  Further, the objections on the ground of premature disclosure of expert opinion are inapplicable here.  “An interrogatory is not objectionable because an answer to it involves an opinion or contention that relates to fact or the application of law to fact or would be based on information obtained or legal theories developed in anticipation of litigation or in preparation for trial.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.010, subd. (b).) 

 

Accordingly, Choung’s motion to compel further responses is granted as to SROGS Nos. 48-51.

 

iii.             SROGs Nos. 52-55

 

Lin objects to No. 52 on the grounds the interrogatory is vague, ambiguous, unintelligible, and compound. Lin cites to No. 52 in response to Nos. 53-55.  (Separate Statement, p. 8-11.)

 

As discussed above, the objections on the ground of vague, ambiguous, unintelligible are inapplicable here, under Deyo.  Further, the objection on the ground of compound is inapplicable, as each interrogatory seeks a single subject.

 

Accordingly, Choung’s motion to compel further responses is granted as to SROGS Nos. 52-55.

 

iv.             Sanctions

 

Choung seeks sanctions in the amount of $3,932.20 against Lin and his counsel of record, jointly and severally, consisting of 3.80 hours of attorney time at an hourly rate of $1,019 per hour and a $60.00 filing fee. While Choung’s counsel cites the Laffey Matrix for attorneys with 11-19 years of experience having a rate of $1,019, counsel does not state his experience in his declaration or explain why an attorney with less experience and a lower hourly rate could not prepare the motion papers.  Further, the hours requested are unreasonable, as there are four motions that make similar arguments.

 

Based on the Court’s experience, the Court awards monetary sanctions in the reduced amount of $1,260.00, consisting of 2 hours of attorney time at an hourly rate of $600.00, and the $60.00 filing fee, against Lin and his counsel jointly and severally. Lin’s request for sanctions is denied.

CASE NAME:                        David L. Lim, et al. vs. Yolette Vasquez

 

MOVING PARTY:                 Defendant Yolette Vasquez

                                               

OPPOSING PARTY:             Plaintiffs David L. Lim and Chery C. Lim, as Trustees of the Lim Family Trust dated 1/14/99

PROCEEDING:                      MOTION TO SET ASIDE/VACATE DEFAULT, DEFAULT JUDGMENT

RULING SUMMARY:          Defendant’s Motion to Set Aside default and default judgment is denied.

                                                                                                                                                           

 

Background

 

On March 7, 2025, David L. Lim and Chery C. Lim, as Trustees of the Lim Family Trust dated 1/14/99 (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed an Unlawful Detainer Complaint against Yolette Vasquez (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 25.

 

On July 11, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging causes of action for (1) breach of lease; (2) quantum meruit; (3) open book account; and (4) account stated.   

On December 2, 2025, the Court entered Default Judgment against Defendant in the total of $83,060.08.

 

Defendant moves to set aside and/or vacate the Default Judgment. Plaintiffs oppose the Motion.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

Plaintiffs request that the Court take judicial notice of: (1) the Grant Deed to the property located at “6027, 6027 ½, 6029 Ferguson Dr., Los Angeles, CA 90022 (APN 6338-022-021) recorded in the official records of the Recorder’s Office of Los Angeles County, California on March 21, 2023 as Document No. 20230180507 and requested by Luis Reyes Vasquez;” and (2) the Grant Deed for the property located at 6031 Ferguson Dr., Los Angeles, CA 90022 (APN 6338-022-018) recorded in the official records of the Recorder’s Office of Los Angeles County, California on March 21, 2023 as Document No. 20230180521 and requested by Luis Reyes Vasquez. (RJN ¶¶ 1-2; Chang Decl., Exs. E-F.)  (Evid. Code § 452, subds. (c), (h).)

 

Defendant objects only to the use of these records to establish disputed factual conclusion. (Response to RJN at p. 1.)

 

The request is granted. (Evid. Code § 452, subds. (c), (h); see Kalnoki v. First American Trustee Servicing Solutions, LLC (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 23, 37.) The Court takes judicial notice of these documents only as to “the existence, content and authenticity of public records and other specified documents;” it does not take judicial notice of the truth of any factual matters asserted in those documents. (Dominguez v. Bonta (2022) 87 Cal. App. 5th 389, 400.)

 

LEGAL STANDARDS

 

“The court may, upon motion of the injured party, or its own motion, correct clerical mistakes in its judgment or orders as entered, so as to conform to the judgment or order directed, and may, on motion of either party after notice to the other party, set aside any void judgment or order.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (d).) A judgment is void when service was improper.  (County of San Diego v. Gorham (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1226.)  Knowledge of the action by a defendant does not satisfy the requirement of adequate service.  (Ibid.) 

“When service of a summons was lawfully effected but has not resulted in actual notice to a party in time to defend the action and a default or default judgment has been entered against the party in the action, the party may serve and file a notice of motion to set aside the default or default judgment and for leave to defend the action.  The notice of motion shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of: (i) two years after entry of a default judgment against the party; or (ii) 180 days after service on the party of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5, subd. (a).)

“A notice of motion to set aside a default or default judgment and for leave to defend the action shall designate as the time for making the motion a date prescribed by subdivision (b) of Section 1005, and it shall be accompanied by an affidavit showing under oath that the party’s lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by the party’s avoidance of service or inexcusable neglect. The party shall serve and file with the notice a copy of the answer, motion, or other pleading proposed to be filed in the action.” (Id., subd. (b).)

“Upon a finding by the court that the motion was made within the period permitted by subdivision (a) and that the party’s lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by the party’s avoidance of service or inexcusable neglect, it may set aside the default or default judgment on whatever terms as may be just and allow the party to defend the action.” (Id., subd. (c).)

DISCUSSION

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 473, subdivision (d) and 473.5, Defendant moves the Court to set aside and/or vacate the Default Judgment on the grounds that the judgment is void due to lack of valid service of summons and complaint.

¿ 

Defendant argues that the Proof of Service falsely states that she was served at 6031 Ferguson Drive, Los Angeles, CA 90022, because she has never resided at this address. (Mot., at p.1.)  Defendant asserts that the address is not associated with her driver’s license, and because she was not personally served, she has not had an opportunity to appear. (Vasquez Decl., ¶¶ 6-7.)  ¿¿ 

 

As a preliminary issue, the Court notes that Defendant has failed to file a copy of a proposed pleading, in violation of the provisions in Code of Civil Procedure section 473.5, subdivision (b). Accordingly, Defendant has failed to properly move under this section.

 

Plaintiffs argue in opposition that Defendant’s motion also fails under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d), because Defendant has failed to overcome the rebuttable presumption of valid service. (Opp., at p. 5.)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 415.10 provides that a summons may be served by personal delivery of a copy of the summons and of the complaint to the person to be served.

 

The filing of a proof of service creates a rebuttable presumption that the service was proper, provided that the proof complies with the statutory requirements. (Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1441.) These proofs of service must also include the county in which the process server is registered and the process server’s registration number. (Code Civ. Proc., § 417.40; Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 22350, 22360.)  Finally, the proofs of service must be filed with the court clerk when defendants have not made a general appearance in this lawsuit. (Code Civ. Proc., § 417.30.)  In addition, under Evidence Code section 647, return of a registered process server upon process “establishes a presumption, affecting the burden of producing evidence, of the facts stated in the return.” 

 

Plaintiffs filed a Proof of Service on September 8, 2025, that indicated personal service was effected at 6031 Ferguson Drive, Los Angeles, CA 90022 (“6031 Property”) on September 1, 2025, at 4:03 PM. (Chang Decl., Ex. B.) Luis Verjan, a registered process server in Los Angeles County (201851867), served the papers on Defendant. (Id.) The Proof of Service creates a rebuttable presumption that service was proper.

 

Plaintiffs offer further evidence in support of proper service.  An email from the Process Server company to Plaintiffs’ counsel on September 2, 2025, indicates that the initial address provided, 6027 Ferguson Dr., Los Angeles, CA 90022 (“6027 Property”) was incorrect, and the owner of the property, Defendant’s father, stated Defendant lives next door at the 6031 Property. (Chang Decl., Ex. A.) Mr. Verjan went to the 6031 Property and “was able to personally serve the subject.” (Id.)  On August 11, 2026, a supplemental declaration was submitted with the Proof of Service reiterating the statements in the email; Mr. Verjan also stated that he spoke with Defendant at the 6031 Property, that she confirmed her identity, and that she accepted the papers after he explained their general nature. (Chang Decl., Ex. D.) He described Defendant as a Hispanic, brown-eyed woman in her 40s who weighed about 140 pounds and is 5’5. (Id.) The Grant Deeds of which the Court has taken judicial notice indicate that Luis Reyes Vasquez, Defendant’s father, owns the 6027 Property and 6031 Property. (Chang Decl., Exs. E-F.)

 

Plaintiffs’ counsel also states that on November 20, 2025, she had a telephone call with Defendant to discuss the lawsuit, during which time Defendant stated that “she was not liable under the lease because it had been assigned to another party” and that she intended to file for bankruptcy should a default judgment be entered against her. (Chang Decl., ¶ 7.) Defendant also indicated that she had moved from the property located at 8988 Kennedy St., Jurupa Valley, CA 92509 – the most recent residence listed on Lexis SmartLinx Person Report – due to a breakup after counsel indicated Plaintiffs were unsuccessful at serving her at this address. (Id., Ex. G.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the burden shifts to Defendant to rebut the presumption of proper service.

 

Defendant provides her declarations as evidentiary support that service was not proper. Defendant declares that: (1) she was not personally served; (2) she is 5’1 and 105 pounds; (3) she did not reside at the 6031 Property at the time of service; (4) the supplemental declaration was created in July, months after service was allegedly effected; (5) the Grant Deed and Lexis evidence do not establish service or her physical location; and (6) her later communications with counsel do not establish service. (Reply Decl., ¶¶ 1-10.)  Exhibit A, attached to the Reply in support of the Motion to Quash but noticed in support of the operative motion, states that Defendant’s height and weight on September 11, 2026, was 108 pounds and 60.50 inches. (Reply MTQ, Ex. A.)

 

Defendant has failed to rebut the presumption of proper service. Defendant’s declaration that she was not the individual served at the 6031 Property is less credible than Plaintiffs’ showing (i.e., Proof of Service, her father’s ownership of the 6031 Property, and the process server’s declaration, as well as Plaintiffs’ counsel’s declaration regarding communications with Defendant). Defendant’s statements in her declaration are conclusory.  She has not provided a copy of her license or any other documentation to support her statements that she was not at 6031 Property. Defendant has not provided declarations from anyone else, including her father, to dispute Plaintiffs’ evidence. In addition, Defendant’s medical information from September 11, 2026, do not make her more credible, because the health information was taken more than a year after alleged service and weight fluctuates; the description was also an estimate, and the difference is not material. Accordingly, Defendant has not shown that the Default Judgment was void.

 

For the reasons explained above, the Court denies the Motion to Set Aside/Vacate. ¿¿ 

 

Conclusion

 

defendant’s Motion to Set Aside/Vacate default and default judgment is denied.

HEARING DATE:                 September 28, 2026

           

CASE NUMBER:                   25STCV06501

CASE NAME:                        David L. Lim, et al. vs. Yolette Vasquez

 

MOVING PARTY:                 Defendant Yolette Vasquez

                                               

OPPOSING PARTY:             Plaintiffs David L. Lim and Chery C. Lim, as Trustees of the Lim Family Trust dated 1/14/99

PROCEEDING:                      MOTION TO QUASH

RULING SUMMARY:          Defendant’s Motion to Quash is denied.

                                                                                                                                                           

 

Background

 

On March 7, 2025, David L. Lim and Chery C. Lim, as Trustees of the Lim Family Trust dated 1/14/99 (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed an Unlawful Detainer Complaint against Yolette Vasquez (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 25.

 

On July 11, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging causes of action for (1) breach of lease; (2) quantum meruit; (3) open book account; and (4) account stated.  

On December 2, 2025, the Court entered Default Judgment against Defendant in the total of $83,060.08.

 

Defendant moves to quash the service of summons and complaint. Plaintiffs oppose the Motion.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

Plaintiffs request that the Court take judicial notice of (1) the Grant Deed to the property located at “6027, 6027 ½, 6029 Ferguson Dr., Los Angeles, CA 90022 (APN 6338-022-021) recorded in the official records of the Recorder’s Office of Los Angeles County, California on March 21, 2023 as Document No. 20230180507 and requested by Luis Reyes Vasquez;” and (2) the Grant Deed for the property located at 6031 Ferguson Dr., Los Angeles, CA 90022 (APN 6338-022-018) recorded in the official records of the Recorder’s Office of Los Angeles County, California on March 21, 2023 as Document No. 20230180521 and requested by Luis Reyes Vasquez. (RJN ¶¶ 1-2; Chang Decl., Exs. E-F.)  (Evid. Code § 452, subds. (c), (h).)

 

Defendant objects only to the use of these records to establish disputed factual conclusions. (Response to RJN at p.1.)

 

The request is granted. (Evid. Code § 452, subds. (c), (h); see Kalnoki v. First American Trustee Servicing Solutions, LLC (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 23, 37.) The Court takes judicial notice of these documents only as to “the existence, content and authenticity of public records and other specified documents;” it does not take judicial notice of the truth of any factual matters asserted in those documents. (Dominguez v. Bonta (2022) 87 Cal. App. 5th 389, 400.)

 

LEGAL STANDARDS

 

“A defendant, on or before the last day of his or her time to plead . . . may serve and file a notice of motion . . . [t]o quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over him or her.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a)(1).)  By filing such motion, “a defendant makes a special appearance for the narrow purpose of contesting personal jurisdiction where the summons is defective.”  (Stancil v. Sup. Ct. (2021) 11 Cal.5th 381, 393.)

“California is a jurisdiction which in general requires strict statutory compliance with requirements for service of original process.”  (In re Abrams (1980) 108 Cal.App.3d 685, 693-694.)  “Until statutory requirements are satisfied, the Court lacks jurisdiction over a defendant.”  (Ruttenberg v. Ruttenberg (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 801, 809.)

“In the absence of a voluntary submission to the authority of the court, compliance with the statutes governing service of process is essential to establish that court’s personal jurisdiction over a defendant.”  (Floveyor International, Ltd. v. Sup. Ct. (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 789, 793; see also Calvert v. Al Binali (2018) 29 Cal.App.5th 954, 961 [“To establish personal jurisdiction, it is essential to comply with the statutory procedures for service of process.”].) “When a defendant challenges that jurisdiction by bringing a motion to quash, the burden is on the plaintiff to prove the existence of jurisdiction by proving, inter alia, the facts requisite to an effective service.”  (Lebel v. Mai (2012) 210 Cal.App.4th 1154, 1160, internal quotations omitted.)

California Rules of Court, rule 3.110, subdivision (b) provides that a summons and complaint must be served on each defendant and a proof of service on each defendant must be filed with the court within 60 days after the complaint is filed. Proof of such service must be made by affidavit of the person making the service “showing the time, place, and manner of service” and including facts showing service was made in accordance with California law. (Code Civ. Proc., §§417.10, subd. (a), 417.30.)  Proof of personal service must be made on a form adopted by the Judicial Council.  (Id., § 417.10, subd. (f).)

“A summons may be served by personal delivery of a copy of the summons and of the complaint to the person to be served. Service of a summons in this manner is deemed complete at the time of such delivery. The date upon which personal delivery is made shall be entered on or affixed to the face of the copy of the summons at the time of its delivery. However, service of a summons without such date shall be valid and effective.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.10.)

If personal service is not possible with reasonable diligence, “a summons may be served by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the person’s dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge of their office, place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, at least 18 years of age, who shall be informed of the contents thereof, and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and of the complaint by first-class mail, postage prepaid to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left. Service of a summons in this manner is deemed complete on the 10th day after the mailing.” (Code Civ. Proc., §415.20, subd. (b).)

discussion

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 418.10(a)(1), Defendant moves the Court to quash service of the Summons and Complaint by Plaintiffs on the grounds that absent service by a statutorily authorized means, the Court has not acquired jurisdiction over Defendant.

¿ 

Defendant lacks standing to bring the operative motion because she remains in default.  Moreover, in denying concurrently Defendant’s Motion to Set Aside or Vacate Default and Default Judgment, the Court concludes that Plaintiff has failed to rebut the presumption of proper service and notice to Defendant.

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court denies the Motion to Quash. ¿¿ 

 

Conclusion

 

defendant’s Motion to Quash is denied.

Case Number: 26STCV03525    Hearing Date: September 28, 2026    Dept: 413

TENTATIVE RULING

HEARING DATE:                 September 28, 2026

CASE NUMBER:                   26STCV03525

CASE NAME:                        Markus Molinari v. John Terzian, et al.

MOVING PARTY:                 Defendants John Terzian, Brian Toll  and The Hwood Group, LLC  

OPPOSING PARTY:             Plaintiff Markus Molinari n/k/a Marsha Molinari

PROCEEDING:                      Motion TO STAY ACTION PENDING APPEAL

RULING SUMMARY:          Defendants’ motion to stay the action pending appeal is denied.

                                                                                                                                                           

Background

 

On February 3, 2026, Markus Molinari n/k/a Marsha Molinari (“Plaintiff”) filed a Complaint against John Terzian (“Terzian”); Brian Toll (“Toll”); The Hwood Group, LLC (collectively “Defendants”) and Does 1 to 50.

 

The Complaint alleges nine causes of action for:

1)     Breach of Partnership Agreement against Terzian and Toll;

2)     Breach of Fiduciary Duty (Partnership) against Terzian and Toll; 

3)     Breach of Fiduciary Duty (LLC) against Terzian, Toll and Does 1-50 as LLC Co-Members;

4)     Constructive Fraud against Terzian, Toll and Does 1-50;

5)     Breach of Contract against The Hwood Group, LLC;

6)     Conversion against Terzian, Toll, the Hwood Group, LLC, and Does 1-50;

7)     Violation of Penal Code § 496 Terzian, Toll, the Hwood Group, LLC, and Does 1-50;

8)     Accounting against Terzian, Toll, the Hwood Group, LLC and Does 1-50; and

9)     Retaliation Under FEHA against The Hwood Group.

 

On March 2, 2026, Defendants filed a motion to compel arbitration.  The Court denied Defendants’ motion on June 11, 2026, finding the ninth cause of action for retaliation implicated the Ending Forced Arbitration Act (“EFAA”).  

 

On July 6, 2026, Defendants filed a Notice of Appeal of the Court’s June 11, 2026, Order denying their motion to compel arbitration.

 

Defendants move to stay the action pending appeal. Plaintiff opposes the Motion.

 

motion TO STAY THE ACTION PENDING APPEAL

 

I.         Legal Standard 

 

“An aggrieved party may appeal from…An order dismissing or denying a petition to compel arbitration. Notwithstanding Section 916, the perfecting of such an appeal shall not automatically stay any proceedings in the trial court during the pendency of the appeal.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1294, subd. (a).)

 

“Even when the statutes do not call for an automatic stay on appeal, the trial and appellate courts both have the power to issue discretionary stays.” (Daly v. San Bernardino County Bd. of Supervisors (2021) 11 Cal.5th 1030, 1039.)  A discretionary stay pending appeal “is appropriate where ‘difficult questions of law are involved and the fruits of a reversal would be irrevocably lost unless the status quo is maintained.’” (Ibid.)

 

II.        Discussion

 

Defendants move for an order staying this action pending Defendants’ appeal on the grounds the governing arbitration agreements expressly invoke the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), which requires a stay pending an appeal as to the issue of arbitrability. 

 

A.        The FAA does not require a stay and does not preempt section 1294(a) of the Code of Civil Procedure

 

Defendants argue the arbitration agreements expressly invoke the FAA, therefore the determination of whether a stay should be ordered should be determined based on federal authorities. Defendants argue this stay is mandatory, citing Coinbase, Inc. v. Bielski (2023) 599 U.S. 736 to support this argument. (Mot., p.11.)

 

Coinbase rests on a federal procedural principle that does not apply to the procedure in state courts related to appeals from an order denying a petition to compel arbitration.  Coinbase held that “a district court must stay its proceedings while the interlocutory appeal on arbitrability is ongoing.” (Id. at 740.)  The stay flows from the interaction of FAA section 16 (a), which confers federal appellate jurisdiction over an appeal from the denial of a motion to compel. (Ibid.)  The Court expressly disclaimed creating a special, arbitration-preferring procedural rule, and found that its holding “simply subjects arbitrability appeals to the same stay principles that courts apply in other analogous contexts where an interlocutory appeal is authorized.” (Id. at 746.)  Nothing in Coinbase purports to bind state courts or to displace state procedural rules governing stays.

 

While the FAA’s substantive provisions apply in state and federal court, its procedural provisions apply only in federal court. (Cronus Investments, Inc. v. Concierge Services (2005) 35 Cal.4th 376, 394.)  Section 16(a) is a procedural provision that operates in the federal appellate courts.  Defendants cannot import Coinbase’s federal-court stay rule into this state proceeding.

 

Section 1294(a) of the Code of Civil Procedure is not preempted by the FAA. The FAA’s section 2 preempts contrary state law only where the state rule stands as an obstacle to the enforcement of arbitration agreements or singles out arbitration for suspect status.  A state procedural rule survives so long as it does not defeat the rights granted by Congress or frustrate section 2’s central policy of enforcing agreements according to their terms. (Cronus, supra, 35 Cal.4th at 390.)  

 

Section 1294(a) was amended by Senate Bill 365 in 2023 to provide that an appeal from an order denying a petition to compel arbitration “shall not stay any proceedings in the trial court during the appeal.”  Defendants have cited no cases since the amendment holding that the provision is preempted by the FAA or that a stay of proceedings must be granted notwithstanding the statute’s provisions. 

 

Section 1294(a) does not preclude enforcement of any arbitration agreement or burden its formation. It governs the sequencing of proceedings after a trial court has already determined that a dispute is not subject to arbitration. It is a procedural rule of the type the FAA leaves to the states.  Therefore, section 1294(a) is not preempted, and no automatic stay applies.

 

            B.        Defendants have not shown grounds for a discretionary stay

 

Defendants argue that, if the Court determines the FAA does not preempt section 1294(a), Defendants are still entitled to a discretionary stay pending appeal. (Mot., p.16.) Defendants argue the appeal presents a novel and substantial question: whether a standalone FEHA retaliation claim, unaccompanied by a separate substantive sexual harassment cause of action, can trigger the EFAA.  (Rep., p.6.)

 

Defendants bear the burden of establishing that a stay is warranted, and the power is reserved for the exceptional situation. (Landis v. North American Co. (1936) 299 U.S. 248, 254-256; People ex rel. S.F. Bay Conserv. and Dev. Comm’n v. Town of Emeryville (1968) 69 Cal.2d 533, 537.)  Defendants have not met their burden here.

 

As a preliminary matter, the parties dispute which standard applies.  Plaintiff invokes a four-factor test set forth in Nken v. Holder, where a court considers “(1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing that he is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties interested in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest lies.” (Opp., p.12; see Nken v. Holder (2009) 556 U.S. 418, 425-426.)  Defendants contend California has not adopted the four-factor test, and that the correct inquiry is asking whether the appeal presents substantial questions or a fair possibility of success. (Rep., p. 5.)  

 

California courts have not adopted the federal four-factor test.  The governing standard, articulated by the California Supreme Court, is whether “difficult questions of law are involved and the fruits of a reversal would be irrevocably lost unless the status quo is maintained" (Daly, supra, 11 Cal.5th at p. 1039.)  The Court applies that standard here.

 

First, the appeal does not present a substantial question likely to result in reversal.  EFAA defines a sexual harassment dispute as a dispute relating to conduct that is alleged to constitute sexual harassment under applicable federal, tribal, or state law. (9 U.S.C. § 401.)  A claim alleging retaliation for complaining about sexual harassment is a sexual harassment dispute. (Liu v. Miniso Depot CA, Inc. (2024) 105 Cal.App.5th 791, 804 n.5.)  Defendants argue this case is distinguishable from Liu because it involved substantive harassment allegations along with retaliation. (Rep., p. 6.)  However, Liu’s reasoning turns on the breadth of the sexual harassment dispute definition, which expressly encompasses retaliation for reporting harassment. (Liu, supra, 105 Cal.App.5th at 804.) 

 

Second, the fruits of a reversal would not be irrevocably lost absent a stay.  Defendants argue that they will lose the benefits of arbitration, including confidentiality, efficiency, and reduced costs, absent a stay. (Mot., p.19.)  The ordinary burdens of litigation do not by themselves establish the clear hardship required for a stay. (Lockyer v. Mirant Corp. (2005) 398 F.3d 1098, 1112 [“being required to defend a suit, without more, does not constitute a ‘clear case of hardship or inequity’”].)  The discovery Plaintiff seeks would also be available in arbitration, in substantial part.  Additionally, any confidentiality concerns may be addressed by the protective order entered in this action. The fact that a protective order may not have the same confidentiality as arbitral confidentiality is not the kind of irreparable injury that warrants a stay.

 

Based on the foregoing, and mindful that a stay is reserved for the exceptional case, Defendants have not justified a stay.

 

C.        The parties’ prior positions under the CAA

 

Finally, Plaintiff argues Defendants are judicially estopped from invoking the FAA because they previously moved to compel and obtained a discovery stay under the CAA, without mentioning the FAA. (Opp., p. 9.)  Defendants argue in response that there is no inconsistency, and that the CAA was the only vehicle for a pre-ruling stay, whereas the present motion addresses a stay pending appeal. (Rep., p. 9.)

 

The Court need not resolve the judicial estoppel question, because the motion fails on the merits regardless of which party’s characterization is correct. The Court observes, however, that Defendants’ repeated reliance on California’s arbitration procedures to obtain the relief they sought is in tension with their present position that federal authorities govern the procedural question of a stay. That tension reinforces the conclusion that California procedure, including section 1294, subdivision (a), governs here.

Conclusion

 

Defendants’ motion to stay the action pending appeal is denied.

Case Number: 26STCV04457    Hearing Date: September 28, 2026    Dept: 413

TENTATIVE RULING

HEARING DATE:                 September 28, 2026

CASE NUMBER:                   26STCV04457

CASE NAME:                        Gonzalez, et al. v. Kashi M Corporation, et al.

MOVING PARTY:                 Specially Appearing Defendant, Kashi M Corporation[1]

OPPOSING PARTY:             Plaintiffs, Rocio Gonzalez, et al.

PROCEEDING:                      Motion to quash service of summons and complaint

RULING SUMMARY:          Specially Appearing Defendant’s motion to quash service is denied.       

Background

 

On February 11, 2026, plaintiffs Rocio Gonzalez, Jonathan Silvestre, and Arcadio Rosas-Maciel (“Plaintiffs”) filed a Complaint against Defendant Kashi M Corporation (“Defendant”) for:

 

1.     Breach of Warranty of Habitability

2.     Breach of Covenant of Quiet Enjoyment

3.     Negligence

4.     Breach of Contract

 

Defendant moves to quash service of the summons and complaint due to the failure to complete service on an authorized agent for service.

 

I.         Legal Standard 

  

A plaintiff has the initial burden to establish valid statutory service of a summons and complaint. (Dill v. Berquist Const. Co., Inc. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1439-40; Floveyor Internat. v. Sup. Ct. (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 789, 794.) “If a copy of the summons and complaint cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to the person to be served, as specified in Section 416.60, 416.70, 416.80, or 416.90, a summons may be served by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the person’s dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge of his or her office, place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, at least 18 years of age, who shall be informed of the contents thereof, and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and of the complaint by first-class mail, postage prepaid to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left. Service of a summons in this manner is deemed complete on the 10th day after the mailing.” (Code Civ. Proc., 415.20, subd. (b).)

Service in this context is allowed “upon a person upon a person whose ‘relationship with the person to be served makes it more likely than not that they will deliver process to the named party.’” (Bein v. Brechtel-Jochim Group, Inc. (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 1387, 1393; see Hearn v. Howard (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1202; Ellard v. Conway (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 540, 546 (fn.3).) “The return of a process server registered [under] Division 8 of the Business and Professions code upon process or notice establishes a presumption, affecting the burden of producing evidence, of the facts stated in the return.” (Evid. Code, § 647.)

II.        Discussion

 

Defendant’s Request for Judicial Notice is granted.

 

Proof of Service by substituted service was filed by Plaintiff on May 14, 2026, showing service on Kashi M Corporation, by “Agent: Lourdes Urrea Maldonado” at 5423 S. Central Ave., Los Angeles, by stubstituted service on John “Antonio” Doe – Co-Employee” on 4/20/2026.  Service was indicated to be at a business address. 

 

Attached to the Declaration submitted with the Opposition was copy of a Proof of Service of Summons filed 09/22/2026, on the same corporation, by the same agency, by substituted service on “Age: 50s, Race: Hispanic; Sex: Male,” with eeight, height and hair descriptions, on 9/2/2026.  Service was indicated to be at a home address. 

 

Defendant acknowledges the accuracy California Secretary of State Statement of Information. The identified officer and agent for service is Lourdes Maldonado. Eric Jiminez is also an identified director. (See Corp. Code, §§ 1502, 1505, 16310.)

 

According to Declarations of Due Diligence also submitted with the Opposition, in eight separate prior attempts from March 19, 2026, to April 9, registered process server Miguel Molina attempted service on a registered agent or officer. The business was either closed, the process server was unable to gain entry, or a person on the premises represented no one was on the premises matching the agent or officer names was employed at this location.  On April 20, 2026, the process server executed substituted service with a “John ‘Antonio’ Doe” on the premises for registered agent, Lourdes Maldonado. Mail service was also completed.

Lourdes Maldanado submitted a declaration admitting the propriety of the address, but denying anyone named “Antonio” worked on the premises. Other than this simple denial of the existence of Antonio, nothing in the declaration or motion actually addresses the numerous prior attempts and the apparent regular closure of the location.

 

The dispute regarding “Antonio” as a proper recipient of substituted service comes down to credibility of Maldanado versus the process server and Plaintiffs’ counsel. [See Declaration of Ashley Soto.]  Mere denial of Antonio without any further justification for the state of office operations suggests an effort to evade service of process. Registration of a corporate entity with the State of California indicates regular systematic operations in the state, rather than a shell operation intended to avoid legal responsibilities. Defendant cannot evade appearing in the action simply based on hiding and denial.

 

Antonio was apparently properly on the premises on April 20, 2026, and spoke with the process server. Whether Antonio was employed by Kashi M Corporation or not in no way establishes a lack of apparent authority of Antonio to greet invitees on the premises and leave notice to Kashi M Corporation representatives at its identified address. (See Chinese Theater, LLC v. Starline Tours USA, Inc. (2025) 115 Cal.App.5th 1048, 1059-1060.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court denies the motion to quash service.

 

Conclusion

 

The motion to quash service of the summons and complaint is denied. Kashi M Corporation to file a responsive pleading within 10 days of this order.
