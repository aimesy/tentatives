Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 07-30-26

Case # 21CHCV00467, Silvas v. Central Valley Occupational Medical Group, Inc., et al.

Trial Date: N/A

 

MOTION TO STRIKE OR TAX COSTS

 

MOVING PARTY:  Plaintiff Vanessa Silvas

RESPONDING PARTIES:  Defendants Central Valley Occupational Medical Group, Inc.; ProHealth Professional Services, LLC; Regency Surgery Center; Pro-Health Valley Occupational Medical Group, Inc.; and Sean Younai

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order striking defendants’ memorandum of costs in its entirety.  In the alternative, order striking expert witness fees, trial binder photocopies, transcript fees, duplicate video processing fee for Plaintiff's deposition, and registered process-server charges.

 

RULING:  Motion is granted in part and denied in part.  The court strikes $4,350 in transcript fees and $105 in duplicate video fees from Defendants’ memorandum of costs.  Defendants are entitled to $113,621.20 in costs.

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

On June 22, 2021, plaintiff Vanessa Silvas (Plaintiff) filed this employment action against defendants ProHealth Professional Services, LLC, Regency Surgery Center, Central Valley Occupational Medical Group, Inc., Pro-Health Valley Occupational Medical Group, Inc., and Sean Younai (Defendants).  The operative Third Amended Complaint asserted twelve causes of action for violations of the Labor Code, violations of the Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA), wrongful termination in violation of public policy, and intentional infliction of emotional distress.

 

On December 1, 2025, Defendants moved for summary judgment on the Third Amended Complaint.  The court granted the motion based on Plaintiff’s lack of standing.  Judgment was entered for Defendants on April 23, 2026, with Defendants being declared the prevailing parties in this action pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1032.  (4/23/26 Judgment at p. 2:24-25.)  Notice of the entry of judgment was mailed to Plaintiff on April 29, 2026.  (4/29/26 Notice of Entry of Judgment or Order at p. 5.)

 

On May 14, 2026, Defendants filed a joint Memorandum of Costs, seeking $118,076.20 in costs.

 

On June 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion to strike or tax the costs.  Defendants filed late opposition papers on Monday, July 20, 2026, which they claim was caused by technical issues with the court’s e-filing system.[1]  Plaintiff replied on July 21, 2026.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Motion to Strike or Tax Costs

 

A prevailing party is entitled to recover costs, as a matter of right, in any action or proceeding absent a statute expressly noting otherwise.  (Civ. Proc. Code, § 1032,¿subd. (b).)  Unless a statute provides otherwise, a court has no discretion to deny costs to a prevailing party.  (Nelson v. Anderson¿(1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 129.)¿¿ A prevailing party is (i) a party that receives a net monetary recovery, (ii) a defendant who obtains a dismissal in its favor (iii) a defendant, when neither the plaintiff nor defendant attained any relief and¿(iv) a¿defendant, where the plaintiff(s) obtains no recovery from the defendant.  (Civ. Proc. Code, § 1032,¿subd. (a)(4).)  “If¿any party recovers other than monetary relief and in situations other than as specified, the ‘prevailing party’¿shall be as determined by the court, and under those circumstances, the court, in its discretion, may allow costs or not and, if allowed,¿may apportion costs between the parties on the same or adverse sides pursuant to rules adopted under¿Section 1034.”  (Id.)  Section 1033 sets forth the allowable costs and notes that allowable costs must be reasonably necessary to “the conduct of litigation.”

¿

A party asserting it is the prevailing party must file a memorandum of costs “within 15 days of notice of entry of judgment or dismissal by the clerk or dismissal by the clerk¿under¿Code of Civil Procedure Section 664.5¿or the date of service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, or within 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is first.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700.)

 

A party opposing those costs may file a motion to strike to challenge all costs or a motion to tax costs to challenge specific costs listed in the prevailing party’s memorandum of costs.  The opposing party bears the burden of demonstrating why certain items should not be permitted and must state specific facts to support the motion.  (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 129; Rojas v. HSBC Card Services (2023) 93 Cal.App.5th 860, 896.)  The motion must be filed and served within 15 days after¿service of the cost memorandum. ¿(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700.)

 

The court must determine whether sections 1032 and 1033.5 expressly allow the prevailing party to recover the requested cost.  (Gorman v. Tassajara Development Corp. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 44, 71; Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a).)  “Allowable costs shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation. . . . (3) Allowable costs shall be reasonable in amount.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(2)-(3).)

 

Defendants timely filed and electronically served their memorandum of costs on May 14, 2026, and Plaintiff timely filed and served her motion to strike or tax costs within 15 days, plus two for electronic service.

 

Defendants ask the court to (1) strike all costs based on a theory of judicial estoppel, or (2) strike and tax various costs:  $77,942.50 in Claimed Section 998 Expert Fees, $4,534.95 for trial binder photocopies, $4,350.00 for transcript fees, $3,020.41 in registered service of process fees for Cathy Graf and Thomas Fera, and $183 for a duplicate video processing fee.

 

                        1.  Plaintiff’s judicial estoppel argument lacks merit.

 

Plaintiff contends judicial estoppel bars Defendants’ costs request because Defendants have consistently argued that Plaintiff is a non-party in this action or not the real party in interest who could not bring or maintain her claims.  (Motion at p. 10:2-14.)

 

“Under California law, judicial estoppel applies when ‘(1) the same party has taken two positions; (2) the positions were taken in judicial or quasi-judicial administrative proceedings; (3) the party was successful in asserting the first position (i.e., the tribunal adopted the position or accepted it as true); (4) the two positions are totally inconsistent; and (5) the first position was not taken as a result of ignorance, fraud, or mistake.’”  (Daar & Newman v. VRL Int’l (2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 482, 491 [quoting Jackson v. County of Los Angeles (1997) 60 Cal.App.4th 171, 183].)

 

Plaintiff’s argument is one of inconsistent positions:  Defendants cannot simultaneously argue Plaintiff lacked standing and then seek costs against Plaintiff as a “real party” in interest.

 

Here, Defendants’ positions are not inconsistent.  First, Defendants have never argued Plaintiff is not a party to this action.  Defendants have consistently argued Plaintiff is a party to this action but lacks the standing to bring the substantive claims in this action.  Considering Plaintiff filed this action and brought Defendants into this litigation, she is a party to the action.  Because Plaintiff lacked standing, she did not prevail on her claims.  As such, Defendants separately seek costs from Plaintiff as the named party who filed this action and did not prevail on her claims.  These are compatible positions:  the standing defect defeats the merits of Plaintiff’s causes of action but does not eliminate the litigation costs incurred in defending this action.  Furthermore, the costs statute operates independently of the merits of the underlying claims basing entitlement to costs on the identity of the prevailing party.

 

Although there is recent case law which holds that costs cannot be recovered against a true non-party who never participated in litigation, that principle does not apply where the Plaintiff herself initiated an action, named herself as a party, and actively litigated the action to summary judgment.  (See Rose v. Hobby Lobby Stores, Inc. (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 162, 172-176 [stating PAGA costs not recoverable against the Labor and Workforce Development Agency where the agency never became a party to the litigation].)

 

Because Defendants obtained a favorable summary judgment ruling and Plaintiff has recovered no relief, Defendant qualifies as the prevailing party and their right to costs is mandatory rather than discretionary.  (Cussler v. Crusader Entertainment, LLC (2012) 212 Cal.App.4th 356, 364, 371-372.)

 

Plaintiff’s argument lacks merit, and Plaintiff cites no case law which supports denying Defendants’ mandatory right to costs.  Absent a good faith basis for modifying or extending an existing law, litigants are generally prohibited from asserting a position in litigation without authority.  (See, e.g., In re Estate of Randall (1924) 194 Cal. 725, 728-729 [“Contentions supported neither by argument nor by citation of authority are deemed to be without foundation, and to have been abandoned.”];  see also Lafferty v. Wells Fargo Bank (2013) 213 Cal. App. 4th 545, 571-572 [“When a point is asserted without argument and authority for the proposition, ‘it is deemed to be without foundation and requires no discussion by the reviewing court.’”];  Atchley v. City of Fresno (1984) 151 Cal. App. 3d 635, 647 [“Where a point is merely asserted by appellant's counsel without any argument of or authority for the proposition, it is deemed to be without foundation and requires no discussion by the reviewing court.”].)

 

Therefore, the court denies Plaintiff’s motion to the extent it is based on “judicial estoppel.”

 

                        2.  Section 998 Expert Fees:  $77,942.50

 

Next, Plaintiff asks the court to strike $77,942.50 in claimed Section 998 expert fees.  According to Plaintiff, Defendants have failed to identify which of the two Section 998 offers triggers the entitlement to costs.  Furthermore, the offers are invalid because they consists of a single lump sum joint offer from all five defendants without apportioning the lump sum amongst each defendant.  California courts have consistently held that such a joint section 998 offer is invalid and cannot trigger section 998 cost-shifting.

 

In opposition, Defendants present copies of both Section 998 Offers and receipts for the expert witness fees.  According to Defendants, the offers were presented from all defendants jointly because Plaintiff pursued all five defendants collectively without differentiation, under the joint unity of interest doctrine.  Furthermore, Defendants’ cited case law is distinguishable because it pertains to joint plaintiffs with a joint unity of interest where an unapportioned offer was made asserting a single community property interest.  A section 998 offer is valid if it is sufficiently certain to permit the offeree to realistically evaluate the litigation.

 

As stated above, Defendants are the prevailing parties because neither Defendants nor Plaintiff obtained any relief in this matter.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (a)(4).)  However, Code of Civil Procedure section 998 augments the general cost-recovery framework of section 1032 by shifting post-offer costs when a plaintiff rejects a defendant’s offer to compromise and then fails to obtain a more favorable judgment or award.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 998, subd. (a).)

 

“Not less than 10 days prior to commencement of trial . . . , any party may serve an offer in writing upon any other party to the action to allow judgment to be taken or an award to be entered in accordance with the terms and conditions stated at that time.  The written offer shall include a statement of the offer, containing the terms and conditions of the judgment or award, and a provision that allows the accepting party to indicate acceptance of the offer by signing a statement that the offer is accepted.  Any acceptance of the offer, whether made on the document containing the offer or on a separate document of acceptance, shall be in writing and shall be signed by counsel for the accepting party or, if not represented by counsel, by the accepting party.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 998, subd. (b).)  “If the offer is not accepted prior to trial . . . or within 30 days after it is made, whichever occurs first, it shall be deemed withdrawn, and cannot be given in evidence upon the trial[.]”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 998, subd. (a)(2).)

 

“If an offer made by a defendant is not accepted and the plaintiff fails to obtain a more favorable judgment or award, the plaintiff shall not recover their postoffer costs and shall pay the defendant’s costs from the time of the offer.  In addition, in any action or proceeding other than an eminent domain action, the court or arbitrator, in its discretion, may require the plaintiff to pay a reasonable sum to cover postoffer costs of the services of expert witnesses, who are not regular employees of any party, actually incurred and reasonably necessary in either, or both, preparation for trial or arbitration, or during trial or arbitration, of the case by the defendant.  In determining whether the plaintiff obtains a more favorable judgment, the court or arbitrator shall exclude the postoffer costs.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 998, subd. (c)(1)-(2)(A).)

 

Defendants served two Section 998 Offers to Compromise on Plaintiff prior to the court’s summary judgment ruling and entry of judgment:  October 5, 2022, offer for $85,000, and a March 3, 2023, offer for $225,000.  (Declaration of Brennan M. Hershey ¶ 7;  Declaration of Freda Tjoarman ¶¶ 14-16, Exhs. L, M.)  Defendants appear to rely on the March 3, 2023, offer as the triggering offer.

 

Both offers were made jointly by all Defendants to Plaintiff as single, undivided lump sums as payment for all damages of any type or nature that Plaintiff could recover in this action, as well as statutory costs and reasonable attorneys’ fees incurred as of the date of the offer.  In exchange, Plaintiff was required to dismiss the matter with prejudice within 15 days of Defendants making payments.  The offers also advised Plaintiff “that failure to accept the offer may have adverse consequences should Plaintiff recover an amount less than this offer at the time of trial or arbitration, including a reduction in attorneys’ fees and costs awarded to Plaintiff, an inability by Plaintiff to recover such costs, or an award of such costs against Plaintiff and in Defendants’ favor. See California Code of Civil Procedure § 998; California Labor Code § 218.5.”  Finally, both offers included provisions that allowed the accepting party to indicate acceptance of the offer by their attorney signing a signature block under the following text:  “Plaintiff hereby accepts this offer to compromise on the terms stated above.”  Plaintiff did not accept either offer.  (Tjoarman Decl. ¶¶ 14-15.)

 

Thus, the offers appear on their face to be valid.

 

“A settlement offer made jointly by several defendants to a specific plaintiff may be sufficient to trigger section 998 penalties where the defendants are jointly and severally liable.  However, in post-Proposition 51 cases, where each defendant is only jointly liable for the plaintiff’s economic damages but severally liable for noneconomic damages in proportion to that defendant’s degree of wrongdoing, the validity of such an offer is questionable.”  (Taing v. Johnson Scaffolding Co. (1992) 9 Cal.App.4th 579, 583-584, citations omitted.)

 

Here, both offers were jointly made by all Defendants.  Plaintiff’s Third Amended Complaint alleges “All defendants were responsible for the events and damages alleged herein, including on the following bases: (a) Defendants, committed the acts alleged; (b) at all relevant times, one or more of the Defendants was the agent or employee, and/or acted under the control or supervision, of one or more of the remaining defendants, and in committing the acts alleged, acted within the course and scope of such agency and employment and/or is or are otherwise liable for Plaintiff’s damages; (c) at all relevant times, there existed a unity of ownership and interest between or among two or more of the Defendants such that any individuality and separateness between or among those Defendants has ceased, and Defendants are the alter egos of one another.”  (TAC ¶ 4.)  Plaintiff alleges all causes of action against each defendant (except the ninth, tenth, eleventh, and twelfth causes of action).  And Plaintiff sought economic damages, equitable relief, and punitive damages from all Defendants.  Although the offers were not apportioned between each defendant, Plaintiff’s alter-ego allegations demonstrate Plaintiff’s intent to hold all Defendants jointly and severally liable for all damages.  Plaintiff contends the offerors are required to “prove a true unity of interest, and the burden rests on them” but fails to cite any case law which supports this contention.  (Reply at p. 2:26-28.)

 

Thus, Plaintiff’s failure to accept the March 3, 2023, offer triggers Section 998(c)(1) and the court in its discretion determines whether the $77,942.50 expert fees are reasonable.

 

Defendants claim the $77,942.50 in expert witness fees were incurred for their medical expert (Paul A. Barkopoulos, M.D., M.P.H.) who billed 108.66 hours at $550.00 per hour ($59,762.50) and their accounting expert (William Buckley) who billed 40.4 hours at $450 per hour ($18,180).  (Tjoarman Decl. ¶ 13, Exh. G.)  A review of the experts’ invoices indicates all fees were incurred for work performed post-offer.  (See id., Exh. G.)

 

After reviewing the attached invoices and seeing no substantive challenges to the invoices from Plaintiff, the court finds the expert witness fees were actually incurred and reasonably necessary to litigate this matter as to Plaintiff’s injuries and claimed damages for Labor Code, FEHA violations, wrongful termination, and tortious conduct.

 

Therefore, the court denies Plaintiff’s motion as to the $77,942.50 in expert fees.

 

 

3.  The court strikes $4,350.00 in Transcript Fees and $105 of the Duplicate Video fee.

 

Item 4:  $183 “Duplicate Video Processing” fee

 

Plaintiff moves to strike Item No. 4: Duplicate Video Processing Fee ($183) because Section 1033.5 does not authorize recovery for a second or duplicate processing of a video recording already covered elsewhere in the deposition invoice.

 

In opposition, Defendants contend section 1033.5(a)(3)(A) permits recovery for video recording of depositions, including “an original and one copy of those taken by the claimant.” The $183.00 charge labeled “Duplicate Video Processing Fee” represents a legitimate charge associated with the video processing of Plaintiff’s deposition.  The vendor label is a billing description, not a legal characterization, and it does not establish that the charge falls outside the scope of allowable deposition video costs.  The deposition was necessary, the video recording was proper, and the processing charge was incurred in connection with it.  Plaintiff presents no evidence that this specific charge lies outside the scope of section 1033.5(a)(3)(A).  (Tjoarman Decl., Exh. Q.)

 

Allowable costs under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(3)(A) include the “[t]aking, video recording, and transcribing necessary depositions, including an original and one copy of those taken by the claimant and one copy of depositions taken by the party against whom costs are allowed.”

 

In reviewing the invoices attached to the Tjoarman declaration, the court finds that the $105 of the $183 duplicate video processing fee goes beyond the scope of allowed costs under Section 1033.5(a)(3)(A).  (Tjoarman Decl., Exh. E at pp. 1-2, 7, Exh. Q.)

 

Therefore, the court strikes the $105 video processing fee, reducing the $183 deposition charge to $78.

 

Item 5: Cathy Graf ($1,902.01) and Thomas Fera ($1,118.40) Registered Service of Process fees for Deposition Subpoenas

 

Plaintiff asserts the subpoena service of process fees for Cathy Graf and Thomas Fera are facially excessive and represent costs well beyond the Los Angeles County Sheriff’s fee for comparable personal service, and Defendants do not support the request with invoices or other documentation.  (Hershey Decl. ¶ 6.)

 

In opposition, Defendants assert the $1,902.01 and $1,118.40 service charges were actually incurred to effect service on Plaintiff’s treating therapist and healthcare provider in this action.  Registered process-server fees are not statutorily capped at the Sheriff’s rate, and courts have consistently held that registered process-server fees in excess of the Sheriff’s fee may be recovered so long as they are reasonably incurred.  (Tjoarman Decl. ¶ 20.)

 

Section 1033.5 specifically provides that when service is by a registered process server, “the recoverable cost is the amount actually incurred in effecting service, including, but not limited to, a stakeout or other means employed in locating the person to be served, unless those charges are successfully challenged by a party to the action.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(4)(B).)  The prevailing party claiming stakeout fees must provide adequate documentation to establish the necessity and reasonableness of the service costs.  (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 132-135.)

 

The court has reviewed the First Legal invoices for Ms. Graf’s deposition subpoenas and Mr. Fera’s deposition subpoenas and finds that Defendants actually incurred $1,902.01 in service of process charges in attempting to serve deposition subpoenas on Ms. Graf and $1,118.40 in service of process fees attempting to serve Mr. Fera.  (See Tjoarman Decl., Exh. F at pp. 3-9, 11-12 [noting at least 10 service attempts for the deposition subpoenas].)  Furthermore, Plaintiff cites no case law requiring the court to limit Defendants’ service of process fees to the Los Angeles County Sheriff’s fee schedule.

 

Therefore, the court denies Plaintiff’s motion as to the registered service processor fees related to serving Cathy Graf and Thomas Fera.

 

 

Item 9: $4,350.00 in Transcript Fees,

 

Plaintiff asks the court to strike $4,350 in “Court-ordered transcript” fees because the court never issued an order directing the parties to prepare or purchase transcripts from various proceedings.  (Hershey Decl. ¶ 5.)  Defendants cannot satisfy section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(9)(A) simply by affixing the label “court-ordered” to a self-serving cost memorandum.  Section 1033.5(b)(5) (b)(5) expressly excludes non-court-ordered transcripts from allowable costs.

 

In opposition, Defendants assert Section 1033.5(a)(9)(A) allows the transcripts because they were obtained in the ordinary course of litigating this matter.  (Tjoarman Decl. ¶¶ 10, 19, Exhs. H, O;  Opposition at p. 12:23-24 [transcripts from four contested hearings on various motions].)

 

Pursuant to Section 1033.5(a)(9), allowable costs include “Transcripts of court proceedings ordered by the court.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(9).)  “Transcripts of court proceedings not ordered by the court,” are not allowable costs.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (b)(5).)

 

Although Defendants actually incurred the transcript costs for contested proceedings, Defendants present no evidence or minute orders indicating the court ever ordered the parties to provide transcripts.

 

Therefore, the court grants Plaintiff’s motion as to the transcript fees and strikes $4,350 in transcript fees.

 

Item 13: $4,534.95 in “Trial Binder” Photocopies

 

Plaintiff contends the court should strike $4,534.95 for “Photocopying/compilation of Trial Binders” because no jury actually received the binders and preparation for a trial that does not occur is not necessary to the conduct of litigation.

 

In opposition, Defendants contend the test is whether the costs were “reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation” at the time they were incurred, not whether, with the benefit of hindsight, the preparation turned out to be unnecessary.  Defendants cite Section 1033.5(c) as a basis for the court to award the trial binder fees.  (Tjoarman Decl. ¶ 18, Exh. N; Opposition at pp. 11:26-12:20.)

 

“Models, the enlargements of exhibits and photocopies of exhibits, and the electronic presentation of exhibits, including costs of rental equipment and electronic formatting, may be allowed if they were reasonably helpful to aid the trier of fact.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(13).)  The threshold requirement is whether the materials were actually used or presented during the proceedings.  “To demonstrate that models, enlargements of exhibits, photocopies of exhibits, and electronic presentations were reasonably helpful to aid the trier of fact under Section 1033.5(a)(13), the prevailing party must show that these materials were actually presented to or used by the trier of fact during the proceedings.  (Segal v. ASICS America Corp. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 651, 666-668.)  To demonstrate reasonable helpfulness of trial exhibits presented through audiovisual equipment, the plaintiff can show that the counsel, witnesses, and the judge actually used paper exhibits and copies of the admitted exhibits went into the jury room.  (Chaaban v. West Seal, Inc. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 49, 59;  see also El Dorado Meat Co. v. Yosemite Meat & Locker Service, Inc. (2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 612, 615-616 [awarding $2,250 in costs for projection equipment used to enlarge exhibits by projection].)

 

However, under Section 1033.5(c)(4), a trial court may “award costs incurred in preparing demonstratives and photocopies of trial exhibits, even though they were not ultimately used at trial, when such materials are reasonably necessary to the conduct of litigation and reasonable in amount.”  (Segal, supra, 12 Cal.5th at p. 667; Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(2)-(4).)

 

Pursuant to Section 1033.5(c), the court finds the binder costs were reasonably necessary to conduct litigation.  In Benach v. County of Los Angeles (2007), the Court of Appeal upheld an award for exhibit photocopies and binders not used at trial, reasoning that “an experienced trial judge would recognize that it would be inequitable to deny as allowable costs exhibits any prudent counsel would prepare in advance of trial,” particularly where “nothing indicates [the party] could have anticipated that they would not be used.”  (Benach v. County of Los Angeles (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 836, 856.)

 

The court also finds the trial binder charges are reasonable in amount and notes the invoices for the trial binders date back to September and October of 2023, indicating Defendants were preparing for trial during one of the previously scheduled trial dates.  (Tjoarman Decl., Exh. I.)  Considering the timing of the filing of the summary judgment (December 1, 2025), the motion hearing (February 20, 2026), the final trial date (March 23, 2026), and the court issuing its final summary judgment ruling on March 16, 2026, it was reasonably necessary for Defendants prepare trial binders and exhibits for trial.  Because issues underlying the judgment were highly contested, Defendants could not have known whether the court would have granted the motion in its entirety for them.

 

Plaintiff contends Defendants cannot seek discretionary relief under Section 1033.5(c)(4) because the memorandum costs lists the binders under Section 1033.5(a)(13).

 

However, Plaintiff cites no California case law that requires a memorandum of costs to cite specific statutory provisions in order to support a cost request.

 

Therefore, the court denies Plaintiff’s motion as to the $4,534.95 in “Trial Binder” Photocopies.

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

Motion to strike costs is granted in part and denied in part.

 

The court strikes $4,350 in transcript fees and $105 in duplicate video fees from Defendants’ memorandum of costs.

 

Defendants are entitled to $113,621.20 in costs.

Case # 25CHCV00039, Fabian, et al. v. Joers, et al.

MOTIONS TO COMPEL RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION, FORM INTERROGATORIES, AND SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES AND TO DEEM REQUESTS FOR ADMISSION AS TRUE AND ADMITTED

 

MOVING PARTY:  Plaintiff Omar Morales

RESPONDING PARTY:  Defense counsel Shelby L. Clark

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

 

Order compelling defendant John Carl Joers to serve verified responses to plaintiff Morales’s first sets of requests for production, form interrogatories, and special interrogatories and to impose monetary sanctions of $1,197.50 against defendant Joers and his counsel of record for each motion.

 

Order plaintiff Morales’s first set of requests for admission to defendant John Carl Joers as true and admitted and imposing $1,197.50 in monetary sanctions against Joers and his counsel Shelby Clark with Hinshaw & Culbertson, LLP.

 

RULING:  Motions are granted.  The court awards plaintiff Morales $3,115 in monetary sanctions.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

The personal injury arises from a motor vehicle collision in which defendant John Carl Joers rearended plaintiffs Estela Yadhira Fabian and Omar Morales.  Plaintiffs allege defendant Joers was driving a vehicle owned by defendant McLogan Supply Co., Inc. and acting within the scope of his employment with McLogan Supply.  On January 6, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a complaint against both defendants, asserting negligence, vicarious liability, and negligent entrustment.  McLogan Supply Co. answered on March 24, 2025, and Joers answered on November 6, 2025.

 

Plaintiff Omar Morales electronically served defendant Joers with his first sets of requests for production, form interrogatories, special interrogatories, and requests for admission on April 7, 2026.  (Declarations of Taylor Scott Kruse, Exhs. 1, 2 [listing email addresses for attorney Clark, Candice Cromer, and RLR Cases of Hinshaw & Culbertson, LLP].)  Responses were due by May 11, 2026, and plaintiff Morales granted an extension to May 15, 2026, to serve objection-only responses.  No responses were served.

 

On June 10, 2026, plaintiff Morales filed motions to compel defendant Joers’s objection-free responses to his first sets of requests for production, form interrogatories, and special interrogatories and a motion to deem the requests for admission as true and admitted.  Plaintiff Morales also seeks to impose monetary sanctions of $4,790 ($1,197.50 per motion) against defendant Joers and his counsel of record, Shelby Clark and Hinshaw & Culbertson, LLP.

 

Attorney Shelby Clark filed an opposing declaration on July 17, 2026.  (Declaration of Shelby L. Clark.)  Mr. Clark claims he was unaware his firm was responsible for the discovery requests until July 16, 2026, because defendants’ insurer stated it was transferring defendants’ representation to different counsel, but the transfer was never completed.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Compelling Discovery Responses and Deeming Requests for Admission As True and Admitted

 

A demanding party may move to compel responses to requests for production where the responding party fails to provide any responses.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.300, subd. (b), 2030.290, subd. (b).)  The demanding party must show the requests were properly served, that the time to respond expired, and no response has been served.  (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 403-404.)  The responding party must respond separately to each demand by agreeing to comply, stating an inability to comply, or objecting to all or part of the demand.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.210, subd. (a).)  The responding party must serve responses within 30 days after the requests for production are served or according to an agreed upon deadline extension.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.260, subd. (a)(1)-(3).)  Failing to respond within these time limits waives objections.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).)

 

A propounding party may move to compel responses to form or special interrogatories where the responding party fails to provide any responses.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (b).)  The propounding party must show the interrogatories were properly served, that the time to respond expired, and no response has been served.  (Leach v. Superior Court (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 902, 905-906.)  Unless excused by a protective order, the responding party must serve responses (an answer, objection, or election to allow inspecting or copy records) within 30 days after the interrogatories are served or according to an agreed upon deadline extension.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.210, subd. (a), 2030.270.)  Failing to respond within these time limits waives objections.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).)

 

When a responding party fails to timely serve answers to requests for admission, the demanding party may move for an order that “the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the request be deemed admitted, as well as for a monetary sanction.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (b).)  Responses to requests for admissions are due 30 days from the date the requests were served on the responding party.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.250, subd. RULING:  Motion is granted in part and denied in part.  The court awards defendant Kledjian $1,635 in monetary sanctions.  The court denies Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

This personal injury action arises from a vehicle collision that occurred on July 31, 2023, around 10:30 a.m. at the intersection of Balboa Boulevard and Parthenia Street.  Plaintiff Sofrone Grassu (Plaintiff) alleges he was rearended by defendant Hratch Kledjian who was driving in an unsafe manner – following Plaintiff’s vehicle too closely.  As a result, Plaintiff sustained personal injuries and damages.  On July 23, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Complaint, asserting a single negligence cause of action.  Defendant Kledjian filed an answer on September 24, 2025.  Plaintiff recently identified defendant Doe 1 as Galpin Motors, Inc.

 

On October 31, 2025, defendant Hratch Kledjian (Defendant) served his first sets of form interrogatories, special interrogatories, and requests for production on Plaintiff.  (Declaration of Megan K. Hawkins ¶¶ 4-5, 7, Exhs. 2-4.)  Plaintiff served responses on January 20, 2026.  (Id. ¶¶ 9-11, Exhs. 6-8.)

 

On March 3, 2026, defense counsel sent Plaintiff a “meet and confer” letter concerning issues with the interrogatory responses.  (Hawkins Decl. ¶ 12, Exh. 9.)  Counsel exchanged emails from March 9, 2026 through March 27, 2026, with defense counsel extending the time for Plaintiff to serve amended interrogatory responses and for Defendant to file motions to compel.  (Hawkins Dec. ¶ 13, Exh. 10.)  On April 6, 2026, Plaintiff served amended responses to the form and special interrogatories.  (Hawkins Decl. ¶¶ 14-15, Exhs. 11-12.) Verifications were served on April 13, 2026.  Defense counsel sent a “meet and confer” letter concerning the responses on May 4, 2026.  (Hawkins Decl. ¶ 16, Exh. 13.)  Counsel exchanged further emails from May 28, 2026 and June 29, 2026, extending time to provide amended responses and the time to file motions to compel to July 6, 2026.  (Id. ¶ 17, Exh. 14.)  Counsel met by telephone on June 29, 2026.  (Hawkins Decl. ¶ 18.)  Plaintiff asserts all responses are code-compliant.

 

On July 2, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to compel Plaintiff’s further, objection-free responses to Defendants first set of form interrogatories (number 17.1) and first set of special interrogatories (numbers 1, 37-41, 43-51, and 62) and to impose $2,160 in monetary sanctions against Plaintiff and Plaintiff’s counsel of record.

 

On July 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition, asserting form interrogatory No. 17.1 (concerning requests for admission numbers 60-64) is improperly directed at Plaintiff regarding matters outside his personal knowledge and seeks information Plaintiff discussed with his attorneys.  Special interrogatory number 1 invades Plaintiff’s right to privacy by seeking Plaintiff’s private information to investigate whether Plaintiff was distracted while driving even though it is Defendant who rear-ended Plaintiff.  Plaintiff’s responses to special interrogatory numbers 37-41, 43-51, and 62 are code-compliant and seek information which is equally available to Defendant.  Plaintiff has been forthright with discovery, providing Defendant with the requested information by disclosing previous accidents (November 8, 2024, December 19, 2022, and August 16, 2018) in response to form interrogatory numbers 10.3 and 11.1.

 

On July 21, 2026, Defendant filed a reply, stating Plaintiff has since served further responses to form interrogatory number 17.1, but only as the same pertains to the lack of unqualified admission to request for admission nos. 65, 66, and 69.

 

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

A motion to compel further responses to interrogatories must include a declaration stating facts showing a “reasonable and good faith attempt” to resolve the issues mentioned in the motion before filing.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2016.040, 2030.300, subd. (b)(1).)  In Department F43, meet and confer means in person or via phone.  A letter or email does not suffice.  (Department F43 Courtroom Information, p. 2.)

 

The “meet and confer” requirement was met because counsel met and conferred telephonically on June 29, 2026.  (Declaration of Megan K. Hawkins ¶ 18.)

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Compelling Further Responses to Form Interrogatory No. 17.1, regarding Requests for Admission

 

A demanding party may move to compel further responses to form interrogatories where the demanding party fails to respond or responds with objections or incomplete answers.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, 2030.300.)  The demanding party must serve the motion to compel further within 45 days after service of verified responses, unless the parties agree, in writing, to extend the time to file.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (c).)  If the responding party serves unverified responses, the 45-day time limit does not run until verified responses are served.  (See Golf & Tennis Pro Shop, Inc. v. Superior Court (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 127, 135-136.)

 

Each interrogatory response must be complete and straightforward and contain (1) an answer containing the information sought to be discovered; (2) an exercise of the party’s option to produce writings; or (3) an objection.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.210, subd. (a), 2030.220, subd. (a).)  Responses containing objections must state the specific ground for the objection.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.240, subd. (b).) 

 

Because further form interrogatory responses were served responses to requests for admission numbers 65, 66, and 69, the court only addresses form interrogatory responses as to requests number 60, 61, 62, 63, and 64.

 

Requests for Admission numbers 60, 61, 62, 63, and 64 asks Plaintiff to “Admit that [Van Nuys Health & Wellness, ProHealth Advanced Medical Imaging, NeuroSpine Institute, Dynamic Physical Therapy, and Skyline Surgery Center were] not able to provide you with any [treatment or] imaging for the injuries you sustained as a result of the accident that gives rise to your complaint without getting prior authorization for that imaging from your attorneys.”

 

Plaintiff objected to each response in part, asserting the request is vague, seeking information protected by attorney-client privilege and the attorney work-product doctrine, seeking information protected by Plaintiff’s Constitutional right to privacy, seeking irrelevant information, and not reasonably calculated to lead to discovery of admissible evidence.

 

Defendant subsequently requested Plaintiff provide responses to corresponding form interrogatory number 17.1 in order to provide the facts underlying Plaintiff’s unqualified responses to the requests for admission.

 

Plaintiff provided identical form interrogatory responses for requests 60-65.

 

Plaintiff’s initial responses, served January 20, 2026, to subdivisions (b) through (d) contain objections:  the request as vague, ambiguous, and overbroad concerning the term “authorization”; seeking premature disclosure of expert opinion in violation of Code of Civil Procedure sections 2034.210, 2034.220, and 2034.270;  seeking information protected by attorney-client privilege and the attorney work-product doctrine;  Evidence regarding availability of health benefits to Plaintiff and evidence purporting to support “mitigation of damages,” is irrelevant, not reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence, time consuming, prejudicial, and misleading (Pebley v. Estrada (2018) Cal.App.5th 1266.);  argumentative, harassing, and improper; as for recoverable medical expenses, the presence or absence of insurance is totally irrelevant; and the this request is not reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence and seek information protected by Plaintiff’s Constitutional right to privacy.) California Code of Civil Procedure section 2017.010; Cal. Const., Art. I, § I.).

 

Plaintiff’s further responses are identical to the initial response.

 

Defendant contends further objection-free responses should be ordered because a plaintiff is only entitled to recover from a defendant the reasonable cost of reasonably necessary medical care that they have received.  Defendant seeks information concerning the persons who controlled the treatment Plaintiff received which Plaintiff is claiming was obtained in connection with this action.  If the named providers are controlled by Plaintiff’s counsel in regards to what treatment Plaintiff could receive, then questions regarding bias and the reasonableness and necessity of that treatment have been raised.  Communications between Plaintiff’s counsel and disclosed medical treaters is not privileged.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff asserts he sought medical care and “was not a party to any alleged backend conversations between his attorneys and the billing departments of his medical providers, nor does he possess personal knowledge of them.”  (Opposition at pp. 3:27-4:2.)  Plaintiff insists that his discussions with his attorney concerning medical treatment are absolutely protected by the attorney-client privilege.  (Opposition at p. 4:3-5.)  What Plaintiff’s counsel may have discussed with a medical provider is privileged attorney work-product, and unless Plaintiff has personal knowledge of these communications from another, independent source, which Plaintiff has already established he does not, this information remains completely protected.  (Opposition at p. 4:5-9.)

 

In response, Defendant further address Plaintiff’s responses to the underlying requests for admission.  Defendant also notes Plaintiff’s “lack of personal knowledge” argument is contrary to law because Plaintiff cannot avoid providing substantive responses to form interrogatory number 17.1 on a ground of personally lacks knowledge.  Plaintiff must make a reasonable inquiry.

 

However, the reasonable inquiry language is a requirement for responding to requests for admission, not the underlying form interrogatory number 17.1.  Defendant has not filed a corresponding motion to compel further responses to requests for admission numbers 60-64 to address Plaintiff’s issues with the underlying requests for admission and Plaintiff’s failure to state a “lack of knowledge” in response to the request for admission.

 

Evidence of an attorney’s referral of a client to lien-physicians is “relevant to the question of the reasonable value of the lien-physicians’ medical care because it may show bias or financial incentives on the part of the lien-physician”—specifically, the incentive to inflate medical bills in order to win future referrals from the attorney.  (Qaadir v. Figueroa (2021) 67 Cal.App.5th 790, 808; see also Nager v. Allstate Ins. Co. (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 284, 291.)

 

However, as phrased, requests numbers 60-64 ask Plaintiff to admit that his providers could not treat him at all without prior authorization or direction from his attorneys—a question that probes the specific terms of the attorney-client relationship and the communications and agreements between Plaintiff and counsel regarding treatment authorization.

 

The physician-patient privilege may be waived where Plaintiff brings an action for personal injuries.  (City and County of San Francisco v. Superior Court (1951) 37 Cal.2d 227, 232-233.)  However, a plaintiff’s personal injury claim that waives the physician-patient privilege does not waive the attorney-client privilege with respect to discussions or communications about the injury.  (Id. at pp. 234-238.)  Moreover, attorney-directed activities receive at least qualified work-product protection as a matter of law.  (Coito v. Superior Court (2012) 54 Cal.4th 480, 496.)  The authorization of medical treatment is precisely the kind of attorney-directed activity that the work-product doctrine is designed to protect.  Compelling Plaintiff to admit that his attorneys had to authorize treatment would reveal the nature and extent of counsel’s involvement in directing the litigation, which is the essence of work product.

 

Furthermore, request numbers 60-64 ask Plaintiff to admit or deny whether his attorney controlled which medical providers could treat or provide imaging for his alleged injuries or told medical providers to treat Plaintiff’s alleged injuries.  This directly implicates facts (the alleged injuries and treatment) and legal strategy (whether counsel told Plaintiff to go to specific medical providers for a strategic purpose) which is protected by the attorney work-product doctrine.

 

This is qualitatively different from asking whether counsel referred Plaintiff to a particular provider, and it squarely implicates both the attorney-client privilege and the work-product doctrine.  Moreover, the term “authorization” is vague and overbroad because it may include both privileged communications and non-privileged communications.  Therefore, the court sustains the attorney-client privilege and work-product doctrine objections.

 

The court finds that the “premature expert opinion” objection lacks merit because Defendant is not seeking a medical diagnosis or a causation analysis but whether certain named providers were not able to treat or provide imaging to Plaintiff for her alleged injuries because of lack of authorization.  No expert opinion is required to admit, deny, or provide underlying facts concerning whether Plaintiff’s personal knowledge of the reasons for the inability to treat and the lack imaging.  The court also sustains the “premature expert opinion” objection.

 

As for Plaintiff’s privacy concerns, a plaintiff who files a lawsuit for damages arising from a personal injury puts the injury and its related body parts in controversy.  (Vinson v. Superior Court (1987) 43 Cal.3d 833, 389-390 (Vinson).)  However, the privacy right waiver created by filing a lawsuit “must be narrowly rather than expansively construed.”  (Id. at p. 842.)  “[A]n implicit waiver of a party’s constitutional rights encompasses only discovery directly relevant to the plaintiff’s claim and essential to the fair resolution of the lawsuit.” (Id.)

 

Here, the “compelling interest” standard applies because of Plaintiff’s fundamental interest and personal autonomy to his medical records records.  Whether a lien arrangement exists between counsel and the named providers is relevant to determining whether the treating providers have inflated the costs of treatment to receive further attorney referrals in the future.  This directly bears on the damages issue.  Although Plaintiff has a valid privacy interest, discovery of the reasonable costs of Plaintiff’s past and future treatment is directly relevant to Plaintiff’s claims.  Thus, Plaintiff has waived her privacy rights as to the requested information and the court overrules the privacy objection.

 

Because the court sustains Plaintiff’s attorney-client privilege, work-product doctrine, and vague and overbroad objections, the court denies Defendant’s motion to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to form interrogatory number 17.1 as it pertains to requests for admission numbers 60, 61, 62, 63, and 64.

 

 

            B.  Compelling Further Responses to Special Interrogatories

 

A demanding party may move to compel further responses to interrogatories where the demanding party fails to respond or responds with objections or incomplete answers.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, 2030.300.)  The demanding party must serve the motion to compel further within 45 days after service of verified responses, unless the parties agree, in writing, to extend the time to file.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (c).)  If the responding party serves unverified responses, the 45-day time limit does not run until verified responses are served.  (See Golf & Tennis Pro Shop, Inc. v. Superior Court (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 127, 135-136.)

 

Each interrogatory response must be complete and straightforward and contain (1) an answer containing the information sought to be discovered;  (2) an exercise of the party’s option to produce writings;  or (3) an objection.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.210, subd. (a), 2030.220, subd. (a).)  Responses containing objections must state the specific ground for the objection.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.240, subd. (b).) 

 

Special Interrogatory number 1 asks Plaintiff to state his cell phone number on the date of the incident.  Plaintiff served an objection-only response on January 20, 2026.  No supplemental response was served.

 

Plaintiff first objected to this response as vague, ambiguous, and overbroad.  The court overrules these objections because the request as phrased is clear and concise.

 

Plaintiff also objected to the interrogatory as seeking information not relevant or reasonably calculated to lead to discovery of admissible evidence.  However, Defendant contends the information is necessary for Defendant to test Plaintiff’s position that he was not on his cell phone during the incident.  Defendant’s position is that Plaintiff suddenly and without warning, made a right turn across the number 2 and number 3 lanes, directly in front of Defendant’s vehicle.  Plaintiff’s phone number will allow Defendant to determine whether Plaintiff was distracted while driving and whether there were any potential witnesses Plaintiff contacted shortly after the accident.  The court agrees and overrules Plaintiff’s not reasonably calculated objection.  (See Puerto v. Superior Court (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1249-1250 [identifying potential witnesses is “routine and essential part of pretrial discovery”].)

 

Next, Plaintiff claims the interrogatory seeks to invade Plaintiff’s right to privacy, demands premature expert opinions, seeks to violate attorney-client privilege and attorney work-product doctrine.  Plaintiff’s response does not provide sufficient evidence for the court to determine whether Plaintiff providing his cell phone number would reveal confidential attorney-client communications or attorney litigation strategy.  Finally, Defendant’s countervailing interest in Plaintiff’s phone number is substantial and directly tied to disputed facts in this matter—determining whether Plaintiff used his phone during the accident—which could establish comparative or contributory negligence.  (See Defendant’s Answer ¶ 2.)  This is a legitimate and important litigation interest which outweighs Plaintiff’s privacy interest as Plaintiff has put his injuries and the facts surrounding the incident at issue by filing this action.  Thus, the court overrules the attorney-client privilege, work-product doctrine, and privacy objections.

 

Therefore, the court grants Defendant’s motion to compel a further objection-free response to special interrogatory number 1 in Defendant’s first set of special interrogatories served to Plaintiff on October 31, 2025.
