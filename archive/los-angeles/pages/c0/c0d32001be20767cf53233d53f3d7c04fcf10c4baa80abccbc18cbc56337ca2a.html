Case Number: 24NWCV00424    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: P

HYNES PACKING COMPANY v. JADE SPEC LLC, ET AL.

CASE NO.:  24NWCV00424 

HEARING:  09/15/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#9

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Plaintiff Hynes Packing Company’s motion for attorney’s fees and costs is GRANTED in the amount of $20,689.24 reflecting $18,112.50 in attorney’s fees and $2,576.74 in costs.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Plaintiff Hynes Packing Company dba Koret Business Park - Santa Fe Springs (Plaintiff) moves for an award of attorney’s fees and costs.

 

Background

 

This is a breach of contract action. On February 8, 2024, Plaintiff filed this action against Defendants Maimouna Jade Spec LLC, a Nevada Corp., Dylan Rodriguez, Steve Weiss (collectively, Defendants) and Does 1 to 10 for commercial unlawful detainer. Following the Court’s ruling on Defendants’ first demurrer, on November 7, 2024, Plaintiff filed a first amended complaint (FAC) against Defendants and Does 1 to 20 alleging breach of contract.

 

The FAC alleges the following: Plaintiff owns the real property located at 13128 Telegraph Road Unit H, Santa Fe Springs, California 90670 (Subject Property), a multi-tenant industrial building. (FAC, ¶¶ 1, 6.) On February 27, 2020, Defendants each entered a written agreement (Agreement) to rent the Subject Property as a month-to-month tenancy. (FAC, ¶ 7.) The complaint alleges Defendants have failed to pay any rent after January 1, 2022. (FAC, ¶ 9.) On January 25, 2024, Weiss was served with a 3-day notice to pay rent or quit on behalf of all tenants, and Defendants subsequently refused to pay rent or vacate the Subject Property. (FAC, ¶ 12.) The FAC asserts a single cause of action for breach of contract against all Defendants.

 

On July 8, 2026, this matter was called for a court trial and the parties informed the Court that the case had settled.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      

 

Legal Standard

 

Civil Code section 1717 subdivision (a) provides, “[i]n any action on a contract, where the contract specifically provides that attorney’s fees and costs, which are incurred to enforce that contract, shall be awarded either to one of the parties or to the prevailing party, then the party who is determined to be the party prevailing on the contract, whether he or she is the party specified in the contract or not, shall be entitled to reasonable attorney's fees in addition to other costs.” “Except as attorney’s fees are specifically provided for by statute, the measure and mode of compensation of attorneys and counselors at law is left to the agreement, express or implied, of the parties; but parties to actions or proceedings are entitled to their costs.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1021.)

 

The matter of reasonableness of a party’s attorney fees is within the sound discretion of the trial court. (Bruckman v. Parliament Escrow Co. (1989) 190 Cal.App.3d 1051, 1062.) When assessing the amount of any attorney’s fee award, courts typically determine what is reasonable through the application of the “lodestar” method. Under the lodestar method, a base amount is calculated from a compilation of (1) time reasonably spent and (2) the reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 48); see also Meister v. Regents of University of California (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 437, 448-449 [holding that the lodestar method applies to statutory attorney fees award unless the underlying statute provides for another method of calculation].)

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiff moves for an award of attorney’s fees and costs pursuant to the parties’ commercial lease (Lease) and the settlement reached between the parties. This motion is unopposed.

 

Plaintiff requests a total of $22,039.24 reflecting (1) $17,212.50 for 38.25 hours in attorney fees actually incurred through July 14, 2026; (2) $2,576.74 in recoverable litigation costs and expenses; and (3) plus up to $2,250.00 for anticipated fees arising out of this motion.

 

          Entitlement to Fees & Prevailing Party

 

Plaintiff provides a copy of the Lease entered into between Plaintiff and Defendants. The Lease provides the following:

 

31. Attorneys Fees. If any Party brings an action or proceeding involving the Premises whether founded in tort, contract or equity, or to declare rights hereunder, the Prevailing Party (as hereafter defined) in any such proceeding, action, or appeal thereon, shall be entitled to reasonable attorneys' fees. Such fees may be awarded in the same suit or recovered in a separate suit, whether or not such action or proceeding is pursued to decision or judgment. The term, "Prevailing Party" shall include, without limitation, a Party who substantially obtains or defeats the relief sought, as the case may be, whether by compromise, settlement, judgment, or the abandonment by the other Party of its claim or defense. The attorneys’ fees award shall not be computed in accordance with any court fee schedule, but shall be such as to fully reimburse all attorneys' fees reasonably incurred. In addition, Lessor shall be entitled to attorneys' fees, costs and expenses incurred in the preparation and service of notices of Default and consultations in connection therewith, whether or not a legal action is subsequently commenced in connection with such Default or resulting Breach.

 

(Orloff Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. A.) Plaintiff contends that Civil Code section 1717, subdivision (b)(2), which provides that ordinarily there is no prevailing party when an action has been dismissed pursuant to a settlement, does not apply here. Plaintiff refers to Jackson v. Homeowners Association Monte Vista Estates-East (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 773, 777-78 in which parties entered into a settlement agreement which reserved the issue of an award of attorney’s fees to the trial court. The Court of Appeal, in affirming the trial court’s award of attorney’s fees, concluded “that ‘where there is no dismissal, a settling party may move for contractually based attorney fees under section 1717 unless the right to them was expressly or impliedly waived in the settlement agreement.’” (Id., at p. 784-85.) The Jackson court noted that the settlement agreement was enforceable under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 and that “[u]nder the settlement agreement, the action would not be dismissed until the various actions contemplated in the agreement had been taken.” (Id., at p. 785-86.) The court reasoned “[s]ince all substantive issues in the case, except for the award of attorney fees, have been resolved by the settlement agreement, the trial court could determine the prevailing party.” (Id., at p. 786.) Plaintiff shows that like in Jackson, here, the Court approved the settlement, stated that “[a]ttorney’s fees shall be sought by noticed motion,” and ordered Plaintiff’s counsel to file a request for dismissal while maintaining jurisdiction under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.4. (Order, 7/8/26.) Thus, under Jackson, the Court agrees that it may determine the prevailing party.

 

Plaintiff contends that pursuant to the settlement, it is the prevailing party under the Lease as it obtained $90,000, possession and control of the premises, final property-removal conditions, protection of fixtures, indemnity for move-related damage, and finality as to the security deposit. (Order, 7/8/26.) Plaintiff argues that this is consistent with its litigation objections as its complaint sought possession, $45,525 in then-accrued rent, ongoing damages, and pre-judgment interest and fees. Based on the recovery sought by Plaintiff, the Court agrees that Plaintiff is the prevailing party as it recovered greater relief in this action on the Lease. (Civ. Code, § 1717, subd. (b)(1) [“the party prevailing on the contract shall be the party who recovered a greater relief in the action on the contract.”].)

 

          Reasonableness of the Fees

 

In assessing a motion for attorney’s fees, “[a] trial court may not rubber stamp a request for attorney fees, but must determine the number of hours reasonably expended.” (Morris v. Hyundai Motor America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 38.)

A “reasonable” hourly rate is the prevailing rate charged by attorneys of similar skill and experience in the relevant community. (PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.) “[C]ounsels’ time records [should be used] as the starting point for [a court’s] lodestar determination.” (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of CSU (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 397.)

 

Plaintiff seeks attorney’s fees and costs arising out of both the unlawful detainer action (Case No. 24NWUD02512) between the parties as well as the instant civil action. Plaintiff contends that the nature of the two actions are intertwined because they seek possession and damages under the same Lease. The Court finds this request consistent with the terms of the Lease. (See Orloff Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. A.)

 

Plaintiff provides a copy of Plaintiff’s counsel Paul Orloff’s billing entries. (Orloff Decl., ¶ 11.) Mr. Orloff has an hourly rate of $450 and 38.25 hours of work. (Orloff Decl., ¶ 2.) Mr. Orloff explains that this action remained active for twenty-nine months, involved possession and lease damages, and required law and motion and trial preparation. (Orloff Decl., ¶ 12.) Mr. Orloff has practiced civil litigation for more than twenty-four years including in commercial lease disputes, contract litigation, and unlawful detainer. (Orloff Decl., ¶ 2.) The Court finds this amount reasonable.

 

Plaintiff additionally seeks up to $2,250 or “up to 5.0 additional hours to finalize and file the motion, review any opposition, prepare a reply, and appear at the hearing.” (Orloff Decl., ¶ 13.) Given that this motion is unopposed, the Court reduces this request by 3 hours, or $1,350.

 

          Costs

 

Plaintiff also requests $2,576.74 in litigation costs. This “consists of $1,585.40 in filing and e-filing charges, $782.74 in service-of process charges, $145.00 in sheriff fees, $15.60 in copies, and $48.00 in trial binders and exhibit dividers.” (Orloff Decl., ¶ 12.) Plaintiff’s request for costs is granted.

 

In sum, Plaintiff’s motion for attorney’s fees and costs is GRANTED in the amount of $20,689.24 reflecting $18,112.50 in attorney’s fees and $2,576.74 in costs.


Case Number: 24NWCV01784    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: P

KORRINE GILYANNA, ET AL. v. CHP HOSPITAL MANAGEMENT, LLC, ET AL.

HEARING:  09/15/2026 @ 9:30 AM

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Plaintiff Korinne Gilyanna’s motion for reconsideration of this Court’s Order on April 20, 2026 is DENIED.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Plaintiff Korinne Gilyanna (Plaintiff) moves for reconsideration of this Court’s Order on April 20, 2026 (4/20/26 Order).

 

Background

 

On June 5, 2024, Plaintiffs Korinne Gilyanna, individually and as successor in interest to the Estate of Steven Esner, and Stuart Esner filed this medical malpractice action against CHP Hospital Management, LLC dba Coast Plaza Hospital (Defendant), Coast Plaza Doctors Hospital, David Tao, M.D. (Dr. Tao), Aida Salatinjants, M.D. (Dr. Salatinjants), Patricia Luu, NP., and Does 1 to 100 (collectively, Defendants). On December 17, 2024, Plaintiffs filed an Amendment to Complaint replacing Doe 11 with Defendant Harris Musafer, M.D. (Dr. Musafer). The complaint alleges that Decedent Steven Esner (Decedent) was a patient of Defendants and that each defendant failed to evaluate and/or treat Decedent resulting in his death on June 18, 2023. The complaint asserts a single cause of action for medical malpractice.

 

On December 19, 2025, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion to compel Defendant to produce (1) Defendant’s current Director of Medical Staff; (2) Defendant’s current secretary that prepares minutes for the Defendant’s Board of Director; and (3) the person most knowledgeable concerning Patricia Luu, NP’s application for privileges for deposition. (12/19/25 Order.)

 

On January 9, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion to produce (1) Victor Carrasco [second session]; (2) Mark David, RN; (3) The person most knowledgeable concerning the identity of each infectious disease physician who was on staff at YOUR hospital in June 2023; (4) Its officer, director, and/or agent who was the nursing supervisor for Haeryun Jeong, R.N. on June 17, 2023; (5) “its officer, director, and/or agent Jocelyn whose name appears on Exhibit 1 attached”; and (6) Vincent Green, M.D for deposition. (1/9/26 Order.)

 

On April 20, 2026, the Court granted in part Plaintiff’s Motion to Enforce Court Orders Requiring Defendant to Produce Deponents for Deposition. (4/20/26 Order.)

 

On July 21, 2026, the Court granted in part, denied in part, and found moot in part Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Deposition of Defendant. The motion was granted as to Defendant’s PMK for the Identity of Board of Directors as of June 1, 2023 and Identity of Secretaries Who Prepared Board Minutes in 2023, among others. (7/1/26 Order.)

 

Legal Standard

 

“When an application for an order has been made to a judge, or to a court, and refused in whole or in part…any party affected by the order may, within 10 days after service upon the party of written notice of entry of the order and based upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, make application to the same judge or court that made the order, to reconsider the matter and modify, amend, or revoke the prior order. The party making the application shall state by affidavit what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a).)

 

A motion for reconsideration under Section 1008 requires that the moving party present new or different facts that were not previously considered by the Court. (New York Times Co. v. Superior Court (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 206, 212-13.) However, the burden under Section 1008 “is comparable to that of a party seeking a new trial on the ground of newly discovered evidence: the information must be such that the moving party could not, with reasonable diligence, have discovered or produced it at the trial.” (Id. at p. 212; Even Zohar Construction & Remodeling, Inc. v. Bellaire Townhouses, LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 830, 833 [finding that Section 1008 imposes the special requirement of having to not only show new or different facts, circumstances, or law, but also to “show diligence with a satisfactory explanation for not presenting the new or different information earlier…”].) A disagreement with a ruling is not a new fact that will support the granting of a motion for reconsideration. (Gilberd v. AC Transit (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 1494, 1500.) “[A] court acts in excess of jurisdiction when it grants a motion to reconsider that is not based on ‘new or different facts, circumstances, or law.’” (Ibid.)

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiff moves for reconsideration of the Court’s 4/20/26 Order or, alternatively, for an order enforcing the 12/19/25 Order requiring Defendant to produce for deposition its current secretary who prepared minutes for its Board of Directors. This motion is unopposed.

 

The 4/20/26 Order sought to enforce the 12/19/25 Order. (4/20/26 Order.) The Court denied the motion as it related to enforcing the 12/19/25 Order regarding “the deposition of the current secretary for the Board of Directors” based on Defendant’s representation that “the deposition of the current secretary for the Board of Directors, Fernando Vasquez has been completed. (Irwin Decl., ¶¶ 7-8.)” (4/20/26 Order.) The Court notes that Plaintiff, at the time, repeated the fact that the deposition had been completed in her reply to that motion. (See Reply to Mot., 4/9/26.)

 

Plaintiff moves for reconsideration of the 4/20/26 Order to the extent that the Court accepted Defendant’s representation that the deposition had been completed.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a) not only requires “new or different facts, circumstances, or law,” but also that the moving party “must provide a satisfactory explanation for the failure to produce the evidence at an earlier time.” (New York Times Co., supra, 135 Cal.App.4th at p. 212.)

 

Here, Plaintiff argues that the purported new fact, that Defendant allegedly misled the Court that the deposition completed, became apparent only after reading the deposition transcript after the April 20, 2026 hearing. However, Plaintiff does not explain why she did not raise this fact during that hearing. Plaintiff supplies the deposition transcript showing that Plaintiff’s counsel deposed Fernando Vasquez on April 1, 2026. (Howard Decl., Exh. 3.) The deposition transcript shows that Fernando Vasquez represented that he does not prepare the minutes for the board meetings. (Howard Decl., Exh. 3.) Thus, it is reasonable to infer that Plaintiff’s counsel was made aware of the alleged fact that Fernando Vasquez was not the “current secretary who prepared minutes for its Board of Directors” prior to Plaintiff’s deadline to reply to the opposition and prior to the April 20, 2026 hearing.

 

Plaintiff does not provide a “satisfactory explanation for the failure to produce the evidence” at the time this motion was heard. (New York Times Co., supra, 135 Cal.App.4th at p. 212.) “The burden under section 1008 is comparable to that of a party seeking a new trial on the ground of newly discovered evidence: the information must be such that the moving party could not, with reasonable diligence, have discovered or produced it at the trial.” (Id., at p. 212-13.)

Plaintiff does not explain why Plaintiff’s counsel, whom the cited documents show conducted the deposition, could not have recalled that Fernando Vasquez was not the proper deponent at the time of Plaintiff’s reply or the April 20, 2026 hearing. Further, Plaintiff does not explain why Plaintiff represented the opposite—that the deposition was completed—during the April 20, 2026 hearing. Plaintiff also does not show why it could not have moved to continue the April 20, 2026 hearing to wait until receipt of the transcript before representing to the Court that the deposition was completed. Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that Plaintiff does not provide a sufficient explanation for not raising this purported misrepresentation at the April 20, 2026 hearing when the relevant facts were directly communicated to Plaintiff’s counsel prior to that hearing.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion for reconsideration is DENIED.

 

Plaintiff’s alternative request, to enforce the 12/19/25 Order, seeks duplicative relief as the instant motion for reconsideration and is therefore also denied.

Case Number: 24NWCV02540    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: P

TONYA RENITA FORD v. ALLINGHAM COURT HOMEOWNERS ASSOCIATION

CASE NO.: 24NWCV02540

HEARING:  09/15/2026 @ 10:30 AM

 

#14

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Plaintiff Tonya Renita Ford’s motion to compel further responses to form interrogatories, set one from Defendant Allingham Court Homeowners Association is DENIED.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Plaintiff Tonya Renita Ford (Plaintiff) moves to compel Defendant Allingham Court Homeowners Association (Defendant) to provide further responses to form interrogatories (FROGs), set one.

 

Background

 

On August 13, 2024, Plaintiff filed this personal injury action against Defendant and Does 1 to 50. The complaint alleges that Defendant and Does 1 through 50 owned and operated the premises located at 13900 Studebaker Rd, Norwalk, CA 90650 (Premises). (Complaint, ¶ 5.) On August 16, 2022, as an invitee on the Premises, “Plaintiff…was injured when a mirror fell on Plaintiff causing the mirror to shatter all over Plaintiff’s body as a result of a dangerous and/or unsafe condition located on said [Premises].” (Complaint, ¶ 10.) The complaint asserts a cause of action for negligence/premises liability.

 

On January 27, 2026, the Court granted in part and denied in part Plaintiff’s motion to compel Defendant to provide further responses to requests for admission (RFAs), set one.

 

Legal Standard

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300, subdivision (a), “[o]n receipt of a response to interrogatories, the propounding party may move for an order compelling a further response if the propounding party deems that any of the following apply:

 

(1) An answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete.

(2) An exercise of the option to produce documents under Section 2030.230 is unwarranted or the required specification of those documents is inadequate.

(3) An objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general.”

 

“As a litigant, it is entitled to demand answers to its interrogatories, as a matter of right, and without a prior showing, unless the party on whom those interrogatories are served objects and shows cause why the questions are not within the purview of the code section.” (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541.) “While the party propounding interrogatories may have the burden of filing a motion to compel if it finds the answers it receives unsatisfactory, the burden of justifying any objection and failure to respond remains at all times with the party resisting an interrogatory.” (Ibid.)

 

Meet and Confer

 

The motion to compel further discovery shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (b)(1).) A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.)

 

Plaintiff’s counsel indicates that Plaintiff raised certain deficiencies to defense counsel via email but Defendant refused to provide a further response. (Lopez Decl., ¶ 6.) While exchanging emails is not sufficient under the Code, the Court will proceed to rule on this motion in the interest of judicial economy and because this defect is not raised by Defendant in opposition.

 

Timeliness

 

“Unless notice of this motion is given within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the propounding party and the responding party have agreed in writing, the propounding party waives any right to compel a further response to the interrogatories.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (c).) The time to make a response is extended by two court days for electronic service. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1010.6., subd. (a)(3)(B).) The 45-day deadline after responses is “mandatory and jurisdictional.” (Sexton v. Superior Court (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1409, citing Vidal Sassoon, Inc. v. Superior Court (1983) 147 Cal.App.3d 681, 685.)

 

Defendant argues this motion is untimely. Plaintiff propounded FROGs, set one, on December 17, 2025 and Defendant served responses on February 13, 2026. (Lopez Decl., ¶ 3.) Defendant explains that it granted an extension of the deadline for two weeks after the IDC. (Chavez Decl., ¶ 11.) Defendant states that the deadline for the Court to decide on the IDC was March 30, 2026, making the deadline to file this motion April 13, 2026, and the deadline to hold the IDC was April 9, 2026, making the latest deadline to file this motion, April 23, 2026. (Chavez Decl., ¶¶ 12-13.) This motion was filed on April 30, 2026.

 

In reply, Plaintiff notes that Defendant granted the extension to “to 2-weeks after the IDC goes forward.” (Lopez Dec., Exh. A.) Plaintiff argues that because the IDC was not scheduled and did not go forward, then the deadline had not manifested.

 

Here, the Court agrees with Defendant that the latest days the Court could have approved the IDC or held the IDC serve as appropriate deadlines. The parties were aware by March 30, 2026, that the IDC would not go forward. Moreover, an IDC was held on March 27, 2026. It is not reasonable for Plaintiff to assume that the deadline to file this motion would extend indefinitely once it was aware that no IDC would take place. Once Plaintiff was aware on April 23, 2026, the deadline for the IDC to go forward, that an IDC was not going to go forward, Plaintiff could have sought another extension. Therefore, the Court finds this motion untimely.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses to form interrogatories, set one is DENIED.

 

The Court does not grant either side’s request for monetary sanctions as the confusion regarding this deadline may have been resolved informally through a good faith meet and confer process.

Case Number: 24NWCV05070    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: P

Jessica Coll v. Alliance RV, LLC, et al.,

This lemon law action was filed on December 30, 2024. Trial is scheduled to begin on January 25, 2027. Defendant Alliance RV, LLC, joined by Giant Inland Empire RV Center, Inc. MARIA JIMENEZ, ET AL. v. FORD MOTOR COMPANY, ET AL.

CASE NO.: 25NWCV01742

HEARING:  09/15/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#11

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

I.             Plaintiffs Maria Jimenez and Victoria Elias’s motion to compel further responses to requests for production, set one is MOOT.

 

II.            Plaintiffs’ motion to compel further responses to special interrogatories, set one, is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART.

 

III.          Plaintiffs’ request for monetary sanctions is DENIED.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Plaintiffs Maria Jimenez and Victoria Elias (Plaintiffs) move to compel further responses from Defendant Ford Motor Company (Defendant) to (1) requests for production (RPDs), set one and (2) special interrogatories (SROGs), set one.

 

Background

 

On May 14, 2025, Plaintiffs this lemon law action against Defendants Ford Motor Company, Ted Jones Ford, Inc. dba Ken Grody Ford Buena Park, and Does 1 through 10. The complaint alleges Plaintiffs experienced defects and nonconformities to warranty with their 2022 Ford Bronco, VIN: 3FMCR9B66NRE15996. (Complaint, ¶¶ 16, 17.) The complaint asserts three causes of action: (1) violation of Song-Beverly Act – breach of express warranty, (2) violation of the Song-Beverly Act section 1793.2, and (3) negligent repair.

 

On July 30, 2026 and on August 7, 2026, Plaintiffs filed Stipulated Protective Orders.

 

Plaintiffs’ motion to compel further responses to RPDs, set one first came on for hearing on August 18, 2026. The Court continued the hearing on the motion based on potential supplemental production and requested supplemental briefing on the matter.

 

Legal Standard

 

          Requests for Production

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.260, subdivision (a), a party must respond to requests for production of documents within 30 days of service. Failure to do so waives all objections including privilege or on the protection of work product. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).) Parties may move for a further response to requests for production of documents where a statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete, a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive, or where an objection is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).) 

 

Interrogatories

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300, subdivision (a), “[o]n receipt of a response to interrogatories, the propounding party may move for an order compelling a further response if the propounding party deems that any of the following apply:

 

(1) An answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete.

(2) An exercise of the option to produce documents under Section 2030.230 is unwarranted or the required specification of those documents is inadequate.

(3) An objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general.”

 

“As a litigant, it is entitled to demand answers to its interrogatories, as a matter of right, and without a prior showing, unless the party on whom those interrogatories are served objects and shows cause why the questions are not within the purview of the code section.” (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541.) “While the party propounding interrogatories may have the burden of filing a motion to compel if it finds the answers it receives unsatisfactory, the burden of justifying any objection and failure to respond remains at all times with the party resisting an interrogatory.” (Ibid.)

 

Motion to Compel Further Responses to Requests for Production

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiffs move to compel Defendant to serve further responses to RPDs, set one, nos. 16-18, 20-30, 45-46, 47, 49-54, and 55-59. Plaintiffs argue that Defendant unilaterally conditioned production on a protective order among other arguments.

 

On August 5, 2026, Defendant filed its opposition. In the opposition, Defendant states that Plaintiffs filed a stipulated protective order on July 30, 2026 and “[o]n July 28, 2026, and August 6, 2026, Ford produced confidential documents subject to the protective order.” (Perez Decl., ¶¶ 14-15.)

 

In Plaintiffs’ supplemental brief, Plaintiffs acknowledge that Defendant produced some documents but argues that the production is incomplete. However, Plaintiffs do not address which requests were supplemented. Plaintiffs’ recourse for supplemental responses is to file a new motion to compel further responses based on the 45-day deadline triggered upon the supplemental production. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).) This requires a new separate statement with the updated arguments. The Court continued this motion to determine whether certain requests were not supplemented and thus remained at issue. Since the supplemental briefing does not affirm what the supplemental responses were responsive to, the Court presumes that all responses were supplemented.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ motion to compel further responses to requests for production, set one is MOOT.

 

Motion to Compel Further Responses to Special Interrogatories

 

Meet and Confer

 

The motion to compel further discovery shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (b)(1).) A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.)

 

The parties exchanged meet and confer letters. (Leite Decl., ¶¶ 5-7.) While a telephonic call or other meeting is preferred, the Court finds the meet and confer process adequate based on their efforts and the interests of judicial economy.

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiffs move to compel Defendant to provide further responses to SROGs, set one, nos. 10, 26, 47, 49 58.

 

          SROGs, set one, no. 10

 

SROGs, set one, no. 10 requests facts supporting Defendant’s contention that the Subject Vehicle does not qualify for repurchase. (Separate Statement.) Defendant responds that it makes that contention and lists certain documents that contain the supporting facts. (Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs argue that the response is improper because it leaves Plaintiffs to speculate what facts within the documents are the relevant facts. Plaintiffs argue that this response does not properly invoke Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.230. Plaintiffs further establish good cause as it is plainly relevant as it relates to the Subject Vehicle.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.230 provides: “If the answer to an interrogatory would necessitate the preparation or the making of a compilation, abstract, audit, or summary of or from the documents of the party to whom the interrogatory is directed, and if the burden or expense of preparing or making it would be substantially the same for the party propounding the interrogatory as for the responding party, it is a sufficient answer to that interrogatory to refer to this section and to specify the writings from which the answer may be derived or ascertained. This specification shall be in sufficient detail to permit the propounding party to locate and to identify, as readily as the responding party can, the documents from which the answer may be ascertained. The responding party shall then afford to the propounding party a reasonable opportunity to examine, audit, or inspect these documents and to make copies, compilations, abstracts, or summaries of them.”

 

The Court agrees that Defendant does not show that the response to this requires “the preparation or the making of a compilation, abstract, audit, or summary of or from the documents of the party to whom the interrogatory is directed, and if the burden or expense of preparing or making it would be substantially the same for the party propounding the interrogatory as for the responding party.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.230.) Moreover, even if Defendant did show that it applies, Defendant does not properly specify where in the documents Plaintiffs could locate and identify the responsive facts. Thus, the Court finds a further response to SROGS, set one, no. 10 warranted.

 

          SROGs, set one, no. 26

 

SROGs, set one, no. 26 is as follows:

 

INTERROGATORY NO. 26:

Describe all training that YOU provided from 2022 to the present regarding how to evaluate a pre-litigation repurchase request under the Song-Beverly Act.

 

(Separate Statement.) In response, Defendant refers to its Warranty Policy and Procedure Manuals for 2022 to present and, beyond that, objects to the SROG. (Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs argue that this SROG is relevant and material. Plaintiffs argue that Defendant’s production does non-responsive because it “does not identify, describe, summarize, or even confirm the existence or nonexistence of any training materials, sessions, modules, instruction, or criteria used to train its personnel on how to evaluate repurchase requests, nor the frequency of the training.” (Mot., 7:11-13.)

 

Here, the Court’s role is not to go through the production and determine whether the documents are responsive or not. However, to the extent that the response cites to documents, the response reflects the same issue as in No. 10, that it does not properly invoke Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.230. Thus, further responses are warranted.

 

          SROGs, set one, nos. 47 & 49-58

 

In part, the requests are as follows:

 

SPECIAL INTERROGATORY NO. 47.

Describe YOUR efforts to preserve relevant and discoverable information in this matter […]

 

SPECIAL INTERROGATORY NO. 49.

IDENTIFY each person responsible for the operations, maintenance, expansion, archiving and upkeep of the network where YOUR customer contact call logs are electronically stored from 2022 to present.

 

SPECIAL INTERROGATORY NO. 51.

IDENTIFY each person responsible for the operations, maintenance, expansion, archiving and upkeep of the network where YOUR policies and procedures for evaluating a repurchase or replacement under the Song-Beverly Act are electronically stored from 2022 to present.

 

SPECIAL INTERROGATORY NO. 56:

State YOUR document/data retention or destruction policies that govern YOUR customer call logs from 2022 to present.

 

(Separate Statement.) In response, Defendant objects on the grounds that the SROGs are overbroad, unduly burdensome, irrelevant, not limited to the allegations, protected under attorney-client privilege and the work product doctrine. (Separate Statement.) Defendant also states that “Plaintiffs have not identified any documents that Ford has allegedly failed to produce in this case or to otherwise articulate why information relating to Ford’s records management program that governs technical assistance cases from 2022 to present is relevant in this case. This is particularly true because no dealer contacted Ford regarding technical assistance for subject 2022 Ford Bronco.” (Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs argue No. 47 is relevant because preservation efforts show the adequacy of Defendant’s production and spoliation issues. Plaintiffs argue that nos. 49-58 go to where and how Defendant maintains consumer complaint and warranty claim information as well as whether Defendant has withheld or destroyed responsive evidence which may be relevant to willfulness.

 

Here, the Court finds that Nos. 47 and 49-58 are not relevant to this action. Nos. 47 and 55-58 are not related to Plaintiffs’ claims, but rather related to Defendant’s information retention process. As argued by Defendant, Plaintiffs have not shown that there has been a loss or spoliation of documents in this action that requires facts regarding preservation efforts. Thus, Plaintiffs do not establish good cause.

 

As to Nos. 49-54, the Court sustains Defendant’s overbreadth objections. The SROGs do not relate to the Subject Vehicle, similar vehicles, or vehicles in the State of California. The SROGs relate to Defendant’s record-keeping processes as a company and Plaintiffs do not show the relevance of such information. Plaintiffs’ cited cases do not support the discoverability of the information sought here. (See, gen., Lukather v. General Motors, LLC (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 1041, 1051; Jensen v. BMW of North America, Inc. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112, 136.) Therefore, the Court does not find further responses to Nos. 47 and 49-58 warranted.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ motion to compel further responses to special interrogatories, set one is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART. Plaintiffs’ motion is GRANTED as to SROGs, set one, nos. 10 and 26 and DENIED as to SROGs, set one, nos. 47 and 49-58.

 

Defendant is ORDERED to provide further responses, as described here, to special interrogatories, set one, nos. 10 and 26 within 30 days of this Order. This date may be extended pursuant to agreement between the parties.

 

Sanctions

 

Based on the Order finding in favor of each party on different SROGs, the Court does not award monetary sanctions.

HECTOR MARQUEZ-MESA v. AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR CO., INC.

CASE NO.: 25NWCV01879 

HEARING: 09/15/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#12

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Plaintiff Hector Marquez-Mesa’s request for attorney’s fees and costs is GRANTED in the amount of $15,756.96 reflecting $14,540 in attorney’s fees and $1,216.96 in costs.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Plaintiff Hector Marquez-Mesa (Plaintiff) moves for an award of attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses.

 

Background

 

On May 23, 2025, Plaintiff filed this lemon law action against Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (Defendant) and Does 1 through 10. The complaint alleges that Plaintiff experienced defects and nonconformities to warranty with his 2022 Honda Pilot. (Complaint, ¶¶ 28-30.) The complaint asserts three causes of action: (1) violation of Song-Beverly Act – breach of express warranty; (2) violation of Song-Beverly Act – breach of implied warranty; and violation of Consumers Legal Remedies Act.

 

On March 19, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Settlement of Entire Case indicating that the parties entered into a conditional settlement agreement.

 

Legal Standard

 

The Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act provides for the award of attorneys’ fees to prevailing plaintiffs as follows: 

 

If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney's fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action. 

 

(Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).) 

 

“Courts have uniformly held that the party moving for statutory attorney fees or sanctions has the burden of proof.” (Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 247, citation omitted.) The matter of reasonableness of a party’s attorney fees is within the sound discretion of the trial court. (Bruckman v. Parliament Escrow Co. (1989) 190 Cal.App.3d 1051, 1062.) When assessing the amount of any attorney’s fee award, courts typically determine what is reasonable through the application of the “lodestar” method. Under the lodestar method, a base amount is calculated from a compilation of (1) time reasonably spent and (2) the reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 48); see also Meister v. Regents of University of California (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 437, 448-449 [holding that the lodestar method applies to statutory attorney fees award unless the underlying statute provides for another method of calculation].)

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

Plaintiff requests the Court takes judicial notice of three minute orders from prior civil actions in the Los Angeles County Superior Court.

 

Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice is GRANTED pursuant to Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (d).

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiff moves for an award of attorney’s fees and costs in in the amount of $21,483.96, including: (1) $12.590 in attorney’s fees, with a 1.3 times multiplier (for a total of $16,367), (2) an additional $3,000 for Plaintiff's counsel to review any opposition, draft a reply, and attend the hearing, with a 1.3 times multiplier (for a total of $3,900), and (3) $1,216.96 costs.

 

          Entitlement to Fees

 

Plaintiff argues that he is the prevailing party pursuant to the parties’ settlement agreement. Plaintiff signed Defendant’s Section 998 Offer to Compromise on March 19, 2026. (Yousef Decl., ¶¶ 20-21.) Defendant does not dispute that Plaintiff is the prevailing party and thus entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees and costs.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff is entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees and costs.

 

Reasonableness of the Hourly Rates

 

A “reasonable” hourly rate is the prevailing rate charged by attorneys of similar skill and experience in the relevant community. (PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.) “The experienced trial judge is the best judge of the value of professional services rendered in his court […].” (Ibid.) “[R]ate determinations in other cases, particularly those setting a rate for the plaintiffs' attorney, are satisfactory evidence of the prevailing market rate.” (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1009.) “Courts have uniformly held that the party moving for statutory attorney fees or sanctions has the burden of proof.” (Mikhaeilpoor, supra, 48 Cal.App.5th at p. 247, citation omitted.)

Plaintiff contends that the hourly rates are reasonable based on the prevailing market and that the hourly rates are supported by prior trial court orders.

 

Defendant contends the rate for Adam Yousef is excessive based on his work performed.

 

Here, the Court finds $500 for Adam Yousef reasonable. However, upon review of the supporting declaration, there is no description for Oliver Tomas, Nikolas Mancera, or Christian Abad. While the Court finds the hourly rate of $150 requested for Nikolas Mancera and Christian Abad reasonable, the rate of $550 for Oliver Tomas is not reasonable without supporting information. The Court reduces this rate to $500. This amounts to a total reduction of $50 based on hourly rates.

 

Reasonableness of the Fees

 

The Court is vested with discretion to determine which claimed hours were reasonably spent and what an attorney’s reasonable hourly rate is. (Dover Mobile Estates v. Fiber Form Products, Inc. (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 1494, 1501; see also Flannery v. California Highway Patrol (1987) 61 Cal.App.4th 629, 644 [“We readily acknowledge the discretion of the trial judge to determine the value of professional services rendered in his or her court.”].) “As the plain wording of section 1794, subdivision (d) makes clear, the trial court is ‘to base the fee award upon actual time expended on the case, as long as such fees are reasonably incurred—both from the standpoint of time spent and the amount charged.’” (Mikhaeilpoor, supra, 48 Cal.App.5th at p. 247, citation omitted.) “[C]ounsels’ time records [should be used] as the starting point for [a court’s] lodestar determination.” (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of CSU (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 397.) In assessing a motion for attorney’s fees, “[a] trial court may not rubber stamp a request for attorney fees, but must determine the number of hours reasonably expended.” (Morris v. Hyundai Motor America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 38.)

 

Plaintiff argues that the 37.30 hours billed in this matter are reasonable as they reflect the actual work spent on this litigation. Plaintiff supplies the billing records in support of the requested fees. (Yousef Decl., Exh. C.)

 

In opposition, Defendant proposes a 50% reduction for improper and unreasonable billing entries. Defendant contends that Plaintiff’s billing records exhibit value billing and block billing. Defendant contends that Plaintiff’s request attributes what Plaintiff’s counsel believes the value of the task was, not the actual task spent. Defendant points to ten entries which it contends exhibits block billing, value billing, or an attorney performing clerical work.

 

Upon review of Plaintiff’s billing records, the Court does not find a 50% reduction in hours warranted. The Court agrees with Plaintiff’s reply argument that a 50% reduction appears to be arbitrary, especially because Defendant only points to ten entries that it contends are unreasonable. Of these ten entries, Defendant contends that “Review D’s responses to SROGs, FROGs, RFAs, and RFPs” for 0.7 hours is block-billing or “Review of notice of CMC from the court” for 0.2 hours exhibits value billing. The Court finds these, and the other entries noted by Defendant, to be reasonably incurred in the commencement and prosecution of this action. (See Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)

 

Plaintiff additionally requests $3,000 in anticipated fees arising out of this motion. This request is not supported by any declaration or breakdown in hours. The Court reduces this request by 1 hour because it is unable to measure the reasonableness of this request. A reduction of 1 hour is aimed in attributing 1 hour to review the opposition, 2 hours to draft and file a reply, and 1 hour to attend the hearing. At an hourly rate of $500, this reflects $2,000, or a $1,000 reduction.

 

Thus, based on the reduction in hourly rates and the reduction in fees, the court reduces a total of $1,050.

 

Lodestar Multiplier

 

The lodestar amount “may be adjusted by the court based on factors including (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Bernardi¿v. County of Monterey¿(2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1379, 1399 [citing Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132].)¿ The purpose of any lodestar and the increase thereto “is intended to approximate market-level compensation for such services” and is entirely discretionary.¿(Ibid.)¿“The purpose of a fee enhancement is not to reward attorneys for litigating certain kinds of cases, but to fix a reasonable fee in a particular action.”¿ (Weeks v. Baker & McKenzie¿(1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1128, 1171-72.)¿  

 

Plaintiff requests a 1.3 multiplier based on the risk of taking this case on a contingent fee basis and the result achieved.

 

The Court declines to apply a positive multiplier. The Court finds that this case was relatively simple—lasting less than one year before settlement and involving no motion practice except this one. There is nothing to indicate this matter was notably complicated or precluded other employment.

 

Costs

 

Plaintiff requests $1,216.96 in costs. This amount is not disputed by Defendant in opposition.

 

Therefore, the Court awards $1,216.96 in costs.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s request for attorney’s fees and costs is GRANTED in the amount of $15,756.96 reflecting $14,540 in attorney’s fees and $1,216.96 in costs.


Case Number: 25NWCV03591    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: P

WCCP CERRITOS, LLC, ET AL. v. ICORE LENDING, INC.

CASE NO.: 25NWCV03591 

HEARING: 09/15/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#13

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Judgment Creditors’ motion for an assignment order and restraining order is GRANTED.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Plaintiffs and Judgment Creditors WCCP Cerritos, LLC; Pacific Service Company, Inc.; PVP Investments, LLC; WCCP Baseline Crossings, LLC; and WCCP Arrowhead Park Place, LLC (Plaintiffs or Judgment Creditors) move for an assignment order and restraining order.

 

Background

 

On October 6, 2025, Plaintiffs filed this unlawful detainer action against Defendant ICore Lending, Inc. (Defendant or Judgment Debtor) and Does 1 to 5 regarding the property located at 12800 Center Court Drive South, Suite 500, Cerritos, California, 90703, Los Angeles County.

 

On January 29, 2026, Judgment was entered in favor of Plaintiffs and against Defendant following a court trial.

 

On July 9, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiffs’ unopposed motion for attorney’s fees.

 

Legal Standard

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 708.510, subdivision (a), the Court, upon noticed motion by the judgment creditor, can order the judgment debtor to assign to the judgment creditor all or part of a right to payment due or to become due, whether or not the right is condition on future developments, including wages from the federal government that are not subject to withholding under an earnings withholding order, rents, commissions, royalties, payments due from a patent or copyright, or an insurance policy loan value.

 

The Court may consider the following factors in determining whether to make an assignment or the amount of an assignment: (1) “[t]he reasonable requirements of a judgment debtor who is a natural person and of persons supported in whole or in part by the judgment debtor”; (2) “[p]ayments the judgment debtor is required to make or that are deducted in satisfaction of other judgments and wage assignments, including earnings assignment orders for support”; (3) “[t]he amount remaining due on the money judgment”; and (4) “[t]he amount being or to be received in satisfaction of the right to payment that may be assigned.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.510, subd. (c).) “[a] right to payment may be assigned pursuant to this article only to the extent necessary to satisfy the money judgment.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.510, subd. (d).)

 

“When an application is made pursuant to Section 708.510 or thereafter, the judgment creditor may apply to the court for an order restraining the judgment debtor from assigning or otherwise disposing of the right to payment that is sought to be assigned.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.520, subd. (a).) The Court may issue the order on a showing of need, may also require the creditor to provide an undertaking, and may modify or vacate the order at any time with or without a hearing. (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.520, subd. (b), (c).) “The order shall be personally served upon the judgment debtor and shall contain a notice to the judgment debtor that failure to comply with the order may subject the judgment debtor to being held in contempt of court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 708.520, subd. (d).)

 

Discussion

 

Judgment Creditors move for (1) an order assigning to them all the right of Judgment Debtor to payment from any of its accounts receivable from any source to the extent necessary to satisfy the judgment entered in favor of Judgment Creditors and against Judgment Debtor on January 29, 2026, in the amount of $211,381.78, plus accrued post-judgment interest, and attorneys’ fees (Judgment) and (2) a restraining order restraining and enjoining Judgment Debtor from assigning, transferring, encumbering, hypothecating, or otherwise disposing of any rights to payment from any of its accounts receivable or any other assignable rights to payment, pending full satisfaction of the Judgment. This motion is unopposed.

 

          Assignment Order

 

Judgment Creditors argue that Judgment Debtor has refused to satisfy the Judgment. Judgment Creditors state that no portion of the Judgment has been satisfied as of the date Judgment Creditors filed this motion. (Petersen Decl., ¶ 3.) Judgment Creditors note that Judgment Debtor is an active, operating business and that during Judgment Debtor’s tenancy, Judgment Debtor provided a financial statement reflecting that Judgment Debtor held accounts receivable in excess of $500,000. (Petersen Decl., ¶¶ 5-6.)

 

Here, Judgment Creditors show that the factors under Code of Civil Procedure section 708.510, subdivision (c) support granting an assignment order. Specifically, Judgment Debtor is not a natural person. Also, the Judgment remains unsatisfied despite Judgment Debtor’s active business operations and, as of June 30, 2025, Judgment Debtor reported accounts receivables in excess of the amount of the Judgment. Moreover, Judgment Creditors establish that accounts receivable are assignable under Code of Civil Procedure section 708.510, subdivision (a). (See Code Civ. Proc., § 708.510, subd. (a); Leg. Comm. Cmts. (“[Section 708.510] also provides an optional procedure for reaching assignable forms of property that are subject to levy, such as accounts receivable, general intangibles, judgments, and instruments.”].)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds the requested assignment order warranted.

 

          Restraining Order

 

Judgment Creditors also request a restraining order prohibiting Judgment Debtor from assigning or otherwise disposing of the rights to the payments sought to be assigned here.

 

Judgment Creditors make this request on the basis of Judgment Debtor’s conduct in this litigation. Judgment Creditors argue that Judgment Debtor has demonstrated a pattern of avoidance and disregard for its obligations by failing to appear for the court trial and then not making any effort to satisfy the Judgment. (Petersen Decl., ¶ 7.) The Court agrees that Judgment Creditors establish a need based on Judgment Debtor’s lack of participation in the litigation and in satisfying the Judgment thus far.

 

Conclusion and Order

 

Judgment Creditors’ motion for an assignment order and restraining order is GRANTED.

 

This Order assigns to Judgment Creditors all of Judgment Debtor’s rights to payment from any of its accounts receivable, and all other assignable rights to payment, only to the extent necessary to satisfy the Judgment, plus accrued post-judgment interest, and attorneys’ fees and costs.

 

The Court also issues a restraining order restraining Judgment Debtor from assigning, disposing of, or encumbering any such rights to payment pending satisfaction of the Judgment.
