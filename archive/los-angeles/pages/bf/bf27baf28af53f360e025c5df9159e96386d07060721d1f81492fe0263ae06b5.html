DEMURRER

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, Diane Strickfaden

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, Kelly Nicole Deckers

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Demurrer to Fourth and Seventh Causes of Action in Third Amended Complaint

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Kelly Nicole Deckers sued her former employer, Defendant City of Redondo Beach, asserting numerous violations of the Fair Employment and Housing Act. After two demurrers were sustained, Plaintiff filed a Third Amended Complaint on November 21, 2025. Plaintiff alleges that the City discriminated against her and that its employees harassed her because of her need to attend oncology appointments due to her prior cancer diagnosis. Plaintiff also brings a defamation claim against the City, Redondo Beach Police Department Communications Supervisor Jason Kilpatrick, and City Director of Human Resources Diane Strickfaden. On April 6, 2026, the Court overruled the City’s demurrer to the fourth and seventh causes of action in Plaintiff’s Third Amended Complaint for harassment and defamation. On June 24, 2026, the Court overruled Kilpatrick’s demurrer to the same claims.  

 

RULING: Overruled.

Defendant Strickfaden demurs to the fourth and seventh causes of action in Plaintiff’s Third Amended Complaint for harassment and defamation, arguing that Plaintiff fails to state her claims. Plaintiff opposes the demurrer, contending that the harassment claim is not asserted against Strickfaden and that she has adequately pled her defamation claim. Strickfaden’s demurrer is overruled.

 

As an initial matter, Strickfaden does not have standing to demur to the harassment claim because it is only asserted against the City. (See Carter v. Prime Healthcare Paradise Valley LLC (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 396, 410 [“Allegations of misconduct directed against one defendant, however, do not state a cause of action against another defendant against whom the allegations of misconduct are not directed”].) Allegations of Strickfaden’s harassing conduct are only alleged for the purposes of establishing the City’s liability. [TAC ¶ 55.] Strickfaden’s demurrer to the harassment claim is therefore overruled and the remaining issue is her challenge to the defamation cause of action.

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.” (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.) “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law . . . .” (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.) In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated. (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.) “If the complaint states a cause of action under any theory, regardless of the title under which the factual basis for relief is stated, that aspect of the complaint is good against a demurrer.” (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.)  

 

Seventh Cause of Action: Defamation

Strickfaden argues that Plaintiff fails to state a defamation claim because she does not allege who made the purportedly defamatory statements, the contents of the statements, and the manner and context in which they were made. She also contends that Plaintiff fails to adequately allege that the statements were made with malice and thus outside the scope of an applicable privilege. Finally, Strickfaden argues that assertion of the defamation claim against her violates the Court’s January 27, 2025 order prohibiting Plaintiff from adding new causes of action in her amended complaint.

 

Strickfaden’s arguments as to the insufficiency of the defamation claim are unavailing for the same reasons discussed in the Court’s April 6, 2026 and June 24, 2026 orders overruling the City and Kilpatrick’s demurrers. Plaintiff adequately alleges the content and context of the purportedly defamatory statements because she states that Strickfaden told her prospective employers that “(1) Plaintiff was in litigation against City, (2) Plaintiff was not eligible for rehire by City, and (3) Plaintiff had been disciplined while employed by City.” [TAC ¶¶ 80-83.] Plaintiff’s allegations are also sufficient whether she is pleading libel or slander. Slander “can be charged by alleging the substance of the defamatory statement” (Okun v. Superior Court (1981) 29 Cal.3d 442, 458) and less particularity is needed for alleging libel where, as here, “it appears that defendant has superior knowledge of the facts, so long as the pleading gives notice of the issues sufficient to enable preparation of a defense.” (Id. at p. 458.) Again, even if Strickfaden made the statements via written references, there is no indication that Plaintiff had access to the references to spell out the words verbatim in her complaint. And Plaintiff sufficiently alleges malice because she claims that Strickfaden knew the statements were false and published them “with the intention of blackballing Plaintiff from employment with any police department.” [TAC ¶ 82.] These allegations therefore defeat Strickfaden’s arguments regarding the inadequacies of Plaintiff’s claim.

 

Plaintiff has also not violated the Court’s prior order by asserting the defamation claim against Strickfaden. That order only restricted Plaintiff from adding new causes of action. Strickfaden was a named defendant in the First Amended Complaint, and the Third Amended Complaint merely adds specificity to the alleged defamatory statements. Strickfaden’s demurrer to the defamation cause of action is thus overruled.

 

Strickfaden to answer the Third Amended Complaint within 10 days of this order.

 

Strickfaden to give notice.

SUMMARY JUDGMENT/ADJUDICATION

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, Culver City Unified School District

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, New Earth

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Summary Judgment, or, in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff New Earth filed the instant breach of contract action against Defendant Culver City Unified School District. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant breached the terms of the parties’ contract when it failed to pay Plaintiff $1.5 million for support services Plaintiff provided to Defendant’s students, as delineated in the Proposal submitted to Defendant. Plaintiff asserts causes of action for: (1) Breach of Contract and (2) Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing.

 

RULING: Granted.

Evidentiary Objections: Sustained in part.

Defendant’s evidentiary objections Nos. 1-2 are sustained on relevance grounds. Objection No. 4 is sustained only as to the portion of the paragraph stating, “when signed by the District, would become a binding contract,” because this is an improper legal conclusion. The remainder of Defendant’s evidentiary objections are overruled.

 

The instant hearing comes after the Court continued Defendant’s motion for summary judgment, or alternatively summary adjudication, to permit supplemental briefing on an issue raised by the Court: does the Purchase Order constitute a counteroffer? (County of Santa Clara v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 292, 325-26.) Based on the supplemental briefing submitted, the Court concludes that Plaintiff fails to raise a triable issue as to whether its proposal was a contract and Defendant’s motion for summary judgment is granted.

 

The pleadings frame the issues for motions, “since it is those allegations to which the motion must respond. (Citation.)” (Scolinos v. Kolts (1995) 37 Cal. App. 4th 635, 640-641; FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 382-83; Jordan-Lyon Prods., LTD. v. Cineplex Odeon Corp. (1994) 29 Cal.App.4th 1459, 1472.) The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atl. Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) A defendant moving for summary judgment “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) “Once the defendant . . . has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Ibid.)

 

“When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) “An issue of fact can only be created by a conflict in the evidence. It is not created by speculation, conjecture, imagination or guesswork.” (Lyons v. Security Pacific National Bank (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 1001, 1041 (citation omitted).)

 

The issue presented by the Court was whether the Purchase Order was a counteroffer. “Consent is not mutual, unless the parties all agree upon the same thing in the same sense.” (Civ. Code, § 1580.) “[T]erms proposed in an offer must be met exactly, precisely and unequivocally for its acceptance to result in the formation of a binding contract.” (Apablasa v. Merritt & Co. (1959) 176 Cal.App.2d 719, 726.) A purported acceptance that includes terms different from those in the offer is a counteroffer, which, if not accepted by the original offeror, “amounts to nothing.” (Id. at p. 726.)

 

The evidence establishes that the Purchase Order was a counteroffer that terminated Plaintiff’s original offer via its Proposal. Under Education Code section 17604, a contract is not valid against a school district “until the same shall have been approved or ratified by the governing board, the approval or ratification to be evidenced by a motion of the board duly passed and adopted.” “Persons dealing with a school district are chargeable with notice of limitations on its power to contract.” (Santa Monica Unified Sch. Dist. v. Persh (1970) 5 Cal.App.3d 945, 952.) Defendant’s governing board approved the contract by issuing the Purchase Order for Year 2 services, which provided for payment of $600,000 in contrast to the $1.5 million contemplated by the Proposal. [Jiménez Decl. ¶ 6, Ex. D; Compl. ¶ 5, Ex. 1.] The Purchase Order incorporated by reference Defendant’s Standard Terms and Conditions, which conspicuously indicated that Plaintiff’s Proposal was an “offer” and the Purchase Order an “acceptance.” [Jiménez Decl. ¶ 7, Ex. E, §§ 1.5, 2.] The Terms and Conditions further provided that the Purchase Order “constitutes the entire agreement between the parties and supersedes any prior or contemporaneous written or oral understanding or agreement and any contrary provisions on packing slips, invoices, or other documents submitted by the Contractor.” [Id. § 23.] “Should any such document from Contractor contain additional or different terms than this Agreement, those terms shall be considered proposals by Contractor which are hereby rejected.” [Ibid.] This evidence sufficiently shows that the Purchase Order was a counteroffer to Plaintiff’s Proposal. The burden therefore shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue as to whether the terms of the Proposal governed the relationship between the parties.

 

Plaintiff fails to meet its burden. The sole item of evidence presented in Plaintiff’s supplemental opposition is Defendant’s response to the following interrogatory: “If you contend that the CONTRACT is not a valid and binding written contract, please describe in detail the facts upon which this contention is based.” [Tomasulo Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. 1, No. 4.] Plaintiff contends that a portion of Defendant’s response stating, “Notwithstanding, the District is presently unaware of facts supporting such a contention,” shows that Defendant conceded the Proposal was the contract between the parties and precludes a contrary finding. [Ibid.] Not so. Plaintiff omits the preceding segment of Defendant’s response where it stated that the Proposal “more closely resembles a proposal by New Earth with a description of New Earth’s proposed services and estimated cost, than it resembles a formal contract,” and identified it lacked typical terms. [Ibid.] Defendant’s response is therefore consistent with the Court’s ruling that the Proposal was not a contract. In any event, “[a]nswers to written interrogatories ‘do not assume the status of judicial admissions’” and may be contradicted by later evidence. (Cortina v. North American Title Co. (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 1030, 1106.) Defendant did so in its moving papers when it argued and presented evidence that the Proposal was not a contract but an offer. [Mot. at pp. 15-16.] Plaintiff therefore fails to raise a triable issue of material fact as to whether the Proposal was a contract.

 

Because Plaintiff’s good faith and fair dealing claim is predicated on Defendant’s failure to pay the $1.5 million contemplated by the Proposal, its claim fails. As outlined above, the parties did not enter into a contract for services at this price and “[w]here there is no underlying contract there can be no duty of good faith arising from the implied covenant.” (Horn v. Cushman & Wakefield Western, Inc. (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 798, 819.) Defendant’s motion for summary judgment is thus granted.

 

Defendant to give notice.

CONFIRM ARBITRATION

 

MOVING PARTY: Petitioner, Suzanne Barton Grant

RESPONDING PARTY: Unopposed/Respondents, Barry Navidi and Lear Rex, LLC

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Petition to Confirm Arbitration Award

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Following an arbitration hearing on July 11, 2025, an award was entered on August 15, 2025 in favor of Petitioner Suzanne Barton Grant against Respondents Barry Navidi and Lear Rex, LLC in the amount of $1,791,625 with interest accruing at the rate of 20%. Petitioner was further granted a 3.5% net profit participation interest in the Lear Rex film production.

 

RULING: Granted.

Petitioner moves to confirm the August 15, 2025 arbitration award issued by arbitrator the Hon. Elizabeth A. White (Ret.). The award reflects that Respondents breached a settlement agreement reached after the demand for arbitration and that the parties conceded that the award should issue. [Pet. Attachment 8(c), pp. 1-2.] An award was entered in Petitioner’s favor in the amount of $1,791,625 with interest accruing at the rate of 20%. [Id. at p. 2.] Petitioner was also granted a 3.5% net profit participation interest in the Lear Rex film production. [Ibid.] The award indicates that it was served on the parties on August 15, 2025. [Id. at p. 3.] Respondents do not oppose the petition. The petition is granted and the arbitration award is confirmed.

    

“After arbitration has resulted in an award, the Arbitration Act permits a party to petition ‘the court to confirm, correct or vacate the award.’ (§ 1285.) The opposing party may respond to such a petition by requesting ‘the court to dismiss the petition or to confirm, correct or vacate the award.’ (§§ 1285.2, 1287.2.) The proponent of the arbitration award (whether it be the petitioner or the respondent) must recite or attach a copy of the arbitration agreement. (§§ 1285.4, subd. (a), 1285.6.) A court presented with such a petition or response is empowered only to confirm, correct, or vacate the award or to dismiss the proceeding. (§ 1286.) If the court confirms the award, it shall enter judgment accordingly. (§ 1287.4)” (Toal v. Tardif (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 1208, 1220.) “[B]efore a court may confirm an arbitration award, the court must first find the existence of a valid arbitration agreement.” (Id. at p. 1221.)

 

The petition satisfies all requirements. On June 2, 2026, Respondents filed a general denial and thus made an appearance in this case. The Loan Agreement that is the subject of the dispute provides that any controversy or claim arising out of or relating to the agreement shall be resolved by arbitration. [Pet. Attachment 5, p. 5.] The Settlement Agreement provides that the “Arbitrator shall retain jurisdiction to enforce the term of this Settlement Agreement.” [Id. Attachment 4(b), pp. 2-3.] Both agreements are signed by Respondents. The arbitration award is therefore confirmed, and Petitioner is entitled to judgment in accordance with the award.

 

Petitioner to submit a proposed judgment.

 

Petitioner to give notice.

TRANSFER/CHANGE VENUE

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, California Department of Transportation

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, Bridgette McGowan

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion to Change Venue/Transfer the Action to San Diego County

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Bridgette McGowan formerly worked as an Operator I for Defendant California Department of Transportation at its Carlsbad maintenance yard. Plaintiff alleges that in March 2023, her supervisor at Carlsbad, Tim Anderson, assigned her to pull signs out of the mold-contaminated “C train” as retaliation because she previously rejected him. Shortly after making a complaint regarding the alleged retaliation, Plaintiff was transferred to Kearney Mesa, which was “a significantly farther commute than the Carlsbad yard which caused a hardship for Plaintiff as a single mother.” Plaintiff was later transferred back to Carlsbad in April 2024.

 

Plaintiff alleges that upon returning to Carlsbad, she experienced offensive and racist comments from co-workers and continued retaliation from Anderson. Plaintiff made complaints regarding these incidents and was transferred to the Escondido yard before being transferred back again to Carlsbad in September 2024. Plaintiff alleges conduct by Anderson that made her feel uncomfortable and that were retaliatory, as well as his alleged favoritism of a white female coworker. Plaintiff was terminated from her position in April 2025. On December 29, 2025, Plaintiff filed the instant action asserting numerous claims under the Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA).

 

RULING: Granted.

Request for Judicial Notice: Granted.

The Court takes judicial notice of the existence of Plaintiff’s complaint filed in the instant action and her complaint filed with the Civil Rights Department. (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (c).) Judicial notice is also taken of pages 3 and 71 of the “California Roster 2026,” published by the Secretary of State, showing that Defendant’s address is in Sacramento and that the Attorney General maintains an office in Los Angeles. (Id. § 452, subd. (h).)  

 

Evidentiary Objections: Overruled in part.

Defendant’s Objection No. 3 to the declaration of Ann Hendrix and its objections to the exhibit subject to Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice are overruled. The Court does not rule on the remaining objections because they are immaterial to disposition of the motion.

 

Defendant moves to transfer the action to San Diego County, arguing that venue lies in San Diego under the FEHA venue statute. Defendant alternatively contends transfer promotes the convenience of witnesses and ends of justice. Plaintiff opposes the motion, maintaining that venue is proper in Los Angeles County. Because venue is proper in San Diego rather than Los Angeles, Defendant’s motion is granted.

 

“Except as otherwise provided in Section 396a, if an action or proceeding is commenced in a court having jurisdiction of the subject matter thereof, other than the court designated as the proper court for the trial thereof, under this title, the action may, notwithstanding, be tried in the court where commenced, unless the defendant, at the time he or she answers, demurs, or moves to strike, or, at his or her option, without answering, demurring, or moving to strike and within the time otherwise allowed to respond to the complaint, files with the clerk, a notice of motion for an order transferring the action or proceeding to the proper court, together with proof of service, upon the adverse party, of a copy of those papers. Upon the hearing of the motion the court shall, if it appears that the action or proceeding was not commenced in the proper court, order the action or proceeding transferred to the proper court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 396b, subd. (a).)

 

“Except as otherwise provided by law and subject to the power of the court to transfer actions or proceedings as provided in this title, the superior court in the county where the defendants or some of them reside at the commencement of the action is the proper court for the trial of the action. If the action is for injury to person or personal property or for death from wrongful act or negligence, the superior court in either the county where the injury occurs or the injury causing death occurs or the county where the defendants, or some of them reside at the commencement of the action, is a proper court for the trial of the action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 395, subd. (a).)

 

“Venue is determined based on the complaint on file at the time the motion to change venue is made.” (Brown v. Superior Court (1984) 37 Cal.3d 477, 482.) In a FEHA action, venue is proper in the county in which: (1) “the unlawful practice is alleged to have been committed”; (2) “the records relevant to the practice are maintained and administered”; or (3) “the aggrieved person would have worked . . . but for the alleged unlawful practice . . . .” (Gov. Code, § 12965, subd. (c)(3).) “[I]f the defendant is not found within any of these counties, an action may be brought within the county of the defendant’s residence or principal office.” (Ibid.) “[T]he special provisions of the FEHA venue statute control in cases involving FEHA claims joined with non-FEHA claims arising from the same facts.” (Brown, supra, 37 Cal.3d at p. 487.)

 

Venue is proper in San Diego County under the FEHA venue statute. The alleged harassment, retaliation, and discrimination occurred at Plaintiff’s different worksites in San Diego County. [Compl. ¶¶ 11-41.] Plaintiff would have continued to work in San Diego County but for Defendant’s alleged unlawful conduct. [Id. ¶¶ 8, 41.] And all of the records relevant to the unlawful conduct are maintained in San Diego County. Defendant submits the declaration of Denella Morgan, the Deputy District Director, Administration, for District 11 who is responsible for “direct resolution of highly technical, highly sensitive and confidential personnel matters” and provides advice to supervisors “regarding effective performance management, progressive discipline, discrimination complaints, and labor relations issues.” [Morgan Decl. ¶ 4.] She states that the employees identified in Plaintiff’s complaint are employed in District 11, San Diego, and that “[s]upervisor’s desk and drop files and relevant records” and “operational records relating to the employment of the Plaintiff and others with whom she would interact through her employment” are maintained in San Diego. [Id. ¶ 7.] Plaintiff’s allegations and the evidence presented thus show that San Diego is the proper venue under the FEHA venue statute.

 

To show venue is proper in Los Angeles County, Plaintiff relies on venue being proper in Sacramento County under the FEHA venue statute. This is because under Code of Civil Procedure section 401, subdivision (1), [w]henever it is provided by any law of this State that an action or proceeding against the State or a department, institution, board, commission, bureau, officer or other agency thereof shall or may be commenced in, tried in, or removed to the County of Sacramento, the same may be commenced and tried in any city or city and county of this State in which the Attorney General has an office.” Thus, if venue is proper in Sacramento County, then it is also proper in Los Angeles County because the Attorney General maintains an office there. [Pl.’s RJN, Ex. 1.] On this point, Plaintiff argues that venue is proper in Sacramento County under the FEHA venue statute because records relevant to the alleged unlawful practice are maintained there by virtue of Plaintiff’s official personnel file being located there as well as those investigating her complaints. Alternatively, if Defendant is not “found” in any of the counties identified in the FEHA venue statute, then venue is proper at its place of business in Sacramento, again making Los Angeles County a proper forum. [Ibid.] For the reasons described below, Plaintiff’s arguments are unavailing.

 

Plaintiff’s arguments do not support a finding of venue in Sacramento County. First, Defendant is “found” in the counties identified by the FEHA venue statute, because, as explained above, the alleged unlawful practices occurred in San Diego County and Plaintiff would have worked there but for the unlawful conduct. Plaintiff’s equating of “found” as used in the statute with residence or principal place of business is contrary to the plain meaning of the statute and makes the fallback provision redundant. Second, the fact that Plaintiff’s official personnel file is located in Sacramento County or that those investigating her complaints were located there does not mean that is where the records “relevant to the practice are maintained and administered.” (Gov. Code, § 12965, subd. (c)(3).) In contrast to Morgan’s testimony that supervisors’ desk and drop files, relevant records to the adverse actions, and operational records relating to Plaintiff and other employees’ employment are maintained in San Diego [Morgan Decl. ¶¶ 6-7], Plaintiff does not explain what information the official personnel files contain. [See Hendrix Decl. ¶ 3.] Furthermore, where an investigation is conducted is not the same as where the records pertinent to the investigation are “maintained and administered.” Plaintiff has therefore failed to show that venue is proper in Los Angeles via proper venue in Sacramento. Defendant’s motion is thus granted.

 

The action is ordered transferred to San Diego County. All scheduled items on calendar are vacated.

 

Defendant to give notice.
