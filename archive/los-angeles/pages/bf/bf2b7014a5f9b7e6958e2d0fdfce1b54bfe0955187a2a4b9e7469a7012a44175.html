      I.          Background

 

Plaintiff’s counsel Angelo F. Campano of Campano Law Group, APC (together “Counsel”) moves the Court to be relieved as counsel.

 

On October 30, 2023, Plaintiff Alexis Garate (“Plaintiff”), a minor by and through his Guardian Ad Litem Diana Perez (“Perez”) filed a complaint against Defendants Magua Mayes II (“Mayes”), Datron Kendrix, Jr. (“Kendrix”), and Palmdale School District (“PSD”) (collectively “Defendants”), asserting eight causes of action for (1) Negligence, (2) Motor Vehicle Negligence, (3) Negligence Per Se (Cal. Veh. Code, § 22350), (4) Negligence Per Se (Cal. Veh. Code, § 27318(c)), (5) Negligence Per Se (Cal. Veh. Code, § 22109), (6) Common Carrier Liability (Civ. Code, § 2100), (7) Negligent Entrustment, and (8) Negligent Hiring, Retention, Supervision, Training.

 

Plaintiff alleges that on September 28, 2022, while a passenger on a school bus owned, controlled, and managed by PSD and operated by Kendrix, a vehicle operated by Mayes struck the school bus while it was at a stop, causing Plaintiff to suffer injury. (Compl., ¶¶ 17-23.)

 

On January 17, 2025, Plaintiff’s Counsel orally informed the Court that the case has settled at the FSC.

 

On January 28, 2026, Mayes filed his Answer and demanded a jury trial.

 

On January 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Settlement of Entire Case and the Court set an OSC hearing re dismissal and order of dismissal for April 2, 2026. The OSC was subsequently continued and set for September 17, 2026.

 

On August 18, 2026, Counsel filed the present motion to be relieved. No opposition has been filed.

 

-----

 

    II.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Motion to be Relieved as Counsel – The Court has discretion to permit an attorney to withdraw from representation, and such a motion should be granted if there is no undue prejudice to the client. (Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 904, 915.)

 

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362 prescribes the following requirements of a movant seeking to be relieved as counsel: (1) The notice of motion and motion must be directed to the client using form MC-051 (“Notice of Motion and Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel-Civil”); (2) The motion must be accompanied by a declaration using form MC-052 (“Declaration in Support of Attorney's Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel-Civil”) and “must state in general terms and without compromising the confidentiality of the attorney-client relationship why a motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 284(2) is brought instead of filing a consent under Code of Civil Procedure section 284(1).”; (3) A proposed order must be lodged with the court along with the moving papers and must use form MC-053 (“Order Granting Attorney's Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel-Civil”) and “must specify all hearing dates scheduled in the action or proceeding, including the date of trial, if known. If no hearing date is presently scheduled, the court may set one and specify the date in the order.”; (4) “The notice of motion and motion, the declaration, and the proposed order must be served on the client and on all other parties who have appeared in the case” either by personal service, electronic service, or by mail.

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362(a) – (e).)  

 

“If the notice is served on the client by mail under Code of Civil Procedure section 1013, it must be accompanied by a declaration stating facts showing that either: (A) the service address is the current residence or business address of the client; or (B) The service address is the last known residence or business address of the client and the attorney has been unable to locate a more current address after making reasonable efforts to do so within 30 days before the filing of the motion to be relieved.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362(d)(1).) “‘[C]urrent’ means that the address was confirmed within 30 days before the filing of the motion to be relieved. Merely demonstrating that the notice was sent to the client’s last known address and was not returned…is not, by itself, sufficient to demonstrate that the address is current.” (Id at rule 3.1362(d).)

 

-----

 

 III.          Discussion

 

Application – Counsel moves to be relieved as counsel for Plaintiff. Counsel has substantially complied with the statutory requirements of Rule 3.1362 and has filed an MC-051 that contains all required information, is directed to Plaintiff, and was served via standard and certified mailing. Counsel has also provided an MC-052 form, declaring that there has been a breakdown in the attorney-client relationship such that Counsel can no longer provide effective and adequate legal counsel to Plaintiff, that Plaintiff has failed to maintain meaningful and reliable contact with Counsel despite Counsel’s requests for information and assistance in finalizing the matter, and as a result, Plaintiff has caused Counsel to needlessly expend significant time and incur needless expenses and resources. Attached to the MC-052 is a Declaration of Counsel Angelo F. Campano, who asserts that between January 27, 2026 and August 17, 2026, Counsel has not received any meaningful response, or any response at all, from Plaintiff through Perez, and details multiple dates of attempted communications and the results of those attempts. Campano attaches appropriately redacted evidence of some of their communication efforts, and states that despite these efforts, Counsel is unable to prepare for trial, which would result in prejudice to Plaintiff. The MC-052 also states Counsel has confirmed Plaintiff’s last known address is current within the previous 30 days by mail, return receipt requested.

 

Finally, Counsel has provided an MC-053 Order form, though the form does not include Plaintiff’s current address and telephone number, or the information required regarding upcoming hearings and trial information. Nonetheless, most of this information has been adequately provided on the MC-052 form, and the Court finds Counsel’s substantial compliance sufficient for granting the motion.

 

The Court also finds minimal prejudice to Plaintiff in granting this motion. It appears the case has settled as to at least some Defendants, with dismissal pending due to the involvement of a minor’s compromise that may require special arrangements be made for any minor trust to be opened, and because Perez has not responded to Counsel’s efforts to obtain medical records to assist in the determination of what, if any, account would be required for Plaintiff Garate. (See Campano Decl., 06/26/26.) It seems, therefore, that finalization of the settlement is contingent on information in Perez’s possession and control, and Plaintiff therefore has the opportunity and ability to move forward in finalization of the settlement without counsel. Moreover, Perez has the opportunity to retain subsequent counsel if she so chooses to finalize settlement. As to Defendant Mayes, no trial date has been set, no hearings are on calendar, Plaintiff may proceed in propria persona or retain subsequent counsel, and Plaintiff will not be prejudiced in the granting of this motion. Finally, the only hearing on calendar, aside from the present motion, is an OSC re Dismissal (Settlement).

 

Accordingly, Counsel’s motion to be relieved is GRANTED. Counsel shall provide Plaintiff’s contact information, if known, in accordance with the Court’s final ruling within 48 hours of this Order.

 

-----

 

 IV.          Conclusion

 

Plaintiff’s counsel Angelo F. Campano of Campano Law Group, APC’s Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel is GRANTED. 

 

Angelo F. Campano and Campano Law Group, APC shall given notice to Plaintiff and all other parties. Further, Campano shall provide Plaintiff’s last known telephone number(s), if any is known, to the Court and Defendants’ counsel within 48 hours of this hearing to be included on the final MC-053 Order. If no telephone number for Plaintiff is known, Campano shall provide Plaintiff’s electronic mailing address to the same within 48 hours of this Order. Otherwise, Campano shall confirm with the Court and Defendants’ counsel that no telephone number, electronic mailing address, or other method of contact for Plaintiff is known within 48 hours of this Order.

Plaintiff,

BAGGETT TRANSPORTATION COMPANY, a Corporation; LOCKHEED MARTIN CORPORATION; and DOES 1 through 30, inclusive,

  [TENTATIVE] 

  RULING ON MOTION

  Dept. A-14 

  Judge William H. Forman 

 

 

      I.          Background

 

Defendant Baggett Transportation Company (“Baggett”) moves the Court for leave to file a Cross-Complaint.

 

On October 10, 2025, Plaintiff Dorene MenaHernandez (“Plaintiff”) filed her form Complaint against Defendants Baggett and Lockheed Martin Corporation (“Lockheed Martin”) (together “Defendants”), asserting one cause of action for General Negligence. Plaintiff alleges that she was lawfully on the premises of Lockheed Martin, located at 1011 Lockheed Way, Palmdale, CA 93599 (the “Facility”) on October 16, 2023, when she was exposed to harmful chemicals through negligent packaging, disclosure, warnings, and practices while performing her duties as a security guard, causing Plaintiff to sustain injuries.

 

On November 12, 2025, Lockheed Martin filed its Answer.

 

On December 15, 2025, Bagget filed its Answer.

 

On August 18, 2026, Baggett filed its Motion for Leave to File a Cross-Complaint. Both Lockheed Martin and Plaintiff have filed separate Statements of Non-Opposition to the motion.

 

-----

 

    II.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Leave to File a Cross-Complaint – A party against whom a cause of action has been asserted in a complaint may file a cross-complaint setting forth any cause of action he has against a person alleged to be liable thereon, whether or not such person is already a party to the action, if the cause of action asserted in his cross complaint (1) arises out of the same transaction, occurrence, or series of transactions or occurrences as the cause brought against him, or (2) asserts a claim, right, or interest in the property or controversy which is the subject of the case brought against him. (Code Civ. Proc., § 428.10, subd. (b).)

 

“A party shall file a cross-complaint against any of the parties who filed the complaint or cross-complaint against him or her before or at the same time as the answer to the complaint or cross-complaint.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 428.50, subd. (a).) “Any other cross-complaint may be filed at any time before the court has set a date for trial.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 428.50, subd. (b).) A party shall obtain leave of court to file any cross-complaint not filed before or at the same time as the answer to the complaint or not filed at any time before the Court has set a date for trial. (Code Civ. Proc., § 428.50, subd. (c).) “A party who fails to plead a cause of action subject to the requirements of this article, whether through oversight, inadvertence, mistake, neglect, or other cause, may apply to the court for leave to…file a cross-complaint, to assert such cause at any time during the course of the action.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 426.50.)

 

Leave may be granted in the interest of justice at any time during the course of the action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 428.50, subd. (c).) Where the proposed cross-complaint arises out of the same transaction as the plaintiff’s claim, the court must grant leave to file the cross-complaint so long as the defendant is acting in good faith and all other statutory requirements have been satisfied, and leave shall be liberally granted to avoid forfeiture of causes of action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 426.50; see also Silver Orgs. V. Frank (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 94, 99 [“motion to file a cross-complaint at any time during the course of the action must be granted unless bad faith of the moving party is demonstrated where forfeiture would otherwise result,” and “[f]actors such as oversight, inadvertence, neglect, mistake or other cause, are insufficient grounds to deny the motion unless accompanied by bad faith”].)

 

-----

 

 III.          Discussion

 

Application – Baggett moves for leave to file a Cross-Complaint against Haas Group International LLC (“Haas”) and Wesco Aircraft Hardware Corp., dba Incora (“Incora”) pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 428.50, subdivision (c) on the grounds that 1) the proposed Cross-Complaint arises out of the same transaction or occurrence as does Plaintiff’s Complaint against Defendants and 2) allowing such filing is in the interests of justice and will promote the efficient resolution of claims between the parties. (Motion, p. 1:26-2:3.) This action is set for trial on February 26, 2027.

 

Baggett asserts that it never received notice from Lockheed Martin indicating the subject shipment sent by Baggett was damaged or compromised in any way, that photographs taken on October 16, 2023 contain no indication that the shipment was damaged or compromised, and that Baggett was unaware of Plaintiff’s claim for injuries and damages arising from the incident until the filing of the complaint. (Motion, p. 3:9-19; Schuler Decl., ¶¶ 4-6.) Since Baggett became aware of Plaintiff’s action, the parties have exchanged written discovery responses and documents, which indicate the shipment sent by Baggett did not appear damaged or to have spilled, leaked, seeped, or otherwise escaped their packaging to be able to cause exposure. (Motion, p. 3:20-27; Schuler Decl., ¶¶ 8-9.)

 

Baggett asserts Plaintiff produced photographs of the contents of Baggett’s trailer that show the company name “HAAS” on one of the containers, and on March 17, 2026, Baggett identified and produced documents showing Incora was the shipper of the contents that Baggett delivered to Lockheed Martin on October 16, 2023. (Motion, p. 3:27-4:4; Schuler Decl., ¶¶ 9-10.) Since this discovery exchange, Baggett has continued fact investigation and discovery to discovery theories on how the alleged exposure could have occurred and who many be responsible, and based on its findings, Baggett is informed and believes that Haas is the manufacturer of certain contents in Baggett’s trailer on October 16, 2023, and Incora was the shipper of such contents to the Facility, thereby identifying both entities as potentially responsible parties. (Motion, p. 4:4-12.)

 

Baggett seeks leave to file a Cross-Complaint to assert causes of action for (1) Equitable Indemnity, (2) Contribution, and (3) Declaratory Relief against Haas and Wesco based on their potential liability for the alleged exposure. (Schuler Decl., Ex. A.) Baggett’s counsel asserts he spoke with counsel for Plaintiff and Co-Defendant Lockheed Martin, that both counsel advised they would not oppose the motion, and both have since filed statements of non-opposition. (Schuler Decl., ¶ 14.)

 

Baggett’s proposed Cross-Complaint arises from the same transaction or occurrence as Plaintiff’s underlying Complaint, being her alleged exposure to harmful chemicals. The Court also finds that Baggett is acting in good faith, requesting leave to file following exchange of discovery leading to the information necessary for discovering Haas and Incora’s respective potential liability for the incident underlying the Cross-Complaint. Plaintiff does not oppose the filing of the Cross-Complaint, and Baggett appears to have moved in a non-dilatory and reasonable manner in moving for leave.

 

Accordingly, Baggett’s motion is GRANTED.

 

The Court notes that page 4 of the proposed Cross-Complaint appears to have inadvertently been attached out of order as the last page of the Cross-Complaint. Baggett shall ensure the Cross-Complaint is properly formatted and file the proposed Cross-Complaint within five (5) days of this Order.

 

-----

 

 IV.          Conclusion

 

Defendant Baggett Transportation Company’s Motion for Leave to File a Cross-Complaint is GRANTED. Defendant Baggett Transportation Company shall file the Proposed Cross-Complaint within five (5) days of this Order.

      I.          Background

 

Plaintiffs Michael Reilly (“Mr. Reilly”), individually and as Successor-in-Interest of the Estate of Amelia Reilly (“Estate”), and Cassandra Reilly (“Mrs. Reilly”) (together “Plaintiffs”) move the Court for leave to file a Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) to allege punitive damages.

 

Plaintiffs allege that on January 15, 2025, five year old Amelia Reilly (“Amelia”), daughter to Mr. and Mrs. Reilly, was scheduled for a dental procedure for composite restoration of cavities in two teeth and conscious sedation. Mrs. Reilly signed a consent form for the procedure and inhalation of nitrous oxide on December 13, 2024. Plaintiffs assert that Amelia had a known diagnosis of Williams-Beuren Syndrome (“Williams Syndrome”) noted in her chart, and that she had been receiving dental treatment from Defendant Yasser E. Sadek, D.D.S. (“Sadek”) at Y Sadek, DDS, Inc., dba Palmdale Children’s Dental (“PCD”) for at least two years on the date of the procedure. Plaintiffs characterize Williams Syndrome as a congenital, multisystem disorder that causes developmental, physical, and mental abnormalities, and that cardiac and renal involvement can be severe, rendering general anesthesia a significant risk.

 

On January 15, 2025, Sadek recommended a tooth extraction and recommended general anesthesia to Mr. Reilly, who asserts he consented based on Sadek’s representations and was unaware of the associated risks. The anesthesia was administered by Thien C. Pham, D.D.S. (“Pham”).

By the time the procedures were completed by Sadek, Amelia’s condition was deteriorating. Paramedics were called and arrived at approximately 8:33 a.m., but were sent away by Defendants. Defendants removed the IV line, blood pressure cuff, and cardiac monitoring. At approximately 8:45 a.m., Amelia stopped moving. After 15 minutes, Defendants reapplied the cardiac leads and attempted to insert a new IV line.  Sadek called paramedics to return. Defendants began performing chest compressions. The paramedics arrived at 9:07 a.m. and took Amelia to the Antelope Valley Medical Center emergency room, where she was pronounced dead at 10:25 a.m.

 

Plaintiffs allege Defendants’ negligence in administering the anesthesia caused Amelia’s death.

 

On October 13, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a Complaint against Defendants Sadek, PCD, and Pham (collectively “Defendants”), asserting nine causes of action for (1) Wrongful Death, (2) Negligence – Bystander, (3) Survivor Action – Negligence, (4) Survivor Action – Lack of Informed Consent, (5) Survivor Action – Battery, (6) Survivor Action – Negligence Misrepresentation, (7) Survivor Action – Fraud by Intentional Misrepresentation, (8) Survivor Action – Fraud by Concealment, and (9) Survivor Action – Constructive Fraud.

 

On December 8, 2025, Plaintiffs filed their First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) asserting the same causes of action against the same Defendants.

 

 

On July 17, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiffs’ ex parte application for leave to file an oversized memorandum of points and authorities not exceeding 25 pages and to file a reply memorandum not exceeding 15 pages.

 

On July 30, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their Motion for Leave to Amend the FAC to allege punitive damages.

 

On September 3, 2026, Sadek and PCD together, and Pham separately, filed their Oppositions.

 

On September 10, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their Reply.

These drugs are relatively contraindicated in children with Williams Syndrome like Amelia Reilly. Use of these specific induction drugs on Amelia Reilly was below the standard of care. Not knowing that these drugs were dangerous to a child with Williams Syndrome, like Amelia Reilly, was wanton, reckless and grossly negligent, in knowing disregard of the health and safety of this child."

2.     Declaration of James W. Tom, D.D.S., MS, DADBA, ¶ 42, Page 7:1-6 – "In my opinion, it was wanton, reckless, despicable (in conscious disregard of the health and safety of the patient) and grossly negligent to not perform adequate CPR immediately on this child when she became bradycardic again.  III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Leave to Amend to Allege Punitive Damages Against a Healthcare Provider – Motions for leave to amend are generally governed by Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (a). However, the complaint in “any action for damages arising out of the professional negligence of a health care provider” cannot include a claim for punitive damages unless “the court enters an order allowing an amended pleading.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.13, subd. (a).) Section 425.13 was enacted “amid concern over routine inclusion of sham punitive damage claims in medical malpractice actions,” and aims to alleviate the problem by “shifting to the plaintiff the procedural burden that would otherwise fall on the defendant to remove a ‘frivolous’ or ‘unsubstantiated’ claim early in the suit.” (College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 717.)

The Court may allow the filing of an amended pleading claiming punitive damages on a motion by the party seeking leave to amend and on the basis of the supporting and opposing affidavits presented that the plaintiff has established that there is a substantial probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim pursuant to Civil Code section 3294. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.13, subd. (a).) The motion must be filed within two years after the complaint or initial pleading is filed or no less than nine months before the date the matter is set for trial, whichever is earlier. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.13, subd. (a).)

A section 425.13 motion “operates like a demurrer or motion for summary judgment in ‘reverse.’” (College Hospital, supra, 8 Cal.4th at 719.) “Rather than requiring the defendant to defeat the plaintiff’s pleading by showing it is legally or factually meritless, the motion requires the plaintiff to demonstrate that he possesses a legally sufficient claim which is ‘substantiated,’ that is, supported by competent, admissible evidence.” (Ibid.) 

“[I]t is only necessary that plaintiff provide ‘a sufficient prima facie showing of facts to sustain a favorable decision if the evidence submitted by the [plaintiff] is credited.’ [Citation.] The trial court is not required to make any factual determination or to become involved in any weighing process beyond that necessarily involved in deciding whether a prima facie case for punitive damages exists. Once the court concludes that such a case can be presented at trial it must permit the proposed amended pleading to be filed. If it concludes that no such case exists, then it properly rejects the proposed pleading amendment. In making this judgment, the trial court's consideration of the defendant's opposing affidavits does not permit a weighing of them against the plaintiff's supporting evidence, but only a determination that they do not, as a matter of law, defeat that evidence.” (Looney v. Superior Court (1993) 16 Cal.App.4th 521, 539.) “[A] prima facie case of entitlement to punitive damages must meet the standard that plaintiffs will be likely to prevail by showing malice, oppression, or fraud by clear and convincing evidence.” (Aquino v. Superior Court (1993) 21 Cal. App. 4th 847, 854-55.)

In ruling on a Section 425.13 motion, the trial court “should not engage in weighing conflicting evidence, making credibility determinations, or drawing inferences from facts in assessing whether a plaintiff has made a sufficient showing of a prima facie case of entitlement to punitive damages. [Citation.]” (Id. at 856.) In considering the affidavits and evidence provided by the party opposing the motion, “any uncontradicted evidence presented in opposition should be considered to fill in any blank areas in the plaintiff's showing, in order to give the court as complete a factual picture as possible,” and “[t]he court must also take care to distinguish between evidentiary facts and argument or hyperbole presented by counsel.” (Ibid.) The Court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the plaintiff. (Id. at 857.)

Standard for Recovery of Punitive Damages – Punitive damages may be recovered upon a proper showing of malice, fraud, or oppression. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) “Malice” is defined as conduct intended to cause injury to a person or despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard for the rights or safety of others. (Turman v. Turning Point of Cent. Cal., Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) “Oppression” means despicable conduct subjecting a person to cruel and unjust hardship, in conscious disregard of the person’s rights. (Ibid.) “Fraud” is an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known by defendant, with intent to deprive a person of property, rights or otherwise cause injury. (Ibid.) Conclusory allegations, devoid of any factual assertions, are insufficient to support a conclusion that parties acted with oppression, fraud or malice. (Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1042.)

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Plaintiffs move for leave to amend their FAC to add a claim for punitive damages against Defendants pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 425.13, 473, and 576, and Civil Code section 3294. (Notice of Motion, p. 2:2-5.) Specifically, Plaintiffs seek to allege punitive damages as to the third cause of action for negligence, the fourth cause of action for lack of informed consent, the fifth cause of action for battery, the sixth cause of action for negligent misrepresentation, the seventh cause of action for intentional misrepresentation, the eighth cause of action for fraud by concealment, and the ninth cause of action for constructive fraud.

 

To prevail on the motion, Plaintiffs must, through competent and admissible affidavits, make a prima facie showing by clear and convincing evidence that there is a substantial probability that they will prevail on their punitive damages claim as to each Defendant. Plaintiffs allege Defendants acted with malice and fraud, thereby subjecting them to punitive liability.

 

“Malice” includes “despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1).) Conscious disregard for the safety of others may constitute malice required to sustain a claim for punitive damages, and exists where the defendant is aware of probable dangerous consequences and willfully and deliberately fails to avoid them. (Blegen v. Superior Court (1981) 125 Cal.App.3d 959, 962-63.) Malice may be inferred from the circumstances of a defendant’s conduct. (Monge v. Superior Court (1986) 176 Cal.App.3d 503, 511.) As with recklessness, a jury may infer conscious disregard where the flaws in a defendant’s policies or conduct are so obvious that the defendant can be found to have essentially turned a blind eye to a high probability of harm. (Samantha B. v. Aurora Vista Del Mar, LLC (2022) 77 Cal.App.5th 85, 100.)

 

As to fraudulent concealment, Plaintiffs must show “(1) concealment or suppression of a material fact; (2) by a defendant with a duty to disclose the fact; (3) the defendant intended to defraud the plaintiff by intentionally concealing or suppressing the fact; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would have acted differently if the concealed or suppressed fact was known; and (5) the plaintiff sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of the material fact.” (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal. 5th 1, 40.) Doctors and healthcare providers have a duty to disclose to their patients “all material information that is necessary to make an informed decision about a proposed treatment” and a separate duty “not to misrepresent the nature of a patient’s medical condition.” (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 749.)

(Renzi Decl., ¶¶ 26-28, 30-31, 33-35, 38-39.)

(Renzi Decl., ¶¶ 39-43.) (Renzi Decl., ¶ 48.)

 

In opposition, Sadek declares that he routinely relies on information supplied by a child’s parents or guardians together with information contained in the patient’s health history and information obtained by the anesthesia provider, that he was aware Amelia had Williams Syndrome but did not personally perform the medical and anesthetic risk assessment for general anesthesia, relied on Pham’s professional anesthetic assessment in proceeding with the dental treatment and believed she could safely undergo anesthesia, and that he asked Plaintiffs whether Amelia had any current medical conditions requiring special precautions and both represented that she was healthy. (Sadek Decl., ¶¶ 5-9.) The evidence also provides that following this incident, Sadek, Pham, and PCD ceased all anesthesia administration for a number of months and placed into effect specific protocols and procedures for all future treatment, including required direct contact with all patients’ pediatricians and evaluation of prior medical records before clearing a child for general anesthesia.

 

The court must determine "whether the evidence presented is such that a jury applying that [clear and convincing] evidentiary standard could reasonably find" for the plaintiff. (Hoch v. Allied-Signal, Inc. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 48, 60.) However, "section 425.13(a) does not authorize the trial court to reject a well-pled and factually supported punitive damages claim simply because the court believes the evidence is not strong enough for probable success before a jury." (College Hospital, supra, 8 Cal.4th at 725-26.)

 

Plaintiffs must substantiate their claims “with ‘factual recitals’ [] ‘made under penalty of perjury’ and ‘set forth competent admissible evidence within the personal knowledge of the declarant.’ [Citation.] College Hospital made clear that plaintiff's own pleading is entitled to no evidentiary weight in the analysis.” (Pomona Valley Hospital Medical Center v. Superior Court (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 828, 837.) On review of the moving papers and proposed SAC, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have sufficiently shown a prima facie case for punitive damages. 

 

As to their fraudulent concealment theory for punitive damages, Plaintiffs must show “(1) concealment or suppression of a material fact; (2) by a defendant with a duty to disclose the fact; (3) the defendant intended to defraud the plaintiff by intentionally concealing or suppressing the fact; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would have acted differently if the concealed or suppressed fact was known; and (5) the plaintiff sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of the material fact.” (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal. 5th 1, 40.)  Direct evidence of intent to defraud is not required. “[F]raudulent intent is an issue for the trier of fact to decide” (Beckwith v. Dahl (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 1039, 1061), and “must often be established by circumstantial evidence.” (Tenzer v. Superscope, Inc. (1985) 39 Cal.3d 18, 30.) The requisite intent to conceal or suppress can be inferred from the defendant’s conduct and the surrounding circumstances.  The heightened specificity ordinarily required to plead fraud applies to claims based on affirmative misrepresentations, not to claims of fraudulent concealment. (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn., Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384.) Plaintiffs’ concealment allegations therefore are not defeated by the absence of the particularity that would be demanded of a claim resting on an affirmative misrepresentation.

 

Plaintiffs have put forth sufficient evidence to establish a prima facie case of fraud sufficient to subject Sadek to punitive damages under their fraudulent concealment theory. Although there is no direct evidence of Sadek’s intent to defraud, the record supplies circumstantial evidence from which a reasonable jury could infer that Sadek intentionally concealed or suppressed facts he was obliged to disclose. Plaintiffs have shown that Sadek had no basis for his assurance that no cardiologist need be contacted prior to general anesthesia, that he had a duty to disclose the absence of any such basis, that Plaintiffs were unaware of that fact and would have acted differently had they known of Sadek’s lack of information and knowledge, and that they sustained damages. From the same evidence, a jury could reasonably infer the intent element: that Sadek, aware he had no basis for his categorical safety assurance,  withheld both that lack of basis and his corresponding failure to investigate in order to obtain Plaintiffs’ consent and to proceed with the anesthesia and procedure. The same inference is available as to Sadek’s failure to disclose his unfamiliarity and inexperience with Williams Syndrome, his non-review of medical records from Amelia’s other medical providers, the characterization of PCD as a practice equipped to treat children with complex or special medical needs, and the availability of referral to another provider for alternate treatment plans unavailable at PCD.

 

Plaintiffs have also met their burden as to malice. “‘Malice’ means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1).) The statute plainly indicates that absent an intent to injure the plaintiff, ‘malice’ requires more than a ‘willful and conscious’ disregard of the plaintiffs' interests, as the additional component of ‘despicable conduct’ must be found. (See, e.g., Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1221-1222.) "The adjective 'despicable' connotes conduct that is…so vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or loathsome that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people." (Lackner v. North (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 1188, 1210.) “The mere carelessness or ignorance of the defendant does not justify the imposition of punitive damages…. Punitive damages are proper only when the tortious conduct rises to levels of extreme indifference to the plaintiff's rights, a level which decent citizens should not have to tolerate." (Ibid.)

 

The sworn evidence presented by Plaintiffs and factual allegations in the proposed SAC do not establish or allege that Sadek intended to injure Amelia, and therefore, Plaintiffs must establish both conscious disregard and despicable conduct beyond mere commission of a tort. Plaintiffs’ evidence establishes that Sadek accepted Amelia as a patient with knowledge of her Williams Syndrome diagnosis but did not have personal experience with or knowledge of Williams Syndrome, that he was not specially trained to identify risks of Williams Syndrome as a general dentist, that he made no effort to contact Amelia’s pediatrician or other healthcare provider to review her medical history, that despite his awareness of her Williams Syndrome and heart murmur, made no effort to look into Williams Syndrome to confirm whether any additional clearance, research, testing, or precautionary measures were necessary before recommending and moving forward with the procedure, and that he never disclosed to Plaintiffs that he had no knowledge of or experience with Williams Syndrome despite advertising his dental practice as one targeted for children with special needs or complex medical cases, a representation that Plaintiffs allegedly relied on in seeking treatment from Sadek and PCD.

 

It also shows that PCD primarily treats children, that Sadek’s website advertises treatment of special needs children and complicated cases, states Sadek is a pediatric dentist, that where a documented condition in a patient’s chart was not known to Sadek to present complications or potential issues with anesthesia, he would not look further into it before authorizing administration of anesthesia, and that in Amelia’s case, he relied solely on Plaintiffs to disclose any and all potential medical issues or complications rather than verifying the information with Amelia’s medical providers or conducting any inquiry into her conditions as a licensed medical provider. It shows that Sadek represented to Mrs. Reilly that no cardiology intervention or clearance was required and that the procedure was safe, prior to Pham assigning an ASA II classification and despite having no basis for those claims, that Sadek left the room during the procedure and shortly thereafter while Amelia should have been monitored and Sadek was under a legal obligation to remain present and assist in any emergency response, that he performed additional dental work on Amelia beyond the scope consented to by Plaintiffs, including work on at least four additional teeth, took no action while Pham dismissed EMS despite Amelia’s unstable condition, allowed the monitoring devices to be removed shortly after Amelia’s first emergency, and failed to adequately intervene and begin emergency life-saving treatment.

 

While Sadek puts forth his declaration  refuting many of these assertions and clarifies the standards and procedures used in his office, the weighing of the evidence and credibility determinations are not the job of the Court, but of the jury. Therefore, while Sadek’s assertions may well be true and he may have viable defenses to these punitive damages claims, the evidence of Plaintiffs, which the Court must credit, meets Plaintiffs’ burden.  

 

Therefore, Plaintiffs have sufficiently stated and demonstrated a triable punitive damages claim, which warrants placing the issue in front of a jury.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ motion is GRANTED as to Sadek.

 

                                                  ii.     Defendant PCD

 

As against PCD, Plaintiffs primarily assert punitive damages are warranted because Sadek was the sole shareholder and Chief Executive Officer, and that his decisions concerning patient selection, office anesthesia, cardiology referral, treatment options, and emergency escalation were made within the practice he owned and controlled, and the punitive showing against Sadek therefore supports punitive liability against PCD. (Motion, p. 26:13-19; Ex. 9, Sadek Depo. 44:2–25.)

 

A corporate employer is liable for punitive damages when an officer, director, or managing agent personally commits, authorizes, or ratifies the fraud, oppression, or malice. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (b).) “An act of oppression, fraud or malice, by an officer, director or managing agent, is sufficient to impose liability on a corporate employer for punitive damages, without any additional showing of ratification by the employer.” (Kelly-Zurian v. Wohl Shoe Co. (1994) 22 Cal.App.4th 397, 420.) A managing agent includes “only those corporate employees who exercise substantial independent authority and judgment in their  corporate decisionmaking so that their decisions ultimately determine corporate policy.” (White v. Ultramar, Inc. (1999) 21 Cal.4th 563, 566-67.) For an employer to be liable for punitive damages, “such conduct must have been performed by an ‘agent … employed in a managerial capacity and … acting in the scope of employment,' " or ratified or approved by a ‘managerial agent’ of the organization” and “in performing, ratifying, or approving the malicious conduct, the agent must be acting as the organization's representative, not in some other capacity.” (College Hospital, supra, 8 Cal.4th at 723.)

 

Here, as discussed, Plaintiffs have stated and sufficiently demonstrated that they have a triable claim for punitive damages as to Sadek, the CEO of PCD. Further, as pled and substantiated, Plaintiffs have sufficiently established that (1) Sadek, as CEO, is a managing agent of PCD, and (2) Sadek was acting in the course and scope of his employment at all relevant times of his alleged conduct.

Dr. Pham was the practice’s anesthesia provider. (Ex. 9, p. 74:7-13, 137:22-138:3; Ex. 11, Pham Depo., p. 166:3-25.) (Ex. 7, p. 20:8–24:9.) (Ex. 11, p. 124:3-125:7, 132:15-18.) Mr. Reilly asked Pham about Amelia’s shallow breathing, and Pham responded that she was “breathing real good.” (Ex. 7, p. 34:6-36:13.) On Amelia’s second cardiac crash, Pham recognized that the IV had been removed and testified that the missing IV delayed epinephrine by approximately one to two minutes. (Ex. 11, p. 134:4-10.) The anesthesia record does not disclose that the IV was removed and reestablished during the second emergency. (Ex. 11, p. 134:4–10, 164:11–17.)

 

Mr. Reilly submits a declaration, stating that Pham never informed him that Williams Syndrome with cardiac issues increased the risk of complications from anesthesia, that he had not contacted Amelia’s cardiologist, reviewed her cardiology records, or obtained a pediatric cardiology clearance, or that he did not know that children with Williams Syndrome should receive general anesthesia only in a hospital setting. (Tom Decl., ¶¶ 26-28.) (Tom Decl., ¶¶ 29-31.) Despite not checking Amelia’s heart with a stethoscope, Pham indicated that her cardiac system was within normal limits, constituting a false entry in the anesthesia records but breaching the standard of care in failing to perform a physical examination. (Tom Decl., ¶ 31.) Tom also asserts that Pham left the room to take a phone call, failed to properly monitor Amelia, removal of the monitoring devices resulted in delayed recognition of Amelia’s second cardiac emergency as evidenced by video of Amelia becoming still and her feet turning pale, that Pham did not properly initiate an emergency response, administered epinephrine and glycopyrrolate, which only temporarily stabilize a patient, and breached the standard of care in turning paramedics away and representing that Amelia was stable. (Tom Decl., ¶¶ 34-37.) Tom also declares that Pham admits in his deposition testimony that he completed the anesthesia record after the emergency, and the record itself appears to contain continuing heart-rate and blood-pressure monitoring during a time period when the video shows that Pham had removed the ECG leads and BP cuff, indicating the entries were fabricated by Pham. (Tom Decl., ¶¶ 46-47.) After Amelia was pronounced dead and Pham returned from the hospital, Tom notes that Pham added a notation regarding “aortic stenosis” to the pre-anesthesia record, and that the anesthesia records created by Pham contained false and misleading information. (Tom Decl., ¶¶ 50, 52.) Tom ultimately opines that to a reasonable degree of medical probability, proper screening would have prevented administration of general anesthesia and Amelia would not have passed away. (Tom Decl., ¶ 54.)

 

In opposition, Pham provides the Declaration of Thomas Einstein, M.D. The Einstein Declaration largely attempts to refute the evidence set forth by Plaintiffs, rather than provide evidence that provides a complete factual picture or fill in gaps left by Plaintiffs’ evidence. As it is improper to weigh such evidence, the Einstein Declaration is largely immaterial to the ruling on this motion. Pham also provides supplemental excerpts from Mr. Reilly’s testimony indicating that the information contained on Pham’s anesthesia report accurately reflects the information provided by Mr. Reilly regarding Amelia’s medical history. (Ex. 7.) Pham also provides Amelia’s Death Investigation Summary from the County of Los Angeles Department of Medical Examiner, which asserts that she had mild aortic stenosis, no grossly identifiable cardiac abnormalities, no significant histopathologic abnormalities, and that while the levels of ketamine, lidocaine, and nitrous oxide were within therapeutic range, there was a temporal relationship between the administration of anesthesia and her death, and the cause of death was determined to be complications of anesthesia administration with Williams Syndrome as a contributing condition. (Ex. 8.)

 

Through affidavit and attached evidence, Plaintiffs have established a prima facie showing that they have a triable punitive damages claim as to Pham on malice grounds. The conduct alleged and substantiated shows that Pham, an anesthesiologist, was made aware of a prior surgery and administration of anesthesia, made aware of Amelia’s Williams Syndrome and heart murmur, Googled Williams Syndrome in Mr. Reilly’s presence and learned that cardiac complications with anesthesia may arise in patients with Williams Syndrome, was informed by Mr. Reilly that Amelia had prior aortic stenosis, failed to review any of Amelia’s medical records and relied solely on representations made by Mr. Reilly in deciding to proceed with anesthesia, and with knowledge of his own ignorance of the diagnosis and the likelihood of complications, nonetheless consciously decided to move forward with administration. It also shows that Pham had some knowledge that Williams Syndrome could have been a contributing cause to the first cardiac crash, as Pham reiterated her diagnosis on Sadek’s first call to paramedics, that Pham administered only temporary stabilization medication and subsequently informed EMS that Amelia was doing fine, removed all but one monitoring device without confirming stability, and was on his phone during the procedure and left the room to take a phone call during the procedure, at which time he was responsible for closely monitoring Amelia’s vital signs. Pham allegedly reassured Plaintiffs that the procedure was safe despite having no personal experience with Williams Syndrome and knowledge that cardiac events were more likely in patients with the diagnosis, and the records created by Pham were modified after Amelia’s death and contain measured information of Amelia’s vital signs, despite there being no monitoring devices placed on Amelia at the time of recording, indicating inconsistencies in the anesthesia report and Amelia’s actual medical status after the first cardiac crash.

 

These factual allegations, substantiated by affidavit, are sufficient to meet Plaintiffs’ burden of establishing a triable claim for punitive damages based on malice, as these facts could render a finding of conscious disregard for Amelia’s safety and despicable conduct on behalf of a healthcare provider, warranting placement of the issue in front of a jury.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ motion is GRANTED as to Pham.

    II.          Legal Standard

Standard for Leave to Amend Complaint – Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (a)(1) provides, in relevant part: “The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect; and may, upon like terms, enlarge the time for answer or demurrer. The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.” This discretion should be exercised liberally in favor of amendments, for “judicial policy favors resolution of all disputed matters in the same lawsuit.” (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1047.)

“The trial court has discretion to permit or deny the amendment of the complaint, but instances justifying the court's denial of leave to amend are rare.” (Armenta ex rel. City of Burbank v. Mueller Co.¿(2006) 142¿Cal.App.4th¿636, 642.)¿ However, “[a]lthough courts are bound to apply a policy of great liberality in permitting amendments to the complaint at any stage of the proceedings, up to and including trial [citation], this policy should be applied only ‘[w]here no prejudice is shown to the adverse party . . .’ [Citation.] A different result is indicated ‘[w]here¿inexcusable delay and probable prejudice to the opposing party’ is shown. [Citation.]” (Magpali¿v. Farmers Group, Inc.¿(1996) 48¿Cal.App.4th¿471, 487.) “If the motion to amend is timely made and the granting of the motion will not prejudice the opposing party, it is error to refuse permission to amend.” (Morgan v. Superior Court of Los Angeles County (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 527, 530.) Prejudice includes “delay in trial, loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation.” (Solit¿v. Tokai Bank, Ltd. New York Branch¿(1999) 68 Cal.App.4th 1435, 1448.) 

Under rule 3.1324, subdivision (a) of the California Rules of Court, a motion to amend a pleading shall (1) include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments; (2) state what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and (3) state what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a).)

Under subdivision (b), a separate declaration must accompany the motion and must specify (1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) the reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier. (Id., subd. (b).)

(Motion, p. 2:11-25.)

 

Plaintiff has substantially complied with California Rules of Court rule 3.1324, subdivision (a). Plaintiff has provided a copy of the proposed FAC, which is serially numbered as the “First Amended Complaint,” and states what allegations and contents in the Complaint are to be modified, added, and deleted. (Grigoryan Decl., Ex. 1.) Plaintiff does not provide a redlined copy of the proposed FAC or identify exactly by page, paragraph, and line number the allegations to be amended, deleted or added. However, the motion and the Declaration of Chris Grigoryan, Esq. make clear to the Court and Defendants exactly what changes are proposed, and generally where such changes are located in the Complaint. The Grigoryan Declaration also puts forth all required information under Rule 3.1324, subdivision (b), specifying the effect of the amendment (Grigoryan Decl., ¶ 7), why it is necessary and proper (Grigoryan Decl., ¶¶ 8-12), when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered (Grigoryan Decl., ¶ 13), and the reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier (Grigoryan Decl., ¶ 14).

 

Finally, as Plaintiff notes, Defendant Role Lancaster has not yet filed a responsive pleading, discovery is in its early stages and no cutoff has been set, no trial date has been set, no dispositive motions have been filed, and the case is in its very early stages.     II.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Motion to be Relieved as Counsel – The Court has discretion to permit an attorney to withdraw from representation, and such a motion should be granted if there is no undue prejudice to the client. (Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 904, 915.)

 

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362 prescribes the following requirements of a movant seeking to be relieved as counsel: (1) The notice of motion and motion must be directed to the client using form MC-051 (“Notice of Motion and Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel-Civil”); (2) The motion must be accompanied by a declaration using form MC-052 (“Declaration in Support of Attorney's Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel-Civil”) and “must state in general terms and without compromising the confidentiality of the attorney-client relationship why a motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 284(2) is brought instead of filing a consent under Code of Civil Procedure section 284(1).”; (3) A proposed order must be lodged with the court along with the moving papers and must use form MC-053 (“Order Granting Attorney's Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel-Civil”) and “must specify all hearing dates scheduled in the action or proceeding, including the date of trial, if known. If no hearing date is presently scheduled, the court may set one and specify the date in the order.”; (4) “The notice of motion and motion, the declaration, and the proposed order must be served on the client and on all other parties who have appeared in the case” either by personal service, electronic service, or by mail.

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362(a) – (e).)  

 

“If the notice is served on the client by mail under Code of Civil Procedure section 1013, it must be accompanied by a declaration stating facts showing that either: (A) the service address is the current residence or business address of the client; or (B) The service address is the last known residence or business address of the client and the attorney has been unable to locate a more current address after making reasonable efforts to do so within 30 days before the filing of the motion to be relieved.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362(d)(1).) “‘[C]urrent’ means that the address was confirmed within 30 days before the filing of the motion to be relieved. Merely demonstrating that the notice was sent to the client’s last known address and was not returned…is not, by itself, sufficient to demonstrate that the address is current.” (Id at rule 3.1362(d).)
