I.       BACKGROUND  

On March 27, 2025, Plaintiff Bassam Kallo (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants General Motors, LLC (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 10 alleging five causes of action: (1) violation of Cal. Civ. Code § 1793.2(d); (2) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(b); (3) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(a)(3); (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability; and (5) fraudulent inducement through concealment. Plaintiff alleges that, on or about July 7, 2017, Plaintiff entered into a warranty contract with Defendant regarding a 2017 Chevrolet Camaro (the “Vehicle”), which was allegedly revealed to have defects. (Complaint at ¶¶ 6 and 11.)

On July 16, 2025, Plaintiff filed his first amended complaint (“FAC”).

On June 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed his second amended complaint (“SAC”) alleging three causes of action: (1) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(d); (2) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(b); and (3) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(a)(3).

On July 6, 2026, Defendant filed the instant demurrer to Plaintiff’s SAC.

On July 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed his opposition.

On July 30, 2026, Defendant filed its reply.

 

II.          LEGAL STANDARD

Demurrer

A demurrer is a pleading that may be used to test the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations in a complaint. (Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 430.10.) There are two types of demurrers: general demurrers and special demurrers. (See McKenney v. Purepac Pharmaceutical Co. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 72, 77.)

General demurrers can be used to attack pleadings for failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action or for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. (CCP § 430.10(e); McKenney, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at 77.) Such demurrers can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable; evidence or extrinsic matters are not considered. (CCP §§ 430.30 and 430.70; Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318; Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) To test the sufficiency of the cause of action, the Court admits “all material facts properly pleaded” and “matters which may be judicially noticed,” but does not consider contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law. [Citation].” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at 318.) The Court gives these facts “a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Ibid.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) The face of the complaint includes exhibits attached to the complaint. (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94.) "If facts appearing in the exhibits contradict those alleged, the facts in the exhibits take precedence." (Holland v. Morse Diesel Intern., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447.)

CCP § 430.41 requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (CCP § 430.41(a).) The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due.  (CCP § 430.41(a)(2).) Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (CCP § 430.41(a)(3).)

When a demurrer is sustained, the Court determines whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) When a plaintiff “has pleaded the general set of facts upon which his cause of action is based,” the court should give the plaintiff an opportunity to amend his complaint, since plaintiff should not “be deprived of his right to maintain his action on the ground that his pleadings were defective for lack of particulars.” (Reed v. Norman (1957) 152 Cal.App.2d 892, 900.) Generally, the court will allow leave to amend on at least the first try, unless there is absolutely no possibility of overcoming the issue. (See Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227 [“Denial of leave to amend constitutes an abuse of discretion unless the complaint shows on its face it is incapable of amendment. (Citation.) Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given."].)

 

 

III.       DISCUSSION

Meet and Confer Requirement

On June 16, 2026, defense counsel met and conferred via telephone with Plaintiff’s counsel regarding the instant demurrer. (Allison Decl. ¶ 2.) The parties were unable to reach an agreement. (Ibid.) Accordingly, Defendant satisfies the meet and confer requirement of CCP § 430.41(a).

 

Demurrer

Defendant demurs to Plaintiff’s entire SAC on the grounds that it is time-barred by the applicable statute of limitation found in CCP § 871.21. (See Demurrer at p. 1.)

CCP § 871.21 provides, in pertinent part, that an action for restitution or replacement of motor vehicle or civil penalties “shall be commenced within one year after the expiration of the applicable express warranty” or “[n]otwithstanding subdivision (a) . . . shall not be brought no later than six years after the date of original delivery of the motor vehicle.” (Civ. Code § 871.21(a) and (b).) Additionally, “[t]he time periods prescribed in subdivisions (a) and (b) shall be tolled . . . [a]s provided by tolling requirements prescribed in the [Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act].” (Civ. Code § 871.21(c)(1).)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that CCP § 871.21 does not apply to the instant action because retrospective application of new procedural statutes to eliminate potential claims is a violation of due process. (Opposition at 2:19-3:13.) Plaintiff cites Rosefield Packing Co. v. Superior Court (1935) 4 Cal.2d 120, 122-123, holding that “where the change in remedy, as for example, the shortening of a time limit provision, is made retroactive, there must be a reasonable time permitted for the party affected to avail himself of his remedy before the statute takes effect. If the statute operates to cut off the existing remedy, or within so short a time as to give the party no reasonable opportunity to exercise his remedy, then the retroactive application of it is unconstitutional as to such party.” (Ibid. [citing Rosefield Packing Co., supra, 4 Cal.2d at 122-123].) Plaintiff argues that “[h]ere, Defendant seeks to have this court apply [CCP] section 871.21 retroactively, and to require Plaintiff to have filed their case by July 2023.” (Ibid.)

In reply, Defendant argues that both the statutory language and legislative history of CCP § 871.21 demonstrates that its statute of limitations applies to any action filed after January 1, 2025. (Reply at 1:14-2:9.) However, Defendant fails to cite any specific legislative history to support this argument.

California law establishes a strong presumption that statutes operate prospectively only. (Quarry v. Doe (2012) 53 Cal.4th 945, 955.) Courts must consider “the nature and extent of the change in the law and the degree of connection between the operation of the new rule and a relevant past event” guided by “familiar considerations of fair notice, reasonable reliance, and settled expectations.” (Id. at 955-956.) Here, Plaintiff entered into the warranty at issue on July 7, 2017. (See SAC at ¶ 6.) Accordingly, if the statute of limitations found in CCP § 871.21 applied, Plaintiff would have had to bring his claim by July 7, 2023, well before CCP § 871.21 was enacted. Therefore, Defendant improperly asks the Court to apply CCP § 871.21 in a manner that violates “familiar considerations of fair notice, reasonable reliance, and settled expectations.” (Quarry, supra, 53 Cal.4th at 955-956.) As Defendant notes, if application of a new statute of limitations and/or statute of repose “raise[s] substantial concerns about the effects of the new rule on the general administration of justice, or would unfairly undermine the reasonable reliance of parties on the previously existing state of the law,” a court can refuse to apply it. (Reply at 6:1-13 [citing Newman v. Emerson Radio Corp. (1989) 48 Cal.3d 973, 983.) Here, Plaintiff should not be bound to a statutory deadline of July 2023 when the applicable deadline did not exist until January 2025. Additionally, a statute “will not be applied retroactively unless such intention clearly appears in the language of the statute itself.” (Krusesky v. Baugh (1982) 138 Cal.App.3d 562, 566.) Defendant’s argument that Plaintiff’s claims are time-barred fails.

 

IV.    CONCLUSION

Defendant’s demurrer is OVERRULED.

Defendant to file an answer within 20 days of this order.

25VECV04250 ANGELINA OCEGUERA, et al. vs JAIME GARCIA

August 6, 2026, at 8:30 a.m.

Van Nuys Courthouse East, Dept. I

Tentative Ruling – Minor’s Comp.

 

 

 

I.        Background

 

On July 28, 2026, Plaintiffs Angelina Oceguera (“Petitioner”), Mario Garcia (“Mario”), and Angelise Garcia (“Claimant”), a minor by and through her guardian ad litem Angelina Oceguera, filed a complaint against Jaime Garcia (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 25 alleging two causes of action: (1) motor vehicle negligence; and (2) general negligence.

 

On January 9, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a notice of settlement.

 

On July 29, 2026, Petitioner filed the instant petition for approval of compromise of claim or action or disposition of proceeds of judgment for minor or person with a disability on behalf of Claimant.

 

II.     Petition for Approval of Compromised Claim

 

Court approval is required for all settlements of a minor’s claim. (Cal. Prob. Code §§ 3500, 3600, et seq.; Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 372.) “‘[W]ithout trial court approval of the proposed compromise of the ward’s claim, the settlement cannot be valid. [Citation.] Nor is the settlement binding [on the minor] until it is endorsed by the trial court.” (Pearson v. Superior Court (2012) 202 Cal.App.4th 1333, 1338.) A minor, like Claimant, “shall appear either by a guardian or conservator of the estate or by a guardian ad litem appointed by the court in which the action or proceeding is pending, or by a judge thereof, in each case.” (CCP § 372(a)(1).) Alternatively, the petitioner may file a declaration demonstrating that he or she has a right to compromise the minor’s claim under Prob. Code § 3500.

 

Regarding the substance of the petition, to obtain court approval of the settlement of a minor’s claim, the petitioner must file a complete and “verified petition for approval of the settlement and must disclose ‘all information that has any bearing upon the reasonableness of the compromise.’ [Citations.]” (Barnes v. Western Heritage Ins. Co. (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 249, 256; Cal. Rules of Court Rule 7.950.)

 

Petitioner seeks court approval for a settlement under which Claimant would receive $6,355.40. (See MC-350 Item 10.) Claimant suffered injury from a motor vehicle accident, incurring various fractures and traumatic injuries. (Id. at Items 5-6.) Notably, an additional $14,796.80 is to be paid to Mario, and $8,847.80 to Petitioner. (Id. at Item 11.) Of the $6,355.40, $2,280 is to be paid as medical costs, $2,118.25 as attorney’s fees, and $287.42 for costs. (Id. at Item 16.) Settlement funds are to be delivered to Petitioner. (Id. at Item 18b.)

 

Petitioner provides Claimant’s medical records as Attachment 8. (See MC-350 Attachment 8.) Petitioner provides a reason for apportionment of settlement as Attachment 11b(3). (Id. at Attachment 11b(3).) Finally, attorney’s fees are proper considering they represent approximately 33% of Claimant’s settlement amount.

 

Based on the foregoing, the instant petition is GRANTED.

 

Petitioner to give notice.

COMPLAINT FILED: September 30, 2025

TRIAL DATE: February 16, 2027

 

I.       BACKGROUND  

This is a Song-Beverly Act (“SBA”) lemon law action arising out of Plaintiff Saeed Anavim’s (“Plaintiff”) purchase of a new 2023 BMW X3 sDriver30i, VIN: 5UX43DP0XP9P44439 (the “Subject Vehicle”) manufactured by Defendant BMW of North America, LLC (“BMW”) and repaired by Defendant Robert A. Smith Inc. d/b/a Bob Smith BMW-MINI, erroneously sued as Bob Smith VC Calabasas LLC (“BSBM”). On September 30, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative Complaint against BMW, BSBM, and DOES 1 through 10 (collectively, “Defendants”), asserting causes of action for: (1) Breach of Express Warranty; (2) Breach of Implied Warranty; and (3) Negligent Repair.

On October 31, 2025, BMW filed its Answer to the Complaint. On November 4, 2025, BSBM filed its Answer to the Complaint.

On June 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion to Compel Deposition of Person Most Qualified (“PMQ”). On July 17, 2026, BMW filed an Opposition. On July 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Reply.

II.          LEGAL STANDARDS

            Pursuant to the Code of Civil Procedure Section 2025.010, any party may obtain discovery (subject to the scope and restrictions provided by law) “by taking in California the oral deposition of any person, including any party to the action. The person deposed may be a natural person, an organization such as a public or private corporation, a partnership, an association, or a governmental agency.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.010.) A party must present a qualified person for each deposition. (Maldonado v. Superior Court (2002) 94 Cal.App.4th 1390, 1398.)

            “If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action or an officer, director, managing agent, or employee of a party, or a person designated by an organization that is a party under Section 2025.230, without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice, the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent's attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (a).)

III.       DISCUSSION  

            Plaintiff moves for an order compelling BMW to produce its PMQ for deposition and responsive documents on the grounds that: (1) BMW failed to produce its PMQ for deposition and/or provide alternative dates; (2) BMW served only boilerplate objections; and (3) Matters for Examination (“MFE”) nos. 1-34 and Request for Production of Documents (“RFP”) nos. 1-16 are relevant and material to Plaintiff’s claims.

A.    Meet and Confer

            “The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).) Specifically, “[a] meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040, subd. (a).) “[A]ttempting informal resolution means more than the mere attempt by the discovery proponent ‘to persuade the objector of the error of his ways” and “a reasonable and good faith attempt at informal resolution entails something more than bickering with [opposing]counsel.... Rather, the law requires that counsel attempt to talk the matter over, compare their views, consult, and deliberate.” (Clement v. Alegre (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1277, 1294.)

            Here, Plaintiff’s counsel of record, Jacob Shayesteh, states that on April 24, 2026, he sent a meet and confer email to defense counsel, Ryan Chang requesting dates for the deposition of BMW’s PMQ. (Shayesteh Decl., ¶8, Ex. 2; Mack Decl., ¶5, Ex. A.) On May 12, 2026, Mr. Shayesteh followed-up on the request for availability of the PMQ’s deposition. (Shayesteh Decl., ¶9, Ex. 3; Mack Decl., ¶6, Ex. B.) On May 14, 2026, the parties spoke on the telephone and Mr. Chang advised that he was working on obtaining a date for the deposition and that the PMQ was newly assigned. (Mack Decl., ¶7.) On May 18, 2026, BMW’s counsel, Dylan Mack offered December 9, 2026 or December 22, 2026 as deposition dates, which Plaintiff rejected. (Shayesteh Decl., ¶¶10-11, Ex. 4; Mack Decl., ¶¶9-11, Ex. C.) Plaintiff, then unilaterally served a deposition notice on May 27, 2026 and BMW served its objections on June 1, 2026. (Shayesteh Decl., ¶¶13-14, Ex. 5; Mack Decl., ¶¶12-13, Ex. D.) Thereafter, Mr. Shayesteh served another meet and confer letter. (Shayesteh Decl., ¶15, Ex. 6; Mack Decl., ¶14, Ex. E.) The parties telephonic met and conferred after the filing of the motion and BMW offered September 17, 2026 for the deposition but Plaintiff conditioned acceptance upon BMW agreeing to produce its PMQ on all 34 MFEs and withdraw its objections.  (Mack Decl., ¶¶17-18, Ex. G.) Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiff fulfilled its meet and confer obligations.

B.     Merits of Motion

            Upon review of the MFEs, the Court finds that MFE nos. 1, 4-10, 12-13, and 31 concern the following categories: (1) documents and questions related to repurchase and replacement requests of the Subject Vehicle including the research and investigation actions taken by BMW; (2) documents and questions related to repair history, procedures, and communications between Defendant, Plaintiff, and/or third-parties for the Subject Vehicle; (3) documents and questions related to warranty history, procedures, and communications between Defendant, Plaintiff, and/or third-parties for the Subject Vehicle. Contrary to BMW’s objections, these MFEs are not vague, ambiguous, and/or overbroad. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010 [“[A]ny party may obtain discovery regarding any matters, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or to the determination of any motion made in that action, if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” ].) 

            Here, MFE nos. 1, 4-10, 12-13, and 31 are relevant and narrow in scope as Plaintiff’s claims against BMW are for negligent repair, breach of express warranty, and breach of implied warranty. These MFEs are limited to communications between the parties and/or third-parties in connection to the Subject Vehicle’s repair and warranty history (negligent repair and warranty claims); and requests documents related to the repurchase and replacement requests Plaintiff made regarding the Subject Vehicle (implied warranty claim). As such, these MFEs are reasonably calculated to lead to admissible evidence that will address Plaintiff’s claims against BMW. Likewise, BMW fails to show that it cannot provide an intelligible response to these MFEs. (See Cembrook v. Superior Court In and For City and County of San Francisco (1961) 56 Cal.2d 423, 430 [“If [the court] finds some requests too ambiguous to allow intelligent reply, it may sustain objection to them or, more consistently with justice, it may order such questions to be rephrased.”].)

            To the extent that (i) MFE nos. 17 and 22 seek information regarding BMW’s policies, procedures, or other guidelines related to repurchases and replacement requests from 2021 to present under the SBA and (ii) MFE no. 2 seeks BMW’s policies, procedures, or other guidelines in responding to consumer complaints alleging the Subject Vehicle is defective from 2021 to present, the Court agrees that such requests are overbroad. Essentially, these MFEs seek information encompassing a five-year span. However, the Subject Vehicle was manufactured 2023. Therefore, MFE nos. 2, 17, 22 should be limited to policies, procedures, or other guidelines related to repurchases and replacement requests, and consumer complaints alleging the Subject Vehicle was defective that were in effect from 2023 to present, i.e., that were in effect when the Subject Vehicle was manufactured. Plaintiff fails to demonstrate good cause exists to seek information under such an expansive timeframe for the Subject Vehicle, which was manufactured in 2023.

            Moreover, the Court finds that MFE nos. 3, 11, 14-15, 18, 23, 29-30, and 32-33 are overbroad and vague. Specifically, Plaintiff seeks “all communications” with Plaintiff; “all” documents BMW reviewed in investigating the cause of the complaints alleged regarding the Subject Vehicle; “all” investigations or analysis performed to determine the defects complained of; “all” telephone calls made by anyone including Plaintiff to BMW’s customer service assistance number regarding the Subject Vehicle; and “any and all” BMW’s responses to Plaintiff’s discovery; “any and all” BMW’s pleadings filed in this instant action. These MFEs should be limited in scope to information related to: (1) diagnostic procedures and policies consulted and followed in diagnosing the defects of the Subject Vehicle, which covers BMW’s investigative procedures used to determine the cause of the alleged defects; and (2) communications with Plaintiff regarding the defects affecting the Subject Vehicle, scheduling repairs for the Subject Vehicle, and requesting repurchase of the Subject Vehicle.

            Lastly, MFE nos. 16, 19-21, and 24-28 seek  “all” policies, procedures or other guidelines regarding recording calls made to BMW’s customer service number; the nature of BMW’s relationship with the company that handles its customer service calls; BMW’s ownership interest in the company that handles its customer service calls; the policies and procedures to be followed by the customer service company; and task and duties BMW authorizes the company that handles its customer service calls from 2021 to present. As for the MFE pertaining to the policies, procedures, or other guidelines regarding the calls made to BMW’s customer service number, it is already encompassed by MFE nos. 1, 4-10, 12-13, and 31.

            With respect to information regarding the duties or tasks BMW authorizes the company that handles its customer service calls to do, such request is relevant to Plaintiff’s warranty claims because they concern whether BMW’s customer service number agents complied with the SBA but should be limited to the year the Subject Vehicle was manufactured. However, Plaintiff fails to demonstrate good cause exists to permit the examination into BMW’s relationship with and/or ownership interest in the company that handles its customer service calls. In fact, Plaintiff has not alleged any claims against the customer service company used by BMW, which would make inquiry into these matters relevant to this instant action or reasonably calculated to lead to admissible evidence.

C.    Request for Monetary Sanctions

            “If a motion under [Code of Civil Procedure, section 2025.450, subdivision (a)] is granted, the court shall impose a monetary sanction . . . in favor of the party who noticed the deposition and against the deponent or the party with whom the deponent is affiliated, unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (g)(1).)

            Here, Plaintiff’s counsel, Mr. Shayesteh requests $5,475.00 in monetary discovery sanctions against BMW and its attorneys of record for 11.4 hours of attorney time reviewing and analyzing correspondence, BMW’s objections and drafting meet and confer correspondence, drafting the present motion and supporting documents; preparing the deposition notice; reviewing the opposition and drafting the reply; preparing for and attending the hearing on the motion at an hourly rate of $475.00 per hour plus $60.00 filing fee. (Shayesteh Decl., ¶¶18-19.) However, BMW’s counsel actively engaged in meet and confer efforts, provided dates available at the time, provided an additional once it became available, and timely served objections while awaiting correspondence from its client regarding the newly assigned PMQ’s availability. (Mack Decl., ¶¶7-18.) As such, imposing sanctions at this time would be unjust.

D.       CONCLUSION

Plaintiff Saeed Anavim’s Motion to Compel Deposition Attendance of BMW’s Person Most Qualified is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART. The request for sanctions is also DENIED.

Defendant BMW of North America, LLC is ordered to produce its Person Most Qualified on September 17, 2026 as to Matters for Examination nos. 1, 2-15, 17-18, 22-24, 26, 29-33 and produce responsive documents subject to the limitations set forth in this order.

Plaintiff Saeed Anavim to give notice.

25VECV06794 CESARIA GAMBOA vs BURLINGTON, et al.

August 6, 2026, at 8:30 a.m.

Van Nuys Courthouse East, Dept. I

Tentative Ruling – Motions to Compel Responses

 

 

 

I.        Background

 

On November 5, 2025, Plaintiff Cesaria Gamboa (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Burlington located at 7866 Van Nuys Blvd., Van Nuys, CA 91402 and Burlington Stores, Inc. (“Defendants”) alleging two causes of action: (1) premises liability; and (2) general negligence.

 

On May 22, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motions to compel Plaintiff’s responses to Request for Special Interrogatories, Set One, Special Interrogatories, Set One, and Request for Production of Documents, Set One. Defendants also request sanctions.

 

On July 24, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel filed a declaration in opposition.

 

On July 30, 2026, Defendants filed their reply.

 

II.     Motions to Compel Responses

 

Interrogatories

 

If a party to whom interrogatories are directed fails to serve a timely response, the propounding party may move for an order compelling responses and for a monetary sanction. (Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.290(b).) The statute does not contain a time limit for a motion to compel where no responses have been served. All that need be shown in the moving papers is that a set of interrogatories was properly served on the opposing party, that the time to respond has expired, and that no response of any kind has been served. (See Leach v. Superior Court (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 902, 905-906.)

 

Where there has been no timely response to interrogatories, the demanding party may seek an order compelling a response. (CCP § 2031.300(b).) Failure to timely respond waives all objections, including privilege and work product. (CCP § 2031.300(a).) Thus, unless the party to whom the demand was directed obtains relief from waiver, he or she cannot raise objections to the interrogatories. Likewise, for failure to respond, the moving party need not attempt to resolve the matter outside court before filing the motion.

 

 

Request for Production of Documents

 

Any party may obtain discovery by inspecting, copying, testing, or sampling documents, tangible things, land or other property, and electronically stored information in the possession, custody, or control of any other party to the action. (CCP § 2031.010(a).) If a party to whom a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling is directed fails to serve a timely response, the demanding party may move for an order compelling response to the demand. (CCP § 2031.300(b).) Failure to timely respond waives any objection to the demand. (CCP § 2031.300(a).)

 

Discussion

 

Defendants served their discovery responses on Plaintiff on March 27, 2026. (Porron Decl. at ¶ 5; Exhs. A-C.) As of the date of this motion, Plaintiff had yet to serve responses. (Id. at ¶ 8.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff’s counsel declares that, on July 24, 2026, Plaintiff served his discovery responses. (Hanassab Decl. at ¶ 11; Exh. A [responses].) In reply, Defendants argue that, due to the delay, this Court should grant monetary sanctions and deem Plaintiff’s objections waived and require objection free responses.

 

The Court agrees. Plaintiff is to serve Defendants with verified, code-compliant, objection-free responses.

 

Additionally, Defendants request sanctions in the total amount of $5,158.00 for all three motions, representing 9.5 hours of work at an hourly rate of $508.00 and $180.00 in filing fees. (Perron Decl. at ¶ 10.) Considering the simplicity of the motions, the Court finds this amount unreasonable and reduces sanctions to $3,000.00.

 

III.  Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendants’ motions to compel Plaintiff’s responses are GRANTED.

 

Plaintiff is ordered to serve Defendants with substantive, verified, and code-compliant responses to Defendants’ Form Interrogatories, Set One, Special Interrogatories, Set One, and Requests for Production of Documents, Set One, within 20 days of this order. Additionally, Plaintiff and Plaintiff’s counsel, jointly and severally, are to pay defense counsel sanctions in the amount of $3,000.00 within 20 days of this order.

25VECV06813 STANLEY WETCH, et al. vs CELTIC CAPITAL CORPORATION, A CALIFORNIA STOCK CORPORATION

August 7, 2026, at 8:30 a.m.

Van Nuys Courthouse East, Dept. I

Tentative Ruling – Motion to Compel Further Discovery

 

 

 

I.        Background

 

On December 1, 2025, Plaintiffs Stanley Wetch (“Wetch”) and La Flor de Mexico, Inc. (“La Flor”)(collectively “Plaintiffs”) filed a complaint against Celtic Capital Corporation (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 250 alleging eight causes of action: (1) conversion; (2) breach of contract; (3) accounting; (4) declaratory relief; (5) breach of fiduciary duty; (6) breach of statutory duty; (7) violations of unfair competition law; and (8) injunctive relief.

 

On January 7, 2026, Defendant filed a cross-complaint against Plaintiffs, the United States Small Business Administration, and Does 1 to 250 alleging two causes of action: (1) interpleader; and (2) breach of guaranty.

 

On June 26, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion to compel Defendant’s further responses to Requests for Production of Documents, Set One.

 

On July 20, 2026, Defendant filed its opposition.

 

On July 22, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their reply.

 

II.     Motion to Compel Further Discovery

 

“If a party filing a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling under Sections 2031.210, 2031.220, 2031.220, 2031.230, 2031.240, and 2031.280 thereafter fails to permit the inspection, copying, testing, or sampling in accordance with that party’s statement of compliance, the demanding party may move for an order compelling compliance.” (Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 2031.320(a).)

 

“. . . [T]he court shall impose a monetary sanction . . . against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel compliance with a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (CCP § 2031.320(b).)

 

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

As a preliminary matter, the Court overrules Defendant’s August 5, 2026, evidentiary objections to allegedly new evidence submitted with Plaintiffs’ reply, specifically, various meet and confer letters dated prior to Plaintiffs’ initial motion. (See August 5, 2026, Evidentiary Objections.) Plaintiffs referenced these letters in their initial moving papers. (See Garcia July 26, 2026, Decl.) Additionally, the Garcia declaration directly addresses points raised by Defendant’s opposition. (Ibid.)

 

Analysis

 

Pursuant to CCP § 2016.040, on April 20, 2026, Plaintiffs' counsel sent defense counsel a meet and confer letter regarding the alleged deficiencies in Defendant's discovery responses. (Garcia Decl. at ¶¶ 5-6.) Plaintiffs provided various extensions for amended responses. (Id. At ¶ 7.) Following several months, Defendant failed to provide supplemental responses. (Ibid.) Accordingly, Plaintiffs satisfy the meet and confer requirement of CCP § 2016.040."

 

Plaintiffs bring this motion to compel Defendant’s further responses to Requests for Production of Documents, Set One, Nos. 1-8 and 10-14. (See Separate Statement.) These requests pertain to, inter alia, documents reflecting negotiations between Defendants and Plaintiffs concerning the Discretionary Clause at issue in the parties’ contract, communications between Defendant and “any borrower in the State of California” concerning the Discretionary Clause, all documents reflecting all income received following Defendant’s foreclosure on La Flor, all documents reflecting all expenses paid from the. Proceeds of Defendant’s foreclosure on La Flor, all documents reflecting accountings Defendant has provided to La Flor following Defendant’s foreclosure, “[a]ll account records, including registers, logs, income received, expenses incurred, payments made following [Defendant]’s foreclosure on La Flor under the Agreement,” “[a]ll DOCUMENTS or COMMUNICATIONS from any person or entity making demand for payment from the time YOU initiated foreclosure under the Agreement, to the date YOU respond to this request,” “[a]ll DOCUMENTS reflecting any demand for payment from the Small Business Administration related to YOUR foreclosure under the Agreement,” “[f]or the period between January 1, 2022, and December 1, 2025, copies of all Loan and Security Agreements entered into between YOU and any borrower in the State of California and wherein the Agreements contained the Discretionary Clause,” and more. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Request No. 1 as Plaintiffs is also in possession of documents that “reflect negotiations” between themselves and Defendant. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Request Nos. 2-3 and 12-13 since the requests are vague and overbroad and loan information of other non-parties is unlikely to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence that is not protected by privacy rights. (See Grisworld v. State of Connecticut (1965) 381 U.S. 479, 484 [disclosure of private financial information of non-parties may be shielded from discovery; right of privacy guaranteed by the U.S. Constitution].)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Requests Nos. 4-5 and 7 as “all income,” “all expenses,” and “[a]ll accounting” following Defendant’s foreclosure on La Flor is impermissibly overbroad and vague. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Request No. 6 since Plaintiffs should also have, in their possession, “accountings [Defendant] provided to La Flor . . .” (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Request No. 8 since “[a]ll DOCUMENTS or COMMUNICATIONS from any person or entity making demand for payment” is impermissibly overbroad and vague. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is granted as to Request No. 10 since documents reflecting any demand(s) for payment from the Small Business Administration are directly relevant to Defendant’s cross-complaint against the Smal Business Administration. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Request No. 11 since “any written demand for wage payments” is impermissibly overbroad and vague. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is granted as to Requests No. 14.  Documents reflecting any subordinate security interests to the surplus proceeds are directly relevant to both Plaintiffs’ claims and Defendant’s cross-complaint. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Sanctions

 

As a final matter, Defendant’s request for sanctions on the grounds that Plaintiffs’ motion was premature for failure to engage in meet and confer is denied since the Court finds that Plaintiffs sufficiently met and conferred regarding the allegedly deficient responses. (See Opposition at 11:8-12.)

 

III.  Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiffs’ motion is GRANTED in part and DENIED in part.

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Requests Nos. 1-8 and 11-13.

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is granted as to Requests Nos. 10 and 14.

 

Defendant is to serve supplemental, verified, objection-free responses to Plaintiffs’ Requests for Production of Documents Nos. 10 and 14 within 20 days of this order.

 

Moving party to give notice.
