[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION AUGMENT THE ADMINISTRATIVE RECORD 

 

Petitioner Robert Renko (“Petitioner”) seeks to augment the administrative record.

The motion is denied.

I.                Background

On February 18, 2026, this Court entered judgment denying the underlying writ petition in this matter.

Petitioner filed a notice of appeal on March 16, 2026. While the appeal was pending, Petitioner sought to add materials to the trial court record through a motion in the Court of Appeal; that request was denied. The Court of Appeal noted only that its denial was “without prejudice” to seeking augmentation “should an included document prove missing.” (Resp. Opp., Organ Decl., ¶ 13, Exh. H.) The instant motion followed in this court.

The instant motion, filed July 1, 2026, seeks to “augment the administrative record” to include an April 26, 2019 letter from Petitioner’s prior counsel to Francis J. Boyd at LACERA (and related materials previously rejected), on the purported basis that these documents were omitted form the certified record and are necessary to correct alleged misconduct or misrepresentations affecting the underlying disability retirement proceedings.

Petitioner previously sought to augment the administrative record in connection with his writ petition. On September 23, 2025, the Court (Hon. James Chalfant) denied that request as to the same materials at issue here, finding that the documents predated the administrative hearing, were available in Petitioner’s counsel’s file years before LACERA’s final decision, and were not shown to be relevant to the asserted theory of misconduct. (See Court Order dated 9/23/2026, addressing Petitioner’s Exhibit 2; see also Resp. Opp., Organ Decl., ¶ 11, Exh. F [Exh. 1 therein].)

On July 17, 2026, Respondents filed their opposition to the motion. On July 20, 2026, Petitioner filed a reply. On July 22, 2026, Petitioner filed a “correction and clarification” of his Reply.[1]

II.             Discussion

1.               Lack of Jurisdiction

The Court lacks jurisdiction to grant the requested relief. Upon filing of the notice of appeal, jurisdiction over matters “embraced in or affected by the judgment” shifted to the Court of Appeal under Code of Civil Procedure section 916.

The instant motion seeks to alter the administrative record underlying the judgment and to reopen merits briefing. However, granting such relief would undoubtedly modify or affect the judgment now on appeal. Petitioner’s reply papers do not meaningfully address Code of Civil Procedure section 916, identify any exception that applies, or otherwise supply authority barring its application to these facts. Further, the Court of Appeal’s order does not authorize renewed litigation of previously denied augmentation requests. On its face, it appears to contemplate only correction where a document already included in the administrative record is missing from the appellate record, which is not the case here.

Additionally, Petitioner’s reply asks this Court to remand the matter to the Board of Retirement for a new determination on an expanded record. This requested relief, which would reopen the merits and direct new administrative proceedings, further confirms that Petitioner is not seeking correction of an inadvertently omitted document from the appellate record, but instead seeks to relitigate issues already resolved by this Court and now pending on appeal.

The motion is therefore DENIED for lack of jurisdiction.

2.               Improper Motion for Reconsideration

Independently, and assuming arguendo that the Court in fact has jurisdiction to hear it, the instant motion constitutes an improper attempt to relitigate an issue previously decided. As reflected in the September 23, 2025 minute order, this Court already denied Petitioner’s request to add the same documents to the administrative record.

Even assuming such motion were proper or timely, Petitioner has not shown “new or different facts, circumstances, or law” as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 1008. The renewed motion is substantively identical to the prior request, and the reply does not rebut that the evidence was available years before the administrative hearing and that the prior denial resolved the same issues now presented.

Accordingly, the motion is DENIED on this separate, alternative basis.

III.           Conclusion

The motion to augment the administrative record is DENIED.

I.         BACKGROUND

1.  Petition, Opposition, and Reply

Petitioner Christopher Mancini (Petitioner), a student at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA or “the University”), filed this action on October 13, 2025, seeking a writ of administrative mandate/review. Petitioner challenges a decision by the Regents of the University of California (Respondent) to impose a nine-quarter suspension on Petitioner after finding Petitioner violated two sections of UCLA’s Student Conduct Code (Conduct Code). Petitioner prays for an order compelling Respondent to set aside its decision and remanding Petitioner’s case for further proceedings consistent with the Court’s order.

Petitioner filed his operative First Amended Petition (FAP) on January 16, 2026.

On January 26, 2026, Respondent lodged the 35-page Administrative Record (AR) with the Court.

On March 25, 2026, Petitioner filed his opening brief in support of his Petition.

On May 14, 2026, Respondent filed its opposition brief.

On June 10, 2026, Petitioner filed his reply brief.

2. Underlying Incidents

The primary incident (Incident) underlying the University’s disciplinary action occurred on February 7, 2025. (AR 10.) On that date, Petitioner worked as a referee overseeing a basketball game at the Wooden Center gymnasium on UCLA’s campus. (AR 10.) Another student, designated “Witness 1” in the administrative record, worked as a Competitive Sports Supervisor and oversaw Petitioner’s work as referee. (AR 10.)

During a break in one basketball game, Petitioner and Witness 1 began verbally arguing. (AR 10.) Witness 1 stated he “gave [Petitioner] feedback” and Petitioner reacted badly. (AR 10.) Petitioner contends he and Witness 1 had a contentious relationship prior to the date of the Incident, and Witness 1 spoke to him disrespectfully, then threateningly. (AR 10.) In either case, the encounter concluded with Petitioner punching Witness 1 in the head, after which both students left the gymnasium. (AR  5 [Incident video]; AR 10 [Hearing Report]; see also FAP, ¶ 20 [third student “helped break up [Witness 1] and Petitioner who separately left”].)

The University of California Police Department (UCPD) arrested Petitioner two days after the Incident. (AR 10-11.) After the arrest, UCPD briefly incarcerated Petitioner in a holding cell. (AR 11.)

The second incident underlying the University’s disciplinary action took place while Petitioner was in holding, when he climbed onto a sink and slapped or punched the security camera placed in his cell (“cell camera”). (AR 6 [incident video], AR 10-11 [Hearing Report].)

3. The University’s Investigation

On February 21, 2025, Associate Dean of Students Benito Nieves (Nieves) notified Petitioner that Petitioner would be charged with violations of Conduct Code sections 102.04b (Damage or Destruction of any University Property) and 102.08 (Conduct that Threatens Health or Safety). (AR 9, AR 33-34.) Nieves’ letter notifying Petitioner of the charges instructs Petitioner to “familiarize yourself with [the Conduct Code]” and explains his right to select an Advisor to participate in disciplinary proceedings. (AR 33-34.)

On March 13, 2025, Nieves met with Petitioner and his Advisor, Stephen Fisch. (AR 24.) Nieves made various initial disclosures regarding Nieves’ role, Petitioner’s rights, and the probable sanctions if a disciplinary hearing panel ultimately found Petitioner had violated the Conduct Code. (AR 24.) The meeting was not transcribed or recorded. (AR 24.) Nieves’ Meeting Notes state that he “confirmed that [Petitioner] received and reviewed the UCPD summary” of events. (AR 24.)

On April 9, 2025, Nieves corresponded with a UCPD representative, who stated that repairs to the cell camera Petitioner had damaged cost approximately $750. (AR 11.)

On April 24, 2025, Nieves met with Witness 1. (AR 11.) Witness 1 characterized Petitioner’s behavior during the Incident as going “‘from zero to 100’” and recalled Petitioner threatening to “‘beat the shit out of’” Witness 1. (AR 11.) During this meeting, Nieves’ notes reflect that he stated that Witness 1 “[was] not required to participate in the process” and could “opt out of sharing” and that Petitioner would “receive a summary, not a transcript, of what [Witness 1] shared” during the meeting. (AR 27.)

On May 1, 2025, Nieves met with Petitioner again. (AR 11, AR 25-26.) At that meeting, Petitioner and Nieves reviewed video footage of the Incident. (AR  11-12, AR 23 [video “viewed with C. Mancini”].) Petitioner identified himself, Witness 1, and a third student, Witness 3, in the footage. (AR 12.) [1] Nieves “shared [with Petitioner] the updates from [Nieves’] meeting with Witness #1”. (AR 25.) As with the March 13 meeting, the May 1 meeting was not transcribed or recorded. (AR 25-26.)

On May 2, 2025, Nieves met with Witness 3. (AR 12, AR 32-33.) Witness 3 recalled that Witness 1 “attempted to discuss the matter [with Petitioner] outside, as the environment was “‘pretty loud;’” that Petitioner became violent after Witness 1 told him, “[t]hat is it, go home. You are done for the day;” and that when Witness 3 attempted to intervene, Petitioner asked, “[d]o you want to go, too?,” and Witness 3 believed Petitioner was about to assault him as well. (AR 12.) As with Witness 1, Nieves informed Witness 3 his participation was optional and no transcript of his statement would be provided to Petitioner. (AR 32.)

On June 26, 2025, Nieves notified Petitioner that his case had been referred to the Student Conduct Committee for a disciplinary hearing. (AR 35.)

4. The Disciplinary Hearing

On July 22, 2025, a Student Conduct Committee Hearing Panel (Panel) conducted a hearing via Zoom. (AR 9.) Nieves participated for the University in that he “provided an overview of the case and was available to answer questions.” (AR 9.) Petitioner also participated, as did as an Advisor and a Support Person on his behalf. (AR 9.)

Nieves presented the Panel with the findings from his investigation, stated above.

At the hearing, Petitioner “took ‘full responsibility for [his] part’” in the Incident and expressed regret that the Incident became physically violent. (AR 14, bracketing in original.) Petitioner characterized the scene as chaotic, with “‘very little space between [him] and [Witness 1]’” with “‘everyone ... yelling.’” (AR 14, bracketing in original.) Petitioner contended he gave Witness 1 the opportunity to disengage from the encounter. (AR 14.) He also stated that Witness 1 had “‘lurched’ forward at [Petitioner]” after “‘getting in [his] face,’” such that Petitioner feared for his safety and acted in self-defense. (AR 14, bracketing in original.)

The Panel issued a written decision after the hearing. (AR  9-18.) The Panel’s decision recited evidence from both parties that it “focused on,” “noted,” or “discussed” in reaching its final decision. (AR 16.) The Panel found that “insufficient information” or “inconsistencies” precluded a finding that Petitioner had made threatening statements that caused Witness 1 or Witness 3 to be “in fear for their own safety” or “in sustained fear for their own safety” during the Incident. (AR 16.) The Panel “found it more likely than not” that Petitioner had violated Conduct Code Section 102.08.

The Panel also recited the evidence in support of Petitioner’s damaging the UCPD cell camera and “found it more likely than not” that Petitioner had violated Conduct Code Section 102.04b. (AR 16-17.)

The Panel recommended a nine-quarter suspension, from Summer 2025 through Summer 2027. (AR 17, 18.)

On August 5, 2025, Jasmine JS Rush, Dean of Students, issued a letter to Petitioner upholding the Panel’s findings without substantive discussion. (AR 7-8.)

5. Petitioner’s Appeal Letter

On August 14, 2025, Petitioner submitted an appeal letter [2] to Monroe Gordon Jr., Vice Chancellor for Student Affairs, contending Respondent should reverse its decision for the following reasons:

1.         Petitioner submitted written questions for witnesses, but they were not posed because the witnesses did not appear at the hearing and Dean Nieves interviewed them before the questions were submitted. A few of Petitioner’s questions were posed to the Dean Nieves during the Hearing, but he did not have percipient knowledge to answer the questions (AR 1);

2.         Petitioner was not provided a copy of the video of the Incident before his hearing (AR 1);

3.         The camera in Petitioner’s holding cell was “restored to functionality” as demonstrated during criminal proceedings against him, contrary to the Panel’s findings after hearing (AR  1-2); and

4.         The University’s sanction was unjustifiably harsh because, among other reasons, (a) Witness 1 was the aggressor and Petitioner acted in self-defense; (b) Petitioner had suffered no prior discipline during his time as a University student; and (c) the discipline fell outside the stated purpose of University disciplinary proceedings, since Petitioner had completed his degree requirements and there was no ongoing threat to student safety and Witness 1 was not injured in the Incident (AR 2-3).

On August 22, 2025, Vice Chancellor Gorden confirmed receipt of Petitioner’s letter and declined to vacate or modify the Panel’s decision. (AR 19.)

On October 13, 2025, Petitioner filed this action, relying on the same grounds for relief stated in his August 14, 2025 appeal letter.

 

II.        LEGAL STANDAR D

The Superior Court may issue a writ of mandate “for the purpose of inquiring into the validity of any final administrative order or decision made as the result of a proceeding in which by law a hearing is required to be given, evidence is required to be taken, and discretion in the determination of facts is vested in the inferior tribunal, corporation, board, or officer[.]” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1094.5(a).)

“The inquiry in such a case shall extend to the questions whether the respondent has proceeded without, or in excess of, jurisdiction; whether there was a fair trial; and whether there was any prejudicial abuse of discretion. Abuse of discretion is established if the respondent has not proceeded in the manner required by law, the order or decision is not supported by the findings, or the findings are not supported by the evidence.” (Id., subd. (b).)

“A university student may challenge a disciplinary sanction by a petition for writ of administrative mandate. [Citations.] ... [¶] ‘ “The statute’s requirement of a ‘ “fair” ’ trial means that there must have been ‘a fair administrative hearing.’ ” [Citation.] Where student discipline is at issue, the university must comply with its own policies and procedures.’ [Citation.]” (Doe v. Regents of University of California (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 1245, 1254 (Doe (2026)).)

For a public university, “[i]n addition to the university’s expertise and familiarity with its student conduct procedures, ‘[t]he California Constitution (art. IX, § 9, subd. (a)) grants [the Regents] “ ‘ “broad powers to organize and govern the university and limits the Legislature’s power to regulate either the university or the [R]egents.” ’ ” ’ ... [¶] This power necessarily includes quasi-judicial administrative authority to resolve disciplinary disputes by applying university policies to particular cases.” (Berman v. Regents of University of California (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 1265, 1272, internal citations omitted.)

 

III.      DISCUSSION

1.         Standards of Review

The Court applies its independent judgment to the bulk of the instant dispute. The primary question is solely of law: whether, on the undisputed facts, Petitioner was provided with a fair hearing that satisfies his due process rights. “On questions of law arising in mandate proceedings, [the court] exercise[s] independent judgment.’”  (Christensen v. Lightbourne (2017) 15 Cal.App.5th 1239, 1251.)

One of Petitioner’s arguments is subject to a heightened standard: his argument that the University imposed a penalty disproportionate to his offenses. Where, as here, relevant facts are undisputed, a court pays the most extreme deference when reviewing the severity of a penalty. A court may overturn a university’s disciplinary sanctions “only in the exceptional case, when it is shown that reasonable minds cannot differ on the propriety of the penalty[.]” (Doe (2016), v. Regents of University of California (2016) 5 Cal.App.5th 1055, 1106.)

2.         Fairness of Hearing

            Petitioner primarily contends he did not receive a fair hearing because he was not provided an opportunity to cross-examine witnesses during Nieves’ investigation or the subsequent disciplinary hearing.

            The Court disagrees.

“While live adversarial questioning may be considered essential in the context of a criminal trial ... , there is no absolute right to a live hearing with cross-examination in administrative proceedings, even where constitutional due process applies. As courts have explained in other administrative contexts, ‘“[d]ifferences in the origin and function of administrative agencies ‘preclude wholesale transplantation of the rules of procedure, trial, and review which have evolved from the history and experience of courts.’ ... The judicial model of an evidentiary hearing is neither a required, nor even the most effective, method of decisionmaking in all circumstances.” ’ ... The fair procedure doctrine similarly recognizes ‘the practical limitations on the ability of private institutions to provide for the full airing of disputed factual issues.’ ” (Boermeester v. Carry (2023) 15 Cal.5th 72, 93-94 (Boermeester).)

“Requiring live hearings featuring real-time cross-examination of witnesses in the accused student’s presence would be contrary to our [Supreme Court’s] ... conclusion that ‘fair procedure does not compel formal proceedings with all the embellishments of a court trial.’ [Citation.] It would also be contrary to [the Supreme Court’s] admonition that courts must refrain from fixing rigid trial-like procedures ‘that must invariably be observed.’ [Citation.]” (Id., at p. 93.) “[T]he ‘“procedures for dismissing college students [are] not analogous to criminal proceedings and could not be so without at the same time being both impractical and detrimental to the educational atmosphere and functions of a university.”’ ... .” (Id., at p. 94, internal citations omitted.)

“‘A university’s primary purpose is to educate students: “[a] school is an academic institution, not a courtroom or administrative hearing room.” ... Although a university must treat students fairly, it is not required to convert its classrooms into courtrooms.’ [Citation.]” (Doe v. Regents of University of California (2016) 5 Cal.App.5th 1055, 1078 (Doe (2016)); see also James v. City of Coronado (2003) 106 Cal.App.4th 905, 912 [“we reject the notion that as a matter of law every administrative appeal ... must afford ... an opportunity to confront and cross-examine witnesses”].)

Respondent cites a standard for fair process stated recently in Ayach v. Regents of University of California (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 331 (Ayach), where two petitioners challenged the decision of the University of California, Riverside to expel them.

The petitioners in Ayach asserted due process arguments nearly identical to those Petitioner asserts here: “that the University’s administrative hearings leading to their expulsions did not afford them due process because (1) the charging documents and evidence presented used pseudonyms to identify witnesses who provided statements to University investigators, and (2) [the petitioners] were purportedly denied the opportunity to confront or cross-examine witnesses at the hearing.” (Ayach, supra, at p. 33.)

The Court of Appeal affirmed the decision denying the petitioners a writ of mandate, holding that “[d]ue process in the context of University discipline proceedings is a flexible concept that depends on the specific circumstances and charges at issue in each particular case,” and “the proceedings afforded [the petitioners] the process they were due, given the nature of the charges and their response.” (Ibid.)

The Ayach court distinguished numerous decisions enforcing a right to confrontation in administrative proceedings in cases where a student was accused of sexual assault. Here, Petitioner relies on the same precedents Ayach distinguished. The decision distinguished those cases as cases that “‘turn[ed] on witness credibility,’” where it was necessary for “‘the adjudicator or adjudicators [to] be able to see the parties’ testimony and testimony of important witnesses so their demeanor may be observed.’” (Id., at pp. 341-342, quoting Knight v. South Orange Community College (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 854, 866, citing Doe v. Regents of University of California (2021) 70 Cal.App.5th 521, Doe v. Westmont College (2019) 34 Cal.App.5th 622, Doe v. Regents of University of California (2018) 28 Cal.App.5th 44; see also Doe v. Regents of University of California (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 1245.)

The Court finds this case more like Ayach than the sexual assault precedents. The Courts of Appeal in the sexual assault precedents went out of their way to emphasize the unique circumstances there that posed particular risks to the accused: for example, the high legal and reputational stakes, and the uniquely private setting for the alleged conduct. The facts in Ayach were not subject to the same special considerations; the court reviewed a widespread toxic hazing scheme that involved simple physical violence inflicted on a number of students. The violations took place in public and were well-supported by witness accounts.

The accusations against Petitioner here are similarly unremarkable. Petitioner’s assault on Witness 1 was a single incident, captured on video, with only a few contextual facts disputed. [3] For the damage to the cell camera in particular, the entire incident was clearly recorded.

While Petitioner relies on Penal Code concepts of self-defense, the University’s disciplinary process is not a criminal prosecution, and criminal standards do not govern student discipline. The question for the Panel was not whether Petitioner would have a viable criminal defense, but whether his conduct violated the Conduct Code. The Panel’s finding of “physical assault” necessarily reflects its determination that Petitioner’s self-defense theory did not negate the violation. Due process does not require the University to issue separate findings disposing of criminal law defenses that are inapplicable in the administrative context. (See Goss v. Lopez (1975) 419 U.S. 565; Doe v. University of Southern California (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 221; Doe v. University of Southern California (2018) 28 Cal,.App.5th 26; Doe (2016), supra, 5 Cal.App.5th 1055; and Ayach, supra, 102 Cal.App.5th 331, all of which reject the importation of criminal law-style procedural and substantive standards into school discipline.)

The relevant decisions emphasize that fair procedure relies on the circumstances of a given offense. (See Boermeester, supra, 12 Cal.5th, at p. 87 [fair procedures “‘depend[ ] largely on “the nature of the tendered issue” ’ ”].) “‘[T]o comport with due process,’ the university’s procedures must ‘“be tailored, in light of the decision to be made, to ‘the capacities and circumstances of those who are to be heard,’ [citation] ... to insure that they are given a meaningful opportunity to present their case.”’ ... .” (Doe v. Regents of University of California (2016) 5 Cal.App.5th, at p. 1078, internal citations omitted.)

On the undisputed facts regarding the disciplinary procedure, the Court finds Petitioner was given a meaningful opportunity to present his case. Witness accounts were relayed to him through Dean Nieves’ testimony, and he was given the opportunity to explain his version of events – to Dean Nieves initially, and then to the Panel. Petitioner reviewed the video of the Incident alongside Dean Nieves long before his hearing (and he does not make a persuasive case that the University was required to give him his own personal copy of the video).[4] As noted, the substance of the Incident was captured on video; for the damage to the cell camera, Petitioner’s conduct was entirely captured, and there were no conflicting witness accounts.[5]

The sexual assault precedents Petitioner relies on are distinguishable, as confirmed in Ayach.[6] This is not the type of credibility-dependent case that triggers the enhanced procedural protections that reviewing courts have recognized in sexual misconduct cases. Those protections have arisen where witness credibility is uniquely dispositive and there is little other evidence to resolve what occurred. In this matter, other than the camera destruction, the core underlying act of Petitioner punching another student in the head is undisputed, supported by video footage, corroborated by Petitioner’s own admissions, and supported by consistent contemporaneous witness statements. The Panel also rejected the alleged “threats” charge based on inconsistencies, which confirms that it did not treat hearsay uncritically.

The remaining dispute centers on Petitioner’s subjective interpretation of the events, not the occurrence of the punch (or the camera destruction) itself. Petitioner’s argument that the Panel was required to make an express finding resolving his asserted theory of self-defense is unpersuasive. Administrative fact-finders in student discipline matters are not obligated to parse or formally reject every alternative explanation offered by the accused, particularly where the core conduct is captured on video and admitted by the student. The Panel’s finding that Petitioner engaged in “physical assault” necessarily reflects its rejection of Petitioner’s self-defense theory. Nothing in Topanga requires an agency to issue line-item findings on speculative or self-serving theories where the Panel’s conclusion is amply supported by the evidence. Petitioner’s assertion that he perceived danger does not, on its own, convert an otherwise straightforward incident into a credibility dispute requiring live testimony.

In light of the capacities of the parties involved, under the circumstances of this case, and based on the nature of the tendered issue, the Court finds Petitioner was given sufficient opportunity to defend himself and challenge other witnesses’ account of events.

The Court also declines to reverse the University’s decision based on the severity of the sanction imposed. As noted above, the University is entitled to extreme deference when determining appropriate disciplinary sanctions. Even if another decisionmaker might have selected a lesser sanction, the question is whether “no reasonable mind” could impose this penalty. On this record, Petitioner has not demonstrated any abuse of discretion.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

Petitioner Christopher Mancini’s Petition for Writ of Administrative Mandate/Review is DENIED.

Plaintiff Leon Ahdoot (“Plaintiff”), individually and as trustee of The L&L Ahdoot Family Trust (“Trust”) seeks an order appointing Stephen J. Donell (“Donell”) as receiver for nominal defendants Oxford Financial, Inc. (“Oxford”), Santa Fe Corner, LLC (“Santa Fe”), and Santa Monica Corner, LLC (“Santa Monica”) (collectively, “Businesses”) pursuant to a joint stipulation by the parties.

I.                Background

1.               Factual Background

In February 2009, Plaintiff and defendant Shahram Mokhtarzadeh (“Mokhtarzadeh”) formed Oxford, a California corporation. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 3.) II.             Legal Standard

The appointment of a receiver is a drastic remedy to be utilized only in “exceptional cases.” As such, a receiver should not be appointed unless absolutely essential and because no other remedy will serve its purpose. (City & County of San Francisco v. Daley, (1993) 16 Cal.App.4th 734, 744.) Generally, a plaintiff who seeks appointment of a receiver of certain property has the burden to establish by a preponderance of the evidence that plaintiff has a joint interest with defendant in the property, that the property is in danger of being lost, removed or materially injured, and that plaintiff's right to possession is probable. (AlhambraShumway Mines, Inc. v. Alhambra Gold Mine Corp., (1953) 116 Cal.App.2d 869, 873.)

The Court is “not at liberty to create or recognize a nonstatutory receiver, even where the parties have agreed to the appointment of one.” (March v. Williams (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 238, 248; Lewis v. Shaw (1926) 77 Cal.App. 99, 102 [“jurisdiction to appoint a receiver cannot be conferred upon a court by stipulation or consent of the parties.”]; First Nat. Bank of Auburn v. Superior Court (1909) 12 Cal.App. 335, 344 [“Where the court has no authority to appoint a receiver, such authority cannot be conferred by consent or stipulation of the parties.”]; Baker v. Varney (1900) 129 Cal. 564, 566; Scott v. Hotchkiss (1896) 115 Cal. 89, 94 [“No stipulation can confer jurisdiction upon the court to appoint a receiver in a case where the court has no such authority given by law.”])

III.           Analysis

When the Court set the instant OSC, the Court ordered Plaintiff to file a brief addressing four issues: (1) the statutory basis for receivership; (2) why a receivership is necessary despite the availability of the less drastic provisional director remedy previously sought; (3) how any proposed receivership powers would be narrowly tailored; and (4) how any proposed order comports with legal standards governing receiverships, including requirements relating to undertakings, court approval mechanisms for professional engagements and expenditures, and the principle that a receiver may not exercise unilateral authority in matters requiring judicial oversight.

Plaintiff relies on Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 564(b)(6) and (9) to justify appointment of a receiver. Subdivision (b)(6) applies “[w]here a corporation is insolvent, or in imminent danger of insolvency, or has forfeited its corporate rights.” (CCP § 564(b)(6).) Subdivision (b)(9) applies “[i]n all other cases where necessary to preserve the property or rights of any party.” (CCP § 564(b)(9).)

However, Plaintiff made no showing, despite the Court’s express order, of why a receivership is necessary despite availability of appointment of a provisional director. Further, Plaintiff made no showing, despite the Court’s express order, as to how the proposed receiver’s power would be narrowly tailored, only offering that “[t]he parties will attempt to streamline their stipulated appointment order to more clearly delineate which authority they wish the receiver to have and remove any unnecessary authority.” (Suppl. Br. at 5-6.)

In short, the Court ordered Plaintiff to explain why such a drastic remedy is necessary—not merely authorized—and how it could be appropriately narrowed. Plaintiff declined. As noted above, a receiver should not be appointed unless absolutely essential and because no other remedy will serve its purpose. (City & County of San Francisco v. Daley, supra, 16 Cal.App.4th at 744.) As Plaintiff has not demonstrated a provisional remedy would be insufficient, the Court finds appointment of a provisional director would adequately protect the parties’ rights. Accordingly, appointment of a receiver is not appropriate.

IV.           Conclusion

The motion to appoint Donell as receiver for the Businesses is DENIED.

If Plaintiff wishes to have its prior motion for appointment of a provisional director placed back on calendar, Plaintiff can so advise the Court at hearing.

III.           Legal Standard

A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the pleading alone and will be sustained where the pleading is defective on its face.

A demurrer may be asserted on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) The person who filed the pleading does not have legal capacity to sue; (c) There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) There is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) The pleading is uncertain (“uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible); (g) In an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct; (h) No certificate was filed as required by CCP section 411.35 or (i) by CCP section 411.36. CCP § 430.10. Accordingly, a demurrer tests the sufficiency of a pleading, and the grounds for a demurrer must appear on the face of the pleading or from judicially noticeable matters. (CCP § 430.30(a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The face of the pleading includes attachments and incorporations by reference (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94); it does not include inadmissible hearsay. (Day v. Sharp, (1975) 50 Cal.App.3d 904, 914.)

As set forth below, Petitioner’s action is time-barred.

The Nursing Practice Act, Business and Professions Code section 2700, et seq., governs the Board. Under Business and Professions Code section 2750, licensees of the Board are subject to disciplinary proceedings conducted under Government Code section 11500, et seq.. (Bus. & Prof. Code § 2750.)

Under Government Code section 11521, the Board may reconsider its own decision, but the power to order reconsideration expires 30 days after delivery or mailing of that decision, or the decision’s effective date if earlier.[1] (Gov. Code § 11521.) Here, the Board’s decision was mailed April 25, 2025 and effective May 23, 2025. (RJN Ex. D.) Therefore, the Board’s power to reconsider expired May 23, 2025. (Gov. Code § 11521.)

Under Government Code section 11523, any petition for mandamus seeking judicial review must be filed within 30 days after the last day reconsideration can be ordered. (Gov. Code § 11523.) Here, 30 days after May 23, 2025 was June 22, 2025. As June 22, 2025 was a Sunday, the last day to file a petition was June 23, 2025. (CCP § 12.) Therefore, to bring a timely action, Petitioner was required to file her petition by June 23, 2025. (Gov. Code § 11523.)

Petitioner filed her petition on April 21, 2026, nearly a year past the close of the period to file for mandamus.

Without deciding that actual notice was required, the Court finds Petitioner had actual notice of the Board’s decision for at least all but the first day of the period to seek judicial review.

Based on the foregoing, the petition is untimely.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: ORDER TO SHOW CAUSE RE: PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION 

 

Petitioner Christine De Anda (“Petitioner”) seeks a preliminary injunction restraining respondent County of Los Angeles, Department of Animal Care and Control (“Respondent”) from euthanizing or destroying any of the approximately 303 animals seized from Petitioner on or about March 20, 2026 (“Seized Animals”).

The preliminary injunction is granted.

I.                Background

Petitioner operates Rock N’ Pawz Rescue (“Rescue”), a nonprofit animal rescue organization. (De Anda Decl., ¶ 2.) Petitioner contends the Rescue’s animals are frequently in poor condition because the Rescue specializes in caring for animals which most shelters would deem unadoptable. (De Anda Decl., ¶ 3.) The Seized Animals include Rescue animals as well as Petitioner’s pets. (De Anda Decl., ¶ 4.)

On March 20, 2026, Respondent executed a search warrant (“Warrant”) and seized the Seized Animals (“Seizure”): 232 dogs, 65 cats, two goats, a goose, parrot, a pig, and a rabbit. (De Anda Decl., ¶¶ 13-17.)

On March 23, 2026, Respondent amputated the rear left leg of a dog identified as A5763585. (Yusin Decl., ¶¶ 5-6, Ex. C at 14-15.)

Petitioner filed her Verified Petition for Writ of Mandate and Complaint for Declaratory, Injunctive, and Other Relief (“Petition”) on May 29, 2026. On July 6, 2026, Respondent demurred to the Petition.

On July 10, 2026, the Court heard and denied Petitioner’s ex parte application for an order to advance hearing date and shorten time on motion to quash warrant. The same day, the Court called Petitioner’s ex parte application for a temporary restraining order (“TRO”) and order to show cause (“OSC”) re: preliminary injunction and continued the hearing to that afternoon. Still that same day, the Court heard Petitioner’s ex parte application for a TRO and OSC, and granted the application in part. The Court granted a TRO restraining Respondent only from euthanizing or destroying any of the seized animals. The Court further granted an OSC re: preliminary injunction only for the same restraint. The Court ordered Respondent could apply for ex parte relief if it determined euthanasia or destruction of any specific Seized Animal was urgently required during the pendency of the TRO. To date, Respondent has made no requests for ex parte relief.

On July 13, 2026, Petitioner filed her supplemental supporting brief pursuant to the OSC. The same day, Petitioner filed a notice of lodging indicating she lodged a flash drive containing video recording. On July 23, 2026, Respondent filed its opposition to the OSC. The same day, Respondent objected to the contents of the flash drive lodged with the Court on the basis that Petitioner has not served Respondent with its contents.

On August 21, 2026, Department A19 at the Antelope Valley Courthouse is scheduled to hear Respondent’s “Motion/Petition for Order re Handling of Seized Animals”, in which Respondent requests relief from the restriction in the Warrant preventing Respondent from euthanizing or destroying the animals.

II.             Evidentiary Objections

Respondent objects to Exhibit E to Petitioner’s supplemental supporting papers, video contained on a flash drive lodged with the Court, on the basis that Respondent has not been served a copy of the video. The objection is sustained. Separately, the contested Exhibit E is not necessary to resolution of the preliminary injunction.

III.           Discussion

In determining whether to issue a preliminary injunction, the trial court considers two factors: (1) the reasonable probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits at trial (Code Civ. Proc. [“CCP”] §526(a)(1)), and (2) a balancing of the “irreparable harm” that the plaintiff is likely to sustain if the injunction is denied as compared to the harm that the defendant is likely to suffer if the court grants a preliminary injunction. (CCP §526(a)(2); 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396, 1402; Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 1279, 1283; Davenport v. Blue Cross of California (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 435, 446; Abrams v. St. John’s Hospital (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 628, 636.) Thus, a preliminary injunction may not issue without some showing of potential entitlement to such relief. (Doe v. Wilson (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 296, 304.)

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending final resolution upon a trial. (See Scaringe v. J.C.C. Enterprises, Inc. (1988) 205 Cal.App.3d 1536; Grothe v. Cortlandt Corp. (1992) 11 Cal.App.4th 1313, 1316; Major v. Miraverde Homeowners Assn. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 618, 623.) The status quo has been defined to mean the last actual peaceable, uncontested status which preceded the pending controversy. (Voorhies v. Greene (1983) 139 Cal.App.3d 989, 995, quoting United Railroads v. Superior Court (1916) 172 Cal. 80, 87; 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396, 1402.)

1.               Probability of Success

“The trial court's determination must be guided by a ‘mix’ of the potential-merit and interim-harm factors; the greater the plaintiff's showing on one, the less must be shown on the other to support an injunction.” (Butt v. State of California (1992) 4 Cal.4th 668, 678. However, “[a] trial court may not grant a preliminary injunction, regardless of the balance of interim harm, unless there is some possibility that the plaintiff would ultimately prevail on the merits of the claim. (Ibid.)

Petitioner claims that Respondent has not complied with Penal Code[1] section 597.1, which she contends governs the Seizure. In opposition, Respondent does not argue it complied with section 597.1, nor does Respondent explicitly contend section 597.1 does not apply. (See generally Opp., Supp. Opp.) Rather, Respondent argues that section 1536 applies, and under that section, the matter is not properly before this Court because section 1536 grants exclusive control of the issue to the magistrate who issued the Warrant or to the criminal court.

This matter is properly before this Court.

Section 597.1 provides for “a postseizure hearing to determine the validity of the seizure or impoundment, or both.” (§ 597.1(f).) Respondent acknowledges a hearing under section 597.1 took place, and the hearing officer issued a decision affirming the Seizure. (Opp. at 3; Supp. Opp. at 3.) Indeed, the postseizure hearing under section 597.1, subdivision (f), applies even when Respondent seizes animals pursuant to a search warrant if section 597.1 provides a basis for the warrant. (Bakos v. Roach (2025) 108 Cal.App.5th 390.) Here, the affiant’s statement of probable cause expressly lists violation of section 597.1 as a basis for the warrant. (Pet. Ex. B.)

Respondent incorrectly conflates the magistrate’s limited authority under Penal Code section 1536 with this Court’s jurisdiction to review administrative determinations under section 597.1. Mandamus review over the post-seizure administrative hearing is appropriate. (See Broden v. Marin Humane Society (1999) 70 Cal.App.4th 1212, 1217-18.) So, while this Court may not have jurisdiction to intercede in any criminal action, it certainly has jurisdiction to review the administrative decision finding the Seizure valid and to make provisional injunctive orders in aid of its jurisdiction. Respondent cites no authority, and the Court is aware of none, holding that Penal Code section 1536 divests a civil court of jurisdiction to review a section 597.1 post-seizure administrative decision. [2]

Based on the foregoing, the matter is properly before the Court. As Respondent argues no other grounds against the merits of Petitioner’s claim, the Court finds Petitioner has shown a probability of success.

2.               Balance of Harms

The Court must balance the irreparable harm that Petitioner is likely to sustain if the injunction is denied as compared to the harm that Respondent is likely to suffer if the court grants a preliminary injunction. (CCP §526(a)(2); 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp., supra, 63 Cal.App.4th at 1402; Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman, supra, 55 Cal.App.4th at 1283.)

If the injunction is not granted, the Seized Animals may be subject to euthanasia without notice. Petitioner argues this is irreversible, incompensable harm. In opposition, Respondent argues Petitioner has only shown speculative harm, and Petitioner has the burden of showing irreparable harm.

While Petitioner does bear the burden here, Respondent overstates that burden. As the Court of Appeal has discussed “irreparable harm” “is somewhat of a misnomer.” (Mae M. v. Komrosky (2025) 111 Cal.App. 5th 198, 221 [“Komrosky”].) Properly, and as noted above, “the trial court ‘compares the interim harm the plaintiff is likely to sustain if the injunction is denied to the harm the defendant is likely to suffer if the preliminary injunction is issued.’ (Tulare Lake, supra, 92 Cal.App.5th at p. 396, 309 Cal.Rptr.3d 493.)” (Ibid.) Irreparable harm does not exclusively mean “injury beyond the possibility of repair,” but also covers “wrongs of a repeated and continuing character”. (Komrosky at 221-22, citing Donahue Schriber Realty Group, Inc. v. Nu Creation Outreach (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 1171, 1184.) An injunction is appropriate “[w]hen it appears, during the litigation, that a party to the action is doing, or threatens, or is about to do, or is procuring or suffering to be done, some act in violation of the rights of another party to the action respecting the subject of the action, and tending to render the judgment ineffectual.” (CCP § 526(a)(3).)

The euthanasia of the Seized Animals would certainly tend to render judgment in favor of Petitioner ineffectual. And, while Petitioner has not shown any concrete plan to euthanize the Seized Animals, Respondent makes its intent clear in its petition to the criminal court:

“It is important to have this preclusion removed because some of the medical issues we are monitoring can change rapidly, and an animal will potentially need immediate euthanasia. That could occurs [sic] in the middle of the night (as things like these usually happen) when there is nobody except the overnight RVT and whichever senior veterinarian is on call on the phone. At that point we will not have the ability to work on making middle of the night requests to the judge with just us. We want to be properly prepared so that we would not end up in that situation.” (Suppl. Stumreiter Decl., Ex. N, p. 32.)

Respondent actively seeks to clear a legal pathway to immediately euthanize any of the Seized Animals without notice to Petitioner or judicial review.[3] Therefore, euthanasia of the Seized Animals is harm Petitioner is likely to suffer if the injunction is not granted.

Conversely, Respondent does not argue it will suffer any harm if the injunction is granted. (See generally, Opp.; Supp. Opp.)

Based on the foregoing, the court finds that the balance of harms favors Petitioner.

3.               Bond

In setting the bond, the court must assume that the preliminary injunction was wrongly issued. (Abba Rubber Co. v. Seaquist (1991) 235 Cal.App.3d 1, 15.) The attorney fees necessary to successfully procure a final decision dissolving the injunction also are damages that should be included in setting the bond. (Id. at 15-16.) While Abba reasoned that the plaintiff’s likelihood of prevailing is irrelevant to setting the bond, a more recent case disagreed, stating that the greater the likelihood of the plaintiff prevailing, the less likely the preliminary injunction will have been wrongly issued, and that is a relevant factor for setting the bond. (Oiye v. Fox, (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 1036, 1062.)

Petitioner requests a nominal bond of $1, or an alternative amount not to exceed $1,000. (Mem. at 10.) Respondent does not object to a nominal bond. The Court sets the bond at $100.

IV.           Conclusion

Petitioner has shown a probability of success on the merits, and the balance of harms favors Petitioner.

Petitioner seeks additional injunctive orders regarding care standard compliance, access for counsel, advance notice of veterinary procedures, and advance notice of animal transport. Apart from the fact that these requests exceed the scope of the OSC, on the present record, Petitioner has not demonstrated a likelihood of prevailing on these additional issues or a sufficient showing of interim harm to justify mandatory or prohibitory relief beyond the narrow euthanasia restraint.

Accordingly, the preliminary injunction is GRANTED on narrow grounds as follows: pending the underlying writ hearing or further order of this Court, Respondents, and all persons acting in concert with them, are restrained and enjoined from euthanizing/destroying any of the Seized Animals.

Nevertheless, should Respondents determine that euthanasia/destruction of any specific Seized Animal (or Animals) is urgently required during the pendency of the preliminary injunction, they may apply ex parte for such relief.

For clarity, the Court notes that this injunction does not alter or modify the search warrant, does not interfere with any criminal court’s jurisdiction, and does not intrude upon the magistrate’s authority under Penal Code section 1536. The injunction applies only to restrain Respondents’ actions and simply preserves the subject matter of the mandamus proceeding (the Seized Animals) so that meaningful review of the post-seizure administrative determination under section 597.1 may proceed without being rendered hollow.

Troy Whiteto (“Plaintiff”) seeks a preliminary injunction restraining defendants Amour Escrow, Inc. (“Amour”), Batu Development, LLC (“Batu”), Quality Loan Service Corporation (“QLSC”), Equity California Real Estate (“ECRE”), Layers Title Company (“LTC”), Kiavi Funding, Inc. (“Kiavi”), Teya McCoy (“McCoy”), Danielle Love (“Love”), Eric Dion Green (“Green”), Shante Deshon Newton (“Newton”), and Shirley Martin (“Martin”) (collectively, “Defendants”) from foreclosing on the real property located at 1858 West 77th Street, Los Angeles, California, 90047, APN 6017-024-014 (the “Property”), and from any eviction or dispossession efforts.

The preliminary injunction is granted as to all Defendants except Batu and Kiavi.

I.                Background

1.               Factual Background

Doris Jeanne Smith (“Decedent”) owned the Property. (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 2.) Decedent passed on December 12, 2024. (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 2.) Plaintiff is co-administrator of Decedent’s estate. (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 2.)

In or around August 2025, Plaintiff was approached by Newton, principal of Batu, who offered to help renovate and sell Decedent’s home. (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 3.) As part of this project, Plaintiff and Newton created TST Investment Group LLC (“TST”). (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 1.) Plaintiff, his sister and co-administrator, and Newton initiated sale of the Property to TST, signing documents including a California Residential Purchase Agreement, Joint Escrow Instructions, and, on October 8, 2025, a Grant Deed. (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 4.)

During escrow, Newton and sales agent Eric Green (“Green”) informed Plaintiff they would need to change the buyer from TST to Batu, because Plaintiff was a part owner of TST and the transaction must be at arm’s length to qualify for a loan. (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 5.) Plaintiff refused to cooperate with the substitution. (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 6.)

Even though Plaintiff and his sister refused, the escrow closed with Batu rather than TST. (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 7.) Some of the relevant documents contain signatures from Plaintiff, though he did not sign those documents. (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 7.) In one case, the signature page from the Grant Deed appeared to be affixed to a new document purporting to transfer the Property to Batu. (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 8, Ex. 2.)

Plaintiff resides at the Property. (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 10.) Plaintiff recently received a Notice of Default and Election to Sell Under Deed of Trust (“Notice of Default”). (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 10, Ex. 3.) Plaintiff also received a “California Three (3) Day Notice to Vacate.” (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 11, Ex. 4.)

Probate for Decedent’s Estate has not closed. (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 13.)

2.               Procedural History

Plaintiff filed his verified complaint on February 5, 2026 against Batu, McCoy, Amour, and Love. Proofs of service on file show Plaintiff served the summons and complaint on Amour by substituted service on February 20, 2026, and on Love by personal service on February 18, 2026. On June 15, 2026, Amour and Love filed their demurrer to the complaint, as well as their motion to strike the complaint. On July 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed fictitious names amendments adding U.S. Bank National Association (“U.S. Bank”) and QLSC as defendants. On July 7, 2026, Plaintiff filed a fictitious name amendment adding Kiavi as defendant.

On February 12, 2026, Plaintiff recorded a notice of pendency.

On July 10, 2026, the Court heard and granted Plaintiff’s ex parte application for a temporary restraining order (“TRO”) and order to show cause (“OSC”) re: preliminary injunction. The Court noted that Batu was not properly served, but that Batu was not involved in the foreclosure proceedings. The Court ordered Plaintiff to personally serve Defendants with the OSC, TRO, summons and complaint, and all moving and supplemental papers no later than July 13, 2026. Any opposition was due no later than July 23, 2026, and any reply by July 27, 2026.

On July 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Declaration of Abraham L. Niman Re: Proof of Service, wherein counsel stated that, with their agreement, Plaintiff served all restrained parties with the notice of ruling, summons and complaint, notice of case assignment, ADR packet, and notice of case management conference, as well as a notice and acknowledgement form for Kiavi’s counsel. (Niman Decl., ¶¶ 2-4, Exs. 1-3.) There is no proof of service for Batu or any other indication Batu has been served.

On July 23, 2026, Kiavi filed its opposition.

On July 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed his verified first amended complaint (“FAC”). The same day, counsel for Plaintiff forwarded the FAC to all known counsel. (Niman Decl., ¶¶ 5-6, Ex. 4.)

C); (4) the Notice of Default, recorded on April 20, 2026 as instrument no. 20260276182 (RJN Ex. D). The requests are granted. (Evid. Code § 452(c).)

III.           Discussion

In determining whether to issue a preliminary injunction, the trial court considers two factors: (1) the reasonable probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits at trial (Code Civ. Proc. [“CCP”] §526(a)(1)), and (2) a balancing of the “irreparable harm” that the plaintiff is likely to sustain if the injunction is denied as compared to the harm that the defendant is likely to suffer if the court grants a preliminary injunction. (CCP §526(a)(2); 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396, 1402; Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 1279, 1283; Davenport v. Blue Cross of California (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 435, 446; Abrams v. St. John’s Hospital (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 628, 636.) Thus, a preliminary injunction may not issue without some showing of potential entitlement to such relief. (Doe v. Wilson (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 296, 304.)

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending final resolution upon a trial. (See Scaringe v. J.C.C. Enterprises, Inc. (1988) 205 Cal.App.3d 1536; Grothe v. Cortlandt Corp. (1992) 11 Cal.App.4th 1313, 1316; Major v. Miraverde Homeowners Assn. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 618, 623.) The status quo has been defined to mean the last actual peaceable, uncontested status which preceded the pending controversy. (Voorhies v. Greene (1983) 139 Cal.App.3d 989, 995, quoting United Railroads v. Superior Court (1916) 172 Cal. 80, 87; 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396, 1402.)

1.               Jurisdiction

Plaintiff has not shown service on Batu. Without effected service of process, the Court has no personal jurisdiction over Batu. A court may not issue injunctive relief against a defendant unless the court has personal jurisdiction over that party. (Rothschild v. Erda (1968) 258 Cal.App.2d 750, 753.)

2.               Probability of Success

“The trial court's determination must be guided by a ‘mix’ of the potential-merit and interim-harm factors; the greater the plaintiff's showing on one, the less must be shown on the other to support an injunction.” (Butt v. State of California (1992) 4 Cal.4th 668, 678.) However, “[a] trial court may not grant a preliminary injunction, regardless of the balance of interim harm, unless there is some possibility that the plaintiff would ultimately prevail on the merits of the claim. (Ibid.)

Plaintiff brings claims for intentional misrepresentation, negligent misrepresentation, quiet title, cancellation of instrument, breach of oral contract, breach of fiduciary duty, and negligence. (FAC at 1.) However, Plaintiff only discusses the invalidity of the Grant Deed in his ex parte moving papers. Plaintiff has not filed supplemental supporting papers.

As Kiavi notes, Plaintiff neither pleads facts in the FAC nor provides any evidence connecting Kiavi to Plaintiff’s causes of action. Kiavi is not specifically mentioned in any allegation in the FAC except the party list. (FAC, ¶ 5.) Kiavi is not mentioned in Plaintiff’s declaration. (See generally Whiteto Decl.) In Plaintiff’s counsel’s declaration, Kiavi is only mentioned in that counsel “noticed this name in a Deed of Trust recorded on the Property”. (Niman Decl., ¶ 5.) Plaintiff does not provide this deed of trust. Kiavi is likewise only mentioned in the ex parte moving papers in two lists of defendants. (Appl. at 1, 6.)

Moreover, Kiavi holds no interest in the Property. (See RJN Exs. A-D.)

As for the other Defendants, who do not oppose, Plaintiff has provided testimonial and documentary evidence tending to show that Newton, Green, Love, and Batu either fabricated or fraudulently induced a grant deed conveying the Property to Batu. (Whiteto Decl., ¶¶ 6-9.) Therefore, the conveyance to Batu would be void. Plaintiff also provides some evidence that the deed is facially invalid, and therefore placing subsequent interest holders on record notice and therefore implicating Batu’s mortgage on the Property and the lender Defendants’ interests. (Whiteto Decl., ¶ 9, Ex. 2.)

Accordingly, the Court finds Plaintiff has shown a probability of success for quiet title and cancellation of instrument as to Defendants excluding Kiavi.

3.               Balance of Harms

The court must balance the irreparable harm that Plaintiff is likely to sustain if the injunction is denied as compared to the harm that Respondent is likely to suffer if the court grants a preliminary injunction. (CCP §526(a)(2); 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp., supra, 63 Cal.App.4th at 1402; Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman, supra, 55 Cal.App.4th at 1283.)

If no injunction issues, Plaintiff will lose his residence. (Appl. at 6-7; Whiteto Decl., ¶ 10.)

Kiavi tacitly acknowledges the balance of harms favors Plaintiff and does not argue the issue. (See, e.g., Opp. at 2.) Regardless, Kiavi no longer has any interest in the Property. There is no harm to Plaintiff if Kiavi is not enjoined, and there is no harm to Kiavi if it is enjoined.

No other defendant has opposed, and on the evidence shown, no defendant will suffer harm beyond a delay of sale if the injunction is granted.

Based on the foregoing, the court finds that the balance of harms strongly favors Plaintiff, except as to Kiavi.

4.               Bond

In setting the bond, the court must assume that the preliminary injunction was wrongly issued. (Abba Rubber Co. v. Seaquist (1991) 235 Cal.App.3d 1, 15.) The attorney fees necessary to successfully procure a final decision dissolving the injunction also are damages that should be included in setting the bond. (Id. at 15-16.) While Abba reasoned that the plaintiff’s likelihood of prevailing is irrelevant to setting the bond, a more recent case disagreed, stating that the greater the likelihood of the plaintiff prevailing, the less likely the preliminary injunction will have been wrongly issued, and that is a relevant factor for setting the bond. (Oiye v. Fox (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 1036, 1062.)

Plaintiff has not addressed the issue of bond.[1] The Court will discuss the matter with counsel at hearing.

IV.           Conclusion

Plaintiff has shown a probability of success on the merits, and the balance of harms strongly favors Plaintiff. However, Plaintiff has not shown a probability of success nor any harm relating to Kiavi. Further, Plaintiff has not properly served Batu. The preliminary injunction is therefore GRANTED as to all Defendants except Batu and Kiavi (“Restrained Defendants”).

Pending final resolution of this matter, Restrained Defendants, and all persons acting in concert with them, are restrained and enjoined from conducting, noticing, or proceeding with any foreclosure sale, trustee’s sale, or other foreclosure-related action affecting the Property. Restrained Defendants, and persons acting in concert with them, are further restrained from taking any steps to dispossess Plaintiff from the Property to the extent such dispossession efforts arise from or depend upon foreclosure-related activity.

1.               Probability of Success

“The trial court's determination must be guided by a ‘mix’ of the potential-merit and interim-harm factors; the greater the plaintiff's showing on one, the less must be shown on the other to support an injunction.” (Butt v. State of California (1992) 4 Cal.4th 668, 678.) Plaintiff seeks, among other things, a declaration of the parties’ rights and interests in the Vehicle, on the basis that he believes Defendants have no or limited right in the Vehicle, conversion for withholding the Vehicle, and breach of contract for failure to perform repairs and failure to return the Vehicle. The Court must balance the irreparable harm that Plaintiff is likely to sustain if the injunction is denied as compared to the harm that Defendants are likely to suffer if the court grants a preliminary injunction. (CCP §526(a)(2); 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp., supra, 63 Cal.App.4th at 1402; Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman, supra, 55 Cal.App.4th at 1283.)
