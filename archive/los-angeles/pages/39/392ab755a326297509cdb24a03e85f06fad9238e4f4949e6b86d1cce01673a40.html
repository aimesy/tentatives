SEBASTIAN RICHARDS,  

 

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

ALINA LANDVER, et al.,

 

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.: 24SMCV00623

 

  Hearing Date: July 2, 2026

 

  [TENTATIVE] ORDER RE:

  MOTION TO STAY EXECUTION

 

 

 

 

 

 

BACKGROUND

This hearing is on Defendants Alina Landver and Landver Law Corporation, APC’s application for a stay of enforcement of judgment pending appeal upon the posting of an undertaking. Plaintiff opposes.

Plaintiff filed this action on February 9, 2024, asserting a claim for quantum meruit for attorney fees. The matter was tried to the Court from July 7 through July 11, 2025. On July 11, 2025, the Court rendered a verdict awarding Plaintiff $17,600 in damages. On October 13, 2025, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion to strike Defendants’ memorandum of costs. On February 23, 2026, the Court entered final judgment for Plaintiff in the amount of $26,642.76, consisting of $17,600.00 in damages and $9,042.76 in costs.

On April 21, 2026, Defendants filed a notice of appeal, which has been assigned Case No. B354325 in the Court of Appeal, Second Appellate District. On May 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed a writ of execution. On May 18, 2026, Defendants filed an undertaking, in the amount of $39,694.14. On May 19, 2026, Plaintiff returned the writ of execution; no levy occurred.

Defendants now move for an order confirming that enforcement of the judgment is stayed pending appeal by operation of the undertaking, and restraining Plaintiff from any enforcement activity during the appeal. Plaintiff opposes on three grounds: (1) the request directed at the writ is moot because the writ has been returned; (2) the motion is unnecessary because a valid undertaking stays enforcement by operation of law without a court order; and (3) the undertaking is deficient because it is approximately $270 less than one and one-half times the judgment.

LEGAL STANDARD

The perfecting of an appeal does not stay enforcement of a money judgment unless an undertaking is given. (Code Civ. Proc., § 917.1, subd. (a)(1).) Where the undertaking is given by an admitted surety insurer, it “shall be for … one and one-half times the amount of the judgment.” (Id., subd. (b).) Costs awarded by the trial court are included in the amount of the judgment for purposes of calculating the undertaking. (Code Civ. Proc., § 917.1, subd. (d); Quiles v. Parent (2017) 10 Cal.App.5th 130.) A bond and an undertaking are interchangeable. (Code Civ. Proc., § 995.210, subd. (a).) A bond is effective without court approval unless the statute providing for it requires approval, and section 917.1 imposes no such requirement. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 995.410, subd. (a), 995.420, subd. (a).) The admitted-surety status of the surety bears on the required amount and on acceptance of the bond without further acknowledgment (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 917.1, subd. (b), 995.630), not on the approval question. When a proper undertaking is filed, the stay arises by operation of law. (California Commerce Bank v. Superior Court (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 582.) The amount requirement is “rigidly formulaic.” (Leung v. Verdugo Hills Hospital (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 205, 212.)

Effectiveness is not unqualified, however. Section 995.420 provides that “[i]f an objection is made to a bond before the time provided, the bond becomes effective when the court makes an order determining the sufficiency of the bond.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 995.420.) The procedure for challenging sufficiency, and the consequence of an insufficient bond, are governed by sections 995.920, 995.930, and 996.010. A determination that a bond is insufficient in amount does not void the bond or forfeit the underlying right; rather, “[u]pon the determination the court shall order that a sufficient new, additional, or supplemental bond be given within a reasonable time not less than five days,” and only “[i]f a sufficient bond is not given within the time required” do “all rights obtained by giving the original bond” cease. (Code Civ. Proc., § 996.010, subds. (c), (d).)

DISCUSSION

A. The Return of the Writ Moots Only the Relief Directed at That Writ.

Plaintiff first argues that the motion is moot because the writ of execution was returned on May 19, 2026, and no levy occurred. The Court agrees in part. A claim is moot when the court can no longer grant effective relief. To the extent Defendants seek an order recalling, quashing, or suspending that particular writ, no effective relief remains available as there is no operative writ before the Court.

However, the return of the writ does not reach the question on which the motion principally turns: whether the undertaking on file is sufficient to effect a stay. The undertaking presently on file is in the amount of $39,694.14, which is $270 less than one and one-half times the judgment, and Plaintiff presently disputes that the undertaking satisfies Code of Civil Procedure section 917.1. The Court can grant effective relief on that live disagreement, namely, a determination of the bond’s sufficiency and, if warranted, an order to cure under section 996.010, subdivision (c). Because effective relief remains available as to the sufficiency of the undertaking, the matter is justiciable, and the Court proceeds to the merits of the undertaking.

B. The Parties’ Dispute Over the Stay Is Properly Before the Court.

Plaintiff next argues that the motion is unnecessary because, if the undertaking is valid, the stay exists by operation of law and no order is required. Plaintiff is correct as a general matter: a bond is effective without court approval unless the statute providing for it requires approval, and section 917.1 imposes no such requirement. But that principle presupposes a sufficient undertaking, and the sufficiency of the undertaking is precisely what is contested. Having declined to concede that the undertaking satisfies section 917.1, Plaintiff cannot at the same time maintain that there is nothing for the Court to decide.

Moreover, the Court’s authority to determine the sufficiency of a bond does not depend on the form or timing of any objection by Plaintiff. A court may determine that a bond is insufficient in amount “upon the court’s own motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 996.010, subd. (b).) The Court exercises that authority here.

C. The Undertaking Is $270 Short of the Statutory Amount, but the Defect Is Curable.

The substance of the dispute is the amount of the undertaking. One and one-half times the judgment is $26,642.76 × 1.5 = $39,964.14. The bond filed by Defendants is in the amount of $39,694.14. The bond is therefore $270.00 less than the amount required by section 917.1, subdivision (b). The shortfall appears on the face of Defendants’ own moving papers, which correctly state the required figure as $39,964.14 while reciting a bond amount of $39,694.14, the result of a transposition of digits.

Because the amount requirement is “rigidly formulaic” (Leung v. Verdugo Hills Hospital (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 205, 212), the Court cannot treat a bond that is less than one and one-half times the judgment as satisfying the statute, and it declines Defendants’ invitation to deem the present bond sufficient on a “de minimis” theory. As filed, the bond does not perfect a stay by operation of law.

That conclusion does not warrant denial of the motion or forfeiture of Defendants’ right to a stay. The remedy for an undertaking that is insufficient in amount is correction, not forfeiture. Having determined the undertaking insufficient, the Court “shall order that a sufficient new, additional, or supplemental bond be given within a reasonable time not less than five days,” and only an uncured defect causes the rights obtained by the bond to cease. (Code Civ. Proc., § 996.010, subds. (c), (d).) Defendants have, in the alternative, requested leave to file a corrected or supplemental undertaking. The Court grants that relief. Defendants shall file a corrected or supplemental undertaking in the amount of $39,964.14 within the time set forth below. Upon the timely filing of a conforming undertaking, enforcement of the judgment is stayed by operation of law pending the appeal.

D. Defendants Are Not Entitled to a Freestanding Injunction Beyond the Statutory Stay.

Finally, Defendants ask the Court to restrain and enjoin Plaintiff and all persons acting on his behalf from any enforcement activity during the appeal. To the extent this seeks relief beyond the stay that section 917.1 itself provides, it is unnecessary and unsupported by authority. A perfected statutory stay already bars enforcement of the judgment during the pendency of the appeal; no separate injunction is required to give it effect, and Defendants cite no authority authorizing one. The Court therefore declines to issue a freestanding injunction and instead confirms that, upon Defendants’ cure of the undertaking, enforcement of the judgment is stayed by operation of law pending the appeal.

CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS IN PART and DENIES IN PART Defendants’ motion, as follows:

1.               To the extent the motion seeks to recall, quash, or restrain the writ of execution returned on May 19, 2026, it is DENIED as moot.

2.               The Court determines, on its own motion (Code Civ. Proc., § 996.010, subd. (b)), that the undertaking on file (SureTec Bond No. 3542730, in the amount of $39,694.14) is insufficient under Code of Civil Procedure section 917.1, subdivision (b), in that it is $270.00 less than one and one-half times the judgment of $26,642.76. Pursuant to section 996.010, subdivision (c), Defendants are ordered to file and serve a corrected or supplemental undertaking, issued by an admitted surety insurer, in the amount of $39,964.14, within ten (10) days of this order. Upon the timely filing of a conforming undertaking, enforcement of the judgment entered February 23, 2026 is stayed by operation of law pending final determination of the appeal in Case No. B354325. If a conforming undertaking is not timely filed, no stay arises from the bond on file, and all rights obtained by giving the bond on file cease. (Code Civ. Proc., § 996.010, subd. (d).)

3.               To the extent the motion seeks injunctive relief beyond the stay provided by Code of Civil Procedure section 917.1, the request is DENIED as unnecessary.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

LINHART GLOBAL, LLC, et al.

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

MICHALE J. KRUPIN, et al.,

                        Defendants.

  Case No.:                  24SMCV00863

  Hearing Date:           7/2/26

  Trial Date:                 8/24/26

 [TENTATIVE] RULING RE:

MOTION FOR TERMINATING SANCTIONS

 

Background

            Plaintiff alleges Defendants executed and delivered a promissory note to Plaintiff on October 28, 2022 promising to pay $410,000.  Plaintiff alleges Defendants were required under the terms of the note to pay Plaintiffs $20,000 per month until the note was paid in full commencing on or about January 10, 2023.  Plaintiff alleges Defendants made their initial payment of $20,000 on January 10, 2023 and failed to make any payments thereafter.  Plaintiff alleges Defendants have defaulted on the balance of the Note in the sum of $390,000.  On February 26, 2024, Plaintiff filed a complaint alleging a single cause of action for breach of promissory note. 

            On May 21, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion for Terminating Sanctions.  On June 18, 2026, Defendant Michael J. Krupin filed an opposition to the motion.  On June 25, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply. 

Evidentiary Objections

            Plaintiff’s Objections to evidence in support of Opposition:  SUSTAIN—Objection Nos. 1-9.

Motion for Terminating Sanctions Standard

            “To the extent authorized by the chapter governing any particular discovery method or any other provision of this title, the court, after notice to any affected party, person, or attorney, and after opportunity for hearing, may impose the following sanctions against anyone engaging in conduct that is a misuse of the discovery process:…(d) The court may impose a terminating sanction by one of the following orders: (1) An order striking out the pleadings or parts of the pleadings of any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process. (2) An order staying further proceedings by that party until an order for discovery is obeyed. (3) An order dismissing the action, or any part of the action, of that party. (4) An order rendering a judgment by default against that party.”  (Code of Civ. Proc., §2023.030, subd. (d).)

            “Misuses of the discovery process include, but are not limited to, the following:…(d) Failing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery….(g) Disobeying a court order to provide discovery…(i) Failing to confer in person, by telephone, or by letter with an opposing party or attorney in a reasonable and good faith attempt to resolve informally any dispute concerning discovery, if the section governing a particular discovery motion requires the filing of a declaration stating facts showing that an attempt at informal resolution has been made.”  (Code of Civ. Proc., §2023.010, subd. (d), (g), (i).)

            Terminating sanctions should not be imposed lightly and a graduated imposition of

sanctions should be used if possible.  (Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsystem (2005) 128

Cal.App.4th 262, 279-280 (decision to impose terminating sanctions should not be made lightly

but such sanctions are justified in cases of repeated discovery abuse and evidence that lesser

sanctions will be ineffectual); Thomas v. Luong (1986) 187 Cal.App.3d 76, 81–82; see also

Morgan v. Ransom (1979) 95 Cal.App.3d 664, 669 (incarcerated, indigent, pro per plaintiff's

delay in serving responses insufficient to justify imposition of terminating sanctions where no

prejudice demonstrated). Moreover, discovery sanctions are not intended to punish but to

accomplish discovery.  (Newland v. Supr. Ct. (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 608, 613.)

            “The discovery statutes…evince an incremental approach to discovery sanctions, starting with monetary sanctions and ending with the ultimate sanction of termination.  Although in extreme cases a court has the authority to order a terminating sanction as a first measure, a terminating sanction should generally not be imposed until the court has attempted less severe alternatives and found them to be unsuccessful and/or the record clearly shows lesser sanctions would be ineffective.”  (Lopez v. Watchtower Bible & Tract Society of New York, Inc. (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 566, 604.) 

            Where a party repeatedly violates discovery orders, including the parties’ discovery stipulations, a court is “not required to allow this pattern of abuse to continue ad infinitum.”  (Mileikowsky, supra, 128 Cal.App.4th at 280.)  Thus, a court does not abuse its discretion in ordering terminating sanctions against a party who has repeatedly refused to answer discovery requests despite numerous extensions, court orders requiring discovery responses, including stipulations, and imposition of monetary sanctions.  (Id.)  Terminating sanctions under these circumstances are proper even if the responding party “only” complied with discovery because of “the threat of terminating sanctions.”  (Id.) 

            “Discovery sanctions are intended to remedy discovery abuse, not to punish the offending party. Accordingly, sanctions should be tailored to serve that remedial purpose, should not put the moving party in a better position than he would otherwise have been had he obtained the requested discovery, and should be proportionate to the offending party’s misconduct.”  (Williams v. Russ (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1223.) 

Analysis

            Plaintiff requests terminating sanctions against Defendant Michael Krupin based on his intentional violation of the Court’s April 2, 2026 order compelling his attendance at deposition. Plaintiff asks that Defendant’s answer be stricken and monetary sanctions be imposed in the amount of $2,800.

            The Court is required to use an incremental approach to imposition of discovery sanctions.  Plaintiff fails to identify a history of repeated discovery abuse or other circumstances that would justify imposition of “doomsday” sanctions against Defendant for violating the Court’s April 2, 2026 discovery order.  Although there has been difficulty scheduling Defendant’s deposition and Defendant has not appeared previously for a scheduled deposition, the inability to do so has largely been attributable to scheduling conflicts and Defendants’ health issues.  (Motion, Cole Dec., ¶¶2-37.) 

            However, the trial date is set for August 24, 2026.  The discovery cut-off is set for July 24, 2026.  Even if Defendant’s claims of medical issues, unavailability and change of counsel is considered, Plaintiff has been attempting to depose Defendant for two years and Defendant undisputedly disobeyed the Court’s April 2, 2026 order compelling his attendance at deposition.  Plaintiff cannot prosecute his case without deposing Defendant, who signed the promissory note on behalf of Krupin Partners Insurance Services, LLC and as an individual.  (Complaint, Ex. A.)  A terminating sanction under these circumstances is warranted to achieve discovery and remedy the effect that Defendant’s noncompliance has had on Plaintiff’s ability to prepare his case.

            Defendant argues in opposition that he did not fail to appear at his November 20, 2025 deposition and his failure to confirm non-attendance of the April 30, 2026 deposition was unintentional.  However, section 2023.010 includes “disobeying a court order to provide discovery” as a “misuse of the discovery process.”  There is no requirement that the failure to disobey be intentional or willful and sanctions may be imposed for “misuse of the discovery process” per section 2023.030.

            An OSC re: Terminating Sanctions is set for July 23, 2026.  The motion for terminating sanctions is continued to that date.  If Defendant has not appeared for deposition by that date, the Court will impose terminating sanctions and strike Defendant’s answer and enter his default.      

            Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions is granted.  Plaintiff requests sanctions in the amount of $2,810 based on 5.5 hours at $500/hr and a $60 filing fee.  Plaintiff’s request is reasonable. 

Conclusion

            Plaintiff’s Motion for Terminating Sanctions is CONTINUED to July 17, 2026 as to the terminating sanctions and GRANTED as to the monetary sanctions.  Defendant is ordered to pay monetary sanctions in the amount of $2,810 by July 16, 2026.

Superior Court of California

Beverly Hills Courthouse / Department 205

 

 

PATRICK BERTRANOU, an individual; and WALKER LAND, LLC, a California limited liability company, 

 

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

KENNETH KAI CHANG, an individual; BERESFORD PROPERTIES, LLC, a California limited liability company; HUI O KA LA, LLC, a Hawaii limited liability company; and DOES 1-25, inclusive,

 

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  24SMCV02781

 

  Hearing Date:  July 2, 2026

  [TENTATIVE] order RE:

  PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION TO

  ENFORCE SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT

 

 

 

BACKGROUND

 

               This case arises from a loan dispute. Plaintiff Patrick Bertranou (Plaintiff) alleges that he loaned $3.5 million to defendants Kenneth Kai Chang, Beresford Properties, LLC, and Hui O Ka La La, LLC (collectively, Defendants) via oral agreement. On June 1, 2024, Plaintiff filed their Complaint against Defendants. Plaintiff alleges Defendants obtained the loan through fraudulent means and did not repay the loan. Defendants allege the loan is memorialized in an option agreement and was an investment in a condominium development property called the Ililani. (Mot., Joint Statement of the Case, p. 2.) On March 23, 2026, the parties negotiated and reached a settlement agreement to resolve the dispute.

On June 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to enforce the settlement agreement and enter judgment against Defendants, alleging that Defendants have not complied with the terms of the settlement by refusing to make the required payments.

On June 22, 2026, Defendants filed an Opposition arguing that they have performed the material terms of the settlement agreement to the extent possible.

On June 25, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Reply.

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 provides “If parties to pending litigation stipulate, in a writing signed by the parties outside the presence of the court or orally before the court, for settlement of the case, or part thereof, the court, upon motion, may enter judgment pursuant to the terms of the settlement. If requested by the parties, the court may retain jurisdiction over the parties to enforce the settlement until performance in full of the terms of the settlement.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 664.6.) “[I]nterest accrues at the rate of 10 percent per annum on the principal amount of a money judgment remaining unsatisfied”. (Code Civ. Proc., § 685.010(a)(1).)

DISCUSSION

Plaintiff argues that Defendants were required to pay $4.8 million by the end of May 2026 pursuant to the terms of the settlement agreement and have not done so. (Mot., p. 5.) Plaintiff argues Defendants were also required to transfer two market rate units at the Ililani to Plaintiff pursuant to the terms of the settlement agreement and have not done so. (Ibid.) Moreover, Plaintiff argues Defendants have no valid excuse for non-performance. (Mot., p. 6.)

Defendants argue that they have completed material obligations under the settlement agreement and matters that remain pending are either pending delivery of paperwork or in the hands of the state of Hawaii. (Opp., p. 9.) Defendants argue that with respect to the two units at the Ililani meant to be transferred to Plaintiff, they have filed requisite paperwork with the state of Hawaii to obtain the necessary tax withholding exemption and are awaiting approval. (Ibid.) Defendants argue that the delay was the result of Plaintiff’s delay in providing the necessary tax information and paperwork. (Ibid.) Defendants state that the terms of the settlement allow Defendants to satisfy the $4.8 million balance with the transfer of six additional units at the Ililani by recording mortgages/deeds of trust, or by direct transfer. (Ibid.) Defendants argue that the paperwork necessary to effectuate direct transfer of the units is complete and should have been delivered to Plaintiff on Tuesday, June 23, 2026. (Ibid.)

In Reply, Plaintiff argues that reason for delay in effectuating transfer of the first two units and the status of paperwork to effectuate transfer of the six additional units is irrelevant and the issue remains that Defendants have not satisfied their obligations under the terms of the settlement by the deadline. (Reply, pp. 2-3.)

The terms of the settlement provide for the transfer of two units at the Ililani, and $5.5 million payment satisfied through (1) payment of $700,000.00 upon execution, and (2) either payment of $4.8 million balance, or transfer of six additional units at the Ililani valued at $4.8 million within 60 days following the execution of the settlement agreement. (Mot., Exh. B, ¶¶ 2-3.) Here, 60 days from execution of the settlement agreement, dated March 20, 2026, is the end of May 2026. Defendants completed timely payment of the $700,000 due upon execution. (Bertranou Decl., ¶ 2.) As of June 23, 2026, Defendants have delivered paperwork to Plaintiff effectuating transfer of the six additional units to satisfy the $4.8 million balance remaining from the $5.5 million payment. (Kim Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) However, Plaintiff must return signed and notarized paperwork to Defendants’ Hawaii counsel to complete transfer. (Kim Decl., ¶ 3.) Although paperwork has been delivered, transfer of the six units satisfying one portion of the settlement agreement has not been completed. Additionally, Defendants have still not effectuated transfer of the two original units at the Ililani pursuant to the terms of the settlement agreement. Since Defendants have failed to transfer the two original units and failed to satisfy the $4.8 million balance by transferring six additional units, Defendants have failed to timely comply with the terms of the settlement agreement. Thus, Plaintiff’s motion to enforce settlement is granted.

CONCLUSION

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff Patrick Bertranou’s motion to enforce settlement agreement and, accordingly, will enter judgment against Defendants.

DEBORAH LEVY, et al.,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

HENRI LEVY, et al.,

                        Defendants.

  Case No.:                  24SMCV06226

  Hearing Date:           7/2/26

  Trial Date:                 9/13/27

 [TENTATIVE] RULING RE:

MOTION FOR JUDGMENT ON THE PLEADINGS

 

Background

 

            Plaintiffs Deborah Levy (“Deborah”) and David Levy (“David”) (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) are brother and sister and the sole heirs and beneficiaries of their late father, Daniel Levy (“Daniel”), who died on January 17, 2022.  Defendant Henri Levy (“Henri”) is the brother of Plaintiffs’ father and Plaintiffs’ uncle. 

In September 2020, Plaintiffs’ aunt, Jafa (or Yafa) Levy, sister of Daniel and Henri, sold a building in Jerusalem, Israel owned by their late father, Prosper Levy (“Prosper”).  The heirs of Prosper agreed that the sale proceeds of $10 million would be divided between them as follows:  55% to Daniel ($5.5 million); 20% to Henri ($2 million); 10% to Jacob Levy ($1 million); and 15% to Jafa ($1.5 million).  At Daniel’s request, Jafa wired Daniel’s portion of the proceeds to Henri to hold for Daniel’s and Plaintiffs’ benefit.  After Daniel’s death in 2022, Plaintiffs learned from Jafa that she wired Daniel’s portion of the sale proceeds to Henri.  Plaintiffs requested that Henri return the $3.5 million to them.  Henri has refused.

On December 12, 2024, Plaintiffs filed a complaint against Henri alleging (1) money had and received; (2) breach of fiduciary duty; (3) conversion; and (4) unjust enrichment.  On March 11, 2025, Henri filed an answer to the complaint.  On May 15, 2026, Deborah and David each filed declarations pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 377.32. 

 

On March 17, 2026, Henri filed the instant Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings.  On June 18, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an opposition to the Motion.  On June 25, 2026, Henri filed a reply.

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

            Defendant’s Request for Judicial Notice is GRANTED pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 452.

Meet and Confer

            “Before filing a motion for judgment on the pleadings pursuant to this chapter, the moving party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion for judgment on the pleadings for the purpose of determining if an agreement can be reached that resolves the claims to be raised in the motion for judgment on the pleadings.”  (Code of Civ. Proc., §439, subd. (a).)

            Defense counsel Rossman only attests to a single meet-and-confer email.  (Motion, Rossman Dec., ¶3.)  Counsel was required to meet and confer in person, by phone or by video conference.  A single email is insufficient to satisfy section 438 subdivision (a).  Nonetheless, the Court exercises its discretion to hear the motion.

Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings Standard

 

            A defendant may move for judgment on the pleadings when the “complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant.”  (Code Civ. Proc. §438(b)(1) and (c)(1)(B)(ii).)  “A motion for judgment on the pleadings may be made at any time either prior to the trial or at the trial itself. [Citation.]”  (Ion Equipment Corp. v. Nelson (1980) 110 Cal.App.3d 868, 877.)

            “A motion for judgment on the pleadings performs the same function as a general demurrer, and hence attacks only defects disclosed on the face of the pleadings or by matters that can be judicially noticed.  Presentation of extrinsic evidence is therefore not proper on a motion for judgment on the pleadings.”  (Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 999 (citations omitted).) 

            The standard for ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings is essentially the same as that applicable to a general demurrer, that is, under the state of the pleadings, together with matters that may be judicially noticed, it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.  (Bezirdjian v. O'Reilly (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 316, 321-322 (citing Schabarum v. California Legislature (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1216).) 

 

Analysis

 

            Henri moves for judgment on the pleadings on grounds of standing.  Henri argues Plaintiffs lack standing to assert claims over Daniel’s estate under either California or French law.  Henri argues French law applies because Plaintiffs are French citizens who reside in France, as was their father Daniel.  Henri argues Plaintiffs have not filed a declaration pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 377.30 to 377.31 attesting to their status as Daniel’s estate’s duly appointed personal representative or as successors in interest who have the legal right to act on behalf of the estate.  Henri argues that until they do so, they cannot assert Daniel’s claims. 

            Henri also argues French Civil Code Article 771 required Plaintiffs to accept or renounce David’s estate within four months of Daniel’s death.  Henri argues Plaintiffs filed the action nearly three years after Daniel’s death.  Henri argues Plaintiffs must demonstrate under French law that they have transitioned from “potential” to “accepted” heirs.

            Henri argues the complaint fails to distinguish between the portion of funds held by him for Daniel’s benefit and the portion held by him for Plaintiffs.  Henri argues this requires a formal acceptance of succession under French law. Henri argues the complaint does not qualify as “tacti acceptance” under French law because Plaintiffs have not filed the requisite declaration under Code of Civil Procedure section 377.32.

            Henri argues Plaintiffs have not filed a section 377.32 declaration and cannot truthfully do so.  Henri argues this pleading omission is grounds to grant judgment on the pleadings without leave to amend.

            Lack of standing may be raised on demurrer if the lack of standing appears on the face of the complaint and judicially noticeable evidence.  (Wimber v. Scott (2025) 113 Cal.App.5th 349, 357.)  Such a complaint is vulnerable to demurrer for failure to state a cause of action.  (Id.) 

Henri fails to cite any authority supporting his claim that French law applies.  Henri states in his opening brief that French law applies to this proceeding because “Plaintiffs allege they are French citizens residing in France and that the decedent—their father, Daniel Levy—lived and died in Paris, France.”  (Motion, 8:6-9.)  Henri’s statement is not supported by any citation to authority.

Moreover, Plaintiffs argue in opposition that they have complied with French law and accepted their succession.  Plaintiffs argue they would have provided such evidence in discovery or during a proper meet and confer if Defendant had met and conferred as required.  (Soloway Dec., ¶¶3-4.)  Soloway’s declaration testimony and attached evidence regarding Plaintiffs’ acceptance of their father’s estate may not be considered, as they are extrinsic evidence to the complaint.  However, regardless of whether Plaintiffs have in fact accepted their succession to Daniel’s estate under French law, Defendant Henri failed to establish as a threshold issue on this motion for judgment on the pleadings that Plaintiffs were required to comply with French law in order to have standing to proceeding with this California civil action. 

            Henri also argues Plaintiffs lack standing under California law, because they have not filed a declaration under Code of Civil Procedure section 377.32.  However, Plaintiffs allege that, prior to his death, Daniel instructed Jafar to wire $3.5 million to Henri for his own and Plaintiffs’ behalf.  (Complaint, ¶13.)  As acknowledged by Henri, Plaintiffs are therefore proceeding on their own direct right to a portion of the proceeds as individuals, not as Daniel’s successors or heirs.  Plaintiffs’ lack of standing as to Daniel’s portion of the funds would not be grounds to grant judgment on the pleadings.  (Santa Clarita Organization etc. v. County of Los Angeles (2024) 105 Cal.App.5th 1143, 1165 (motion for judgment on the pleadings, like a demurrer, cannot be granted as to only a portion of the cause of action).)

            Henri also argues Plaintiffs lack standing because they failed to file a declaration pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 377.32.  Section 377.32(a) requires that a person suing as a decedent’s successor-in-interest submit a declaration establishing standing as the decedent’s successor-in-interest or as a person authorized to act on behalf of the decedent’s successor in interest.  “The person who seeks to commence an action or proceeding…as the decedent’s successor in interest under this article, shall execute and file an affidavit or declaration under penalty of perjury under the laws of this state stating all of the following….”  (Code of Civil Proc., §377.32.)

Plaintiffs filed declarations pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 377.32 on May 15, 2026.  Henri’s objection on this ground is therefore moot.

Conclusion

Henri’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is denied.  Henri fails to establish Plaintiffs’ lack of standing based on the face of the complaint or judicially noticeable evidence.  Henri to answer within 20 days.

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

LUXURY LAB LLC dba DOLL FACE BEAUTY, et al.,

                        Defendants.

  Case No.:  25SMCP00401

  Hearing Date:  July 2, 2026

  Trial Date:  None (post-judgment)

 [TENTATIVE] RULING RE:

Motion to Enforce Sister-State Judgment

 

Background

 

Judgment Creditor Triumph Financial Services, LLC n/k/a TBK Bank, SSB (“Triumph”) obtained a judgment against Judgment Debtors Luxury Lab LLC dba Doll Face Beauty and Ross Hilton Kemper in the amount of $553,859.29, plus attorneys’ fees and costs of $24,826.81, and interest at $170.87 per day from April 16, 2025. The judgment was entered in the District Court of Dallas County, Texas, on June 9, 2025, and was domesticated in this Court as a sister-state judgment on July 21, 2025, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1710.25. (Murray Decl. ¶¶ 9-10, Exs. 8-9.)

Ross Hilton Kemper is a trustee, settlor, and beneficiary of the Ross Hilton Kemper & Linda Kerkorian Kemper Family Trust dated May 7, 2002 (the “Trust”). Ross Hilton Kemper and Linda Kerkorian Kemper are husband and wife. The Trust holds title to three parcels of real property: 23872 Malibu Road, Malibu, California; 1114 Calle Vista Drive, Beverly Hills, California; and 1115 La Altura Road, Beverly Hills, California 90210. (Murray Decl. ¶¶ 2-8, 12, Exs. 1-7.)

By this petition, Triumph seeks to levy on the Trust’s assets to satisfy its judgment against Mr. Kemper, contending that because the Trust is Mr. Kemper's revocable living trust, its property is his and is reachable by his creditors under Probate Code section 18200. Triumph explains the order is needed because the Trust is not named in the judgment or writ, and the levying officer will not levy where the recorded title-holder does not match the named debtor.

This is the petition’s second time before the Court. The Court granted a nearly identical, unopposed motion on March 24, 2026, where counsel specially appeared for Mr. Kemper to contest service. On April 24, 2026, the Court granted Mr. Kemper’s motion to quash service by publication and vacated the March 24 order for lack of personal jurisdiction. Triumph re-served the petition by mail on Mr. Kemper's counsel on May 28, 2026, and the matter now comes before the Court on its merits.

No opposition has been filed.

Legal Standard

Under Probate Code section 18200, “[i]f the settlor retains the power to revoke the trust in whole or in part, the trust property is subject to the claims of creditors of the settlor to the extent of the power of revocation during the lifetime of the settlor.” A creditor who has established a claim against the settlor may levy directly on the trust assets that are subject to revocation during the settlor’s lifetime. (Bank One Texas, N.A. v. Pollack (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 973, 980.) Property held in a revocable inter vivos trust is deemed the property of the settlor, who effectively retains full ownership and control over any property transferred to the trust. (Steinhart v. County of Los Angeles (2010) 47 Cal.4th 1298, 1319-1320.)

Under Probate Code section 15400, a trust is presumed revocable “[u]nless [it] is expressly made irrevocable by the trust instrument.” That presumption applies, however, only “where the settlor is domiciled in this state when the trust is created, where the trust instrument is executed in this state, or where the trust instrument provides that the law of this state governs the trust.” The burden to establish that a trust is irrevocable rests with the party so asserting. (Prob. Code, § 15400.)

Analysis

 

A. The Trust Is Revocable and Its Assets Are Subject to Triumph’s Claim.

As with the prior motion, Triumph does not attach a copy of the Trust instrument, and there is therefore no direct evidence of its terms. Triumph again invokes the presumption of revocability under Probate Code section 15400. On the earlier motion, the Court declined to apply that presumption because Triumph had presented no evidence that Mr. Kemper was domiciled in California when the Trust was created, that the instrument was executed in California, or that the instrument provides that California law governs. The same is true here.

In any event, the recorded instruments independently establish that the Trust is revocable. The deeds characterize the Trust as a “revocable living trust” and recite that the conveyances transfer the grantors’ interest into their revocable trust. Moreover, the 1115 La Altura Road property was transferred into and out of the Trust on numerous occasions between 2002 and 2018, conveyances that would not have been possible had the Trust been irrevocable. On this record, the Court concludes that Triumph has established that the Trust is revocable. As settlor of a revocable trust, Mr. Kemper is deemed the owner of the Trust property, which is accordingly subject to the claims of his creditors under Probate Code section 18200. The burden to establish that the Trust is irrevocable rests with Mr. Kemper, and no such showing has been made.

B. The Trust Assets Are Community Property Liable for the Judgment.

Under Family Code section 910, “the community estate is liable for a debt incurred by either spouse before or during marriage, regardless of which spouse has the management and control of the property and regardless of whether one or both spouses are parties to the debt or to a judgment for the debt.” The character of property as separate or community is determined at the time of its acquisition. (Fam. Code, § 760; In re Marriage of Valli (2014) 58 Cal.4th 1396, 1400.) Property acquired during marriage is presumptively community property, and the burden is on the party claiming a separate-property interest to prove it. Accordingly, to the extent the Trust assets are community property, they may be applied to satisfy the judgment against Mr. Kemper notwithstanding that Linda Kerkorian Kemper is not herself a named judgment debtor.

The Kempers have not filed an opposition. The recorded deeds reflect that the three properties were acquired by the Kempers during their marriage, and several of the deeds identify them as husband and wife holding title as community property before the properties were placed in the Trust. There is no claim or evidence that any of the three properties is the separate property of either spouse. The Court therefore concludes that the three identified properties are community property subject to levy.

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS Judgment Creditor’s motion to enforce the Judgment against the following assets of the Ross Hilton Kemper & Linda Kerkorian Kemper Family Trust dated May 7, 2002: 23872 Malibu Road, Malibu California; 1114 Calle Vista Drive, Beverly Hills, California; and 1115 La Altura Road, Beverly Hills, California 90210.

Plaintiff to give notice.

BACKGROUND

 

               This case arises from an employment misconduct dispute. Plaintiff Macias Gini & O’Connell, LLP (Plaintiff), an accounting firm, is the former employer of defendants Will Price and Buwa Ijirigho (collectively, Defendants). Defendants resigned from Plaintiff’s accounting firm on October 15, 2025.

On November 7, 2025, Plaintiff sent both Defendants a notice letter alleging that Defendants had engaged in unfair competition and soliciting customers in violation of a Non-Solicitation Agreement.

On December 23, 2025, Plaintiff initiated arbitration based on a purported arbitration agreement. Defendants did not submit to arbitration arguing that the agreements do not state a forum for arbitration and that they did not consent to JAMS.

On April 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed their Complaint alleging (1) breach of contract, (2) breach of implied covenant of good faith, (3) aiding and abetting breach of fiduciary duty, (4) breach of duty of loyalty, (5) intentional interference with prospective economic advantage, (6) negligent interference with prospective economic advantage, and (7) unfair competition concurrently with the instant motion to compel arbitration.

This hearing is on Plaintiff’s motion to compel arbitration.  Plaintiff seeks an order compelling Defendants to comply with the arbitration agreements they signed. Plaintiff also seeks a stay of the proceedings pending the completion of arbitration.   

LEGAL STANDARD

Pursuant to the terms of the arbitration agreement, the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”) governs the agreement. (Fink Decl., Exh. A-B.)

Under the FAA, “any doubts concerning the scope of arbitrable issues should be resolved in favor of arbitration …”. (Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital v. Mercury Constr. Corp. (1983) 460 U.S. 1, 24–25.) This federal policy favoring arbitration preempts any state law impediments to the policy’s fulfillment.  If a state law interferes with the FAA’s purpose of enforcing arbitration agreements according to their terms, the FAA preempts the state law provision, no matter how laudable the state law’s objectives. (AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion (2011) 563 U.S. 333, 352.)  Under the supremacy clause of the United States Constitution (art. VI, cl. 2), the FAA requires any conflicting state law to give way. (Nitro-Lift Technologies, L. L. C. v. Howard (2012) 133 S.Ct. 500, 504).

However, even when the arbitration agreement is governed by the FAA, the agreement may be enforced via the summary procedures provided by California arbitration law.  (Rosenthal v. Great Western Financial Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 409-410.)  It is a “general and unassailable proposition . . . that States may establish the rules of procedure governing litigation in their own courts,” even though the controversy is governed by substantive federal law.  (Felder v. Casey (1988) 487 U.S. 131, 138.)  By the same token, however, a state procedural rule must give way “if it impedes the uniform application of the federal statute essential to effectuate its purpose, even though the procedure would apply to similar actions arising under state law.” (McCarroll v. L.A. County etc. Carpenters (1957) 49 Cal. 2d 45, 61, 62.) 

“We think it plain the California procedures for a summary determination of the petition to compel arbitration serve to further, rather than defeat, the enforceability policy of the [FAA.]”  (Rosenthal, 14 Cal.4th at 409.)  Code Civ. Proc. § 1281.2 and 1290.2 are neutral as between state and federal law claims for enforcement of arbitration agreements.  (Id.)  “They display no hostility to arbitration as an alternative to litigation; to the contrary, the summary procedure provided, in which the existence and validity of the arbitration agreement is decided by the court in the manner of a motion, is designed to further the use of private arbitration as a means of resolving disputes more quickly and less expensively than through litigation.”  (Id.)

As with federal law, under California law, public policy favors arbitration as an efficient and less expensive means of resolving private disputes.  (Moncharsh v. Heily & Blase (1992) 3 Cal.4th 1, 8-9; AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion, 563 U.S. at 339.)  To further that policy, Code Civ. Proc. §1281.2 requires a trial court to enforce a written arbitration agreement unless it finds (1) no written agreement to arbitrate exists, (2) the right to compel arbitration has been waived, (3) grounds exist for rescission of the agreement or (4) litigation is pending that may render the arbitration unnecessary or create conflicting rulings on common issues.   

When seeking to compel arbitration, the initial burden lies with the moving party to demonstrate the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of evidence.  (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 841-42; Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021), 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 164-65.)  It is sufficient for the moving party to produce a copy of the arbitration agreement or set forth the agreement’s provisions.  (Gamboa, 72 Cal.App.5th at 165.)  The burden then shifts to the opposing party to prove by a preponderance of evidence any defense to enforcement of the contract or the arbitration clause.  (Ruiz, 232 Cal.App.4th at 842; Gamboa, 72 Cal.App.5th at 165.)  The trial court then weighs all the evidence submitted and uses its discretion to make a final determination.  (Id.)  “California law, like [federal law], reflects a strong policy favoring arbitration agreements[.]” (Wagner Const. Co. v. Pacific Mechanical Corp. (2007) 41 Cal.4th 19, 31 (internal quotations omitted).)

If the court orders arbitration, then the court shall stay the action until arbitration is completed.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.)

DISCUSSION

Existence of an Agreement

In ruling on a motion to compel arbitration, the Court must first determine whether the parties actually agreed to arbitrate the dispute, and general principles of California contract law help guide the court in making this determination. (Mendez v. Mid-Wilshire Health Care Center (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 534, 541; Victoria v. Superior Court (1985) 40 Cal. 3d 734, 835.)  

Although “[t]he law favors contracts for arbitration of disputes between parties” (Player v. Geo. M. Brewster & Son, Inc. (1971) 18 Cal.App.3d 526, 534), “there is no policy compelling persons to accept arbitration of controversies which they have not agreed to arbitrate[.]” (Weeks v. Crow (1980) 113 Cal. App. 3d 350, 353..)  “[A]rbitration is a matter of contract and a party cannot be required to submit to arbitration any dispute which he has not agreed so to submit.” (AT&T Technologies v. Communications Workers (1986) 475 U.S. 643, 648 (citations and internal quotations omitted); see also Sparks v. Vista Del Mar Child & Family Services (2012) 207 Cal.App.4th 1511, 1518  (“Because arbitration is a contractual matter, a party that has not agreed to arbitrate a controversy cannot be compelled to do so”).)       

Plaintiff has met its burden to show that arbitration agreements exist. The agreements stipulate that “[Defendants] and the Company agree to utilize binding arbitration as the sole and exclusive means to resolve all disputes that may arise out of or be related in any way to [their] employment”. (Fink Decl., Exh. A, ¶ 2, Exh. B, ¶ 2.) Defendants signed and dated the arbitration agreements. (Ibid.) Defendants do not dispute the existence of an arbitration agreement. Thus, arbitration agreements exist.

 

Arbitrability of Claims    

The arbitration agreement is broad enough to cover Plaintiff’s suit regarding misconduct.  The agreement requires arbitration of “all disputes that may arise out of or be related in any way to [Defendants’] employment” and “any claim, dispute, and/or controversy that …the Company may have against me” (Fink Decl., Exh. A, ¶ 2, Exh. B, ¶ 2.)  Plaintiff’s claims arise out of Defendants’ employment term and subsequent professional conduct. Thus, Plaintiff’s claims are subject to arbitration.

 

Unconscionability

The Court must next consider whether the arbitration agreements are unconscionable.  Unconscionability generally includes the absence of meaningful choice on the part of one of the parties together with contract terms that unreasonably favor the other party. (Carboni v. Arrospide (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 76, 82-83.)  Unconscionability has both a ‘procedural’ and a ‘substantive’ element.” (A & M Produce Co. v. FMC Corp. (1982) 135 Cal.App.3d 473, 486.)  An agreement to arbitrate is unenforceable only if both procedural and substantive unconscionability is shown. (Stirlen v. Supercuts, Inc. (1997) 51 Cal.App.4th 1519, 1533.)

Procedural unconscionability focuses on whether there is “oppression” arising from an inequality of bargaining power or “surprise” arising from buried terms in a complex printed form. (Id. at 1280.)  Substantive unconscionability addresses the existence of overly harsh or one-sided terms. (Id.) 

Defendants have the burden of proving both procedural and substantive unconscionability. (Crippen v. Central Valley RV Outlet. Inc. (2004) 124 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1165).  Both, however, need not be present to the same degree.  A sliding scale is applied so that the “more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.” (Morris v. Redwood Empire Bancorp (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1305, 1317; see also A & M Produce Co., 135 Cal.App.3d at 486.) 

Procedural Unconscionability
               Here, Defendants argue the contract is adhesive and procedurally unconscionable because it was offered on a “take it or leave it” basis and was presented as a mandatory condition of employment. (Opp., p. 3.)   

However, even if adhesive, the showing of procedural unconscionability is slight given the arbitration agreement was not hidden or convoluted.  The agreement is only three pages long, and the arbitration agreement is titled “EMPLOYMENT AT WILL AND ARBITRATION AGREEMENT”) in bold, and in bold letters above the signature line advises the employe is advised to read the agreement. (Fink Decl., Exh. A-B.) Therefore, the Court does not find surprise under these facts.

Beyond adhesion, there is no evidence of any further procedural unconscionability. Defendants’ argument that the offer letter did not refer to an arbitration agreement and that they could not negotiate employment forms reflect ordinary employment paperwork and low procedural unconscionability. The Court in Ramirez v. Charter Communications, Inc. held that “adhesion alone generally indicates only a low degree of procedural unconscionability” in the employment context because they are a fact of modern life but still bear the risk of overreaching. (Ramirez v. Charter Communications, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 478, 494.)

In any event, “a finding of procedural unconscionability does not mean that a contract will not be enforced, but rather that courts will scrutinize the substantive terms of the contract to ensure they are not manifestly unfair or one-sided.” (Gentry v. Superior Court (2007) 42 Cal.4th 443, 469.) We now address whether the arbitration agreement is substantively unconscionable.

Substantive Unconscionability

In order to find an agreement substantively unconscionable, it must be so “one-sided as to shock the conscience.”  (Pinnacle Museum Tower Assn. v. Pinnacle Market Development (US), LLC (2012) 55 Cal.4th 223, 246.) 

            Defendants argue that in determining unconscionability, the arbitration agreement should be considered along with the non-solicitation agreement and NDA. Defendants cite Alberto v. Cambrian Homecare which held that when considering substantive unconscionability, the arbitration agreement should be considered with other parts of the agreement when “[t]hey were executed on the same day. They were both separate aspects of a single primary transaction…They both governed, ultimately, the same issue—how to resolve disputes arising between [employee] and [employer] arising from…employment”. (Alberto v. Cambrian Homecare (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 482, 490-491.) Here, the arbitration agreement was signed by each Defendant along with the NDA and Non-Solicitation agreement as part of one transaction on the same day. (Price Decl., ¶ 3; Ijirigho Decl., ¶ 4.) Moreover, the NDA and Non-Solicitation agreement govern issues arising from Defendants’ employment. Thus, the NDA and Non-Solicitation agreements can be considered in evaluating substantive unconscionability.

            Defendants argue that the injunctive relief provisions in the Non-Solicitation agreement and NDA are unconscionable. (Opp., pp. 7-8.) Here, the injunctive relief provision in the Non-Solicitation agreement extends entitlement to injunctive relief only to Plaintiff. (Price Decl., Exh. D, ¶ 20; Ijirigho Decl., Exh. D, ¶ 20.) The NDA injunctive relief provision states that “the Firm will be entitled to seek extraordinary relief in court, including…temporary restraining orders, preliminary injunctions and permanent injunctions without the necessity of posting a bond or other security”. (Price Decl., Exh. E, ¶ 7(f); Ijirigho Decl., Exh. E, ¶ 7(f).) The injunctive relief provisions lack mutuality. The Court in Gurganus v. IGS Solutions LLC held that “provisions, which go beyond merely permitting provisional injunctive relief and instead “authorize the stronger party to obtain injunctive relief without establishing all of the essential elements for the issuance of an injunction,”  such as those which “seek injunctive relief []without the posting of any bond and without proof of actual damages” lack mutuality and are substantively unconscionable. (Gurganus v. IGS Solutions LLC (2025) 115 Cal.App.5th 327, 338 [338 Cal.Rptr.3d 36].) The Court finds these provisions substantively unconscionable.

            Defendants argue that the non-compete provisions found in the Non-Solicitation agreement and NDA are illegal and substantively unconscionable. (Opp., pp. 9-10.) Here, the non-compete clause in the Non-Solicitation agreement states that for a period of one-year Defendants shall not use the names, addresses, or any other information pertaining to clients at the firm after termination and shall not contact them. (Price Decl., Exh. D, ¶ 19; Ijirigho Decl., Exh. D, ¶ 19.) Under Business & Professional Code section 16600.1, it is unlawful to include a noncompete clause in an employment contract, or to require an employee to enter a noncompete agreement that does not satisfy an exception in this chapter. (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 16600.1 (a).) The non-compete provision in the Non-Solicitation agreement does not qualify under any exception allowing non-competes to move forward. Thus, the Court finds the provision is unlawful and unconscionable.

Defendants argue that the arbitration agreement is substantively unconscionable because it is non-mutual. Defendants argue that the arbitration agreement requires Defendants to arbitrate all disputes that arise out of their employment, but under the NDA and Non-Solicitation agreements executed on the same day, Plaintiff reserves its right to litigate claims against employees. (Opp., p. 6.) Here, the arbitration agreements are not one sided, the arbitration agreements state that that “[t]he Company and [Defendants] each specifically waive and relinquish [their] right to bring a claim against the other”. (Fink Decl., Exh. A, ¶ 2, Exh. B, ¶ 2.) The arbitration agreement affords both parties the right to demand arbitration. (Ibid.)

Defendants argue that the arbitration agreement limits recovery of attorney’s fees. (Opp., pp. 10.) Here, the applicable provision in the agreement does not limit recovery of attorney’s fees, it states that “An arbitrator will not have authority to award attorneys’ fees unless a statute or contract at issue in the dispute authorizes the award of attorneys’ fees to the prevailing party, in which case the arbitrator shall have the authority to make an award of attorneys’ fees as required or permitted”. (Fink Decl., Exh. A, ¶ 3, Exh. B, ¶ 3.) The clause does not limit attorney’s fees beyond what it allowed through applicable law.

Defendants argue that the arbitration agreement imposes an unconscionable confidentiality provision. (Opp., p. 11.) Here, the agreement states that “all communications during or in connection with the arbitration proceedings shall be considered privileged” this provision as applied to both parties is not so one sided that it shocks the conscious. (Fink Decl., Exh. A, ¶ 7, Exh. B, ¶ 7.)

Defendants argue that the arbitration agreement requires agreement to a waiver of the ability to pursue PAGA claims. (Opp., p. 12.) Here, the agreement states that “this agreement is not intended to interfere with [Defendants] rights to collectively bargain, to engage in protected, concerted activity, or to exercise other rights protected under the National Labor Relations Act”. (Fink Decl., Exh. A, ¶ 6, Exh. B, ¶ 6.) PAGA claims asserting violations of the California Labor Code are protected under the Private Attorneys General Act, “an employee suing under PAGA “does so as the proxy or agent of the state's labor law enforcement agencies”. (Kim v. Reins International California, Inc. (2020) 9 Cal.5th 73, 81.) Thus, according to the terms of the agreement, PAGA claims are unaffected.

In sum, the Court finds slight procedural unconscionability and slight substantive unconscionability. As discussed above, if the Court finds slight amounts of procedural unconscionability, there must be a high amount of substantive unconscionability to make up for it and deem the agreement unconscionable. (Morris, supra, 128 Cal.App.4th at 1317.) Here, the slight amounts of procedural and substantive unconscionability do not slide the scale as to make the agreement unenforceable. Accordingly, the arbitration agreement is enforceable.

 

Stay of Proceedings

Code Civ. Proc. §1281.4 provides that if the court has ordered the arbitration of a controversy, it “shall, upon motion of a party to such action or proceeding, stay the action or proceeding until an arbitration is had in accordance with the order to arbitrate or until such earlier time as the court specifies.”  Pursuant to Section 1281.4, therefore, the Court stays this action pending conclusion of the arbitration proceedings.  

 

CONCLUSION

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff Macias Gini & O’Connell, LLP’s motions to compel arbitration and for a stay.
