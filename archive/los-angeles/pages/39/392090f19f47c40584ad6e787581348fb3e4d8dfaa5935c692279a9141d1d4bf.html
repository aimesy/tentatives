III.      LEGAL STANDARD

Under both the Federal Arbitration Act and
California law, arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable,
except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a contract. (Winter
v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943,
947.) The party moving to compel arbitration must establish the existence
of a written arbitration agreement between the parties. (Code Civ. Proc. §
1281.2.[1])
This is usually done by presenting a copy of the signed, written agreement to
the court. “A petition to compel arbitration or to stay proceedings pursuant to
Code of Civil Procedure sections 1281.2 and 1281.4 must state, in addition to
other required allegations, the provisions of the written agreement and the
paragraph that provides for arbitration. The provisions must be stated verbatim
or a copy must be physically or electronically attached to the petition and
incorporated by reference.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330.) The moving
party must also establish the other party’s refusal to arbitrate the
controversy. (§ 1281.2.) The filing of a lawsuit against the moving party
for a controversy clearly within the scope of the arbitration agreement affirmatively
establishes the other party’s refusal to arbitrate the controversy. (Hyundai
Amco America, Inc. v. S3H, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 572, 577.)

“Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2
requires a trial court to grant a petition to compel arbitration ‘if the court
determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists.’” (Avery
v. Integrated Healthcare Holdings, Inc. (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 50, 59 (Avery)
(quoting § 1281.2).) Accordingly, “when presented with a petition to compel
arbitration, the court’s first task is to determine whether the parties have in
fact agreed to arbitrate the dispute.”  (Ibid.) A petition to
compel arbitration is in essence a suit in equity to compel specific
performance of a contract.  (Id. at 71.) As with any other
specific performance claim, “a party seeking to enforce an arbitration
agreement must show the agreement’s terms are sufficiently definite to enable
the court to know what it is to enforce.” (Ibid. [internal citations
omitted].)  “Only the valid and binding agreement of the parties,
including all material terms well-defined and clearly expressed, may be ordered
specifically performed.” (Ibid.) An arbitration agreement “must be
so interpreted as to give effect to the mutual intention of the parties as it
existed at the time of contracting, so far as the same is ascertainable and
lawful.” (Civ. Code § 1636.) The language of the contract governs its
interpretation if it is clear and explicit. (Civ. Code § 1368.) If uncertainty
exists, “the language of a contract should be interpreted most strongly against
the party who caused the uncertainty to exist.” (Civ. Code § 1654.)

The party seeking to compel arbitration bears
the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the
preponderance of the evidence. (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group,
Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 972.) It would then be the burden of the party
opposing the motion to prove by a preponderance of the evidence any fact
necessary to her opposition. (Ibid.) “In these summary proceedings
the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits,
declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony
received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination. (Ibid.)

 

IV.       DISCUSSION

TOE argues this action is subject to
arbitration as Walsh entered into a valid arbitration agreement with non-party
SwipeCast, Inc. (“SwipeCast”), agreeing to resolve any arising disputes via
binding arbitration. For the Court to grant a motion to compel arbitration, the
moving party must demonstrate the existence of a valid arbitration agreement between
the moving and refusing party, as well as the refusing party’s refusal to
arbitrate the controversy. (§ 1281.2.)

A.        Existence
of Arbitration Agreement Between Parties

TOE provided a copy of SwipeCast’s Terms and
Conditions which include an arbitration clause, that were in effect as of
September 26, 2019. (Mot., Fitzpatrick Decl. ¶ 9, Ex. 11)

The terms and conditions, in pertinent part,
provides as follows:

“YOU AND SWIPECAST AND ITS
RESPECTIVE CORPORATE PARENTS, SUBSIDIARIES, AFFILIATES, PREDECESSORS IN
INTEREST, SUCCESSORS, PERMITTED ASSIGNS, AND ANY OTHER PARTIES ON WHOSE BEHALF
YOU ARE ACCESSING THE SERVICE AGREE TO ARBITRATION (EXCEPT FOR MATTERS THAT MAY
BE TAKEN TO SMALL CLAIMS COURT), AS THE EXCLUSIVE FORM OF DISPUTE RESOLUTION
EXCEPT AS PROVIDED FOR BELOW, FOR ALL DISPUTES AND CLAIMS ARISING OUT OF OR
RELATING TO THIS EULA OR THE SERVICE.” (Ibid.)”

It is undisputed that SwipeCast’s terms and
conditions included an arbitration clause at the time in question. However, the
Court does not find there is sufficient evidence to establish Walsh signed this
agreement and agreed to the pertinent terms. “The petitioner bears the burden
of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of
the evidence, while a party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving
by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense.
[Citation.] The trial court sits as the trier of fact, weighing all the
affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, and any oral
testimony the court may receive at its discretion, to reach a final
determination. [Citation.].” (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Grp. (2014) 232
Cal. App. 4th 836, 842.)

The only evidence offered in support of this
assertion is found in the declaration of Peter Fitzpatrick (“Fitzpatrick”), the
custodian of records for SwipeCast. Fitzpatrick attests that the provided
agreement “was in effect at the time Talent, David Walsh, registered
for and used the SwipeCast platform[,]” but provides no basis for this claim. (Id.
at ¶ 9.) Fitzpatrick does not attest as to the existence of any records of
Walsh ‘registering’, or how Fitzpatrick obtained this information. Therefore,
this statement is subject to objection on the basis of lack of foundation and
personal knowledge.

There
is no other evidence to establish Walsh utilized SwipeCast or agreed to
SwipeCast’s terms and conditions at any point, much less when the provided
terms were in effect. Absent evidence Walsh agreed to the arbitration
agreement terms, the Court cannot find the existence of an arbitration
agreement between Walsh and SwipeCast, much less TOE.

B.        Equitable Estoppel

Additionally, the terms and
conditions only provides that it is an agreement with SwipeCast and SwipeCast’s
intended beneficiaries. “Generally speaking, one must be a party to an
arbitration agreement to be bound by it or invoke it.” (Westra v. Marcus
& Millichap Real Estate Investment Brokerage Co., Inc. (2005) 129
Cal.App.4th 759, 763.) One pertinent exception is based on the doctrine of
equitable estoppel. (Boucher v. Alliance Title Co., Inc. (2005) 127
Cal.App.4th 262, 268 (Boucher); Goldman v. KPMG, LLP (2009) 173
Cal.App.4th 209, 220.) TOE is not a party to this agreement but does argue that
it should be included under the doctrine of equitable estoppel.

TOE argues it should be
included under the doctrine of equitable estoppel, as Walsh’s claims are
“founded in and inextricably intertwined” with the SwipeCast transaction,
because the photos and images at issue in this case would not exist but for the
SwipeCast transaction. (Goldman v. KPMG, LLP
(2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 209, 221.) The doctrine of equitable estoppel allows the
court to compel arbitration when the claims against the nonsignatory are
founded in and inextricably bound up with the obligations imposed by the
agreement containing the arbitration clause. (Id. at 219.) In Goldman,
the Court specifically noted a plaintiff could not ‘have it both ways’ by
relying on the terms of the agreement to assert claims against the
nonsignatory, then repute the nonsignatory’s application of the arbitration
agreement. (Id. at 220.)

There is no evidence to suggest Walsh is relying upon the
terms of the SwipeCast agreement to sue TOE. Walsh’s claims are based upon TOE
allegedly continuing to use Walsh’s image outside of the contracted 6-month
period. The complaint does not require or rely upon on the existence of the
SwipeCast agreement to bring actionable claims against TOE. The mere fact the
SwipeCast agreement allegedly put Walsh and TOE into contact, eventually
leading to a distinct image contract, does not make the SwipeCast agreement “inextricably
intertwined.”

The facts of this case are similar to Pacific Fertility
Cases (2022) 85 Cal.App.5th 887. In Pacific Fertility, fertility
clinic patients brought an action against a manufacturer and seller of
allegedly defective cryogenic storage tank that failed to preserve their eggs
and embryos. (Ibid.) The court found the manufacturer could not enforce
the fertility clinic’s arbitration agreement via equitable estoppel, even
though the plaintiffs only encountered the defective tank because of
their contracts with the fertility treatment center. (Id. at 897.) This
was despite the fact the plaintiffs were suing the fertility clinic for
separate causes of action, relating to the incurred harm. (Id. at 898.) “Plaintiffs...are
not seeking to enforce the terms of their agreements with [feritlity clinic]
against [manufacturer]. Nor does the court need to look to the plaintiffs’
agreements with [fertility clinic] to determine liability as to [manufacturer].
To the contrary, plaintiffs’ claims against [manufacturer] are viable without
reliance on the terms of the fertility services agreements with [fertility
clinic]. Thus, for this reason, as well, the court's order denying arbitration
as to [manufacturer] withstands challenge on appeal.” (Id. at 899.)

Like in Pacific Fertility, Walsh
is not seeking to enforce the SwipeCast agreement against TOE. Walsh’s claims
against TOE do not require the Court evaluate Walsh’s agreement with SwipeCast.
Therefore, Walsh’s claims are not interconnected to the SwipeCast arbitration
agreement. Even if TOE had provided evidence demonstrating Walsh agreed
to the relevant terms and conditions, nonsignatory TOE would have no right to
enforce the arbitration agreement.

V.        CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, Defendants’ motion to compel
arbitration is DENIED.

Walsh is ordered to give notice.

Plaintiff,

vs.

ODINAMBA CULTURAL ASSOCIATION, INC., et al.,

Defendants.

ORDER DENYING MOTION FOR SANCTIONS

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Pursuant to contract, Plaintiff Bigginslaw, Inc. (“Bigginslaw”) allegedly provided professional services to Defendants Odinamba Cultural Association (“OCA”) and Okwii Agbo (“Agbo”), for which Defendants never paid. Bigginslaw sued Defendants for breach of contract.

On November 14, 2016, the Court entered default judgment against Agbo. Bigginslaw renewed judgment against Agbo on September 8, 2020.

On March 30, 2026, Bigginslaw filed the instant motion for sanctions, requesting the Court impose issue preclusion sanctions, evidentiary sanctions, further monetary sanctions, and an order compelling code-compliant responses, along with further written responses. No party has filed an opposition.

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030[1] gives the court the discretion to impose sanctions against anyone engaging in a misuse of the discovery process. A court has broad discretion in selecting the appropriate penalty. (See Lopez v. Watchtower Bible and Tract Society of New York, Inc. (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 566, 604.)

When a party violates a discovery order, a trial court may prohibit the violating party from introducing evidence at trial that contradicts the opponent’s claims or supports the violating party’s defenses.  (Juarez v. Boy Scouts of America (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 377, 387.) Evidentiary sanctions may be imposed only after a motion to compel is made and granted and the party to be sanctioned has failed to comply with that order.  (Karlsson v. Ford Motor Co. (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1202, 1214.)  However, violation of a discovery order is not a prerequisite to evidentiary sanctions when the offending party has engaged in a pattern of willful discovery abuse that causes the unavailability of evidence.  (Id. at pg. 1215.)

Sanctions are not to be imposed for punishment purposes but instead are used to encourage fair disclosure of discovery to prevent unfairness resulting for the lack of information. (See Midwife v. Bernal (1988) 203 Cal.App.3d 57, 64 [superseded on other grounds as stated in Kohan v. Cohan (1991) 229 Cal.App.3d 967, 971].)

 

III.      discussion

Bigginslaw requests the Court impose issue preclusion sanctions, evidentiary sanctions and further monetary sanctions, as Agbo allegedly failed to comply with the Court’s January 5, 2026, order. Pursuant to section 2023.030, the Court may impose sanctions for engaging in conduct constituting misuse of the discovery process. Misuse of the discovery process includes failing to respond to or submit to an authorized method of discovery and disobeying a court order to provide discovery. (§ 2023.010, subd. (d) and (f).)

On August 13, 2025, Bigginslaw served post-judgment requests for production (“RPD”) on Agbo. (See January 5, 2026, Minute Order.) Bigginslaw filed a motion to compel responses to the RPD, which the Court granted on January 5, 2026. (Ibid.) The Court ordered Agbo to serve verified responses, without objections, within 30 days of notice of the order. (Ibid.) Notice of the order was served on January 15, 2026. (Mot., Biggins Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. A.)

On February 17, 2026, Agbo served verified responses. (Mot., Biggins Decl. ¶ 7, Ex. C.) A portion of the verified responses were as follows: “After a diligent search, responding party has determined that the requested documents either do not exist or responding party does not know the whereabouts of the documents and/or does not have access to to the requested documents,” failing to comply with the specific requirements of section 2031.230. (Ibid.) Other responses consisted of one-word responses, with no indication of intent to produce certain documents. No documents were produced with written responses. (Ibid.)

Following meet and confer efforts, Agbo served 201 pages of documents on March 26, 2026. (Mot., Biggins Decl. ¶ 11, Ex. D.) Certain documents were produced out of order and redacted, while others were not produced at all. (Ibid.) This motion was filed four days later.

Based upon the facts alleged, the Court does not find sufficient grounds to issue evidentiary, issue, or further monetary sanctions. As currently alleged, there is no indication of substantive failure to comply with discovery obligations, creating a pattern indicating Bigginslaw will be unable to obtain discovery. The only discovery at issue is the allegedly insufficient RPD responses.

Bigginslaw’s account clearly demonstrates that Agbo served verified responses, without objections; Agbo also provided documentation, albeit at a later date. The issue is to the quality and extent of compliance. When initial discovery responses have been produced, but the party receiving the responses finds them insufficient, the appropriate motion is a motion to compel further discovery. Sanctions are not to be imposed for punishment purposes but instead are used to encourage fair disclosure of discovery to prevent unfairness resulting for the lack of information. (See Midwife v. Bernal (1988) 203 Cal.App.3d 57, 64.) Here, Agbo, in part, complied with the Court order by serving responses. If a party continues to produce deficient responses after a court has granted such a motion and ordered additional responses, then the Court may consider issuing sanctions that impact a party’s ability to advocate for themselves.

Similarly, the Court will not set an OSC re: Contempt as the issue is not of compliance, but of the quality of compliance. The Court will also not rule on the issue of further responses, as that must be done via noticed motion, which should only be heard after parties attend an IDC.

Therefore, the Court denies the motion.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

Plaintiff Bigginslaw, Inc.’s motion for sanctions is DENIED.

Agbo is ordered to give notice.
