Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

Torrance Dept. E

 

LISA CHAN and KURT SELBACH

 

 

 

Plaintiffs,

 

Case No.:

 

 

22STCV23537

 

v.

 

 

[Tentative] Denied

 

LYFT, INC., a Delaware Corporation; RICHARD DARRELL JONES, an Individual; JAKE MUNGARAY, an Individual; and DOES 1 to 50, Inclusive

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

Defendants.

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

Hearing Date:             August 12, 2026

 

Moving Parties:          Plaintiffs Lisa Chan and Kurt Selbach

 

Responding Party:      Defendants Richard Darrell Jones and Jake Mungaray

 

HEARING: Motion for a New Trial

           

The Court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers.

 

RULING

 

The Court denies the Motion for New Trial.

 

BACKGROUND

           

            On July 21, 2022, Plaintiffs Lisa Chan and Kurt Selbach filed the Complaint against Lyft Inc., Richard Darrell Jones, and Jake Munagary, alleging a single cause of action for negligence.

 

            April 13, 2026, the jury returned a complete defense verdict finding.

 

            On June 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Motion for a New Trial.

 

            On June 29, 2026, Defendants filed the Opposition.

 

            On August 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“The verdict may be vacated and any other decision may be modified or vacated, in whole or in part, and a new or further trial granted on all or part of the issues, on the application of the party aggrieved, for any of the following causes, materially affecting the substantial rights of such party: 

“1. Irregularity in the proceedings of the court, jury or adverse party, or any order of the court or abuse of discretion by which either party was prevented from having a fair trial. 

“2. Misconduct of the jury; and whenever any one or more of the jurors have been induced to assent to any general or special verdict, or to a finding on any question submitted to them by the court, by a resort to the determination of chance, such misconduct may be proved by the affidavit of any one of the jurors. 

“3. Accident or surprise, which ordinary prudence could not have guarded against. 

“4. Newly discovered evidence, material for the party making the application, which he could not, with reasonable diligence, have discovered and produced at the trial. 

“5. Excessive or inadequate damages. 

“6. Insufficiency of the evidence to justify the verdict or other decision, or the verdict or other decision is against law. 

“7. Error in law, occurring at the trial and excepted to by the party making the application.” 

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 657.)

 

 DISCUSSION

 

Timeliness

 

Defendants request the Court not consider Plaintiffs' Reply as it was filed late. However, as all parties had time to read the reply days before the hearing, the Court finds that any issue with its timeliness was non-prejudicial. Therefore, the Court exercises its discretion to consider all papers received. (Iverson v. Superior Court, (1985) 167 Cal. App. 3d 544, 546.)

 

Merits

 

Plaintiffs first argue that counsel for Defendant Mungaray committed prejudicial misconduct during the opening statement by telling the jury that Defendant Mungaray doesn't have the resources to retain expert witnesses. Plaintiffs characterize this as a deliberate effort to frame Defendant Mungaray as a sympathetic underdog before any witness was sworn, in violation of the settled rule that a deliberate showing of a party's poverty is highly prejudicial and may constitute reversible error. (Hart v. Wielt (1970) 4 Cal.App.3d 224, 234.) Plaintiffs note that the rule is now codified in CACI No. 117, which instructs jurors not to consider the wealth or poverty of any party.

 

Plaintiffs contend that the misconduct was incurable and that the Court's re-reading of CACI No. 117 did not cure it, because evidence of poverty operates on jurors' emotions rather than their reasoning from disputed facts. They emphasize that counsel for Defendant Jones, who had no incentive to assist Plaintiffs, told the Court that the genie was out of the bottle and analogized the comment to improperly disclosing liability insurance, which is presumptively prejudicial because it invites a verdict based on who is paying rather than who is at fault. (Evid. Code, § 1155.) Plaintiffs argue the prejudice is reflected in the verdict, because the investigating officer's report and testimony, the citation, and the subsequent finding of fault established that Defendant Mungaray violated Vehicle Code section 22517, the dooring statute, yet the jury still returned a defense verdict.

 

Plaintiffs next argue that denial of their mistrial motion was an abuse of discretion, because the comment came from an experienced attorney during opening statement, was a direct declarative statement about the defendant's finances rather than a fleeting aside, and was immediately recognized as incurable by counsel for a co-defendant with no motive to manufacture favorable error. (Code Civ. Proc., § 657, subd. (1).)

 

Plaintiffs also argue that the refusal to give their requested instructions on Vehicle Code sections 21754, 360, and 22517 was prejudicial error in law, because a party is entitled to instruction on every theory supported by substantial evidence. (Code Civ. Proc., § 475; Cal. Const., art. VI, § 13.) They contend that Vehicle Code section 21754 was needed to give the jury a statutory benchmark for lawful passing on the right and to rebut the defense theory that Plaintiffs' tandem bicycle was unsafely positioned, that Vehicle Code section 360 supplied the definition of highway needed to determine whether the rules of the road applied to the location, and that Vehicle Code section 22517, the most prejudicial omission, was the very statute the investigating officer identified as the primary collision factor. Plaintiffs argue that general negligence instructions such as CACI No. 400 and CACI No. 700 were no substitute for these specific statutory standards and that, without them, the jury could not properly test Defendant Jones's theory that Plaintiffs threaded the needle between the stopped vehicles and the curb. They conclude that the resulting verdict is against law under Code of Civil Procedure section 657, subdivision (6), and the product of error in law under subdivision (7).

 

Plaintiffs further argue in the alternative that the cumulative effect of these errors independently warrants a new trial, because a jury primed to view the case through sympathy was simultaneously deprived of the statutory framework needed to anchor its deliberations and then asked to weigh an unforeseen defense theory without that framework. They contend it is reasonably probable a more favorable result would have been reached absent these errors.

 

In opposition, Defendant Jones first argues that Plaintiffs waived any challenge based on the opening-statement comment by counsel for Defendant Mungaray because they neither objected nor moved for a mistrial. Defendant Jones states that during opening statements on March 26, 2026, counsel for Defendant Mungaray told the jury that Defendant Mungaray retained no experts because he lacked the resources to do so, and that Plaintiffs did not object when the statement was made. Defendant Jones explains that it was his own counsel who brought the comment to the Court's attention and later formally moved for a mistrial on April 1, 2026, arguing the comment was prejudicial to Defendant Jones and improperly implied that Defendant Mungaray lacked the means to defend himself or satisfy a judgment. He notes that the Court denied that motion and re-read CACI No. 117, and that Plaintiffs stood silent throughout and never joined the motion. Defendant Jones reasons that Plaintiffs declined to object because the comment fit their trial strategy and benefitted them in the moment, and that they raised the issue only weeks after an adverse verdict.

 

Defendant Jones grounds the waiver argument in the rule that a party who becomes aware of misconduct or irregularity during trial must promptly bring it to the court's attention if he wishes to object or be deemed to have waived the point as a ground for a new trial. (Weathers v. Kaiser Foundation Hospitals (1971) 5 Cal.3d 98, 103.) He also relies on the principle that a party who learns of an objectionable fact in time to seek a remedy may not sit in silence, take his chances on a favorable verdict, and complain only after a hostile one. (Sepulveda v. Ayako Ishimaru (1957) 149 Cal.App.2d 543, 547.)

 

Defendant Jones next argues that Plaintiffs' own decision not to prosecute their case against Defendant Mungaray undermines the motion. He contends that Plaintiffs made a conscious choice to point the finger solely at Defendant Jones and to divert attention from Defendant Mungaray, so they cannot credibly claim the opening comment prejudiced them. Defendant Jones points out that the direct examination of Plaintiffs' sole liability expert, Dr. Kuba Glazek, focused entirely on Defendant Jones being in the best position to see and avoid Plaintiffs rather than on Defendant Mungaray. He adds that Plaintiffs' closing argument and accompanying presentation directed the jury to hold only Defendant Jones accountable and included statements supporting Defendant Mungaray's account of asking permission before opening the door. Defendant Jones further notes that Plaintiffs' counsel told the jury that Defendant Mungaray did everything he was supposed to do and that his conduct was not a substantial factor in causing harm, and even demonstrated marking no on the special verdict question asking whether Defendant Mungaray's negligence was a substantial factor in causing harm to Plaintiffs. In Defendant Jones's view, the jury did exactly what Plaintiffs asked with respect to Defendant Mungaray, which negates any claim of prejudice.

 

Defendant Jones then argues that Plaintiffs' challenge to the refusal of special instructions on Vehicle Code sections 21754, 360, and 22517 lacks merit. He first points out that Plaintiffs did not request the section 22517 instruction, that Defendant Jones requested it, and that Plaintiffs did not join the request, so Plaintiffs waived any reliance on that ruling.  Defendant Jones also argues that Plaintiffs' reliance on the investigating officer's finding of fault and the citation issued to Defendant Mungaray misrepresents the trial record, because it was Plaintiffs who filed two motions in limine to exclude Officer Hanson's finding of fault and the citation under section 22517, and the Court granted both motions over Defendant Jones's objection. He contends that the jury never heard this evidence precisely because Plaintiffs fought to keep it out, so Plaintiffs cannot now claim prejudice from a result flowing from their own strategy. As to sections 360 and 21754, Defendant Jones argues that the definition of highway in section 360 was tied to the section 22517 instruction that Plaintiffs never requested, and that the Court declined to give section 21754 because it addressed vehicles overtaking and passing on the right, a situation not presented in this case, and that Plaintiffs made no argument when the Court announced it would not give these instructions.

 

“The trial court, no less than the appellate court, is expressly enjoined by article VI, section 4 ½, of our Constitution to deny a new trial for error of law unless such error is prejudicial.” (Brown v. George Pepperdine Foundation (1943) 23 Cal.2d 256, 262.) “Instructional error in a civil case is prejudicial where it seems probable that the error prejudicially affected the verdict.” (Soule v. General Motors Corp. (1994) 8 Cal.4th 548, 580.)

 

Here, the Court finds that the alleged misconduct was not prejudicial. First, as to the comments made about Defendant Mungaray’s wealth, Plaintiffs’ theory of the case rested on pinning the incident on Defendant Jones, as shown by Plaintiffs’ closing arguments asking the jury to not give rideshare drivers an out by blaming their passengers. (Page Decl. Exhibit (c).) Furthermore, the Court re-read CACI No. 117 directing the jury not to consider any party's wealth right after the disputed statement. Thus, any comments made about Defendant Mungaray's alleged poverty were likely to favor Plaintiff’s theory of the case and were not prejudicial. Second, the alleged misinstructions were also not prejudicial. Vehicle Code Section 22517 defines the duty of a person opening a car door into traffic, which would run to Defendant Mungaray, not Defendant Jones. (Veh. Code § 22517.) Vehicle Code sections 21754 and 360 define highways and the passing-on-the-right standard. (Veh. Code § 360, 21754.) However, the general negligence and ordinary-care instructions gave the jury the framework to evaluate whether Plaintiffs rode reasonably, and Plaintiffs have not shown a reasonable probability that a specific passing-on-the-right instruction would have changed the verdict. (Soule, supra 8 Cal.4th 548, 580-582.)

 

Therefore, the Court denies the Motion for a New Trial.

Plaintiffs,

 

Case No.:

 

25TRCV00350

 

vs.

 

 

[Tentative] Granted

 

HOMEPLUS FINANCE CORPORATION, a California corporation; and DOES 1-20

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

Defendants.

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

Hearing Date:             August 12, 2026

 

Moving Parties:          Defendant HomePlus Finance Corporation

 

Responding Party:      Plaintiff Jay H. Zimmerman

 

HEARING: Motion to Compel Compliance

 

            The Court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers.

 

RULING

 

            The Court grants the Motion to Compel Compliance.

           

BACKGROUND

 

            On January 31, 2025, Plaintiff Jay H. Zimmerman filed the Complaint against Defendant HomePlus Finance Company, alleging causes of action for breach of promissory note, open book, account stated, money had and received, and violations of the Corporations Code.

 

            On May 5, 2025, Defendant served its first set of Requests for Production of

Documents on Plaintiffs.

 

            On July 7, 2025, Plaintiffs served their response to Defendant’s discovery requests.

 

            On June 18, 2026, Defendant served the Motion to Compel Compliance with Responses to Requests for Production.

 

            On July 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Opposition.

 

            On August 5, 2026, Defendant filed the Reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

           

            The propounding party may file motions to compel further responses to requests for production if it believes the responses received are (1) evasive, (2) incomplete, or (3) if the objections raised are meritless or overly general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a

The burden is on the moving party to “set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).)  This burden “is met simply by a fact-specific showing of relevance.” (TBG Ins. Servs. Corp. v. Superior Court (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.) In response to a motion to compel further responses, the respondent bears the burden of justifying any objections. (Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (2000) 22 Cal.4th 245, 255.)

 

DISCUSSION

 

Meet and Confer

 

A motion to compel must be “accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310 subd. (a).) Section 2016.040 states that “a meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.)

 

Here, the parties have exchanged numerous meet and confer emails. Therefore, the Court finds that Defendant has satisfied the meet and confer requirements.

 

            Defendant requests that the Court compel Plaintiff to comply with his RFP responses.

 

            Timeliness

           

“Unless notice of this motion is given within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the demanding party and the responding party have agreed in writing, the demanding party waives any right to compel a further response to the demand.” (Code Civ Proc., § 2031.310 subdivision (c).) “If the party fails to make a production in accordance with its statement of compliance, the opposing party may file another motion to compel seeking compliance. No time limit is placed on such a motion.” (Board of Registered Nursing v. Superior Court (2021) 59 Cal.App.5th 1011, 1034.)

 

Here, the parties dispute whether the instant motion is a motion to compel further or a motion to compel compliance. A party can respond to a request for production with either a statement of compliance, a representation of inability to comply, or an objection. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.210.) An objection can only be attacked by a motion to compel further; a statement of compliance may be enforced with a motion to compel compliance.

 

Here, the discovery requests at issue were responded to with a litany of objections followed by the statement “Subject to and without waiving the foregoing objections, Defendant responds as follows: Plaintiff is informed and believes that all non-privileged Documents responsive to this Request already were produced to, or otherwise are in the possession, custody, or control of, or equally or superiorly available to, Defendant. To the extent any such Documents were not already produced to, or otherwise are not already in the possession, custody, or control of, or equally or superiorly available to, Defendant, Plaintiff will produce all such non-privileged Documents responsive to this Request on a mutually agreeable date.” The Court finds that this is a viable statement of compliance agreeing to produce documents at a later agreed-upon date. Thus, the instant motion is a Motion to Compel Compliance. However, as this is a valid Motion to Compel Compliance, Plaintiff must only produce the documents he agreed to produce, i.e., those not subject to his objections, not in the control of or equally available to Defendant, or not already produced. Last, while Plaintiff makes a res judicata argument, res judicata does not apply to interim discovery rulings. (People v. Scott (2000) 85 Cal.App.4th 905, 918.)

 

Therefore, the Court grants the Motion to Compel Compliance.

           

Sanctions

 

“Except as provided in subdivision (d), the court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel compliance with a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.320 subd. (b).)

 

Defendant requests that the Court impose sanctions on Plaintiff in the amount of $ 18,417.50. However, the Court finds that Plaintiff had substantial justification to oppose the instant motion given the ambiguous language in the statement of compliance.

 

Therefore, the Court denies the request for sanctions.

[Tentative] Sustained

 

TORRANCE TERRACE INVESTMENTS LP A CALF LIMITED PARTNERSHIP

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

Defendant.

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

Hearing Date:             February 18, 2026

 

Moving Parties:          Defendant Torrance Terrace Investments LP

 

Responding Party:      Plaintiff Angie Kiroloss

 

HEARING: Demurrer

           

The Court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers.

 

RULING

 

            The Court sustains the Demurrer without leave to amend.

 

BACKGROUND

 

            On May 16, 2025, Plaintiff Angie Kiroloss filed the Complaint against Defendant Torrance Terrance Investments LP.

 

            On October 13, 2025, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint.

 

            On July 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Second Amended Complaint (SAC) against Defendant, alleging causes of action for breach of the covenant of quiet enjoyment, wrongful eviction, fraud, constructive eviction, retaliatory eviction, IIED/NIED, breach of the implied warranty of habitability, and breach of UCL.

 

            On July 8, 2026, Defendant filed the Demurrer.

 

            On July 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Opposition (titled Motion for Leave to Amend).

           

            On August 8, 2026, Defendant filed the Reply.

           

LEGAL STANDARD

 

A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 

53 Cal.4th 861, 872.) For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.) A demurrer “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.) 

 

A pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous or unintelligible. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) A demurrer for uncertainty may lie if the failure to label the parties and claims renders the complaint so confusing defendant cannot tell what he or she is supposed to respond to. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2.) However, “[a] demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)

           

DISCUSSION

           

Meet and Confer 

 

Before filing a demurrer, “the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) 

 

            On June 4, 2026, Defendant sent a meet and confer letter to Plaintiff, which Plaintiff did not respond to. The Court finds this is sufficient to satisfy the meet and confer requirement.

 

            Uncertainty

 

Defendant demurs to the SAC as uncertain. Defendant contends that even under California's liberal pleading rules, a complaint must apprise the adversary of the factual basis of the claim and state the essential facts with particularity sufficient to acquaint a defendant with the nature, source, and extent of the cause of action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.10, subd. (a)(1).)

Defendant argues that the SAC does not meet this standard. The caption names only Defendant Torrance Terrace Investment LP, yet the body lists Torrance Terrace Investment LLP, the manager Maggie Olvera, and Torrance Yukon Holdings LLC as defendants, none of whom appears in the caption. No cause of action identifies the defendant against whom it is asserted, and although each refers to Defendants collectively, the incorporated narrative attributes nearly all of the described conduct to Maggie personally, to neighboring tenants, to the Torrance Housing Authority, or to the judge who presided over Plaintiff's prior unlawful detainer trial.

 

Defendant further notes the twenty-one pages of unbroken, all-capital narrative that cite Civil and Penal Code provisions interchangeably, including a description of the covenant of quiet enjoyment under Civil Code section 1927 as a violation of section 1927 of the Penal Code. Defendant maintains that it cannot determine what Defendant Torrance Terrace Investment LP itself is alleged to have done, which of the eight causes of action it must answer, or how the facts connect to the relief sought. Because the pleading remains ambiguous and unintelligible, Defendant argues that the demurrer for uncertainty should be sustained as to the entire pleading and as to each cause of action.

 

Here, the SAC does add a section at the end listing the alleged causes of action, each with a conclusory paragraph describing the conduct involved in the cause of action. However, the SAC still includes a 14-page all caps narrative of events without paragraph breaks, which is very difficult to parse through, a summary of events that does not differentiate which actions are taken by which Defendants, and a cause of action section that does not state which Defendant is being sued for which cause of action, or what underlying facts listed in the 14-page narrative connect to which cause of action. Therefore, the Court finds that the SAC is still uncertain. Furthermore, as Plaintiff has now had three attempts to rectify the issue and failed to do so, the Court finds that there is no reasonable probability that the defects can be amended.

 

Therefore, the Court sustains the Demurrer without leave to amend.

26TRCV00883

 

vs.

 

 

[Tentative] Granted

 

DOLLAR TREES, INC., a Virginia corporation; RAY FOX, an individual; and DOES 1-100, inclusive

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

Defendants.

 

 

 

 

 

 

 

Hearing Date:             August 12, 2026

 

Moving Parties:          Defendant Dollar Tree

 

Responding Party:      Plaintiff Leo Javier

 

HEARING: Motion to Compel Arbitration

           

The Court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers.

           

RULING

 

            The Court grants the Motion to Compel Arbitration.

           

BACKGROUND

           

            On March 10, 2026, Plaintiff Leo Javier filed the Complaint against Defendants Dollar Tree Stores Inc and Ray Fox alleging causes of action for failure to pay overtime wages, failure to pay minimum wage, failure to provide meal periods, failure to provide rest breaks, failure to pay wages upon termination, failure to issue accurate and itemized wage statements, and unfair business practices.

 

            On May 5, 2026, Defendant filed the Motion to Compel Arbitration.

 

            On July 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Opposition.

 

            On August 5, 2026, Defendant filed the Reply.

LEGAL STANDARD

 

The Federal Arbitration Act (the “FAA”) applies in both federal and state courts to contracts evidencing a transaction involving interstate commerce. (9 U.S.C. §§ 1-2; Southland Corp. v. Keating (1984) 465 U.S. 1, 12.) The FAA preempts conflicting state law. (Preston v. Ferrer (2008) 552 U.S. 346, 353.) The party that contends the FAA applies bears the burden to demonstrate that the arbitration agreement is in a “contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce.” (Woolls v. Super. Ct. (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 197, 211.) 

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2, “[o]n petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party thereto refuses to arbitrate such controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists unless it determines that: (a) The right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or (b)¿Grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement.”¿ 

 

DISCUSSION

 

The instant motion is based on an arbitration agreement found in Plaintiff’s 2014 onboarding process and an updated October 2020 agreement.

 

Existence of Valid Arbitration Agreement and Claims Covered by Arbitration Clause¿ 

 

“[T]he petitioner bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence…” (Giuliano v. Inland Empire Personnel, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1276, 1284.) “In determining whether an arbitration agreement applies to a specific dispute, the court may examine only the agreement itself and the complaint filed by the party refusing arbitration [citation]. The court should attempt to give effect to the parties’ intentions, in light of the usual and ordinary meaning of the contractual language and the circumstances under which the agreement was made.” (Weeks v. Crow (1980) 113 Cal.App.3d 350, 353.) “To determine whether a contractual arbitration clause requires arbitration of a particular controversy, the controversy is first identified, and the issue is whether that controversy is within the scope of the contractual arbitration clause.” (Titolo v. Cano (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 310, 316.) “Doubts as to whether an arbitration clause applies to a particular dispute are to be resolved in favor of sending the parties to arbitration. The court should order them to arbitrate unless it is clear that the arbitration clause cannot be interpreted to cover the dispute.” (California Correctional Peace Officers Ass’n v. State (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 198, 205.) 

 

“[A] party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense. [Citation.] In these summary proceedings, the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Giuliano, supra, 149 Cal.App.4th at p. 1284.) 

           

“The burden of persuasion is always on the moving party to prove the existence of an arbitration agreement with the opposing party by a preponderance of the evidence: Because the existence of the agreement is a statutory prerequisite to granting the [motion or] petition, the [party seeking arbitration] bears the burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence.” (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.) However, the burden is a three-step framework. “First, the moving party bears the burden of producing prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy. The moving party can meet its initial burden by attaching to the [motion or] petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party's] signature. Alternatively, the moving party can meet its burden by setting forth the agreement's provisions in the motion.” (Ibid.) “For this step, it is not necessary to follow the normal procedures of document authentication.” (Ibid.)

 

“If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then in the second step, the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement.” (Ibid.) “The opposing party can do this in several ways. For example, the opposing party may testify under oath or declare under penalty of perjury that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement.” (Ibid.)

 

Last, “if the opposing party meets its burden of producing evidence, then in the third step, the moving party must establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties.” (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff argues that Defendant cannot compel arbitration because the parties never formed a valid agreement to arbitrate. Plaintiff maintains that Defendant has not met its initial burden, and that even if it had, his declaration that he never saw and does not remember signing the agreement shifts the burden back to Defendant to establish a valid agreement with admissible evidence.

 

Plaintiff argues that Defendant must satisfy basic contract formation principles and authenticate the agreement before its contents may be received in evidence, and that there is a critical gap in the evidence because Defendant has not established that any agreement to arbitrate exists. (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 841-845, 848.) Plaintiff contends that the declarations of Ashley Carter and Jonathan Cobb are conclusory and lack personal knowledge, describing only what Plaintiff was required to do and the step-by-step onboarding process, without stating that either declarant was present for the execution of the agreement. Plaintiff argues that a witness may not testify absent personal knowledge, which requires the capacity to perceive and to recall, and that a bare statement of personal knowledge unsupported by facts should be ignored.

 

Plaintiff further argues that Defendant has not shown that his signature is valid because Defendant presented no document signed by Plaintiff and the signature was purportedly affixed electronically. Plaintiff contends that an electronic signature may be attributed to a person only if it was the act of that person, and that where a party denies or does not recall signing, the proponent must prove authenticity by a preponderance of the evidence. Plaintiff argues that Defendant never explains how the digital signature of LEO S JAVIER was placed on Exhibit A or how it was an act attributable only to Plaintiff.

 

In reply, Defendant argues that Plaintiff conceded the delegation clauses in both Arbitration Agreements are enforceable by failing to address that both agreements delegate to the arbitrator exclusive authority over any dispute as to their interpretation, validity, applicability, unconscionability, or enforceability. Defendant contends that where the parties clearly and unmistakably delegate gateway issues to the arbitrator, a court has no power to consider them, that California enforces such provisions, and that unless Plaintiff challenged the delegation provision specifically, it must be treated as valid and enforced, leaving any challenge to the agreement as a whole for the arbitrator. (Henry Schein, Inc. v. Archer & White Sales, Inc. (2019) 586 U.S. 63, 68.) Defendant therefore argues the Court should grant the motion and leave the validity and unconscionability questions to the arbitrator.

 

Defendant further argues that Plaintiff agreed to both agreements. As to the 2020 Agreement, Defendant contends Plaintiff's failure to address it concedes that it is valid, enforceable, and covers his claims. Defendant also argues that its October 23, 2020 mailing of the agreement creates a presumption of receipt that holds absent believable contrary evidence, and that unlike a plaintiff who at least denied receipt, Plaintiff offers no testimony about the mailer at all, nor any evidence to counter Defendant's notice through his wage statements, posted store notice, and his continued employment through February 10, 2025.

 

Defendant contends that it need only prove agreement formation by a preponderance of the evidence, and that it did so as to the 2014 Agreement with a copy electronically signed by Plaintiff. (Rosenthal v. Great W. Fin. Sec. Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413.) Defendant contends that Ms. Carter need not have witnessed the signing because a corporate affiant may testify from business records to acts she did not observe, and that her testimony and the Votta declaration establish an onboarding process requiring associates to review the full agreement and re-enter a confidential password to affix a dated digital signature saved to a secure vendor database. Last, Defendant contends that the burden of authenticating an electronic signature is not great and that a unique, secure username and password may suffice. (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 844.)

 

Here, Defendant has provided signed copies of both the 2014 and 2020 agreements, which both included an arbitration agreement. (Carter Decl. Exhibits A and B.) Thus, the Court finds that Defendant has passed its initial burden. In response, Plaintiff has provided his own declaration stating that he does not remember signing the 2014 agreement. (Javier Decl. ¶ 2-3.) However, Plaintiff has not provided any evidence disputing the 2020 agreement. Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiff has not met his burden and Defendant has shown the existence of a valid arbitration agreement.

 

Furthermore, the 2020 agreement includes the language, “The arbitrator shall have the exclusive authority to resolve any disputes or claims regarding arbitrability or the formation, interpretation, validity, applicability, unconscionability, or enforceability of this Agreement.” (Carter Decl. Exhibit B.) While this provision does not stop the Court from analyzing the existence of the claimed agreement, it does prohibit the Court from analyzing Plaintiff’s other arguments, including unconscionability. (Tiri v. Lucky Chances, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 231, 242.)

 

Therefore, the Court grants the Motion to Compel Arbitration.

JUDICIAL NOTICE

           

The Court may take judicial notice of records of (1) any court of this state or (2) any court of record of the United States or of any state of the United States. (Evid. Code § 452 subd. (d).) The Court may also take judicial notice of “Official acts of the legislative, executive, and judicial departments of the United States and of any state of the United States.” (Evid. Code § 452 subd. (c).)

 

            Defendant requests that the Court take judicial notice of true and correct copies of publicly available images on the Google Maps website which show the location 10211 Long Beach Boulevard, Lynwood, California 90262 as it appeared in February 2021. These images were obtained on July 9, 2026, by first accessing the website www.google.com/maps.

 

            The Court denies Defendants' request for judicial notice as “simply because information is on the Internet does not mean that it is not reasonably subject to dispute.” (Huitt v. Southern California Gas Co. (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 1586, 1650 fn.10.) Furthermore, while the Court has taken judicial notice of maps from Google Maps, the Court has generally drawn the line at photographs taken from Google Maps. (In re Gary F. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1076, 1078 fn. 2.)

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 

53 Cal.4th 861, 872.) For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.) A demurrer “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.) 

 

A pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous or unintelligible. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) A demurrer for uncertainty may lie if the failure to label the parties and claims renders the complaint so confusing defendant cannot tell what he or she is supposed to respond to. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2.) However, “[a] demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)

           

 

 

 

 

DISCUSSION

           

Meet and Confer 

 

Before filing a demurrer, “the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) 

 

            On July 8, 2026, the parties conferred via email regarding the instant demurrer. Therefore, the court finds that Defendants have satisfied the meet and confer requirements.

 

            Statute of Limitations

 

Defendants contend that the entire Complaint is untimely under the 3-year statute of limitations for fraud. Because the applicable limitations period turns on the gravamen of each cause of action, Defendants argue that the Court must look to the true nature of Plaintiffs' claims rather than their labels. (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) Plaintiffs assert eight causes of action, six expressly labeled as fraud and two more that sound in fraud because they incorporate and arise from the allegations that Defendants fraudulently induced the Opham contracts, falsely promised to perform them, and concealed the St. Francis facility. On that reading, the gravamen of every count is fraud, so Defendants maintain that a three-year limitations period governs the entire Complaint. (Vera v. REL-BC, LLC (2021) 66 Cal.App.5th 57, 64-68.)

 

Defendants further argue that a fraud claim accrues, and the limitations period begins to run, when the plaintiff suspected or should have suspected that an injury was caused by wrongdoing. (Vera, supra, 66 Cal.App.5th 57, 68-69.) They emphasize that the period starts once a plaintiff has information that would put a reasonable person on inquiry, that the plaintiff need not be aware of the specific facts necessary to establish a claim, and that wrong and wrongdoing are understood in their lay rather than legal sense, so that a fraud claim accrues even without actual knowledge when the plaintiff knows facts that should raise suspicion and trigger a further investigation. (Id. at 69.)

 

Applying that standard, Defendants point to the St. Francis facility, which they describe as the admitted heart of Plaintiffs' claim that Opham was abandoned in bad faith for improper and fraudulent reasons. Defendants argue that by no later than early 2021 Plaintiffs were on notice and reasonably should have suspected the wrongdoing they now allege, namely that DaVita was acquiring the Compton Facility's assets and establishing the Opham business while, at the same time, operating a competing venture at the nearby St. Francis facility. Defendants add that the OA itself states that TRC and its affiliates could operate competing dialysis centers. They further note that Dr. Hon is a local nephrologist who treats patients at six DaVita dialysis centers in south Los Angeles within a five-mile radius of the Opham facility, so he could and should have discovered the nearby St. Francis facility in early 2021. (Compl. ¶ 19.)

 

Because the St. Francis facility was open, publicly visible, and discoverable in early 2021, and because Plaintiffs did not bring their claims within three or even four years after that date, Defendants conclude that the entire Complaint is untimely and that dismissal is required.

 

            In opposition, Plaintiffs respond that the Demurrer wrongly assumes that discoverability of the St. Francis facility starts the limitations period, and they argue that a claim instead accrues only when the cause of action is complete with all of its elements of wrongdoing, harm, and causation. (People v. Experian Data Corp. (2024) 106 Cal.App.5th 799, 807-808.) They contend their claims did not accrue until Defendants decided to abandon the Opham unit, because there was no damage before then, and Defendants did not disclose the abandonment until June 29, 2023.

 

Plaintiffs argue the gravamen is tortious conduct, namely fraud and breach of fiduciary duty. (City of Atascadero v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 445, 482.) They note the three-year fraud period runs only from discovery of the facts constituting the fraud, and that the discovery rule postpones accrual until the plaintiff discovers or should discover the fraud. They add that breach of fiduciary duty carries a four-year period likewise subject to the discovery rule. (Jogani v. Jogani (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 823, 856.)

 

Plaintiffs further argue the limitations issue is normally a question of fact for the trier of fact, becoming one of law only when reasonable minds can reach a single conclusion, which they say is not the case here. They contend what matters is when Plaintiffs actually knew or reasonably suspected Defendants' fraudulent intent, not what Defendants believe Plaintiffs should have known, and that Dr. Hon did not learn of St. Francis until in or about February 2025 and had no cause to suspect misconduct until the June 29, 2023 abandonment. The signage and proximity mean nothing, they argue, if Dr. Hon never saw or knew of the facility, and the demurrer offers no contrary evidence.

 

The statute of limitations for fraud is 3 years. (Code Civ. Proc., § 338 subd. (d).) “Generally speaking, a cause of action accrues at the time when the cause of action is complete with all of its elements.” (Fox v. Ethicon Endo-Surgery, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 797, 806.)  “An important exception to the general rule of accrual is the discovery rule, which postpones accrual of a cause of action until the plaintiff discovers, or has reason to discover, the cause of action… A plaintiff has reason to discover a cause of action when he or she has reason at least to suspect a factual basis for its elements.” (Fox, supra, 35 Cal.4th 797, 807.) “A plaintiff whose complaint shows on its face that his claim would be barred without the benefit of the discovery rule must specifically plead facts to show (1) the time and manner of discovery and (2) the inability to have made earlier discovery despite reasonable diligence.” (Id. at 808)

 

            Here the Complaint alleges that “in or about the year 2021, the DaVita Defendants entered into a plan, agreement, arrangement and conspiracy to further dominate the renal dialysis business as to which they hold a thirty (35) percent market share by fraudulently acquiring renal dialysis facilities based on the intentional misrepresentation and active concealment of the fact that the DaVita co-conspirators would continue to operate the acquired facilities and would retain one or more of the physicians who were nephrologists to act as the medical directors for such acquired facilities.” (Complaint ¶ 17.) The Complaint also alleges that in July 2021, Defendants and Plaintiffs entered into an agreement for Defendants to purchase Plaintiffs' dialysis facilities. (Complaint ¶ 26-32.)  Last, the Complaint alleges that Defendants intentionally misled Plaintiffs into believing that Defendant would “take all reasonable and necessary steps to develop Opham’s business” and that “had Plaintiffs known the actual facts, they would not have entered into the Contribution Agreement, the Operating Agreement or the Medical Director Agreement or made the capital contribution to Opham.” (Complaint ¶ 50, 53.) Thus, the Court finds that all the alleged elements of fraud were present in 2021, as Defendants' misrepresentation, Plaintiff’s reliance in entering the contracts, and damages were present. While Plaintiffs argue damages did not occur until the abandonment of the facility, the damages occurred when the contract was entered into, as it was allegedly entered into based on fraud. (See, WA Southwest 2, LLC v. First Am. Title Ins. Co. (2015) 240 Cal.App.4th 148, 156.) Consequently, the Complaint is untimely on its face, and the Court must look to the delayed discovery rule.

 

            The Complaint’s discovery allegations read, “In or about February 2025, Plaintiffs discovered, for the first time, that on or about March 29, 2021, Defendant Palo had opened a dialysis center known as St. Francis Dialysis located at 10211 Long Beach, Boulevard, Lynwood, California 90262 (the “St. Francis Dialysis Center”), approximately three and half (3.5) miles away from the Opham facility.” (Complaint ¶ 20.) “The existence of the St. Francis Dialysis Center and its processing of licensing applications was within the exclusive knowledge of the DaVita Defendants and could not have been discovered by Plaintiffs. In addition, it was not until on or about June 29, 2023 that the DaVita Defendants informed Plaintiffs that the Opham facility was being abandoned.” (Complaint ¶ 22.)

           

Thus, while the Complaint alleges that the existence of the St. Francis center was within the exclusive knowledge of Defendants and could not have been discovered by Plaintiffs, allegations under the discovery rule must be pled with particularity, and “could not have been discovered by Plaintiffs” is conclusory. Thus, Plaintiffs have failed to plead that earlier discovery was not possible with particularity. (Complaint ¶ 22; Fox, supra, 35 Cal.4th 797, 808.) Thus, the delayed discovery allegations are deficient, and the claims are untimely.

 

Last, the Court also finds that the breach of implied covenant and fiduciary duty claims sound in fraud, as they are based on the same factual misrepresentations, and thus are also governed by the three-year statute of limitations. (City of Vista v. Robert Thomas Securities, Inc. (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th 882, 889.)

 

Therefore, the Court sustains the Demurrer with leave to amend.
