LEGAL STANDARDS



 



A.    
Issue
Sanctions 



 



Code of Civil
Procedure section 2023.030, subd. (b), provides:



 



To the extent authorized by the
chapter governing any particular discovery method or any other provision of
this title, the court, after notice to any affected party, person, or attorney,
and after opportunity for hearing, may impose the following sanctions against
anyone engaging in conduct that is a misuse of the discovery process:



 



(b) The court
may impose an issue sanction ordering that designated facts shall be taken as
established in the action in accordance with the claim of the party adversely
affected by the misuse of the discovery process. The court may also impose an issue
sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the
discovery process from supporting or opposing designated claims or defenses.



  



B.    
Evidence
Sanctions 



 



Under Code of
Civil Procedure section 2023.030, subd. (c), “The court may impose an evidence
sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the
discovery process from introducing designated matters in evidence.”



 



C.    
Monetary Sanctions



 



i.                   
Section 2023.010



 



Section 2023.010 of the Code of Civil Procedure defines
“misuse of the discovery process” as follows: 



 



Misuses of the discovery process include, but are not
limited to, the following: (a) Persisting, over objection and without
substantial justification, in an attempt to obtain information or materials
that are outside the scope of permissible discovery. (b) Using a discovery
method in a manner that does not comply with its specified procedures. (c) Employing
a discovery method in a manner or to an extent that causes unwarranted
annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense. (d) Failing
to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery. (e) Making,
without substantial justification, an unmeritorious objection to discovery. (f) Making
an evasive response to discovery. (g) Disobeying a court order to provide
discovery. (h) Making or opposing, unsuccessfully and without substantial
justification, a motion to compel or to limit discovery. (i) Failing to
confer or to attempt to confer, in person, by telephone, or by videoconference
with an opposing party or attorney in a reasonable and good faith attempt to
resolve informally any dispute concerning discovery, if the section governing a
particular discovery motion requires the filing of a declaration stating facts
showing that an attempt at informal resolution has been made.



 



ii.                 
Section 2023.030



 



Monetary sanctions are governed generally by section
2023.030 of the Code of Civil Procedure, as follow: 



 



The court may impose a monetary
sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or
any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses,
including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct. The
court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that
another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney
who advised that assertion, or on both. If a monetary sanction is authorized by
any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it
finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification
or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.



 



iii.               
Section 2030.290



 



Monetary sanctions with regard to interrogatories in
particular are authorized by section 2030.290 of the Code of Civil Procedure,
as follows: 



 



The court shall impose a monetary
sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party,
person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a
response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the
sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make
the imposition of the sanction unjust. If a party then fails to obey an order
compelling answers, the court may make those orders that are just, including
the imposition of an issue sanction, an evidence sanction, or a terminating
sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010). In lieu of or in
addition to that sanction, the court may impose a monetary sanction under
Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010).



 



iv.               
Section 2030.300



 



Code of Civil
Procedure section 2030.300, subd. (e) provides for sanctions for violating an
order compelling responses to interrogatories, as follows:



 



If a party then fails to obey an
order compelling further response to interrogatories, the court may make those
orders that are just, including the imposition of an issue sanction, an
evidence sanction, or a terminating sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with
Section 2023.010). In lieu of, or in addition to, that sanction, the court may
impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010).



 



v.                 
Section 2031.300



 



Sanctions with
regard to orders related to Demands for Production are governed by section
2031, subdivision (c) of the Code of Civil Procedure, as follows:



 



Except as provided in subdivision
(d), the court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing
with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who
unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to a demand for inspection,
copying, testing, or sampling, unless it finds that the one subject to the
sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make
the imposition of the sanction unjust. If a party then fails to obey the order
compelling a response, the court may make those orders that are just, including
the imposition of an issue sanction, an evidence sanction, or a terminating
sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010). In lieu of or in
addition to this sanction, the court may impose a monetary sanction under
Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010).



 



D.    Terminating
Sanctions



Finally, Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030, subd.

The court may impose a terminating sanction by one of

D.    Terminating Sanctions



 



Defendant requests terminating sanction in the
alternative “[i]f the Court concludes that lesser sanctions will not secure
compliance.” (Mot., at p. 11.)



 



Terminating sanctions are premature.
“[S]anctions are generally imposed in an incremental approach[.]” (Department
of Forestry & Fire Protection v. Howell (2017) 18 Cal.App.5th 154,
191-192, disapproved of on other grounds in Presbyterian Camp &
Conference Centers, Inc v. Superior Court (2021) 12 Cal.5th 493, 516, fn.
17.) Courts apply discovery sanctions to correct prejudice, not to punish
misbehavior. (See McGinty v. Superior Court (1994) 26 Cal.App.4th 204,
210.) “Discovery sanctions serve to remedy the harm caused to the party
suffering the discovery misconduct. [Citation.] Because discovery sanctions are
not designed to punish, ‘“sanctions should be tailored to serve that remedial
purpose, should not put the moving party in a better position than he would
otherwise have been had he obtained the requested discovery, and should be
proportionate to the offending party's misconduct.”’ [Citation.]” (Kwan
Software Engineering, Inc. v. Hennings (2020) 58 Cal.App.5th 57, 74.) 



 



Based on the foregoing, the Court denies
Defendant’s request for terminating sanctions.

CASE NUMBER:                   25STCV09330 

CASE NAME:                        CHRISTY KRILETICH v. FORD MOTOR COMPANY, et al.

 

MOVING PARTY:                 Defendant Ford Motor Company

OPPOSING PARTY:             Plaintiff Christy Kriletich

PROCEEDING:                      MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT/SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

RULING SUMMARY:          The Motion is denied.

                                                                                                                                                           

 

Background

 

This is an action brought under the Song-Beverly Act. Plaintiff Christy Kriletich (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on July 31, 2017, she entered into a warranty contract with defendant Ford Motor Company (“Defendant”) concerning a 2017 Ford Focus (the “Vehicle”). Plaintiff alleges that, during the express warranty period, defects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves in the Vehicle, substantially impairing the use, value, and/or safety of the Vehicle.

 

 

Defendant moves for summary judgment of Plaintiff’s claims against it or, in the alternative, summary adjudication of the first through fourth and sixth causes of action, which are the only causes of action pled against Defendant.  Plaintiff has opposed the motion. 

MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT/SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

I.         Legal Standard

 

The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.”¿ (Aguilar v. Atl. Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal. 4th 826, 843.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c (c) requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) Summary adjudication may be granted as to one or more causes of action within an action, or one or more claims for damages. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(f).)¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿ 

A defendant moving for summary judgment bears two burdens: (1) the burden of production – presenting admissible evidence, through material facts, sufficient to satisfy a directed verdict standard; and (2) the burden of persuasion – the material facts presented must persuade the court that the plaintiff cannot establish one or more elements of a cause of action, or a complete defense vitiates the cause of action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(p)(2);¿Aguilar,¿supra, 25 Cal.4th at pp. 850-851.) A defendant may satisfy this burden by showing that the claim “cannot be established” because of the lack of evidence on some essential element of the claim.¿¿(Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 574, 590.)¿¿Once the defendant meets this burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a “triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or defense thereto.”¿(Id.)¿

¿ 

“On ruling on a motion for summary judgment, the court is to ‘liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.’ ” (Cheal v. El Camino Hospital¿(2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 736, 760.) On a summary judgment motion, the court must therefore consider what inferences favoring the opposing party a factfinder could reasonably draw from the evidence. While viewing the evidence in this manner, the court must bear in mind that its primary function is to identify issues rather than to determine issues. [Citation.]” (Binder v. Aetna Life Ins. Co.¿(1999) 75 Cal.App.4th¿832, 839.)¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿ 

Defeating summary judgment requires only a single disputed material fact. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(c) [motion for summary judgment “shall be granted if all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law”] [emphasis added].) Thus, any disputed material fact means the court must deny the motion – the court has no discretion to grant summary judgment. (Zavala v. Arce (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 915, 925, fn. 8; Saldana v. Globe-Weis Systems Co. (1991) 233 Cal.App.3d 1505, 1511-1512.) 

 

II.        Discussion

 

A.              Plaintiff’s complaint is not time-barred

 

Defendant moves for summary adjudication of the first through fourth causes of action on the grounds that the complaint is time-barred under Code of Civil Procedure § 871.20.  Defendant contends that under section 871.20, the statute of repose for a Song-Beverly action is one year after the expiration of the express warranty, and therefore Plaintiff’s action is time-barred, as Plaintiff purchased the Vehicle in 2017 and the warranty expired on July 30, 2022. In opposition, Plaintiff contends that section 871.20’s time limits apply only prospectively, not retroactively.

Where a change in remedy is retroactively made in law, such as the shortening of a time limit provision, there must be a reasonable time permitted for the affected party to exercise their remedy before the statute takes effect. (Rosefield Packing Co. v. Superior Court in and for City and County of San Francisco (1935) 4 Cal.2d 120, 122 (Rosefield).) If the statute operates immediately to cut off the existing remedy for a party, then the retroactive application of it is unconstitutional as to that party. (Id. at pp. 122-123.) “In California, statutes of limitations, being procedural, are normally retroactively applied to accrued causes of action; but the court must inquire whether, in a given case, that retrospective application may violate due process by in effect eliminating the plaintiff's right.” (Aronson v. Superior Court (1987) 191 Cal.App.3d 294, 297 (Aronson).)

This action was filed on March 28, 2025. The warranty agreement was entered into on July 31, 2017. (Rogerson Decl., Ex. A.) According to the retail installment sales contract, this is the date on which the Vehicle was purchased. (Rogerson Decl., Ex. A.)  Using this date, the statute of repose would require the instant action to have been filed not later than July 31, 2023, which is before section 871.21’s effective date of January 1, 2025. The enactment of this statute operated to immediately cut off Plaintiff’s existing remedy and, under Rosefield, cannot constitutionally be retroactively applied to the instant action. (See also Aronson, supra, 191 Cal.App.3d at p. 297 [“statutes of limitations, being procedural, are normally retroactively applied to accrued causes of action; but… [i]f no time is left [to file suit], or only an unreasonably short time remains, then the statute cannot be applied at all.].)

Although Aronson and Rosefield directly pertain to statutes of limitation rather than statutes of repose, the same due process concerns are applicable to one as to the other. “Whereas statutes of limitations affect a remedy, statutes of repose extinguish a right of action after the period has elapsed…. The effect of a statute of repose ‘is [thus] harsher than a statute of limitations in that it cuts off a right of action after a specified period of time, irrespective of accrual or even notice that a legal right has been invaded.’ [Citation.]” (PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Internat., Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 156, 177.)  A statute of repose that extinguishes a plaintiff’s right of action immediately upon its effective date based on retroactive application therefore infringes on the plaintiff’s due process rights, as is the case here, as it leaves no reasonable time in which to sue after the effective date of the statute.

“Whether there was a reasonable time in these cases is not a matter committed to the discretion of the trial court. The question is one of constitutionality of the statute which in terms applies to the pending case; and if it appears that there was a reasonable time for exercise of the remedy before the statutory bar became fixed, the lower court cannot consider individual hardship or other circumstances, but must give effect to the express provisions of the law.” (Rosefield, supra, 4 Cal.2d at p. 124.) Correspondingly, where there is not a reasonable time for exercise of the remedy after the amendment’s effective date, it is similarly outside of the court’s discretion whether the statute may be applied, as it is a question of constitutionality of the statute as applied to the case.

 

Defendant contends that there was a reasonable time to file this action before the statute’s effective date; however, that is not the correct test. Whether there was a reasonable time to file the action before the statute’s effective date is irrelevant to whether there was a reasonable time to file after the statute’s effective date and therefore whether the statute may constitutionally be applied.

 

Defendant also contends that the statute applies retroactively, citing the trial court case Pinkney v. FCA US LLC, case number 25STCV19899 (Pinkney). Pinkney does not constitute binding authority. Moreover, the court in Pinkney does not state that the statute applies retroactively as to time to file an action, but as to the opt-in provisions of the statute. As such, Pinkney is inapposite.

 

Plaintiff’s complaint is not time-barred.  Defendant’s motion for summary judgment, as well as Defendant’s motions for summary adjudication of Plaintiff’s first through fourth causes of action, are denied.

 

B.        There is a triable issue of material fact as to the sixth cause of action for fraudulent inducement – concealment

 

Defendant contends that Plaintiff’s fraudulent inducement – concealment cause of action fails as a matter of law because Plaintiff has failed to show that a transmission defect, as alleged in the complaint, existed in the car.

 

The elements of a fraud claim are (1) a misrepresentation; (2) the defendant’s knowledge of the falsity; (3) the intent to induce reliance; (4) justifiable reliance on the defendant’s statements or lack thereof; and (5) resulting damages. (Hoffman v. 162 North Wolfe LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 1178, 1185-1186.)  To demonstrate fraudulent concealment, a plaintiff must show that (1) the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) the defendant concealed a material fact from the plaintiff; or (4) the defendant makes some partial representations but also suppresses some material facts. (Los Angeles Memorial Coliseum Com. v. Insomniac, Inc. (2015) 233 Cal.App.4th 803, 831.)

Fraud in the inducement is a subset of fraud that occurs when a promisor’s consent to an agreement is induced by fraud.  (Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 839 (Dhital).)  

 

In support of its contention, Defendant offers as evidence the declaration of Eric Kemnitz, an engineer employed by Defendant, who states that based on his review of Defendant’s standard claims list and dealership repair orders, there were no presentations, concerns, or repairs related to the Subject Vehicle’s six-speed transmission. (Kemnitz Decl. ¶ 14.) Defendant contends that the existence of the transmission defect constitutes the misrepresentation alleged in the complaint, and that therefore Plaintiff cannot establish the existence of a transmission defect.

 

This evidence does not establish as a matter of law that Plaintiff cannot prove her claim, because evidence that the Vehicle was not taken in for transmission repairs does not establish that a latent defect, of which Plaintiff was not aware at the time of repairs, did not exist in the Vehicle. Plaintiff’s awareness of the defect at the time of purchase or at the time of repairs is immaterial to the existence of the defect or of the cause of action, as indicated by the applicability of the delayed discovery rule to fraud actions.  (See Weatherly v. Universal Music Publishing Group (2004) 125 Cal.App.4th 913.) Defendant has, therefore, failed to carry its burden.

 

Moreover, even if Defendant had carried its burden, Plaintiff testified that the Vehicle experienced problems with changing gears to and from park and reverse, and that Plaintiff informed repair workers of this issue when taking the Vehicle for repairs.  (Lister Decl., Ex. 1, 42:22 – 43:25; 53:9 – 53:25.)  Plaintiff has therefore demonstrated a triable issue of material fact as to the existence of a transmission defect in the Vehicle.  Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication of Plaintiff’s sixth cause of action is denied.

 

Conclusion

 

Defendant’s motion for summary judgment or, in the alternative, summary adjudication is denied.

Background

 

On September 3, 2025, William L. Gonzalez (“Plaintiff”) filed a Complaint against Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC (“JLRNA”) and Galpin Jaguar Lincoln Mercury, Inc. (“Galpin”) (collectively “Defendants”) and Does 1 to 10.

 

On December 11, 2025, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging causes of action for (1) Violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (d); (2) Violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (b); (3) Violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (a)(3); (4) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability (Civ. Code, §§ 1791.1, 1794, 1795.5); (5) Negligent Repair, and (6) Fraudulent Inducement.   

By order on March 26, 2026, the Court sustained Defendants’ demurrer to the fifth cause of action with leave to amend and overruled as to the sixth cause of action.

 

On April 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) alleging the same six causes of action as the FAC.

 

Defendants demur to the fifth cause of action in the SAC. Plaintiff opposes the Motion.

 

LEGAL STANDARDS

 

A.        Demurrer 

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)¿“To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.”¿(C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)¿For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded.¿ (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.)¿A demurrer “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.”¿(Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)¿¿ 

  

B.        Leave to Amend 

 

“Where the defect raised by a motion to strike or by demurrer is reasonably capable of cure, leave to amend is routinely and liberally granted to give the plaintiff a chance to cure the defect in question.” (CLD Construction, Inc. v. City of San Ramon (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 1141, 1146.) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)¿¿¿ 

 

Demurrer[1]

 

I.         Discussion

 

Defendants demur to the fifth cause of action for negligent repair pleaded against Galpin, on the grounds that the claim is barred by the economic loss rule and the pleading is deficient.

¿ 

“The elements of a cause of action for negligence are well established. They are (a) a legal duty to use due care; (b) a breach of such legal duty; [and] (c) the breach as the proximate or legal cause of the resulting injury.” (Ladd v. County of San Mateo¿(1996) 12 Cal.4th 913, 917 [internal quotations omitted].) The existence of a legal duty is a question of law for the court to decide. (Adams v. City of Fremont¿(1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 243, 265.) “However, the elements of breach of that duty and causation are ordinarily questions of fact for the jury's determination.” (Vasquez v. Residential Investments, Inc. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 269, 278.) “One who undertakes repairs has a duty arising in tort to do them without negligence.” (Southwest Forest Industries, Inc. v. Westinghouse Elec. Corp.¿(9th Cir. 1970) 422 F.2d 1013, 1020.)¿¿ 

 

Plaintiff alleges the following: On October 23, 2023, Plaintiff entered into a warranty contract with JLRNA for a 2023 Land Rover Range Rover Sport (the “Vehicle”) that JLRNA manufactured and/or distributed. (SAC ¶ 7.) The contract pertained to bumper-bumper, powertrain, and emission warranties. (SAC ¶ 8.) The Vehicle was purchased from Defendant Galpin, an authorized retail dealership. (SAC ¶¶ 9-10.) Plaintiff was not advised prior to purchase of the Vehicle’s 3.0-liter engine’s defects. (SAC ¶ 11.) The Vehicle experiences defects and non-conformities to warranty including, but not limited to, “hesitation, overheating, a malfunction indicator light, the infotainment screen goes black, jerking, difficulty connecting or remaining connected to smart devices, and the automatic side steps deploy and retract improperly.” (SAC ¶ 14.) These defects have substantially impaired the use, value, and/or safety of the Vehicle and have rendered the Vehicle worthless. (SAC ¶¶ 19-20.)  “Defendant JLRNA and its representatives in this state have been unable to service or repair the Vehicle to conform to the applicable express warranties after a reasonable number of opportunities.”  (SAC ¶ 34.) 

 

Plaintiff also alleges that the causes of action in the SAC “arise out of the warranty obligations of JLRNA in connection with a motor vehicle for which JLRNA issued a written warranty”  (SAC ¶ 17), and that “. . . Defendant JLRNA and its representative failed to commence the service or repairs within a reasonable time and failed to service or repair the Subject Vehicle so as to conform to the applicable warranties within 30 days . . .” ((SAC ¶ 40). 

 

In the fifth cause of action, specifically, Plaintiff alleges that: “Plaintiff delivered the Subject Vehicle to Defendant GALPIN for substantial repair on at least one occasion;” that “Defendant GALPIN owed a duty to Plaintiff to use ordinary care and skill in storage, preparation and repair of the Subject Vehicle in accordance with industry standards;” that “Defendant GALPIN breached its duty to Plaintiff to use ordinary care and skill by failing to properly store, prepare and repair the Subject Vehicle in accordance with industry standards;” and that “Defendant GALPIN's negligent breach of its duties owed to Plaintiff was a proximate cause of Plaintiff's damages.” (SAC ¶¶ 53-56.)

 

The Court notes that these allegations are identical to those alleged in the FAC generally and as to the fifth cause of action. (FAC ¶¶ 7-11, 14, 19-20, 52-55.) The only difference in the relevant sections is the addition of paragraph 26: “Plaintiff is entitled to recover any ‘cover’ damages under Civil Code, section 1794, et seq.”

 

Plaintiff argues that the SAC meets the burden for alleging negligent repair. (Opp., at p. 3.) He asserts that Galpin’s argument that the SAC completely disregards the March 26, 2026 Order sustaining the demurrer to the FAC is “mistaken.” (Opp., at p. 3.) Plaintiff points to paragraphs 53 to 56 in support of this contention. (Id.)  But, as the Court noted above, these paragraphs and allegations are identical to those alleged in the FAC’s fifth cause of action. (SAC ¶¶ 53-56; FAC ¶¶ 52-55.) The Court sustained Defendants’ demurrer to the FAC’s fifth cause of action in its March 26, 2026 Order, and found:

 

“The Court agrees that the fifth cause’ [sic] of action is conclusory and devoid of facts explaining how the Subject Vehicle was damaged and why the repair was negligent. The FAC fails to allege facts as to what damages Plaintiff sustained due to Defendant Galpin’s failure to ‘properly store, prepare and repair the Subject Vehicle’ and its storage, preparation, and repair work deviated from ‘industry standards.’ (FAC ¶¶ 53, 54.)”

 

As Plaintiff copy and pasted the same allegations from the FAC, which this Court already found insufficient to survive a demurrer into the SAC, the Court’s prior ruling regarding the insufficiency of the pleading remains the same. 

 

The Court sustains the demurrer to the fifth cause of action.  Because Plaintiff failed to allege facts to address the problems identified in the Court’s prior ruling sustaining the demurrer to the FAC with leave to amend, despite having had to opportunity to do so, the Court does not grant further leave to amend in this ruling.[2] ¿¿
