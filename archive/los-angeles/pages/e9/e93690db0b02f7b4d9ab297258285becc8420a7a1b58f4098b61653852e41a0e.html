 [TENTATIVE] RULING RE:

Motion for Instructions re Settlement Agreement

 

Background

 

            On May 14, 2024, Plaintiff Bespoke Funding 2021A LLC (“Plaintiff”) commenced this contractual fraud action against Defendants Falcon International Corp.; Falcon California, Inc.; Falcon Brands, Inc.; Coastal Harvest II, LLC; V1 Perez, LLC; G1 Perez, LLC; Industrial Court L11, LLC (Manufacturing); Industrial Court L11, LLC (Distribution, Cathedral City); Industrial Court L11, LLC (Distribution, Vernon); Steve Gutterman; and DOES 1 through 10.

 

On September 13, 2024, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants Falcon International Corp.; Falcon California, Inc.; Falcon Brands, Inc.; Coastal Harvest II, LLC; V1 Perez, LLC; G1 Perez, LLC; Industrial Court L11, LLC (Manufacturing); Industrial Court L11, LLC (Distribution, Cathedral City); Industrial Court L11, LLC (Distribution, Vernon); Steve Gutterman; Monterey Ocean Growth-GH, LLC; Base Camp Cannabis Company, LLC; PCC Cathedral City, LLC; A1 Canyon, LLC; Atwater Commerce, LLC; and DOES 1 through 10 (collectively, “Defendants”). The FAC asserts causes of action for: (1) Breach of Loan Agreement; (2) Money Lent; (3) Breach of Guaranty; (4) Breach of Indemnification; (5) Fraud; (6) Promissory Fraud; and (7) Conversion.

 

On October 15, 2024, Falcon Brands, Inc.; Coastal Harvest II, LLC; V1 Perez, LLC; G1 Perez, LLC; Industrial Court L11, LLC (Manufacturing); Industrial Court L11, LLC (Distribution, Cathedral City); Industrial Court L11, LLC (Distribution, Vernon); Base Camp Cannabis Company, LLC; A1 Canyon, LLC; and Steve Gutterman (jointly, “Falcon Defendants”) filed their Answer to the FAC.

 

On November 27, 2024, Plaintiff filed an Ex Parte Application for Appointment of Receiver and Issuance of Preliminary Injunction, which was granted on December 3, 2024.

 

On May 13, 2026, Court-Appointed Receiver Ryan C. Baker (“Baker”) filed the instant Motion for Instructions re Settlement Agreement. No opposition has been filed.

 

Legal Standard

 

      “The receiver has, under the control of the Court, power to bring and defend actions in his own name, as receiver; to take and keep possession of the property, to receive rents, collect debts, to compound for and compromise the same, to make transfers, and generally to do such acts respecting the property as the Court may authorize.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 568.)

 

      “The function of the receiver is to aid the court in preserving and managing the property involved in a particular lawsuit for the benefit of those to whom it can ultimately be determined to belong.” (City of Sierra Madre v. SunTrust Mortgage, Inc. (2019) 32 Cal.App.5th 648, 656.) “A receiver is an officer of the court and is subject to the court’s continuing control; a receiver only has those powers granted to it by statute or an order of the court.” (Id.) “The receiver, acting for the court, is not the agent of any party but acts for the benefit of all holding an interest in the receivership property.” (Id.) For example, “a receiver has no right or power to make a contract binding the property or fund in his custody without the authority or approval of the court and that an unauthorized agreement of a receiver does not bind the court in charge of the receivership,….” (Nulaid Farmers Ass'n v. LaTorre (1967) 252 Cal.App.2d 788, 793.)

 

Analysis

 

            Baker contends that the proposed Settlement Agreement (“Settlement”) between Falcon Defendants and Terry William Ireland, Trustee of the T.W. Ireland Revocable Living Trust DTD 4/28/92 (“Ireland”) is in the best interests of the receivership estate, creditors, and other parties. (Baker Decl., ¶¶2, 3, 6, Ex. A.) Specifically, Baker states that the Settlement was negotiated at arm’s length and with assistance of counsel. (Id., ¶4.) Furthermore, reasonable steps were taken to investigate and confirm there is no collusion between Ireland and First Western Holding, LLC (Falcon Defendants’ landlord) including its related entities, and/or James Kunevicius (“Kunevicius”). (Id.) Moreover, the Settlement amount of $75,000.00 resolves the threatened claims at a fraction of Ireland’s demand, which consists of: (1) $287,419.00 for fair market “use and occupancy” rent and (2) $32,800.00 in debris-removal charges. (Id., ¶5.) As such, the Settlement preserves substantial assets of the receivership estate for the benefits of the creditors and other parties. (Id.) Likewise, the Settlement will avoid the uncertainty, expense, and delay of litigation. (Id., ¶6.)

 

            Finally, there is no objection and/or opposition by Plaintiff or other parties who have appeared in this action, therefore, they concede on whether or not the Settlement is in the best interests of the receivership estate, creditors, and other parties. (See California Rules of Court Rule 3.1113(a); see also Rule 3.1320(f); Herzberg v. County of Plumas (2005) 133 Cal.App.4th 1, 20 [the party who fails to argue an issue abandons that issue].)

 

Conclusion

            Based on the foregoing, Court-Appointed Receiver Ryan C. Baker’s Motion for Instructions re Settlement Agreement is GRANTED.

 

Court-Appointed Receiver Ryan C. Baker is ordered to enter into and perform in accordance with the proposed Settlement Agreement.

Superior Court of California

KOUROSH MAGHEREFETEH,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

MARKELLA THANOU, et al.,

                        Defendants.

  Case No.:  25SMCV01038

  Hearing Date:  07/01//26

  Trial Date:  None

 [TENTATIVE] RULING RE:

Defendant’s Motion for Reconsideration of the Court’s 5/21/26 Order Denying Defendant’s “Motion for Relief from Default and Vacate Default and Order of 12/3/25”

 

Background

 

This is a breach of contract case arising from a failure to pay rent. Plaintiff Kouroush Magherefeteh (Plaintiff) entered a lease for residential property with Defendants Markella Thanou (Thanou) and Vasileios Palaioroutas (Palaioroutas). Plaintiff alleges the defendants failed to pay rent when due and damaged the property before surrendering possession.

 

On June 1, 2026, Palaioroutas filed the instant motion for reconsideration of the Court’s ruling on May 21, 2026, denying a motion that the defendant filed on April 23, 2026, seeking relief from default and vacation of prior court order.

 

On June 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition to the motion for reconsideration.

 

As of June 29, 2026, no reply in support of the motion for reconsideration has been filed.  

 

Motion for Reconsideration Standard

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1008 (“Section 1008”): “When an application for an order has been made to a judge, or to a court, and refused in whole or in part, or granted, or granted conditionally, or on terms, any party affected by the order may, within 10 days after service upon the party of written notice of entry of the order and based upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, make application to the same judge or court that made the order, to reconsider the matter and modify, amend, or revoke the prior order.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a).)

 

“A motion made in accordance with section 1008 must include reference to new or different facts, circumstances, or law before the earlier order may be reconsidered.” (In re Marriage of Oropallo (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 997, 1002.) “[A] party must provide a satisfactory explanation for failing to offer the evidence in the first instance.” (In re Marriage of Herr (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1463, 1468 [internal citations omitted].)

 

“According to the plain language of the statute, a court acts in excess of jurisdiction when it grants a motion to reconsider that is not based upon ‘new or different facts, circumstances, or law.’” (Gilberd v. AC Transit (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 1494, 1500.)

 

Analysis

 

Addressing the merits of the motion, the Court sets forth below a summary of the procedural history of this case.

 

Relevant Procedural History

 

The party moving for reconsideration, Defendant Palaioroutas, was initially defaulted on July 21, 2025.

 

Palaioroutas then filed a motion to quash service of summons on September 29, 2025, arguing that he was never properly served with any court papers, including the request for entry of default.

 

On October 24, 2025, the Court held a hearing on the motion to quash and stated that it had initially intended to hold an evidentiary hearing given the parties’ conflicting testimony. However, because Plaintiff failed to appear at the hearing on Palaioroutas’s motion to quash, the Court entered a final order granting the motion to quash (the “October 24 Ruling”). Although the ruling did not state whether the default against Palaioroutas had been vacated, the Register of Actions reflects that the default was vacated on the day of the hearing. Indeed, a default entered due to lack of service was void. (See Ellard v. Conway (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 540, 544 [“[A] default judgment [or default] entered against a defendant who was not served with a summons in the manner prescribed by statute is void. [Citation]”].) 

 

On November 4, 2025, Plaintiff filed a motion to set aside the October 24 Ruling, arguing that he did not appear at the hearing on October 24, 2025, because he was never served with the moving papers for the motion to quash.

 

On December 1, 2025, the Court held the hearing on Plaintiff’s motion to set aside the October 24 Ruling. Only Plaintiff appeared at that hearing. The Court granted the motion, including Plaintiff’s request to place the evidentiary hearing back on the calendar (the “December 1 Ruling”). The Court stated: “Evidentiary Hearing 402 EC is scheduled for 12/03/2025 at 8:30 AM in Department 205 at Beverly Hills Courthouse.”

 

The December 1 Ruling ordered Plaintiff to give notice of the ruling “via mail,” a fact that is relevant, as later shown in this ruling.

 

On December 3, 2025, the Court held the evidentiary hearing on Palaioroutas’s motion to quash, but because the defendant failed to appear, the Court denied the motion (the “December 3 Ruling”). However, the December 3 Ruling did not state whether the default entered on July 21, 2025, was still in effect.

 

On December 10, 2025, Palaioroutas filed an Answer to the Complaint.

 

On March 9, 2026, the Court issued a non-appearance Court Order Re Setting of Order to Show Cause for Entry of Default Judgment. The Court stated that defendants remained in default status. Accordingly, the Court set an “Order to Show Cause Re: Entry of Default Judgment … for 07/01/2026 at 8:30 AM in Department 205 at Beverly Hills Courthouse.” The Court ordered the clerk to give notice to Plaintiff.

 

On April 23, 2026, Palaioroutas filed a “Motion for Relief from Default and Vacate Default and Order of 12/3/25” (the “April 23 Motion”), arguing for an order “vacat[ing] the default of July 21, 2025, on grounds that service did not occur, and to vacate the ruling of 12/3/25 denying the motion to quash on grounds that Defendant was not properly served [with the notice of the hearing] and thereby did not receive notice of the hearing to attend [on December 3, 2025].” (April 23 Motion, p. 19:19-23.)

 

On May 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition to the April 23 Motion.

 

According to the papers submitted in support of that opposition, Plaintiff had served Palaioroutas with notice of the December 1 Ruling “via email” instead of “via mail” as ordered by the Court. (See Opposition filed May 12, 2026, Declaration of Dennis P. Block, ¶ 7(i); Exh. 12 – a copy of the Notice of Ruling, p. 2 [showing that a person named HG Long served the notice of the December 1 Ruling on defendants at [email protected] instead of via mail].)

 

On May 21, 2026, the Court held the hearing on Palaioroutas’s April 23 Motion and issued a Minute Order stating that “[t]he motion is denied for the reasons set forth in the Opposition filed by Plaintiff” (the “May 21 Ruling”).

 

On June 1, 2026, Palaioroutas filed the instant motion for reconsideration of the May 21 Ruling, arguing that “the Court erroneously concluded that the Answer [the defendant had filed on December 10, 2025] was precluded by a default, and new facts and circumstances therefore exist to warrant reconsideration.” (Motion for Reconsideration, p. 1:20-22.)

 

On June 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed a default judgment packet against Defendants Palaioroutas and Thanou.

 

On June 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition to the instant motion for reconsideration.

 

On June 17, 2026, Palaioroutas filed an opposition to the Order to Show Cause Re Entry of Default Judgment, which is set for hearing on the same day as the motion on July 1, 2026. That opposition argues that Defendant Thanou passed away on December 8, 2024, three months before this lawsuit was initiated in 2025, service of summons on Palaioroutas was improper, and that Palaioroutas is in the process of securing formal legal counsel.

 

Analysis

Palaioroutas argues that, although the Court denied his April 23 Motion for the reasons set forth in Plaintiff’s opposition, the opposition “did not contend that the Answer was precluded by the prior default …,” and the Register of Actions shows that the default entered on July 21, 2025, was vacated. (See Motion for Reconsideration, Palaioroutas Decl., pp. 3-4 [providing a printout of the Register of Actions, which shows that the default was vacated on October 24, 2025, when the Court granted the defendant’s motion to set aside the default].) According to the defendant, “Plaintiff misled the court into thinking that the default was still in place, but knew that it wasn’t. That is why [the defendant’s] motion to quash was allowed to be filed after July 21, 2025.” (Id. at p. 3:9-11.) Therefore, the defendant asks the Court to vacate the default and allow him to resume his defense. (Id. at p. 2:10-11.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues, among other things, that there are no new circumstances that warrant reconsideration because the Register of Actions was available to the defendant before the hearing on May 21, 2026, and could, therefore, have been presented with his original motion. (Opposition, p. 2:12-14.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 1008 does not permit a disappointed litigant to withhold or overlook available court-record information, then use that same information as a basis to relitigate an adverse ruling.” (Id. at p. 2:15-17.)

Plaintiff also argues: “The procedural history had been litigated repeatedly: default was entered; Defendant moved to quash; Defendant failed to appear; Defendant refiled the motion to quash; the October 24 order granting quash was vacated; the motion to quash was denied on December 3; Defendant filed an Answer while default remained in place; and the Court later noted that Defendants remained in default status. Defendant cannot claim surprise from the Court’s consideration of the very procedural issue that his own motion required the Court to address.” (Opposition, pp. 8:12-9:2.)

Lastly, Plaintiff argues that even assuming that the Court were to grant reconsideration, Defendant has still not established grounds for changing the Court’s May 21 Ruling denying his “Motion for Relief from Default and Vacate Default and Order of 12/3/25.” (Opposition, p. 5:5-6.)

Even if the Court were to agree with Plaintiff that Palaioroutas has not presented “new or different facts, circumstances, or law” warranting reconsideration of the May 21 Ruling under Section 1008, the Court can, on its own authority and upon realizing an error in the prior ruling, choose to reconsider the May 21 Ruling. (See Boschetti v. Pacific Bay Investments Inc. (2019) 32 Cal.App.5th 1059, 1070 [“Our Supreme Court has made clear that, while Code of Civil Procedure section 1008 prohibits a party from making a renewed motion not based on new facts or law, it does “not limit a court's ability to reconsider its previous interim orders on its own motion, as long as it gives the parties notice that it may do so and a reasonable opportunity to litigate the question.” (Le Francois v. Goel (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1094, 1096–1097, 29 Cal.Rptr.3d 249, 112 P.3d 636 (Le Francois).) The court explained, ‘We cannot prevent a party from communicating the view to a court that it should reconsider a prior ruling .... [I]t should not matter whether the “judge has an unprovoked flash of understanding in the middle of the night” [citation] or acts in response to a party’s suggestion. If a court believes one of its prior interim orders was erroneous, it should be able to correct that error no matter how it came to acquire that belief.’ (Id. at p. 1108, 29 Cal.Rptr.3d 249, 112 P.3d 636; see Minick v. City of Petaluma (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 15, 34, 207 Cal.Rptr.3d 350 [‘Trial courts always have discretion to revisit interim orders in service of the paramount goal of fair and accurate decisionmaking’]; In re Marriage of Barthold (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1301, 1308, 70 Cal.Rptr.3d 691 [‘Le Francois simply requires that the trial court reconsider a prior ruling based on its own realization that the ruling was erroneous, and not based upon a determination that [an improper] motion to reconsider should itself be granted on the merits.’]; accord, Nieto v. Blue Shield of California Life & Health Ins. Co. (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 60, 73–74, 103 Cal.Rptr.3d 906)”].)

Here, the Court grants the request for reconsideration of the May 21 Ruling based on the following findings. In its December 1 Ruling, the Court ordered Plaintiff to give Palaioroutas notice of the evidentiary hearing on December 3, 2025, “via mail.” In the underlying motion at issue (i.e., the “Motion for Relief from Default and Vacate Default and Order of 12/3/25,” that the defendant filed on April 23, 2026), the defendant argued that he did not give consent for his “email to be used to serve [him] papers. …. I do not look at my email very often, which is the primary reason for not accepting emailed notice. Alleged services to me by my email were invalid, including the notice of the December 3, 2025 hearing, which is why I did not contest it.” (April 23 Motion, p. 5:5-8.) Although electronic service would have been a faster form service for an evidentiary hearing taking place a few days later, the Court finds that the defendant was nonetheless prejudiced by Plaintiff’s failure to serve the notice of December 1 Ruling through mail as ordered by the Court. The ruling at issue on this motion for reconsideration (i.e. the May 21 Ruling) denied the defendant’s April 23 Motion for the same reasons presented in Plaintiff’s opposition to that motion. However, because Plaintiff’s opposition failed to take into account the fact that Plaintiff failed to serve the defendant in the manner ordered by the Court and stated by the defendant, reconsideration of the Court’s May 21 Ruling denying the defendant’s “Motion for Relief from Default and Vacate Default and Order of 12/3/25” is warranted.

For the reasons discussed above, the Court grants the defendant’s motion for reconsideration, vacates its May 21 Ruling, and also vacates the December 3 Ruling denying the defendant’s motion to quash. The Court shall place the defendant’s motion to quash back on the calendar and give the parties one last opportunity to appear at the evidentiary hearing as ordered below.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the Motion for Reconsideration is GRANTED. The orders entered on December 3, 2025, and May 21, 2026, are vacated.

 

The Court places the Motion to Quash Service of Summons filed by Defendant Vasileios Palaioroutas on 09/29/2025 back on active calendar for an evidentiary hearing on July 31, 2026 at 8:30 a.m., Dept. 205, Beverly Hills Courthouse. All parties and relevant witnesses, including process servers, must attend the evidentiary hearing. Defendant’s failure to appear at the evidentiary hearing will automatically result in a denial of his motion to quash, striking of the Answer filed on December 10, 2025, and default reinstated. Plaintiff’s failure to appear will result in the motion to quash being granted, the default entered on July 21, 2025, vacated, and the Answer filed on December 10, 2025, deemed filed. If all parties appear at the evidentiary hearing, the Court shall proceed with an evidentiary hearing regarding service of summons on the defendant. If the Court finds, after the evidentiary hearing, that the defendant has established grounds for quashing service, the Court shall enter an order granting the motion to quash, vacating the entry of default, and deeming his Answer filed as of the date of the evidentiary hearing. Conversely, if the Court finds that the defendant has failed to establish grounds for granting the motion to quash, the Court shall deny the motion, reinstate the entry of default entered on July 21, 2025, and strike the Answer that was filed on December 10, 2025.

 

The Order to Show Cause Re: Entry of Default Judgment is taken off calendar.

KRISTYN YANCY ANTELIN,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

PALISADES GROWTH CAPITAL, et al.,

                        Defendants.

  Case No.:  25SMCV02554

  Hearing Date:  7/1/26

  Trial Date:     4/24/28

 [TENTATIVE] RULING RE:

Motion for Leave to File Third Amended Complaint

 

Background

 

            On May 19, 2025, Plaintiff Kristyn Yancy Antelin (“Plaintiff”) commenced this FEHA action against Defendants Palisades Growth Capital; Centre Partners Management LLC (“CPM”); Paul D’Addario (“D’Addario”); and DOES 1 through 25 (collectively, “Defendants”).

 

            On August 26, 2025, Plaintiff filed an Amendment to Complaint correcting Defendant Palisades Growth Capital’s name to Palisades Management, LLC (“PML”).

 

            On November 18, 2025, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants, asserting causes of action for: (1) Gender Violence (Civ. Code § 52.4); (2) Battery; (3) Assault; (4) Negligence (Retention/Supervision); (5) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress; and (6) Violation of Labor Code Sections 226 and 1198.5.

 

            On December 22, 2025, D’Addario filed an Answer to the FAC. On January 26, 2026, PML and CPM (collectively, “Corporate Defendants”) filed a Demurrer to the Complaint, which was sustained with leave to amend on February 25, 2026. On March 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) against Defendants.

 

            On June 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion for Leave to File Third Amended Complaint (“TAC”). On June 24, 2026, the Corporate Defendants filed an Opposition. On June 26, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Reply.

 

Legal Standard

 

      The court may, in furtherance of justice and on any proper terms, allow a party to amend any pleading by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect. (Code Civ. Proc., section 473, subd. (a)(1); Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Association (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) The court may also, in its discretion and after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code. (Code Civ. Proc., section 473, subd. (a)(1); Branick, supra, 39 Cal.4th at 242.) As judicial policy favors resolution of all disputed matters between the parties, leave to amend is liberally granted. (See Kolani v. Gluska (1998) 64 Cal.App.4th 402, 412.) The court may deny the plaintiff’s leave to amend if there is prejudice to the opposing party, such as delay in trial, loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation. (Id.) The court also has discretion to deny leave to amend where a proposed amendment fails to state a valid cause of action as a matter of law and the defect cannot be cured by further amendment. (See California Casualty General Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1985) 173 Cal.App.3d 274, 281 (overruled on other grounds by Kransco v. American Empire Surplus Lines Ins. Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 390).)

 

      “Any judge, at any time before or after commencement of trial, in the furtherance of justice, and upon such terms as may be proper, may allow the amendment of any pleading or pretrial conference order.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 576.) 

 

      A motion to amend a pleading before trial must (1) include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments; (2) state what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and (3) state what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324, subd. (a).) A separate supporting declaration specifying (1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) the reason why the request for amendment was not made earlier must accompany the motion. (Id., rule 3.1324, subd. (b).)

 

Preliminary Matters

 

      The Court briefly notes that the opposition was filed late per Code of Civil Procedure Section 1005, subdivision (b). The opposition was due June 24, 2026, but the Corporate Defendants filed it on June 26, 2026. However, Plaintiff’s reply brief substantively addresses the opposition. Thus, the Court will exercise its discretion and rule on the merits below. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1300, subd. (d) [“If the court, in its discretion, refuses to consider a late filed paper, the minutes or order must so indicate.”]; Mann v. Cracchiolo (1985) 38 Cal.3d 18, 30 [210 Cal.Rptr. 762, 767, 694 P.2d 1134, 1139] overruled in part by Perry v. Bakewell Hawthorne, LLC (2017) 2 Cal.5th 536 on other grounds.)

 

Analysis

 

            Here, Plaintiff seeks to amend the SAC to add a ratification theory against the Corporate Defendants, i.e., that the Corporate Defendants failed to investigate, discipline, or terminate D’Addario for his alleged prior sexual harassment of other female workers at PML and CPM. (Mot. at 1:13-23.) Both the moving papers and supporting declaration set forth the page and line numbers where the proposed additions are to be made including a redlined copy. (Mot. at 5:5-28; Medler Decl., ¶¶9-12, 24, Ex. 1.) Likewise, Plaintiff’s counsel of record, Sabrina Medler states that amendment is necessary to cure the deficiencies in the SAC. (Id., ¶13.) Ms. Medler further states that the facts supporting ratification developed incrementally over the course of discovery, thus it could not be pled earlier. (Id., ¶¶14, 19-22.) Moreover, Ms. Medler attests that the proposed amendments bolster support for Plaintiff’s negligence claims already alleged against the Corporate Defendants and ensure all of Plaintiff’s claims including the ratification theory, are properly pled in the interest of justice and judicial economy. (Id., ¶24.) Accordingly, the proposed amendment complies with California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324.

 

            In opposition, the Corporate Defendants argue that the proposed amendment is prejudicial because they have already expended costs, time, and resources briefing and arguing the demurrer to the FAC; met and conferred and prepared another demurrer to the SAC; opposed two motions for leave to amend; and responding to discovery directed to the negligence claim. (Opp. at 7:22-8:3; Leung Decl., ¶5.) Although the Corporate Defendants have expended time, resources, and costs associated with defending against the negligence claim, the trial in this matter is nearly two years away, so there is time to conduct additional discovery on the new theory of liability; the proposed amendment only seeks to add an additional theory of liability as opposed to multiple new causes of action; and the theory of liability is based upon the same facts already alleged against the Corporate Defendants. Also, Plaintiff clarified that facts supporting the ratification theory was discovered gradually through discovery including Plaintiff’s own document productions in October 2025; D’Addario’s responses to Request for Admissions in December 2025; and the Corporate Defendants document production on April 30, 2026. (Medler Decl., ¶¶14-19.) Therefore, the Corporate Defendants fail to demonstrate that they would suffer prejudice in permitting the proposed amendment at this time.

 

Conclusion

            Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff Kristyn Yancy Antelin’s Motion for Leave to File Third Amended Complaint is GRANTED.

HNM PROPERTY MANAGEMENT, INC.,

 [TENTATIVE] RULING RE:

MOTION FOR AN ORDER COMPELLING A RESPONSE TO PLAINTIFF’S SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES, SET ONE, FORM INTERROGATORIES, SET ONE, REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION, SET ONE, AND ESTABLISHING THE TRUTH OF PLAINTIFF’S REQUESTS FOR ADMISSION, SET ONE; IMPOSITION OF SANCTIONS

 

Background

 

            On December 17, 2025, Plaintiff HNM Property Management, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants Michael Anthony and Tiffany Clinton, Micro-Boutique Venice LLC (collectively, “Defendants”), and Does 1 to 10, alleging nine causes of action: (1) quiet title; (2) fraud; (3) civil theft; (4) conversion; (5) unjust enrichment; (6) declaratory relief; (7) breach of contract; (8) breach of contract; and (9) breach of contract. This action arises from alleged breaches of contracts and false representations by Defendants regarding funds provided by Plaintiff for various real estate deals involving six properties: (1) 401 Franklin St, Natchez, MS 39120-3263 (APN 0005-0001-0005) (“Franklin property”); (2) 107 Arlington Ave, Natchez, MS 39120 (APN 0052-0001-0007) (“Arlington property”); (3) 998 Cedar Bluff Rd, Centre, AL 35960 (APN 13-14-06-14-1-000-030.000) (“Cedar property”); (4) 232 W.             No opposition has been filed.

 

 

Motion to Compel Discovery Requests & Deem RFAs Admitted Standard

 

I.      Requests for Production and Interrogatories

            A plaintiff may make a demand for production of documents and propound interrogatories without leave of court at any time 10 days after the service of the summons on, or appearance by, the party to whom the demand is directed, whichever occurs first. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.020, subd. (b); Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.020, subd. (b).) The demand for production of documents is not limited by number, but the request must comply with the formatting requirements in Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.030. A party may propound 35 specially prepared interrogatories that are relevant to the subject matter of the pending action and any additional number of official form interrogatories that are relevant to the subject matter of the pending action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.030, subd. (a)(1) - (a)(2).)

 

            The party to whom the request is propounded upon is required to respond within 30 days after service of a demand, but the parties are allowed to informally agree to an extension and confirm any such agreement in writing. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.260, subd. (a); Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.260, subd. (a); Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.270, subd. (a) - (b); Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.270, subd. (a) - (b).) 

 

            If a party fails to timely respond to a request for production or interrogatories, the party to whom the request is directed at waives any right to exercise the option to produce writings under Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.230, and waives any objection, including one based on privilege or on the protection for work product. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a); Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).)¿¿The party propounding the discovery requests may move for an order compelling responses. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (b); Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (b).) Unlike a motion to compel further discovery responses, a motion to compel initial discovery responses does not have any meet and confer requirements.

 

            Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030, subdivision (a) provides, in pertinent part, that the court may impose a monetary sanction on a party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process to pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct. A misuse of the discovery process includes failing to respond or submit to an authorized method of discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subd. (d).)¿¿¿A court has discretion to fix the amount of reasonable monetary sanctions. (Cornerstone Realty Advisors, LLC v. Summit Healthcare Reit, Inc. (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 771, 791.) 

 

II.   Request for Admissions¿

            “Any party may obtain discovery . . . by a written request that any other party to the action admit the genuineness of specified documents, or the truth of specified matters of fact, opinion relating to fact, or application of law to fact. A request for admission may relate to a matter that is in controversy between the parties.”¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.010.)¿ “Within 30 days after service of requests for admission, the party to whom the requests are directed shall serve the original of the response to them on the requesting party, and a copy of the response on all other parties who have appeared ....” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.250, subd. (a).)

 

            If a party to whom request for admissions are served fails to provide a timely response, the party to whom the request was directed waives any objections, including based on privilege or the work product doctrine. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (a).) The requesting party can move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the request be deemed admitted, as well as for monetary sanctions. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (b).) The court shall issue this order unless the party to whom the request was made serves a response in substantial compliance prior to the hearing on the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).) Unlike a motion to compel further discovery responses, a motion to deem RFA admitted does not have any meet and confer requirements.

 

Analysis

 

I.      Discovery Requests

            Plaintiff moves to compel initial responses to its discovery requests and deem the content of its RFAs admitted.

     

            On April 13, 2026, Plaintiff electronically served initial written discovery to Defendants that included its first set of SROGs, FROGs, RPDs, and RFAs. Defendants failed to serve initial verified responses by the statutory deadline, May 15, 2026. (Moghadam Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. C.) Plaintiff’s counsel emailed opposing counsel on May 19, 2026, regarding Defendants’ failure to respond. (Moghadam Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. C.) On May 21, 2026, opposing counsel responded to “please see the attached drop box with documents responsive to your discovery request. I will prepare a formal response and get it to you early this next week.” (Moghadam Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. D.) In his declaration attached to the motion to compel responses to RPDs, Plaintiff’s counsel states that the Dropbox contained “a handful of correspondence. There was no formal response to the RFPs, much less a verified response.” (Moghadam RPD Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. D.) As of the date of these filings, Defendants have not responded to any of the requests. (Moghadam Decl., ¶ 8.)

 

      Defendants have not filed an opposition. It is undisputed that the initial discovery responses at issue were not provided by the statutory deadline or by the filings of these motions.

 

      Accordingly, the Court grants the present motions. Plaintiff’s request for admissions, set one, are deemed admitted.

II.             Sanctions

Plaintiff requests a total of $21,882 for all four discovery motions: $5,883.00 for SROGs, $4933.00 for RPDs, $5,083.00 for FROGs, and $5,983.00 for RFAs. Counsel is requesting 1.2 hours for drafting a single correspondence in all four motions, 8.6 hours in total for drafting all four motions (2.5 hours for the FROGs, 1.7 hours for the FROGs,1.3 hours for the RPDs, and 2.6 hours for the RFAs), 8 hours in total for reviewing the oppositions and drafting replies, and 4 hours in total for preparing and arguing at the hearing for all four motions. (Moghadam Decl., ¶ 10.) Counsel bills at a rate of $1000/hour as an attorney who has been practicing since 2012 and attached Exhibits of the Laffey Matrix and an order from Judge Mark A. Young, dated March 24, 2026, valuing his services at $950/hour. (Moghadam Decl., ¶ 12-13, 15, Exhs. E-F.)

 

It is mandatory that the Court “impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (c).)

 

It is mandatory that the court “impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposed a motion to compel a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (c).)

 

It is mandatory that the court “impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) on the party or attorney, or both, whose failure to serve a timely response to requests for admission necessitated this motion” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).)

 

            The Court does not find Defendant to have acted with substantial justification, and Defendants do not offer an opposition that speaks to other circumstances that would make the imposition of sanctions unjust. Defendants were told about their lack of responses by the statutory deadline and failed to serve verified responses even after indicating they would.

 

However, the Court finds the hours billed for attorney’s work in association with the four motions to be excessive. First, the Court reduces the total by 8 hours because no oppositions have been filed. Second, the court reduces 1.2 hours for drafting a single correspondence email to opposing counsel, which was requested four times even though it is the same email. Third, the Court finds 8.6 hours for drafting simple discovery motions to be excessive and unreasonable, especially for an attorney who has more than a decade of experience, and reduces the total hours to 4, 1 hour per motion. The Court also reduces the hours for appearing and preparing for the hearings to 1 hour total because all four motions will likely be heard together and no oppositions have been filed.

 

Accordingly, the Court grants sanctions in the reduced total amount of $4750.00, at a rate of $950/hour.

 

Conclusion

Plaintiff’s motions to compel initial responses to the first set of special interrogatories, form interrogatories, requests for production, and to deem requests for admissions admitted are GRANTED.

 

Plaintiff’s requests for sanctions are granted in the reduced total amount of $4750.00, at a rate of $950/hour.

 

The Court further orders Defendants to provide complete and verified responses to Plaintiff’s Requests for Special Interrogatories, Set One, Form Interrogatories, Set One, and for Production, Set One, without objections, and to pay sanctions of $4750.00 to Plaintiff, within 20 days of the date of this Order

 

Plaintiff shall provide notice of this order.

 [TENTATIVE] RULING RE:

MOTION TO COMPEL BINDING ARBITRATION

 

Background

 

This is a lemon law action filed by plaintiff, Emanoel Couhen (Plaintiff) against defendant, Mercedes Benz USA LLC (Defendant) and DOES 1 through 100.

 

On May 15, 2024 Plaintiff leased a 2024 Mercedes-Benz EQS450V (Vehicle). (Ameripour Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 2.)  On that day, Plaintiff signed a motor vehicle lease agreement (Lease Agreement), which included an agreement to arbitrate. (Ibid.)  The Lease Agreement identifies Plaintiff as the Vehicle’s lessee and WI Simonson as the lessor. (Ibid.) 

 

Plaintiff pleaded that defects and non-conformities to warranty manifested in the Vehicle during the applicable warranty period.  Plaintiff pleaded he delivered the Vehicle to Defendant for repairs, but Defendant was unable to conform the Vehicle to the applicable warranty after a reasonable number of repair attempts. (generally, Complaint.) 

 

On March 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed his Complaint.

 

On April 15, 2026, Defendant asked Plaintiff to stipulate to arbitration, but Plaintiff did not agree. (Ameripour Decl., ¶ 3.)

 

On April 15, 2026, Defendant filed its motion to compel Plaintiff to arbitrate his dispute with Defendant and requested that the Court stay the proceedings in this case.  Defendant asserts that Plaintiff leased the Vehicle subject to the terms of an arbitration provision contained in the Lease Agreement. 

 

On June 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition brief.  

 

On June 24, 2026, Defendant filed a Reply brief. 

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

Defendant requests the Court take judicial notice of Plaintiff’s Complaint filed on March 3, 2026.

 

The request is granted. (Evid. Code § 452(d), (h); See¿Kalnoki v. First American Trustee Servicing Solutions, LLC (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 23,37.) However, the court only takes judicial notice of the foregoing documents only as to “the existence, content and authenticity of public records and other specified documents”; it does not take judicial notice of the truth of the factual matters asserted in those documents. (Dominguez v. Bonta (2022) 87 Cal. App. 5th 389, 400.)¿

 

Legal Standard

 

Parties may be compelled to arbitrate a dispute upon the court finding that: (1) there was a valid agreement to arbitrate between the parties; and (2) said agreement covers the controversy or controversies in the parties’ dispute.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961.)

 

Analysis

 

Defendant moves to compel arbitration.  The motion will be granted. 

 

Defendant asserts Plaintiff is bound to arbitrate his dispute with Defendant because there is an agreement to arbitrate contained in the Lease Agreement which designates Defendant as a third-party beneficiary. 

 

The Lease Agreement signed by the parties on May 15, 2024, contains an arbitration provision, which reads, in pertinent part, as follows: 

 

Any claim or dispute, whether in contract, tort or otherwise (including any dispute over the interpretation, scope, or validity of this lease, arbitration section or the arbitrability of any issue), between you and us or any of our employees, agents, successors, assigns, or the vehicle distributor, including Mercedes-Benz USA LLC (each a "Third-Party Beneficiary"), which arises out of or relates to a credit application, this lease, or any resulting transaction or relationship arising out of this lease (including any such relationship with third parties who do not sign this contract) shall, at the election of either you, us, or a Third-Party Beneficiary, be resolved by a neutral, binding arbitration and not by a court action.

(Ameripour Decl., Ex. 2, p. 4.)

 

The Lease Agreement’s arbitration provision further provides:

 

This lease evidences a transaction involving interstate commerce. Any arbitration under this lease shall be governed by the Federal Arbitration Act (9 USC 1, et seq).

(Ameripour Decl., Ex. 2, p. 4.)

 

The Lease Agreement expressly states that Defendant shall be allowed to enforce the arbitration provision as a third-party beneficiary and expressly designates Defendant as such.

 

A defendant may be able to enforce an arbitration agreement to which it is not a signatory if it qualifies as a third-party beneficiary.  (See Mance v. Mercedes-Benz USA (N.D.Cal. 2012) 901 F.Supp.2d 1147, 1155.)  The elements necessary to establish a third-party beneficiary relationship are (1) the third party would in fact benefit from the contract; (2) a motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to provide a benefit to the third party; and (3) permitting the third party to enforce the contract is consistent with the objectives of the contract and the reasonable expectations of the contracting parties.  (Goonewardene v. ADP, LLC (2019) 6 Cal.5th 817, 830.)  “Whether the third party is an intended beneficiary or merely an incidental beneficiary involves construction of the intention of the parties, gathered from reading the contract as a whole in light of the circumstances under which it was entered.”  (Cione v. Foresters Equity Servs. (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 625, 636, quoting Eastern Aviation Group, Inc. v. Airborne Express, Inc. (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 1448, 1452.)

 

Here, the terms of the Lease Agreement executed by Plaintiff and WI Simonson expressly designate Defendant, the Vehicle’s manufacturer/distributor, as an intended third-party beneficiary.  Defendant stood to benefit from the Lease Agreement by way of its receipt of indirect profits. 

 

Plaintiff argues that he filed the instant action against Defendant alone, asserting three causes of action for breach of implied warranty, breach of express warranty, and violation of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act. (Opp., p. 4.)  Plaintiff contends that all three causes of action arise from Defendant’s new-vehicle warranty and its statutory duties as the manufacturer, not from any term of the Lease Agreement. (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff argues the instant motion should be denied based on the holdings in Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2025) 17 Cal.5th 1122 and Goonewardene v. ADT LLC (2019) 6 Cal.5th 1145 because Defendant was not a signatory to the Lease Agreement and is not a “third-party beneficiary”.  (Opp., p. 2.)  However, Plaintiff’s reliance on both Ford Motor Warranty Cases and Goonewardene is misplaced. 

 

In Ford Motor Warranty Cases, the Supreme Court declined to permit a manufacturer to compel arbitration under principles of estoppel where the arbitration agreement contained no language authorizing enforcement by a third party in the defendant’s position. (Ford Motor Warranty Cases, supra, 17 Cal.5th 1122 at 1138.)  This case is distinguishable from Ford Motor Warranty Cases in that the Lease Agreement specifically extends arbitration rights Defendant.  (Ameripour Decl., Ex. 2, p. 4.)  Because Defendant is named as an intended third-party beneficiary in the Lease Agreement, the reasoning in Ford Motor Warranty Cases does not apply. 

 

In Goonewardene, the court laid out three elements required for a finding third-party beneficiary status: (1) whether the third party would in fact benefit from the contract, but also (2) whether a motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to provide a benefit to the third party, and (3) whether permitting a third party to bring its own breach of contract action against a contracting party is consistent with the objectives of the contract and the reasonable expectations of the contracting parties. (Goonewardene v. ADP, LLC, supra, 6 Cal.5th 817 at 830.)  Here, all the elements are met. First, Defendant benefits from the Lease Agreement as the manufacturer. Second, there is a clear “intent to benefit the third party” as the provision unambiguously includes Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC as a third-party beneficiary. (Lease, Exhibit 2 to Ameripour Decl., page 4.) (Goonewardene, supra, 6 Cal.5th 817 at 830.)  And third, it is reasonable to infer that the Lease Agreement anticipated Defendant’s possible enforcement of the Lease Agreement, otherwise, it would not have been expressly identified.

 

Therefore, Defendant may enforce the arbitration provision.

 

Conclusion

 

Defendant Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC’s motion for an order compelling Plaintiff Emanoel Couhen to arbitrate his complaint against Defendant Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC is GRANTED. 

 

This action is hereby STAYED pending a resolution of arbitration.  See Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.

Defendant Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC is ORDERED to give notice.
