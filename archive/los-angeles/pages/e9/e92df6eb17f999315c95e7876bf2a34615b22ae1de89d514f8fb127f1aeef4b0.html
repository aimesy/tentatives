DEPARTMENT 316 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV09557    Hearing Date: July 20, 2026    Dept: 316

BACKGROUND¿ 

This case is an employment discrimination matter. Plaintiff Mauricio Gomez-Contreras was employed by Defendants Bodycote Thermal Processing Inc. and Innovative Resource Management. While working for Defendants, Plaintiff alleges that he was subject to unlawful discrimination and retaliation because of his disability.

On July 14, 2025, Plaintiff filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants. The FAC alleges five employment-related causes of action stemming from the facts alleged above.

On August 26, 2025, the parties stipulated to submit Plaintiff’s claim to arbitration and to stay court proceedings pending the arbitration. Accordingly, on the same day, the Court issued a Minute Order sending the case to arbitration and staying all proceedings.  

On June 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Motion to Vacate Order Compelling Arbitration and to Lift Stay (“Motion”).

On July 7, 2026, Defendants filed an opposition.

On July 13, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff’s Motion to Vacate Order is GRANTED. The stay is lifted and this matter shall resume under the Court’s jurisdiction.

Defendants shall pay Plaintiff fees and costs in the amount of $23,611.10.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

“¿In an employment or consumer arbitration that requires . . . that the drafting party pay certain fees and costs during the pendency of an arbitration proceeding, if the fees or costs required to continue the arbitration proceeding are not paid within 30 days after the due date, the drafting party is in material breach of the arbitration agreement, is in default of the arbitration, and waives its right to compel the employee or consumer to proceed with that arbitration as a result of the material breach.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.98, subd. (a)(1).) Accordingly, the employee then may withdraw their claims from arbitration and proceed in a court of appropriate jurisdiction. (Id. at subd. (b)(1).) 

DISCUSSION

Here, Defendants do not oppose vacating the Court’s August 26, 2025 Order compelling arbitration. (See Opposition, pp. 1:5-8, 14:23-24.) Rather, Plaintiffs dispute whether the Court should award Plaintiff any sanctions, fees, or costs. Therefore, the Court will focus its analysis on this specific issue.

I.               Sanctions, Fees, and Costs

The Court will award Plaintiff fees and costs as sanctions in the amount of $23,6611.10.

If the drafting party waives its right to compel arbitration and an employee proceeds with an action in court, the court shall impose monetary sanctions on the drafting party. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.98, subd. (c)(2); id. at § 1281.99, subd. (a).) The sanctions shall include attorney’s fees and costs. (See id. at § 1281.99, subd. (a).) 

Because Defendants do not oppose vacating the Court’s August 26, 2025 Order, there is no dispute that they waived their rights to compel arbitration. Accordingly, the Court must award Plaintiff monetary sanctions, including fees and costs. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.98, subd. (c)(2); id. at § 1281.99, subd. (a).) 

Despite this, Defendants contend that monetary sanctions should not be awarded because their failure to timely pay all arbitration fees was the result of a good faith mistake, inadvertence, or other excusable neglect. (See Opposition, pp. 4:4-5:8, 11:19-12:12.) However, these factors are only considered when determining whether Defendants should be stripped of their rights to compel arbitration. (See Hohenshelt v. Superior Court (2025) 18 Cal.5th 310, 323 [“[W]e find no indication that it intended to strip companies and employers of their contractual right to arbitration where nonpayment of fees results from a good faith mistake, inadvertence, or other excusable neglect.”.) Here, Defendants waived their right to compel arbitration by not opposing to vacate the Court’s August 26, 2025 Order. Therefore, whether Defendants’ nonpayment was the result of a good faith mistake, inadvertence, or other excusable neglect is irrelevant; sanctions shall still be awarded. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.98, subd. (c)(2); id. at § 1281.99, subd. (a).) 

Accordingly, the Court must now determine whether the requested sanctions are reasonable.

A.    Reasonableness of Requested Sanctions

Plaintiff requests $36,361.10 in monetary sanctions.

Plaintiff’s counsel, Jared Beilke, previously worked at an hourly rate of $950/hour. (Beilke Decl., ¶ 13.) Beilke’s current rate is now $1000/hour. (Ibid.) Beilke claims they spent 17.3 hours on this case, amounting to a total of $15,235. (Id. at ¶ 17, Exh. A.) However, Beilke is counting hours spent on work that was not related to the arbitration proceedings or this Motion. Therefore, the Court will not award sanctions for hours before 2/1/2024. (See id. at Exh. A.) This reduces the total award amount for Beilke’s hour worked to $6,735. Beilke also anticipates spending an additional four hours on future work relating to this Motion, which would amount to $4,000. The Court finds this to be excessive given Defendants did not oppose the primary relief sought in the motion. The Court will award an additional two hours for counsel Beilke; that is for one hour of work on the reply brief and one hour to appear at the hearing. Therefore, in total, the Court will award $8,735 for Beilke’s work.

Plaintiff’s other counsel, Karina Godoy, previously worked at an hourly rate of $650/hour. (Godoy Decl., ¶ 29.) Godoy’s current rate is now $750/hour. (Id. at ¶ 30.) Godoy claims they spent 17.6 hours on this case, amounting to total of $12,950. (See id. at Exh. I.) All of Godoy’s hours were spent on work that had utility towards the arbitration proceedings or this Motion. (See ibid.) Therefore, the Court will award the full $12,950. Godoy also anticipates spending an additional five hours for future work relating to this Motion, which would amount to $3750. The Court finds this excessive given Defendants did not oppose the primary relief sought in the motion. The Court will award an additional two hours of work on the reply brief as reasonable. Therefore, in total, the Court will award $14,450 for Godoy’s work.

When combining the amounts for Beilke’s work, Godoy’s work, and the additional $426.10 in costs, the total amount comes to $23,611.10.

CONCLUSION¿

For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiff’s Motion to Vacate Order is GRANTED. The stay is lifted and this matter shall resume under the Court’s jurisdiction. The Status Conference re Arbitration set on August 26, 2026, at 8:30 a.m. shall now be set as a Case Management Conference.

Defendants shall pay monetary sanctions in the amount of $23,611.10.

Case Number: 26STCV01803    Hearing Date: July 20, 2026    Dept: 316

BACKGROUND

This case is a vehicular collision matter. Plaintiff Jong Hee Kim alleges that Defendant Oscar Sandoval Ulloa negligently crashed into Plaintiff while driving for Lyft, Inc. Plaintiff also alleges that he was involved in a second motor vehicle collision caused by Defendants Humberto Rolando Ostorga, Dmytro Sydorov, and Sezai Cengiz.

On April 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants Lyft, Inc., Oscar Sandoval Ulloa, Humberto Rolando Ostorga, Dmytro Sydorov, Sezai Cengiz, and Dimarisla Corp. The complaint alleges seven causes of action from the facts alleged above.

On April 15, 2026, Defendant Oscar Sandoval Ulloa filed a Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiff’s FAC (“Motion”).

On July 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.

On July 9, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant’s Motion to Strike is DENIED.

LEGAL STANDARD

“Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof, but this time limitation shall not apply to motions specified in subdivision (e).” (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(2).) “The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: (a) Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (b) Strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.)

DISCUSSION

I.               Meet and Confer

Before filing a motion to strike, the demurring party shall meet and confer with the opposing party in person, by telephone, or by video conference. (Code Civ. Pro. § 435.5, subd. (a).) The demurring party must file a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 435.5, subd. (a)(3).)

“A determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to grant or deny the motion to strike.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 435.5, subd. (a)(4).)    

Here, the parties discussed the motion to strike via email. (See Portillo Decl., Exh. A.) Thus, the meet and confer requirement has not been satisfied. (See Code Civ. Pro. § 435.5, subd. (a).) Despite this, the Court will consider the merits of Defendant’s Motion. (See Code Civ. Pro. § 435.5, subd. (a)(4).) 

II.             Motion to Strike

The Court will not strike any portions of Plaintiff’s FAC or the corresponding request for punitive damages.

To succeed on a motion to strike punitive damages, it must be said as a matter of law that the alleged behavior was so vile, base, or contemptible that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people. (See Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1228-29.) Civil Code section 3294 provides that punitive damages are available in non-contract actions where defendant is guilty of malice, oppression, or fraud, defined as follows:    

1. “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.  

2. “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.  

3. “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.      

(Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c).)      

Here, Defendant seeks to strike any allegations of Defendant’s drunk driving (i.e., FAC, ¶¶ 61-63, Prayer ¶ 5). Defendant seeks to strike these allegations because Plaintiff does not “allege specific facts to support that Defendant was aware of the probable consequences of his conduct.” (Motion, p. 6:11-12.)

However, Plaintiff’s allegations that Defendant consciously disregarded the rights and safety of others by driving drunk is sufficient at this stage to support a request for punitive damages. (See Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 854, 897 [“One who willfully consumes alcoholic beverages to the point of intoxication, knowing that he thereafter must operate a motor vehicle, thereby combining sharply impaired physical and mental faculties with a vehicle capable of great force and speed, reasonably may be held to exhibit a conscious disregard of the safety of others.”]; FAC, ¶ 14 [“in willful and conscious disregard of the safety of others”]; ¶ 16 [“willfully and deliberately operated his vehicle in conscious disregard of the safety of Plaintiff Jong Hee Kim”]; ¶ 24 [“Defendant Lyft’s officers, directors, and/or managing agents consciously disregarded the probable dangerous consequences of allowing an alcohol-impaired and/or alcohol-history driver with disqualifying or material DMV issues to continue driving passengers for compensation.”]; ¶ 61 [“The conduct of Defendant Oscar Sandoval Ulloa was despicable and was carried on with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights and safety of others, including Plaintiff.]; ¶ 62 [“Defendant Oscar Sandoval Ulloa’s conduct after the first collision further evidences malice, oppression, and conscious disregard.”].) Whether Defendant was aware of the probable consequences of his alleged conduct is an issue that Plaintiff can only establish through discovery. Further, even assuming arguendo that the allegations regarding Defendant’s drunk driving cannot by themselves support a finding of conscious disregard, Plaintiff also alleges that Defendant attempted to flee the scene with passengers in his car, drove up onto a sidewalk, crashed into a telephone pole, and caused the vehicle to ignite. (See FAC, ¶¶ 18, 62.) This conduct, taken together with the allegations that Defendant was drunk during this time, is sufficient at this stage of the proceedings to proceed with an allegation of conscious disregard for the safety and rights of others.

Accordingly, the Court will deny the motion.

CONCLUSION 

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant’s Motion to Strike is DENIED
