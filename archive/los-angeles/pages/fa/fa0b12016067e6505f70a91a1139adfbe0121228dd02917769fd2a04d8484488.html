BACKGROUND



This is a breach of contract case. In August of 2022, a tear
gas incident took place at a property located at 5009 Lankershim Boulevard in
North Hollywood California (the Property). On August 15, 2022, Defendant
Morrison Studios Ltd. L.P. (Defendant) executed a written authorization for

On July 2, 2024, Plaintiffs filed the First Amended
Complaint (FAC) alleging causes of action for: (1) breach of written contract
(asserted by plaintiff Restoration), (2) breach of oral contract (asserted by
plaintiff Abatement), (3) breach of oral contract (asserted by plaintiff LY),
(4) quantum meruit (asserted by plaintiff Restoration), (5) quantum meruit
(asserted by plaintiff Abatement), (6) quantum meruit (asserted by Plaintiff

On April 7, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion
seeking summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication on each
cause of action.



On June 22, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition.



As of July 6, 2026, no reply has been filed.



LEGAL
STANDARD



            In reviewing a motion for summary
judgment or adjudication, courts must apply a three-step analysis: “(1)
identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving
party has negated the opponent’s claims; and (3) determine whether the
opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual
issue.”¿(Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135
Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) 



            “[T]he initial burden is always on
the moving party to make a prima facia showing that there are no triable issues
of material fact.”¿(Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128
Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.)¿When a plaintiff seeks summary judgment, the plaintiff
must produce admissible evidence on each element of each cause of action on
which judgment is sought. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(p)(1).) The plaintiff is not
required to disprove any affirmative defenses to discharge this burden. (Oldcastle
Precast, Inc. v. Lumbermens Mutual Casualty Co. (2009) 170 Cal.App.4th 554,
564-65.)¿



If the moving party fails to carry its burden, the
inquiry is over, and the motion must be denied. (See Code Civ. Proc., §
437c, subd. (p)(2); see also Consumer Cause, Inc. v. SmileCare (2001)
91 Cal.App.4th 454, 468.)¿Even if the moving party does carry its burden, the
non-moving party will still defeat the motion by presenting evidence of a
triable issue of material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001)
25 Cal.4th 826, 849-50.) The supporting evidence can be in the form
of affidavits, declarations, admissions, depositions, answers to
interrogatories, and matters of which judicial notice may be taken.¿(Aguilar, supra,
25 Cal.4th at p. 855.) 



            “Once the [moving party] … has met
that burden, the burden shifts to the [non-moving party] … to show that a
triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or
a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) The non-moving
party may not merely rely on allegations or denials of its pleadings to show
that a triable issue of material fact exists, but instead, “shall set forth the
specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the
cause of action.”¿(Ibid.)¿“If the [non-moving party] cannot do so,
summary judgment should be granted.”¿(Avivi v. Centro
Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463,
467.) 



            The court
must “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary
judgment and resolve all doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that
party,” including “all inferences reasonably drawn therefrom.”¿(Yanowitz v.
L’Oreal USA, Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1037; Aguilar, supra,
25 Cal.4th at pp. 844-45.) “On a summary judgment motion, the court must
therefore consider what inferences favoring the opposing party a factfinder
could reasonably draw from the evidence.¿While viewing the evidence
in this manner, the court must bear in mind that its primary function is
to identify issues rather than to determine issues. [Citation.] Only
when the inferences are indisputable may the court decide the issues as a matter
of law.¿ If the evidence is in conflict, the factual issues must be
resolved by trial.”¿(Binder v. Aetna Life Ins. Co. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th
832, 839.)¿Further, “the trial court may not weigh the evidence in the manner
of a factfinder to determine whose version is more likely true.
[Citation.] Nor may the trial court grant summary judgment based on the court’s
evaluation of credibility. [Citation.]” (Id. at p. 840; see also Weiss
v. People ex rel. Department of Transportation (2020) 9 Cal.5th 840,
864 [“Courts deciding motions for summary judgment or summary adjudication may
not weigh the evidence but must instead view it in the light most favorable to
the opposing party and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of that
party”].) 



DISCUSSION           



Plaintiffs move for summary judgment
against Defendant on the complaint. In the alternative, Plaintiffs move for
summary adjudication on the first cause of action for breach of written
contract; second and third causes of action for breach of oral contract; and
the fourth, fifth, and sixth causes of action for quantum meruit. Specifically,
Plaintiffs move for summary adjudication of all causes of action on the
following overlapping issue: Defendant has judicially admitted the validity and
exact amounts of Plaintiffs’ claims in a federal civil action against the City
of Los Angeles. (Motion, pp. 2-3.)



Judicial Estoppel



Judicial estoppel prevents a party
from asserting a position in a legal proceeding that is contrary to a position
previously taken in the same or some earlier proceeding. (Gottlieb v. Kest
(2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 110, 130-31.) “In California, courts consider five
factors in determining whether to apply judicial estoppel: ‘The doctrine [most
appropriately] applies when (1) the same party has taken two positions; (2) the
positions were taken in judicial or quasi-judicial administrative proceedings;
(3) the party was successful in asserting the first position (i.e., the
tribunal adopted the position or accepted it as true); (4) the two positions
are totally inconsistent; and (5) the first position was not taken as a result
of ignorance, fraud, or mistake.’” (Id. at p. 131 [quoting Aguilar v.
Lerner (2004) 32 Cal.4th 974, 986-87].)



Movant’s Burden



Plaintiffs argue that the doctrine
of judicial estoppel prevents Defendant from denying any part of Plaintiffs’
claims. (Mot., p. 4.) Plaintiffs argue that at the federal trial in which
Defendant sued the City of Los Angeles for damage caused by the government raid
and tear gas contamination on the property, Defendant adopted the amounts owed
to Plaintiffs as its owed claimed damages incurred from the incident. (Mot., p.
5.) Plaintiffs argue that this shows Defendant took the position that they owe
these amounts to Plaintiffs. (Ibid.) Plaintiffs argue Defendant even
called Plaintiffs’ principal, Yonan Benjamin (Benjamin), as a witness in the
case to testify that these outstanding costs were incurred and are still owed.
(Ibid.) Thus, Plaintiff argues that Defendant cannot take the position
now that they do not owe these amounts to Plaintiffs. (Ibid.)



Here, Plaintiffs have not
established judicial estoppel applies. Defendant’s federal lawsuit does concern
the same incident; however, Defendant does not take a clear position on the
dispute with Plaintiffs. In Defendant’s federal lawsuit against the City of Los
Angeles, Defendant claims approximately $900,000 in damages to the Property;
however, Defendant does not refer to the agreements with each of the
Plaintiffs. (Benjamin Decl., Exh. 5, ¶ 9.) In Benjamin’s witness testimony,
Defendant’s counsel acknowledges the instant lawsuit and asks Benjamin to
identify the total cost of remediation. (Benjamin Decl., Exh. 6, pp. 16-17.)
However, Defendant’s counsel does not take a position on whether Defendant owes
these amounts to Plaintiffs. Defendant’s counsel only asks if Benjamin expects
to be repaid, not by whom. (Benjamin Decl., Exh. 6., p. 37, ¶¶ 20-22.) Benjamin
also testifies that Farmers Insurance is the entity that hires him, and
impliedly Plaintiffs, to perform these restoration services. (Benjamin Decl., Exh.
6, p. 9, ¶¶ 4-16.) Defendant’s counsel does not confirm any privity between
Defendant and Plaintiffs or admit that Defendant must repay this debt. Plaintiffs
have not established that Defendant took an inconsistent position in the prior
proceeding or that the position was successful. 



Thus, Plaintiff has not established
judicial estoppel, and the burden does not shift to Defendant. However, the
Court will review individual causes of action under Plaintiff’s motion for
summary adjudication.



First Cause of Action for Breach of
Written Contract (Asserted by Plaintiff Restoration)



To state a cause of action for
breach of contract, Plaintiff must be able to establish “(1) the existence of
the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3)
defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis
West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.)



Movant’s Burden



Restoration alleges breach of
written contract against Defendant. (FAC, ¶¶ 20-25.) Restoration argues that
the authorization establishes a written contract between Defendant and
Restoration. (Mot., p. 5.) Restoration argues that at Defendant’s own federal
trial, Defendant confirms that the $378,950.05 invoice reflects work performed
by Restoration and remains unpaid. (Ibid.)



Here, Plaintiffs have provided the
written authorization executed by Defendant through ServiceMaster requesting
repairs on the Property. (Benjamin Decl., Exh. 1.) Plaintiffs also provide the
alleged invoice for plaintiff Restoration establishing the agreement, work
performed, and costs incurred. (Benjamin Decl., Exh. 2.) The invoices also
reflect the damages allegedly incurred by Restoration in Defendant’s failure to
perform. However, the authorization is dated August 2023 even though repairs in
the invoice reflect August 2022. (Benjamin Decl., Exh. 1-2.) Moreover,
Restoration is not listed on this invoice; the only company listed is
ServiceMaster and Plaintiffs have not established a connection between
Restoration and ServiceMaster. Since Restoration alleges breach of written
contract, and has not provided a sufficient written contract, Restoration has
not established the existence of a written contract.



Plaintiff Restoration has not met
their burden. Thus, summary adjudication is denied as to the first cause of
action.



Second Cause of Action for Breach of
Written Contract (Asserted by Plaintiff Abatement)



To state a cause of action for
breach of contract, Plaintiff must be able to establish “(1) the existence of
the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3)
defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis
West Realty, LLC, supra, 51 Cal.4th at 821.)



Movant’s Burden



Abatement alleges breach of oral
contract against Defendant in August of 2023. (FAC, ¶¶ 26-31.) Abatement argues
that Defendant’s federal trial confirms the agreement reflected by the $16,000
invoice between Defendant and Abatement. (Mot., p. 5.) Abatement argues that at
trial, Defendant confirmed that this invoice remains unpaid. (Ibid.)



Abatement does not establish breach
of oral contract against Defendant. Abatement provides an invoice establishing
work performed and costs incurred in August of 2022. (Benjamin Decl., Exh. 3.)
The invoices also reflect the damages allegedly incurred by Abatement in
Defendant’s failure to perform.  However,
Abatement has not provided evidence to establish existence of an oral contract.
Benjamin’s witness testimony does not confirm that the parties entered an oral
contract, rather, Benjamin’s testimony confirms that Farmers Insurance retains
Plaintiffs to provide restoration services. (Benjamin Decl., Exh. 6, p. 9, ¶¶
4-16.) Thus, Abatement has not established the existence of an oral contract
between the parties and has not established breach of oral contract.



Plaintiff Abatement has not met
their burden. Thus, summary adjudication is denied as to the second cause of
action.



Third Cause of Action for Breach of
Oral Contract (Asserted by Plaintiff LY)



To state a cause of action for
breach of contract, Plaintiff must be able to establish “(1) the existence of
the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3)
defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis
West Realty, LLC, supra, 51 Cal.4th at 821.)



Movant’s Burden



LY alleges breach of oral contract
against Defendant reached in August of 2023. (FAC, ¶¶ 32-37.) LY argues that
Defendant’s federal trial confirms the additional $6,375 invoice between
Defendant and LY. (Mot., p. 5.) LY argued that at Defendant’s trial, Benjamin
confirmed that this invoice remains unpaid. (Ibid.)



LY does not establish breach of oral
contract against Defendant. LY provides an invoice establishing work performed
and costs incurred in August of 2022. (Benjamin Decl., Exh. 4.) The invoices
also reflect the damages allegedly incurred by LY in Defendant’s failure to
perform. However, LY has not provided evidence to establish existence of an
oral contract. As discussed above, Benjamin’s testimony confirms that Farmers
Insurance retains Plaintiffs to provide restoration services. (Benjamin Decl.,
Exh. 6, ¶¶ 4-16.) Thus, LY has not established the existence of an oral
contract between the parties and has not established breach of oral contract.



Plaintiff LY has not met their
burden. Thus, summary adjudication is denied as to the third cause of action.



Fourth, Fifth, and Sixth Causes of
Action – Quantum Meruit



“Quantum meruit refers to the
well-established principle that the law implies a promise to pay for
services performed under circumstances disclosing that they were not
gratuitously rendered. To recover in quantum meruit, a party need not prove the
existence of a contract, but it must show the circumstances were such that the
services were rendered under some understanding or expectation of
both parties that compensation therefor was to be made.” (Chodos v.
Borman (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 76, 96, citations and quotation marks
omitted.) “[I]n order to recover under a quantum meruit theory, a plaintiff
must establish both that he or she was acting pursuant to either an express or
implied request for such services from the defendant and that the services
rendered were intended to and did benefit the defendant.” (Day v. Alta Bates
Medical Center (2002) 98 Cal.App.4th 243, 248.)



In the fourth, fifth, and sixth
causes of action, Plaintiffs Restoration, Abatement, and LY allege quantum
meruit against Defendant based on identical facts. Thus, the Court will address
the causes of action together.



Movant’s Burden



Plaintiffs argue that Defendant’s
request for services from each of the Plaintiffs was both express and implied.
(Mot., p. 6.) Plaintiffs argue that Defendant’s Complaint in the federal
lawsuit reveals that Defendant expressly requested Plaintiffs’ services and at
the very least was aware of the benefit being incurred. (Ibid.)
Moreover, Plaintiffs argue that Defendant was aware and intended to benefit
from Plaintiffs remediating the property. (Ibid.) 



Here, Plaintiffs do not establish
quantum meruit. The service request executed by Defendant displays an express
request for restoration services from ServiceMaster. (Benjamin Decl., Exh. 1.)
Moreover, in Defendant’s federal lawsuit against the City of Los Angeles and
United States, Defendant alleges that they were “[required] to obtain hazmat
cleaning, environmental cleaning and repairs on the Property caused by the
deployment of the twenty-five rounds of gas cannisters”. (Benjamin Decl., Exh.
5, ¶ 9.) However, Plaintiffs do not establish a request between Defendant and
each of the Plaintiffs specifically. No connection is formed between the
purported service request with ServiceMaster and each of the Plaintiffs
invoices. Moreover, Benjamin’s witness testimony in Defendant’s federal lawsuit
only confirms amounts charged, the line of questioning does not confirm that
Defendant requested services from each of the Plaintiffs. (Benjamin Decl., Exh.
6.) Although Defendant logically intended to benefit from these services
because these repairs would make the Property usable and profitable again,
Plaintiff provides no evidence to establish this element. Thus, Plaintiffs have
not established the elements of quantum meruit.



Plaintiffs have not met their
burden. Thus, summary adjudication is denied as to the fourth, fifth, and sixth
causes of action.



Since Plaintiffs have not
established judicial estoppel applies as to each cause of action, the motion
for summary judgment is DENIED. The motion for summary adjudication is DENIED
as to each cause of action.



 



CONCLUSION AND ORDER



Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment and summary
adjudication is DENIED.

BACKGROUND

This is a personal injury case. On May 15, 2023, Plaintiff Albert Valle (“Plaintiff”) alleges to have suffered third-degree burns after he tripped on debris, miscellaneous items, and/or an exposed concrete slab at 9867 Milbur Dr., Sun Valley, CA 91352 (the “Property”) and hot tar spilled on him. (Compl., Prem. L-1.) Defendants Eduard Barakyozyan (“Barakyozyan”), Adrine Isayan (“Isayan”), Albert Iskhanyuan, and Albert Iskhanyan (“Defendants”) allegedly owned, leased, occupied, or controlled the Property. (Ibid.)

On August 18, 2023, Plaintiff filed his Complaint.

On September 18, 2025, Plaintiff propounded Special Interrogatories, Set Two (“Special Interrogatories”) and Requests for Production of Documents, Set Four (“Production of Documents”) on Isayan and Barakyozyan. (Ducey Decl., ¶ 3.)

After receiving extensions, Isayan and Barakyozyan failed to respond to discovery by the December 3, 2025 deadline. (Id. at ¶ 13.)

On February 13, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Motion to Compel Verified Responses to Special Interrogatories, Set Two, from Adrine Isayan and a Motion to Compel Verified Responses to Requests for Production of Documents, Set Four from Barakyozyan (“Motion to Compel”). No opposition has been filed to the Motion to Compel.

On March 9, 2026, Isayan and Barakyozyan filed a Motion for Relief from Waiver of Objections (“Motion for Relief”). No opposition has been filed.

 

MOTION FOR RELIEF FROM WAIVER OF OBJECTIONS

  1. Legal Standard

The party to whom the interrogatories are directed waives any right to exercise the option to produce writings under Section 2030.230, as well as any objection to the interrogatories, including one based on privilege or on the protection for work product under Chapter 4 (commencing with Section 2018.010). (Civ. Proc. Code, § 2030.290, subd. (a).) The court, on motion, may relieve that party from this waiver on its determination that both of the following conditions are satisfied: (1) The party has subsequently served a response that is in substantial compliance with Sections 2030.210, 2030.220, 2030.230, and 2030.240; and (2) The party's failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Ibid.)

The party to whom a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling has been directed shall respond separately to each item or category of item by any of the following: (1) a statement that the party will comply with the particular demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling by the date set for the inspection, copying, testing, or sampling pursuant to paragraph (2) of subdivision (c) of Section 2031.030 and any related activities; (2) a representation that the party lacks the ability to comply with the demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling of a particular item or category of item; or (3) an objection to the particular demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling. (Civ. Proc. Code, § 2031.210, subd. (a).)

Similarly, the party to whom the demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling is directed waives any objection to the demand, including one based on privilege or on the protection for work product under Chapter 4 (commencing with Section 2018.010). (Id. at Civ. Proc. Code, § 2031.300, subd. (a).) The court, on motion, may relieve that party from this waiver on its determination that both of the following conditions are satisfied: (1) The party has subsequently served a response that is in substantial compliance with Sections 2030.210, 2030.220, 2030.230, and 2030.240; and (2) The party's failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Ibid.)

The party to whom interrogatories have been propounded shall respond in writing under oath separately to each interrogatory by any of the following: (1) An answer containing the information sought to be discovered; (2) An exercise of the party's option to produce writings; or (3) An objection to the particular interrogatory. (Civ. Proc. Code, § 2030.210, subd. (a).)

 

  1. Discussion

Isayan and Barakyozyan move for an order granting relief from waiver of objections as to Special Interrogatories, Set Two (“Special Interrogatories”) and Requests for Production of Documents, Set Four (“Production of Documents”) on Isayan and Barakyozyan.

Here, the Court finds that Isayan and Barakyozyan’s failure to timely serve responses resulted from mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Isayan and Barakyozyan demonstrated that their failure to serve responses by the December 3, 2025, deadline resulted from an inadvertent calendaring oversight in counsel’s office. (Ducey Decl., ¶ 13.) The calendaring clerk was on a pre-scheduled international vacation from November 26, 2025 through December 5, 2025. (Ibid.) Thus, Isayan and Barakyozyan’s failure to respond was due to mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.

Isayan and Barakyozyan have filed and served responses in the form of objections to the Production of Documents and Special Interrogatories. (Id. at ¶ 20-23, Exh. I-L.) Because sections 2031.210 and 2030.210 permit objections as a form of response, Isayan and Barakyozyan have served a substantially compliant response. Accordingly, the Motion for Relief is granted. Isayan and Barakyozyan are ordered to serve substantive responses within 10 days.

 

MOTION TO COMPEL

  1. Legal Standard

A party must respond to interrogatories within 30 days after service. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.260, subd. (a).) If a party to whom interrogatories are directed fails to serve a timely response, the party propounding the interrogatories may move for an order compelling response to the interrogatories. (Civ. Proc. Code, § 2030.290, subd. (b).) The party also waives the right to make any objections, including one based on privilege or work-product protection. (Id. at § 2030.290, subd. (a).) There is no time limit for a motion to compel responses to interrogatories other than the cut-off on hearing discovery motions 15 days before trial. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2024.020, subd. (a), 2030.290.) No meet and confer efforts are required before filing a motion to compel responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290; Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 411.)

The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. (Civ. Proc. Code, § 2030.290, subd. (c).) In lieu of or in addition to that sanction, the court may impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010). (Ibid.)

 

  1. Discussion

Plaintiff seeks to compel Isayan to respond to Special Interrogatories and seeks monetary sanctions because Plaintiff contends that Isayan failed to timely respond to the Special Interrogatories by the deadline. Because the Court grants the Motion for Relief and orders Defendants to serve verified responses to Special Interrogatories and Production of Documents, Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Isayan to respond to Special Interrogatories is moot and denied as a whole. Consequently, Plaintiff’s Request for Monetary Sanctions is also denied.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendants Eduard Barakyozyan and Adrine Isayan’s Motion for Relief from Waiver of Objections is GRANTED.

Defendants Eduard Barakyozyan and Adrine Isayan are ordered to serve verified responses to Special Interrogatories, Set Two, and Requests for Production of Documents, Set Four on Isayan and Barakyozyan within 10 days of the Court’s order.

Because the Court is granting the Motion for Relief from Waiver of Objections and ordering responses, the Motion to Compel Verified Responses to Special Interrogatories, Set Two, and Request for Monetary Sanctions from Defendant Adrine Isayan is DENIED.

BACKGROUND

LEGAL STANDARD

            Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480 provides in pertinent part: 

            “(a) If a deponent fails to answer any question or to produce any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing under the deponent's control that is specified in the        deposition notice or a deposition subpoena, the party seeking discovery may move the         court for an order compelling that answer or production.

            (c) Notice of this motion shall be given to all parties and to the deponent either orally at           the examination, or by subsequent service in writing. If the notice of the motion is given     orally, the deposition officer shall direct the deponent to attend a session of the court at          the time specified in the notice.”

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subds. (a)-(c).)

DISCUSSION           

a.     Procedural Issue

As a preliminary matter, the Court notes Dr. Khalili has made an improper and informal request for a continuance rather than filing a properly noticed ex parte application. Accordingly, the Court will not grant a continuance and will hear the motion on its merits.

Between February 23, 2026, and March 26, 2026, the parties were in email communication regarding Dr. Khalili’s deposition. On March 23, 2026, Dr. Khalili’s office confirmed they received the check but had to reschedule the deposition. (Babadjanian Decl., ¶ 21, Ex. V.) BACKGROUND 

This is a personal injury case. On January 16, 2024, plaintiff Annalisa Wilson (Plaintiff) filed the operative Complaint asserting a single cause of action for Dangerous Condition of Public Property pursuant to Government Code sections 815.2 and 835 against defendants Burbank-Glendale-Pasadena Airport Authority (Defendant), City of Burbank, County of Los Angeles, State of California, California Department of Transportation, and DOES 1 to 50, inclusive. On September 3, 2024, Plaintiff dismissed City of Burbank from the action. On September 13, 2024, Plaintiff dismissed State of California, California Department of Transportation, and County of Los Angeles from the action. Plaintiff maintains the action only against Defendant. 

Plaintiff alleges she sustained injuries while walking at or near a sidewalk from Terminal B of the Hollywood Burbank Airport. Defendant filed its Answer on September 26, 2024.

On March 27, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion seeking to amend their answer. On June 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition. On July 2, 2026, Defendant filed a Reply.

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civ. Proc. § 473(a)(1), provides, in relevant part: “The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect; and may, upon like terms, enlarge the time for answer or demurrer.  The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.” 

Under California Rules of Court Rule, Rule 3.1324, subdivision (a), a motion to amend a pleading shall: 

(1) include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments; 

(2) state what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and 

(3) state what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.  


In addition, under California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324, subdivision (b), a separate declaration must accompany the motion and must specify:  

(1) the effect of the amendment; 

(2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; 

(3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and 

(4) the reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier. 

  

The Court’s discretion to grant leave “should be exercised liberally in favor of amendments, for judicial policy favors resolution of all disputed matters in the same lawsuit.”  (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1047.) Ordinarily, the Court will not consider the validity of the proposed amended pleading in ruling on a motion for leave since grounds for a demurrer or motion to strike are premature.  The Court, however, does have discretion to deny leave to amend where a proposed amendment fails to state a valid cause of action as a matter of law and the defect cannot be cured by further amendment. (See California Casualty General Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1985) 173 Cal.App.3d 274, 281 (overruled on other grounds by Kransco v. American Empire Surplus Lines Ins. Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 390).) 

DISCUSSION

Defendant has provided the Court with a proposed amended Answer adding two affirmative defenses. (See McGarry Decl., Exh. J.) Defendant has specified and stated the two affirmative defenses to be added to the pleading. (Mot., p. 8; McGarry Decl., Exh. J.) 

Defendant argues that the proposed affirmative defense under Government Code, section 830.8 would apply to Plaintiff’s allegations that Defendant failed to warn of the allegedly dangerous condition as public entities are immune from liability for injuries caused by a lack of traffic signs or warnings. (Mot., pp. 9-10.) Defendant argues that proposed affirmative defense under Government Code, section 831 may be applicable because public entities are immune from liability for injuries caused by the effects of weather. (Mot., 10.)

Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s delay in filing the instant motion is inexcusable. (Opp., p. 3.) Plaintiff argues that Defendant was aware of the facts underpinning this amendment prior to discovery and amendment at this stage would be unfairly prejudicial as Plaintiff has framed their case around the original answer and may be required to expend additional resources to address the amendment. (Ibid.)  

In reply, Defendant argues that Plaintiff has identified no concrete prejudice arising from the proposed amendment and merely speculates that additional resources might be required. (Reply, p. 2.) Moreover, Defendant argues that the facts underpinning the amendment were revealed during discovery and Defendant acted diligently in filing the instant motion after the relevant facts had developed. (Ibid.) 

Defendant provides the Court with a separate declaration stating the effect of the amendment, why it is necessary, when the facts giving rise to the amendment were discovered, and why amendment was not made earlier. Defendant has declared that the Answer seeks to add two immunities that may apply under the circumstances. (McGarry Decl., ¶ 9.) Defendant declares that discovery provided facts which may support additional affirmative defenses. (Ibid.) Defendant alleges that Plaintiff provided inconsistent narratives concerning the nature and location of her fall, provided varying accounts as to whether she fell while descending from a curb, walking in a crosswalk, or while ascending onto a curb. (Ibid.) Defendant further alleges that Plaintiff did not mention the weather or its alleged role in causing her fall in her operative Complaint. (Ibid.) The facts underpinning the amendment were discovered following Plaintiff’s October 2025 deposition and December 2025 independent medical examination, which is why amendment to add the proposed affirmative defenses was not made earlier. (Ibid.) 

Here, Plaintiff will suffer no prejudice due to Defendant’s amended Answer. Although Plaintiff argues that Defendant substantially delayed filing the instant motion to amend their answer, Defendant filed the instant motion in March of 2026, three months after discovering the facts which support it. (McGarry Decl., ¶ 9.) Moreover, any additional discovery required of Plaintiff would not be substantial as they allege to have been aware of the facts underlying the amendment from the inception of the case. (Hakim Decl., ¶ 6.)

Plaintiff relies on P & D Consultants, Inc., which held that the trial court did not err in denying leave to amend where plaintiff waited until after the trial readiness conference to attempt amending the complaint and offered no reason for the substantial delay. (P & D Consultants, Inc. v. City of Carlsbad (2010) 190 Cal.App.4th 1332.) However, the instant case concerns an amended reply, which is substantially dissimilar from an amended complaint proposed after the trial readiness conference. Moreover, Defendant’s motion is made well in advance of trial and Defendant has provided an explanation for the delay, unlike the amending party in P & D Consultants, Inc. 

Additionally, Defendant conferred with Plaintiff to discuss proposed affirmative defenses to be added to amended Answer on February 10, 2026. (McGarry Decl., ¶ 10.) Plaintiff was put on notice.

Accordingly, Defendant satisfied the statutory requirements for leave to amend pleading.

BACKGROUND

On or about December 12, 2023, Plaintiff Joseph A. Carnahan (“Carnahan”) leased a Porsche automobile from Porsche Downtown LA through Porsche Financial Services (“Porsche”). (SAC, ¶ 6.) Carnahan developed a romantic relationship with Defendant Michelle Crosby (“Crosby”) and loaned her the Porsche. (Id. at ¶ 8.) On or about March 6, 2024, Carnahan revoked the loan and requested return of the Porsche. (Id. at ¶ 9.) Crosby did not return the Porsche and turned off the factory-installed tracking system. (Id. at ¶ 10.) On September 21, 2024, Carnahan was notified that the Porsche was impounded by the Beverly Hills Police Department. ((Id. at ¶ 12.) Carnahan was able to recover possession of the Porsche and discovered extensive damage. (Id. at ¶ 12, 13.)

On July 31, 2024, Carnahan filed a Complaint. On July 30, 2025, Carnahan filed the operative Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) alleging (1) conversion and (2) harassment.

On September 5, 2025, Crosby filed a Cross-Complaint.

On March 17, 2026, Carnahan filed this Motion for Leave to File Third Amended Complaint (“Motion”).

On July 6, 2026, Carnahan filed a late reply.

LEGAL STANDARD

            The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect; and may, upon like terms, enlarge the time for answer or demurrer. The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code. (Civ. Proc. Code § 473, subd. (a)(1).)

            It is the general rule that amendments to pleadings should be liberally allowed. (Simons v. Kern Cnty. (1965) 234 Cal.App.2d 362, 367.) If the motion to amend is timely made and the granting of the motion will not prejudice the opposing party, it is error to refuse permission to amend, and where the refusal also results in a party being deprived of the right to assert a meritorious cause of action or a meritorious defense, it is not only error but an abuse of discretion. (Morgan v. Superior Ct. of Cal. In & For Los Angeles County (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 527, 530.) It is an abuse of discretion to deny leave to amend where the opposing party was not misled or prejudiced by the amendment. (Atkinson v. Elk Corp. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 739, 761.) “Although courts are bound to apply a policy of great liberality in permitting amendments to the complaint at any stage of the proceedings, up to and including trial, this policy should be applied only where no prejudice is shown to the adverse party. (Ibid.) If the proposed amendment fails to state a cause of action, it is proper to deny leave to amend. (Foxborough v. Van Atta (1994) 26 Cal.App.4th 217, 220.)

According to California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324, a motion to amend a pleading before trial must: (1) Include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments; (2) State what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and (3) State what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324.) A separate declaration must accompany the motion and must specify: (1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier. (Ibid.)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                                

According to California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324, a motion to amend a pleading before trial must: (1) Include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments; (2) State what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and (3) State what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324.) A separate declaration must accompany the motion and must specify: (1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier. (Ibid.)

            Here, Carnahan clearly states the allegations that are proposed to be added, changed, or removed and the locations of those changes. Carnahan seeks to amend the SAC by (1) re-captioning it the Third Amended Complaint; (2) adding Defamation and Intentional Interference with Contractual Relation causes of action to the caption; (3) identifying Plaintiff as “Plaintiff and Cross-Defendant” and Defendant as “Defendant and Cross-Complainant”; (4) insert additional causes of action for Defamation and Intentional Interference with Contractual Relations to include paragraph 32 through 43; and (5) revise the prayer for relief to reflect and correspond the changes in the causes of action. Carnahan also included a copy of the proposed Third Amended Complaint (“TAC”) as an exhibit with the Motion. (See Ex. 1.)

            However, the declaration attached with the Motion does not describe (1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier. (See Slavkin Decl.) The declaration merely states the amendments Carnahan seeks to add to the proposed pleadings. (Ibid.) Thus, the declaration did not comply with Rule 3.1324.

            Accordingly, Carnahan’s Motion did not meet all the requirements of Rule 3.1324. As Carnahan did not comply with Rule 3.1324, the Court denies Carnahan's Motion. (See Hataishi v. First Am. Home Buyers Prot. Corp. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 1454, 1469 [finding no abuse of discretion in the trial court requiring Plaintiff to bring a motion, compliant with the Rules of Court].)

In opposition, Crosby argues both proposed additions fail to state a cause of action. As to defamation, Carnahan does not identify the alleged statement’s substance or specific words. Moreover, the statements mirror Cross-Complaint alleges protected by the litigation privilege. As to intentional interference with contractual relations, Crosby contends that the proposed pleading does not identify a contract or a disruption of that contract. Finally, Crosby argues that the proposed pleading does not coherently state the relief it seeks on the claims it seeks to add.

In reply, Carnahan argues the proposed additions do not fail to state causes of action. As to defamation, Carnahan argues he identifies the substance of the statements alleged, if not the exact words. Carnahan also argues that the litigation privilege does not apply. As to intentional interference with contractual relations, Carnahan argues that Crosby’s argument that this cause of action is deficient is entirely based on Crosby’s conclusion that the Defamation cause of action is deficient.

Here, the Court does not need to address these arguments as Carnahan failed to comply with Rule 3.1324. Even so, arguments as to the sufficiency of pleadings should be argued through the filing of a demurrer, rather than on a motion for leave to amend.

Accordingly, Carnahan’s Motion is denied.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Carnahan’s Motion for Leave to File Third Amended Complaint is denied.

 LEGAL
STANDARD



            A party may propound 35 specially prepared
interrogatories that are relevant to the subject matter of the pending action. (Civ.
Proc. Code, § 2030.030, subd. (a)(1).) Subject to the right of the responding
party to seek a protective order under Section 2030.090, any party who attaches
a supporting declaration as described in Section 2030.050 may propound a
greater number of specially prepared interrogatories to another party if this
greater number is warranted because of any of the following:



(1) The complexity or the quantity of the existing and potential issues
in the particular case.



(2) The financial burden on a party entailed in conducting the discovery
by oral deposition.



(3) The expedience of using this method of discovery to provide to the
responding party the opportunity to conduct an inquiry, investigation, or
search of files or records to supply the information sought.



If the responding party seeks a protective order on the ground that the
number of specially prepared interrogatories is unwarranted, the propounding
party shall have the burden of justifying the number of these interrogatories.
(Civ. Proc. Code, § 2030.040, subd. (a)-(b).) When
interrogatories have been propounded, the responding party, and any other party
or affected natural person or organization may promptly move for a protective
order. This motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under
Section 2016.040. (Civ. Proc. Code, § 2030.090, subd. (a).)



The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires
to protect any party or other natural person or organization from unwarranted
annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense. This
protective order may include, but is not limited to, one or more of the
following directions: (1) That the set of interrogatories, or particular
interrogatories in the set, need not be answered. (2) That, contrary to the
representations made in a declaration submitted under Section 2030.050, the
number of specially prepared interrogatories is unwarranted. (Id. at §
2030.090, subd. (b)(1)-(2).) The court shall impose a monetary sanction under
Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or
attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion for a protective order
under this section, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted
with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition
of the sanction unjust. (Id. at § 2030.090, subd. (d).)

Analysis

is the propounding party’s burden to justify the number of special
interrogatories. (Civ. Proc. Code §
2030.040.) The greater number is warranted due to (1) complexity or quantity of
the issues; (2) financial burden in conducting oral deposition; or (3) the
expedience in using this method of discovery. (Ibid.) The declaration
must describe the “reasons why any factor relied on is applicable to the
lawsuit.” (Civ. Proc. Code § 2030.050.)

has not met his burden of justifying 429 special interrogatories. Carnahan
lists all three factors in attempting to justify the amount of special
interrogatories. (Ex. 7, Slavkin Decl., ¶ 8.) Carnahan merely states that the case

13.) However, Carnahan reasons this is for efficiency and to allow inquiry into

specific lawsuit. While the Court agrees that written discovery is a useful
tool in preparation of deposition, Carnahan’s reasoning does not justify the
429 special interrogatories propounded rather than the statutory amount of 35.
Nor does Carnahan’s conclusive statements that the special interrogatories are
not cumulative or burdensome and are narrowly tailored strengthen this
argument.

MOTION FOR TERMINATING SANCTIONS

Hearing Date: 7/10/26¿ 

CASE NO./NAME: 25NNCV01805 William King III et al. v. San Fernando Subacute Rehabilitation Center, LLC, et al.

Moving Party: Defendants San Fernando Subacute Center, LLC dba Golden Legacy Care Center, Antonio Moya, Jr., and Christine Maloles

Responding Party: Unopposed

Notice: Sufficient

Ruling: GRANT

 

NOTICE

The Court is not requesting oral argument on this matter.  The Court is guided by California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1308(a)(1) whereby notice of intent to appear is requested.  Unless the Court directs argument in the Tentative Ruling, no argument is requested and any party seeking argument should notify all other parties and the court by 4:00 p.m. on the court day before the hearing of the party’s intention to appear and argue.  The tentative ruling will become the ruling of the court if no argument is received.  

Notice may be given either by email at [email protected] or by telephone at (818) 260-8422.

 

BACKGROUND

This is a wrongful death case. On March 13, 2025, Plaintiffs William King III, individually and as successor-in-interest to William King and Brian Christopher King, individually (collectively, Plaintiffs) filed a Complaint against defendants San Fernando Subacute Rehabilitation Center, LLC dba Golden Legacy Care Center, RG Legacy I, LLC dba Golden Rose Care Center, Antonia Moya Jr., Christine Maloles, Ragini Kaur, Erickzen Alibadbad (collectively, Defendants) and DOES 1 through 250, inclusive.

On November 24, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a First Amended Complaint alleging causes of action for (1) elderly adult abuse by neglect, (2) negligence, and (3) violation of Patient’s Bill of Rights (Health and Safety Code § 1430 subd. (b)), (4) wrongful death – elder abuse by neglect, and (5) wrongful death (negligence).

On March 13, 2026, the Court sustained Defendants’ demurrer to the first, third, fourth, and fifth causes of action. The cause of action for negligence moved forward. On April 10, 2026, Defendants filed an Answer.

On June 2, 2026, defendants San Fernando Subacute Center, LLC dba Golden Legacy Care Center, Antonio Moya, Jr., and Christine Maloles (hereinafter, Movants) filed the instant motion for terminating sanctions based on Plaintiffs’ repeated failures to comply with discovery and the Court’s prior orders.

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure § 2023.030 gives the court the discretion to impose sanctions against anyone engaging in a misuse of the discovery process. Misuse of the discovery process includes failure to respond to an authorized method of discovery or disobeying a court order to provide discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subds. (d), (g).) A court may impose terminating sanctions by striking pleadings of the party engaged in misuse of discovery or entering default judgment. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (d).) A violation of a discovery order is sufficient for the imposition of terminating sanctions. (Collison & Kaplan v. Hartunian (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 1611, 1620.) Terminating sanctions are appropriate when a party persists in disobeying the court’s orders. (Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 795-796.)

The court should consider the totality of the circumstances, including conduct of the party to determine if the actions were willful, the detriment to the propounding party, and the number of formal and informal attempts to obtain discovery. (Lang v. Hochman (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1225, 1246.) If a lesser sanction fails to curb abuse, a greater sanction is warranted. (Van Sickle v. Gilbert (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1495, 1516.) However, “the unsuccessful imposition of a lesser sanction is not an absolute prerequisite to the utilization of the ultimate sanction.” (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 787.) Before any sanctions may be imposed the court must make an express finding that there has been a willful failure of the party to serve the required answers. (Fairfield v. Superior Court for Los Angeles County (1966) 246 Cal.App.2d 113, 118.) Lack of diligence may be deemed willful where the party understood its obligation, had the ability to comply, and failed to comply. (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 787; Fred Howland Co. v. Superior Court of Los Angeles County (1966) 244 Cal.App.2d 605, 610-611.) The party who failed to comply with discovery obligations has the burden of showing that the failure was not willful. (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 788; Cornwall v. Santa Monica Dairy Co. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 250; Evid. Code, §§ 500, 605.)

A terminating sanction is a “drastic measure which should be employed with caution.” (Deyo, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 793.) “A decision to order terminating sanctions should not be made lightly. But where a violation is willful, preceded by a history of abuse, and the evidence shows that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with the discovery rules, the trial court is justified in imposing the ultimate sanction.” (Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsystem (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 262, 279-280.) While the court has discretion to impose terminating sanctions, these sanctions “should be appropriate to the dereliction and should not exceed that which is required to protect the interests of the party entitled to but denied discovery.” (Deyo, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 793.) “[A] court is empowered to apply the ultimate sanction against a litigant who persists in the outright refusal to comply with his discovery obligations.” (Ibid.)
DISCUSSION

Movants argue that Plaintiffs have willfully and repeatedly refused to comply with the Court’s order. (Mot., p. 4.) Movants argue that Plaintiffs have also refused to attend any Court ordered hearings. (Ibid.) Movants argue that they have been forced to incur costs in responding to the Complaint, serving discovery, filing several motions to compel responses due to Plaintiffs’ refusal to participate in the litigation process. (Ibid.) Movants argue that a less severe sanction would not produce compliance with the discovery rules because Plaintiffs’ violations are willful. (Mot., p. 5.)

Here, the Court finds Plaintiffs repeated violations were willful. On December 19, 2025, Plaintiff made their last appearance. (Eng Decl., ¶ 2.) Movants counsel, Kevin Eng, declares that Plaintiff William King, III stated in open court that he wished to dismiss the case. (Ibid.) On December 29, 2025, Plaintiff did not appear at the hearing for their former counsel’s Motion to be Relieved. (Eng Decl., ¶ 3.) Following this hearing, Defendants counsel provided Plaintiff with Requests for Dismissal to sign and return, Plaintiff did not respond. (Eng Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 4.) On February 2, 2026, Plaintiff was served by mail and email with written discovery. (Eng Decl., ¶ 4.) Responses were due March 6, 2026, however Defendants have not yet received a response. (Ibid.) On March 11, 2026, Plaintiffs were served by mail and email with a meet and confer letter regarding written discovery, Plaintiffs did not respond. (Eng Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. 5.) On May 8, 2026, Movants’ motions to compel responses to form and special interrogatories (Interrogatories) and requests for production of documents (RPD) were granted by the Court. (Eng Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. 1.) On May 13, 2026, Plaintiff failed to appear at a hearing for order to show cause regarding sanctions and order to show cause regarding dismissal for failure to timely comply with default. (Eng Decl., ¶ 6. Exh. 2.)

Since December of 2025, Plaintiffs have refused to appear in 3 consecutive hearings, refused to participate in the discovery process, and refused to comply with the Court’s orders to respond to discovery requests. (Eng Decl., ¶¶ 2-6.) Plaintiffs have also failed to respond to any communications from Movants’ counsel. (Ibid.) Thus, the Court finds repeated violations a willful abandonment of the litigation process.

The burden then shifts to Plaintiff to show the failures were not willful. (Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 788; Cornwall v. Santa Monica Dairy Co. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 250; Evid. Code, §§ 500, 605.) Plaintiff filed no opposition and therefore made no showing that the repeated failures to appear were not willful.

The Court finds lesser sanctions would not be effective. Although California law favors an incremental approach to sanctions, that approach has already been attempted here. Plaintiff failed to appear on multiple occasions.

The evidence also suggests Plaintiffs abandoned the action in December of 2025. Since then, Plaintiffs have neither appeared nor otherwise participated in the case. Under these circumstances, the Court finds lesser sanctions would not secure compliance and that terminating sanctions are warranted.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Movants San Fernando Subacute Center, LLC dba Golden Legacy Care Center, Antonio Moya, Jr., and Christine Maloles’s motion for terminating sanctions is GRANTED.

Movants shall provide notice of this order.

BACKGROUND

This is a personal injury case arising from a motor vehicle accident that occurred on July 31, 2025. On September 4, 2025, Plaintiff Marvin Wright (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against defendant Cecilia Navarrete (Defendant) alleging personal injury. On October 17, 2025, Defendant filed an Answer.

On April 21, 2026, the Court continued the hearings on the motions to compel special interrogatories and RPD from May 15, 2026, to May 22, 2026. The hearing on the motion to deem RFA admitted was originally set for May 22, 2026.  

On May 11, 2026, the Court continued all three hearings on the instant motions from May 22, 2026, to July 10, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

A party must respond to interrogatories within 30 days after service. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.260, subd. (a).) If a party to whom interrogatories are directed does not provide timely responses, the requesting party may move for an order compelling responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (b).) The party also waives the right to make any objections, including one based on privilege or work-product protection. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).) There is no time limit for a motion to compel responses to interrogatories other than the cut-off on hearing discovery motions 15 days before trial. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2024.020, subd. (a), 2030.290.) No meet and confer efforts are required before filing a motion to compel responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290; Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 411.)

A party must respond to requests for production of documents within 30 days after service. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.260, subd. (a).) If a party to whom requests for production of documents are directed does not provide timely responses, the requesting party may move for an order compelling responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (b).) The party also waives the right to make any objections, including one based on privilege or work-product protection. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).) There is no time limit for a motion to compel responses to production of documents other than the cut-off on hearing discovery motions 15 days before trial. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.020, subd. (a), 2031.300.) No meet and confer efforts are required before filing a motion to compel responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300; Sinaiko, supra, 148 Cal.App.4th at 411.)

A party must respond to requests for admissions within 30 days after service of such requests. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.250, subd. (a).)  “If a party to whom requests for admission are directed fails to serve a timely response…(a) [that party] waives any objection to the requests, including one based on privilege or on the protection for work product…” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (a).) “The requesting party may move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the requests be deemed admitted, as well as for a monetary sanction under Chapter 7.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (b).) 

DISCUSSION           

Special Interrogatories, Request for Production of Documents, and Requests for Admission

Plaintiff contends that Defendant failed to timely respond to the Special Interrogatories, RPDs, and RFAs on or by the statutory deadline. (Mot. to Compel Special Interrogatories, p. 3; Mot. to Compel RPDs, p. 3; Mot. to Deem RFAs Admitted, p. 3.)

Here, Plaintiff’s counsel served Defendant with the Special Interrogatories, RPDs, and RFAs by electronic service on October 21, 2025. (Omofoma Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.) On November 23, 2025, a day before the original deadline, Defendant’s counsel requested a two-week extension, and Plaintiff’s counsel granted the extension. (Omofoma Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. B.) On December 8, 2025, the day responses were due under the first extension, Defendant’s counsel asked for a second extension and Plaintiff’s counsel granted the second extension making responses due December 15, 2025. (Omofoma Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. B.) As of January 16, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel had not received any responses, despite giving Defendant several extensions by request and as a professional courtesy. (Omofoma Decl., ¶¶ 5-6.) 

Since Defendant was properly served and Defendant has failed to respond to discovery requests by the statutory deadline and has failed to respond after multiple extensions, Plaintiff is entitled to an order compelling Defendant to provide responses without objections to the Special Interrogatories and RPDs and an order deeming RFAs admitted.

Sanctions

Sanctions are mandatory against the party, the person, or the attorney whose failure to serve a timely response to the request necessitated the motion to deem RFA as admitted. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(c).) The Court shall impose sanction on the party, the person, or the attorney whose failure to serve a timely response to the request necessitated the motion to compel responses to interrogatories or RPD. (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.300(c), 2030.290(c); see also Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1348(a) [the court can award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party seeking to compel discovery even though no opposition was filed, the opposition was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed].)

“A request for a sanction shall, in the notice of motion, identify every person, party, and attorney against whom the sanction is sought, and specify the type of sanction sought. The notice of motion shall be supported by a memorandum of points and authorities and accompanied by a declaration setting forth facts supporting the amount of any monetary sanction sought.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.040.)

The Court finds Plaintiff’s Notice of Motion for each motion complies with Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.040. Here, Defendant failed to serve timely responses to Plaintiff’s Special Interrogatories, RPD, and RFA by the statutory deadline. Thus, sanctions are warranted against Defendant. (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290(c), 2031.300(c), 2033.280(c); see also Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1348(a).)

Plaintiffs request monetary sanctions of $1,800, based on $600 for each motion, reflecting one hour incurred on preparing each motion at an hourly rate of $600 per hour, plus the $60.00 filing fee for all Motions. (Omofoma Decl., ¶ 8.)

Given the similarity of the motions to each other, the shared hearing date, the lack of Oppositions and Replies, and the straightforward nature of the Motions, Plaintiffs’ request for monetary sanctions against defendant Lockwood is granted in total amount of $900, reflecting 1.5 hours incurred at $600 per hour.

Hearing Date: 7/10/26

CASE NO./NAME: 26NNCV00784 Karl Marsh, et al. v. City of Glendale

Moving Party: Defendant City of Glendale

Responding Party: Plaintiffs Karl Marsh and Mai Ly Marsh

Notice: Sufficient 

Ruling: SUSTAINED without leave to amend

 

NOTICE:

The Court is not requesting oral argument on this matter.  The Court is guided by California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1308(a)(1) whereby notice of intent to appear is requested.  Unless the Court directs argument in the Tentative Ruling, no argument is requested and any party seeking argument should notify all other parties and the court by 4:00 p.m. on the court day before the hearing of the party’s intention to appear and argue.  The tentative ruling will become the ruling of the court if no argument is received.  

Notice may be given either by email at [email protected] or by telephone at (818) 260-8422.

 

BACKGROUND

This is a dangerous condition of public property action. Plaintiffs Karl Marsh and Mai Ly Marsh (collectively Plaintiffs) reside at 1261 Cordova Ave., Glendale California 91207. (Complaint, ¶ 11.) Plaintiffs complained to defendant The City of Glendale (Defendant) about a dangerous condition, a leaning tree, existing on public property adjacent to Plaintiffs residential address. (Complaint, ¶ 12.) However, Defendant did not repair the tree and on January 7, 2025, the tree fell onto Plaintiff’s residential property causing significant damage. (Complaint, ¶¶ 13-14.)

On February 2, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the operative Complaint asserting two causes of action for (1) negligence, and (2) dangerous condition of public property. On March 19, 2026, Defendant filed the instant demurrer to the first cause of action for negligence. On June 26, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an opposition. On July 2, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a reply. 

 

LEGAL STANDARD

            “[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

Further, the court may, upon motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false, or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

 

DISCUSSION           

Defendant demurs to the first cause of action for Negligence asserted by Plaintiffs for failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. Specifically, Defendant argues that because they are a public entity, they are not liable for tortious injury unless imposed by statute. (Dem., p. 4.) Defendant further argues that they are not subject to vicarious liability under section 815.2 subdivision (a). (Dem., p. 5.) Defendant argues that the proper cause of action under this set of facts, dangerous condition of public property, has already been brought by Plaintiffs. (Ibid.)

In California, all government tort liability must be based on statute. (Rodriguez v. Inglewood Unified School Dist. (1986) 186 Cal.App.3d 707, 716.) Here, Plaintiffs allege a cause of action for negligence under section 815.2 subdivision (a) on the theory that if Defendant were not a public entity, their actions would have given rise to liability. (Gov. Code § 815.2(a).)

Plaintiffs argue that they have pled facts sufficient to state a claim for negligence. (Opp., p. 3.) Plaintiffs argue that Defendant owned and controlled the tree, received notice of the dangerous condition, failed to remedy it, and the tree subsequently fell onto Plaintiffs’ residence causing damage. (Opp. p. 4.) Plaintiffs argue that whether or not Defendant is subject to liability is a substantive determination, not a question of the sufficiency of the pleadings. (Ibid.) In reply, Defendant argues that the claim cannot be successfully stated because a public entity such as a City cannot be held liable on a theory of common law negligence. (Reply, p. 1.)

In Van Kempen, a work bench owned by a park, a public entity, was leaning against a wall and fell on the minor plaintiff injuring his leg. Plaintiff in that case argued that the park was vicariously liable under section 815.2. However, the court in Van Kemper stated that the argument was misplaced and held that “public entity liability for property defects is not governed by the general rule of vicarious liability provided in section 815.2, but instead by the specific provisions set forth in sections 830-835.4”, which is the statute governing dangerous conditions of public property. (Van Kempen v. Hayward Area Park Etc. Dist. (1972) 23 Cal.App.3d 822, 825.) The proper cause of action under these facts is dangerous condition of public property. Plaintiffs have already stated the cause of action for dangerous condition of public property in the Complaint. (Complaint, ¶¶ 23-27.)

Defendant, a public entity, cannot be held liable under a theory of common law negligence as pleaded by Plaintiffs. Thus, Plaintiffs have failed to state a viable claim for negligence.

An amendment to the first cause of action for negligence cannot cure the defect as common law negligence is not a statute and the City of Glendale is a public entity. Thus, Defendant’s demurrer to the second cause of action for negligence is sustained without leave to amend.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant City of Glendale’s demurrer to the first cause of action for Negligence is sustained without leave to amend.

Defendant City of Glendale shall provide notice of this order.
