DEPARTMENT 400 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 26STCV06196    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO LIFT STAY

 

The Court tenders the following decision in the matter Shannon Yliz v. Empathyhands Homecare LLC, Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 26STCV06196, set for hearing on September 23, 2026. Shannon Yliz (Plaintiff) moves for an order lifting the stay in this matter and withdrawing the case from arbitration pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.98. Plaintiff also requests $4,760 in sanctions. Empathyhands Homecare LLC (Defendant) does not oppose lifting the stay but does oppose the imposition of sanctions. The motion is granted. Plaintiff is awarded $2,950 in sanctions, payable by Defendant.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

“In an employment or consumer arbitration that requires . . . that the drafting party pay certain fees and costs during the pendency of an arbitration proceeding, if the fees or costs required to continue the arbitration proceeding are not paid within 30 days after the due date, the drafting party is in material breach of the arbitration agreement, is in default of the arbitration, and waives its right to compel the employee or consumer to proceed with that arbitration as a result of the material breach.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.98, subd. (a)(1).) If the drafting party is in breach as described in subdivision (a), the employee may “[w]ithdraw the claim from arbitration and proceed in a court of appropriate jurisdiction.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.98, subd. (b)(1).)

 

B.     Discussion


Plaintiff commenced the present suit against Defendant on February 25, 2026. The parties entered a stipulation to arbitrate this proceeding on April 15, 2026. (Santos Decl. ¶ 6, Ex. B.) Thereafter, the parties proceeded with arbitration through Judicate West. (Id. ¶ 9.) Defendant failed to timely pay the arbitration fees and costs required for the arbitration to proceed. (Id. ¶¶ 18–19.) Defendant concedes that it has not paid the fees and that withdrawal from arbitration and the lifting of the stay in this matter is proper. Thus, the Court finds that Defendant has breached the arbitration agreement as defined under Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.98, subdivision (a) by failing to pay the fees required to continue the arbitration proceeding. Accordingly, Plaintiff is entitled to withdraw from arbitration pursuant to subdivision (b)(1) and her motion is granted.

 

Plaintiff requests monetary sanctions with respect to the present motion. “The court shall impose a monetary sanction against a drafting party that materially breaches an arbitration agreement pursuant to . . . subdivision (a) of Section 1281.98, by ordering the drafting party to pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees and costs, incurred by the employee or consumer as a result of the material breach.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.99, subd. (a).) Here, monetary sanctions are proper because of Defendant’s breach of section 1281.98.

 

Plaintiff requests $4,760 in sanctions based on 6 hours of work at a $700 hourly rate, the $500 filing fee Plaintiff paid to initiate arbitration, and the $60 filing fee for the present motion. Defendant challenges this amount as unreasonable under Wilson v. TAP Worldwide, LLC (2025) 114 Cal.App.5th 1077. There, the Court of Appeal held that a party is only entitled to fees incurred “for work no longer having utility given termination of the arbitration proceeding.” (Id. at p. 1083.)

 

Here, the Court credits Plaintiff’s counsel’s $700 hourly rate as reasonable. The Court finds that the $500 fee paid by Plaintiff to initiate the arbitration proceedings constitutes work “no longer having utility” now that the proceeding has concluded due to Defendant’s failure to pay fees and is thus properly compensated through section 1281.99 sanctions. The Court further finds that the alleged $60 filing fee paid by Plaintiff regarding the present motion is not properly compensated because Plaintiff has not provided evidence the fee was paid. Finally, the Court finds that 3.5 hours is a more reasonable estimate of the time spent by counsel preparing the present motion and reply and attending the hearing: 1.5 hours to prepare the relatively simple motion given counsel’s claimed experience, 1 hour to prepare the reply to Defendant’s opposition related only to sanctions, and 1 hour to attend the hearing on this matter. Accordingly, the Court awards $2,950 in sanctions ([$700 per hour] x [3.5 hours] + [$500 arbitration initiation fee] = $2,950).

 

Conclusion:


The motion is granted. Plaintiff is awarded $2,950 in sanctions, payable by Defendant.


Case Number: BC709376    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: 400

DISCOVERY MOTIONS

 

            The Court tenders the following decision in the matter Iman Sadeghi v. Pinscreen Inc., et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number BC709376, set for hearing on September 23, 2026. Iman Sadeghi (Plaintiff), brings the following 8 motions: (1) compelling Defendant Hao Li to serve further responses to his form interrogatories, set two (FROGs), pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300; (2) compelling Defendant Pinscreen, Inc. (Pinscreen) to serve further responses to his FROGs pursuant to section 2030.300; (3) compelling Li to serve further responses to his special interrogatories, set two (SPROGs), pursuant to section 2030.300; (4) compelling Pinscreen to serve further responses to his SPROGs pursuant to section 2030.300; (5) compelling Li to serve further responses to his requests for production, set two (RFPs), pursuant to section 2031.310; (6) compelling Pinscreen to serve further responses to his RFPs pursuant to section 2031.310; (7) compelling Li to comply with the Court’s February 24, 2026, order pursuant to section 2031.320; and (8) compelling Li and Pinscreen (collectively, Defendants) to comply with the Court’s March 16, 2026, order pursuant to section 2031.320. Plaintiff also requests a collective $13,565 in monetary sanctions, along with evidence and issue sanctions. The Court will address each motion in turn.

 

A.    Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses to FROGs are granted with respect to FROGs 4.1, 4.2, 12.1–12.7, 14.1, 14.2, 15.1, and 50.1–50.6 only.

 

Plaintiff moves to compel further responses to FROGs propounded on Defendants pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300. A party may move for an order compelling further responses to interrogatories if the propounding party deems that responses are incomplete, evasive, contain meritless objections, or improperly exercise the option to produce documents under section 2030.230. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (a).) Specifically, Plaintiff seeks further responses to FROGs 4.1, 4.2, 12.1–12.7, 13.1, 13.2, 14.1, 14.2, 15.1, 16.1–16.10, 17.1 and 50.1–50.6 from Defendants. Though Plaintiff’s separate statements in support of both motions identify other interrogatories, further responses were not sought in the respective notices of motion. The Court limits its analysis to those interrogatories identified in the notice.

 

Plaintiff served the relevant FROGs on Defendants on January 23, 2026. (Zaffos Decl. FROGs ¶ 7.) Thereafter, he granted multiple extensions creating a new response deadline of March 6, 2026. (Id. ¶ 9.) Defendants served unverified responses on March 7, 2026, at 12:48 A.M. (Serpik Decl. FROGs ¶ 3.) The failure to timely respond to interrogatories waives any objections as to the interrogatories.  C.C.P. section 2030.290(a). The parties met and conferred over the following few months but failed to reach an informal resolution. (Zaffos Decl. ¶¶ 11–15.) Plaintiff filed the present motions to compel further responses on July 6, 2026.

 

Defendants argue that they have properly responded to the FROGs. First, they contend that they need not accept Plaintiff’s definition of the relevant “incident.” Plaintiff defines the incident as “Dr. Hao Li's offer for sale of securities in form of equity awards constituting 2.3% of Pinscreen’s shares, vesting over four years to Dr. Iman Sadeghi.” (Zaffos Decl., Ex. 1 ¶ 4(a)(2).) As Defendants note, they dispute this premise. However, this dispute does not prevent them from responding to the relevant discovery requests as there is no mystery as to the subject matter of the request.. Plaintiff properly identifies Defendants’ responses as evasive, as they refuse to provide relevant information based on the terminology used in Plaintiff’s definition of the relevant incident.

 

Second, Defendants argue that they provided complete responses to FROGs 4.1, 4.2, 12.2–12.7, 13.1, 13.2, 14.1 and 14.2. FROGs 4.1 and 4.2 ask for insurance information from Defendants, who refuse to provide it based on Plaintiff’s definition of the incident that occurred. This response is evasive and Defendants must provide further responses. FROGs 12.1–12.7 ask Defendants to provide information related to any individuals who may have witnessed or made a report about the incident. Once again, Defendants’ objection to the definition of incident is without merit and further responses must be provided. FROGs 14.1 and 14.2 require further responses for the same reason. However, Defendants have provided sufficient responses to FROGs 13.1 and 13.2 in denying that they conducted any surveillance.

 

Third, Defendants argue that they need not provide responses to FROG 15.1 because it seeks to reopen discovery which has been closed. However, this FROG is proper with respect to Plaintiff’s 8th through 11th causes of action. Defendants must provide further responses related to those causes of action only.

 

Fourth, Defendants argue that responses to FROGs 16.1–16.10 are not necessary because those FROGs relate to personal injury matters. Indeed, those FROGs are irrelevant to the “incident” defined by Plaintiff, which does not involve a personal injury. Thus, further responses are not required and Defendants’ answers are sufficient.

 

Fifth, Defendants argue that they have sufficiently responded to FROG 17.1. Indeed, Defendants properly explained their responses to Plaintiff’s requests for admission. Thus, further responses are not required.

 

Sixth, Defendants argue that they need not offer further responses to FROGs 50.1–50.6. However, their responses improperly object to Plaintiff’s definition of the relevant incident and are thus evasive in failing to provide the requested information. Additionally, the FROGs do not seek to force Defendants to provide information related to causes of action other than the 8th through 11th. Thus, further responses are required. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses are granted in part: Defendants are ordered to provide further responses to FROGs 4.1, 4.2, 12.1–12.7, 14.1, 14.2, 15.1, and 50.1–50.6 only.

 

B.     Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses to SPROGs are granted in full.

 

Plaintiff moves to compel further responses to SPROGs propounded on Defendants pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300. A party may move for an order compelling further responses to interrogatories if the propounding party deems that responses are incomplete, evasive, contain meritless objections, or improperly exercise the option to produce documents under section 2030.230. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (a).) Specifically, Plaintiff seeks further responses to SPROGs 1–13 from Defendants.

 

Plaintiff served the relevant SPROGs on Defendants on January 23, 2026. (Zaffos Decl. FROGs ¶ 7.) Thereafter, he granted multiple extensions creating a new response deadline of March 6, 2026. (Id. ¶ 9.) Defendants served unverified responses on March 7, 2026, at 12:48 A.M. (Serpik Decl. FROGs ¶ 3.) The parties met and conferred over the following few months but failed to reach an informal resolution. (Zaffos Decl. ¶¶ 11–15.) Plaintiff filed the present motions to compel further responses on July 6, 2026.

 

As discussed with respect to Plaintiff’s motions for further responses to FROGs, Defendants’ late responses to the SPROGs waived all objections and Defendants have not sought relief from waiver. By providing late responses, Defendants also waived their right to produce writings pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.230. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).) Defendants’ responses to SPROGs 1–6 and 10–13 are replete with waived objections; thus, further responses are required. Additionally, Defendants improperly seek to produce writings pursuant to section 2030.230 despite their waiver in response to SPROGs 7–9. Further responses are thus required to these SPROGs as well. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motions are granted in full.

 

C.     Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses to RFPs are granted in full.

 

Plaintiff moves to compel further responses to RFPs propounded on Defendants pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.310. A party may move for an order compelling further responses to requests for production if the propounding party deems that responses are incomplete, evasive, or contain meritless objections. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).) “The motion shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (Id., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) Specifically, Plaintiff seeks further responses to RFPs 1–38 from Defendants.

 

Plaintiff served the relevant RFPs on Defendants on January 23, 2026. (Zaffos Decl. FROGs ¶ 7.) Thereafter, he granted multiple extensions creating a new response deadline of March 6, 2026. (Id. ¶ 9.) Defendants served unverified responses on March 7, 2026, at 12:48 A.M. (Serpik Decl. FROGs ¶ 3.) The parties met and conferred over the following few months but failed to reach an informal resolution. (Zaffos Decl. ¶¶ 11–15.) Plaintiff filed the present motions to compel further responses on July 6, 2026. As discussed with respect to Plaintiff’s motions for further responses to FROGs, Defendants’ late responses to the RFPs waived all objections and Defendants have not sought relief from waiver. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).)

 

As a threshold matter, Plaintiff has set forth facts demonstrating good cause exists for the present discovery. The RFPs seek information related to Plaintiff’s 8th through 11th causes of action. Specifically, they seek documents related to: (1) Plaintiff’s alleged equity interest; (2) Pinscreen’s valuation; (3) capitalization tables within a certain timeframe; (4) investor meetings; (5) the Pinscreen equity incentive program; and (6) Plaintiff’s allegations and Defendants’ affirmative defenses. (Zaffos Decl. ¶ 16.) This is a sufficient showing to justify Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses.

 

Further, Defendants offer objections to every RFP despite them having been waived by the late response. These objections are sufficient grounds for further responses to be compelled. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motions are granted in full.

 

D.    Plaintiff’s motion to compel compliance with the Court’s February 24, 2026, prior order is granted. The motion to compel compliance with the March 16, 2026, order is denied.

 

Plaintiff moves to compel compliance with the Court’s prior orders from February 24 and March 16 of this year. After a trial court has previously ordered a party to provide discovery responses, “if a party then fails to obey an order compelling inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, the court may make those orders that are just, including the imposition of an issue sanction, an evidence sanction, or a terminating sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010). In lieu of or in addition to that sanction, the court may impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010).” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.320, subd. (c).)

 

On February 24, 2026, the Court ordered Li to provide further responses to Plaintiff’s first set of RFPs, numbers 1–7, within 10 days. Li provided amended responses on September 10, 2026, offering a response to RFP 6 for the first time and amending his responses to RFPs 1–5 and 7. (Serpik Decl. ¶ 10, Ex. D.) The amended responses are objection free, as required under the Court’s order. But Li has not shown that he has produced the relevant documents, and the amended responses were provided far after the 10-day deadline. Thus, Plaintiff’s motion is still properly granted and sanctions are still warranted.

 

On March 16, 2026, the Court ordered Pinscreen to “(a) identify those individuals whose investments in Pinscreen form the basis for the valuation(s) and (b) to the extent that the names of the investors may be obtained by reviewing documents already produced, Defendant may respond by identifying by Bates number where that information may be found.” Additionally, Pinscreen was instructed to produce the information contained on the hard drive of Plaintiff’s further work computer in its native format. The Court set a compliance deadline of April 13, 2026. On April 15, 2026, Pinscreen provided information regarding investments made and in which documents those investments could be found. Pinscreen also produced the requested digital documents that same day, with history and cache data stored on Plaintiff’s work computer. While further compliance is not required, Pinscreen did offer responses after the deadline, justifying sanctions.

 

E.     Plaintiff is awarded $7,407.50 in sanctions.

 

Sanctions are warranted when a party unsuccessfully opposes a motion to compel further responses to inspection demands or interrogatories without substantial justification. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, subd. (d), 2031.310, subd. (h).) Additionally, the Court may impose a monetary sanction for a party’s failure to comply with a prior order demanding production. (Id., § 2031.320, subd. (c).) The Court declines to impose issue or evidence sanctions at this time, viewing monetary sanctions as sufficiently effective. Thus, the Court imposes monetary sanctions on Defendants with respect to Plaintiff’s motions to compel compliance, motions to compel further responses to SPROGs, and motions to compel further responses to RFPs. The Court finds that Defendants demonstrated substantial justification for opposing Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses to certain FROGs.

 

Counsel alleges 9 hours at $330 per hour and 0.5 hours at $350 per hour of attorney time and a $60 filing fee with respect to Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses to FROGs and RFPs. Because the Court only awards sanctions with respect to the RFP motions, it reduces the requested fees by half. Thus, the Court awards $1,632.50 with respect to these motions ([$330 per hour] x [9 hours] + [$350 per hour] x [0.5 hours] + [$60 filing fee] = $1,632.50). Counsel alleges an additional 4 hours at $330 per hour and 0.5 hours at $350 per hour of attorney time and a $60 filing fee with respect to Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses to SPROGs. The Court finds this estimate reasonable and thus awards $1,555 in sanctions with respect to those motions ([$330 per hour] x [4 hours] + [$350 per hour] x [0.5 hours] + [$60 filing fee] = $1,555). Counsel alleges 11 hours of attorney time at $395 per hour and 0.5 hours at $150 per hour and a $60 filing fee related to the motion to compel compliance with the Court’s February 24 order. The Court finds a 5 hour estimate of the attorney time at $395 per hour more reasonable, and thus awards $2,110 with respect to this motion ([$395 per hour] x [5 hours] + [$150 per hour] x [0.5 hours] + [$60 filing fee] = $2,110). Finally, Counsel alleges 10 hours of attorney time at $395 per hour and 0.5 hours at $150 per hour and a $60 filing fee related to the motion to compel compliance with the Court’s March 16 order. The Court finds a 5 hour estimate of the attorney time at $395 per hour more reasonable, and thus awards $2,110 with respect to this motion ([$395 per hour] x [5 hours] + [$150 per hour] x [0.5 hours] + [$60 filing fee] = $2,110). Accordingly, Plaintiff is awarded $7,407.50 in sanctions, payable by Defendants’ counsel.

 

Conclusion:


            The Court grants Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses to RFPs and SPROGs and full and FROGs in part. The Court grants Plaintiff’s motion to compel compliance with the February 24, 2026, order and denies Plaintiff’s motion related to March 16 as moot. The Court awards Plaintiff $7,407.50. Any further responses are due within 20 days. Sanctions to be paid within 30 days.
