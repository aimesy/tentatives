            Plaintiff alleges that a grant deed was forged conveying Plaintiff’s properties, which was then encumbered, all without Plaintiff’s authorization. Plaintiff brings a quiet title action.

Attorney Eideh Manavi moves to be relieved as counsel for Plaintiff Cynthia Beck.

TENTATIVE RULING

The Court would be inclined to grant the motion for the reasons set forth in the moving papers.  However, the motion indicates that counsel's communications with Plaintiff have been via email, and there is no physical address for Plaintiff set forth in any of the moving papers.  Thus, if the Court grants the motion, there would be no physical address for other parties (or the Court) to use as the address to serve documents relating to the case.  Rule of Court 2.253 provides that courts may not require a self-represented party to comply with electronic filing and service requirements, and the Court cannot grant a motion to relieve Plaintiff's counsel that would result in the inability to serve documents on Plaintiff.   

Unless moving counsel provides evidence that Plaintiff has agreed to accept electronic service of documents, or alternatively, provides a valid mailing address at which Plaintiff may be served, the Court will be unable to grant the motion.  If Plaintiff does not provide either of these alternatives, the Court may have no choice but to consider the issuance of an order to show cause why the complaint should not be dismissed for lack of prosecution.

Discussion          

            The Court has reviewed the moving papers, the opposition and the reply. The Court will only address the matters which it deems pertinent to the disposition of this motion.

A motion for judgment on the pleadings may be granted in favor of a defendant if “[t]he complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (c)(3)(B)(ii).) This ground “shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice.” (Id., subd. (d).) The statute explicitly authorizes California trial courts to bring their own motions for judgment on the pleadings and to grant such motions. (Id., subd. (b)(2).)

 

(Foster v. Sexton (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 998, 1019.)

The Court bifurcated the case and held a bench trial as to Phase I, after which it issued a Statement of Decision finding in favor of Plaintiff. Plaintiff Matthew Behdjou moves for judgment on the pleadings granting dismissal of the Cross-Complaint filed by Defendants Maria Beronica Lopez and Joel Lopez Mercado.

The motion is based on the following grounds:

(1) That the Court has already ruled for Plaintiff in its March 6, 2026, Statement of Decision on the Phase I bench trial, awarding Plaintiff quiet title to the real property located at 2829 S. Rimpau Blvd., Los Angeles, CA (“Subject Property”) pursuant to Plaintiff’s Second Cause of Action, and cancellation of a July 23, 2024, Quitclaim Deed (“July Quitclaim Deed”) by which Defendants fraudulently clouded Plaintiff’s title to the Subject Property pursuant to the Sixth Cause of Action;

(2) That Plaintiff has since dismissed the remaining causes of action of his Complaint and/or the Court dismissed them as a result of Defendants’ Demurrer; and

(3) That as a necessary part of the Statement of Decision, the Court also ruled on the legal and factual foundation of the First, Second and Third Causes of Action of Defendants’ Cross-Complaint – the only causes of action remaining in the Cross Complaint – issuing findings going to the heart of the three causes of action and thereby barring Defendants from relitigating those rulings by the doctrine of collateral estoppel/issue preclusion, leaving nothing else to be decided with regard to the Cross-Complaint. 

(4) Further, as a necessary part of the Phase I Statement of Decision, the Court also issued detailed findings rejecting all of Defendants’ proffered affirmative defenses and the legal and factual foundation they are based on, and issuing findings that go to the heart of the First, Second and Third Causes of Action of Defendants’ Cross-Complaint – the only remaining claims. 

Collateral estoppel does not apply to the same lawsuit. (All Bay Mill & Lumber Co. v. Sur. Co. (1989) 208 Cal.App.3d 11, 18 n.3 [bold emphasis added].) However, issues adjudicated in an earlier phase of a bifurcated trial are binding on later phases and need not be relitigated:

 “All of the trial court's equitable findings were binding on the [plaintiff's] legal claims, regardless of whether they were necessary for the judgment. ‘ Issues adjudicated in earlier phases of a bifurcated trial are binding in later phases of that trial and need not be [*32]  relitigated. [Citations.] No other rule is possible, or bifurcation of trial issues would create duplication, thus subverting the procedure's goal of efficiency. [Citation.] “[D]uplication of effort is the very opposite of the purpose of bifurcated trials.”’” (Citation omitted.)

(Reliant Life Shares, LLC v. Cooper (2023) 90 Cal.App.5th 14, 31-32.) 

The remaining causes of action in the Cross-Complaint are as follows: 

1.         First Cause of Action (Fraudulent Concealment).

            The Cross-Complaint alleges at ¶¶ 7, 20 – 22:

7. The house was worth about 1,050,000 in December 2023. They convinced Joel to list the house and contract to sell it at about $950,000. In about March of 2025, Joel balked at the selling price and cancelled. Samuel and Matthew continued to text and call him, trying to convince him to sell to them. As the foreclosure sale date approached, they eventually got a sale contract and deed for the low price of $700,000. The sales contract was not compliant with Civ 2945.2 or 2945.3. There was no notice of right to cancel or method to cancel included.

            . . .

20. Cross defendants concealed the right to cancel from Cross Complainants. This is something a licensed agent and a sophisticated investor should be aware of, and is statutory – required to be in bold letters on the contract. However, instead of using the required form of contract, Cross defendants created their own abbreviated contract which omitted and concealed a number of required disclosures, rights of seller, right to cancel, etc.

 

21. The concealment was of information the cross complainants would have wanted to know, that would have materially helped them had they known, and allows them to cancel the bargain sale of their property to keep or negotiate a fair sales price.

 

22. Cross defendants allege that the act of Cross Defendants in pushing them near the foreclosure date and then pressuring them to sell the house at a steep discount is oppressive and fraudulent, as well as concealing the right to cancel.

     (Cross-Complaint, ¶¶ 7, 20 – 22 [bold emphasis added].)

            However, Civil Code § 2945.2 and 2945.3—which form the basis for the cross-claim for fraudulent concealment (Cross-Complaint ¶ 7) is part of the Mortgage Foreclosure Consultants regulation, codified at Civil Code § 2945 et seq. See Civil Code § 2945(c):

(c) The intent and purposes of this article are the following:

 

(1) To require that foreclosure consultant service agreements be expressed in writing; to safeguard the public against deceit and financial hardship; to permit rescission of foreclosure consultation contracts; to prohibit representations that tend to mislead; and to encourage fair dealing in the rendition of foreclosure services.

 

(2) The provisions of this article shall be liberally construed to effectuate this intent and to achieve these purposes.


     (Civ. Code § 2945(c).)

            However, in the Statement of Decision, the Court found that “the Foreclosure Consultant Act does not apply to Plaintiff.” (Statement of Decision, Page 11.) As such, the sales contract need not have been compliant with Civil Code 2945.2 or 2945.3, including giving notice of right to cancel or method to cancel. This issue cannot be re-litigated.

            As such, the motion for judgment on the pleadings is GRANTED as to the first cause of action.

2.         Second Cause of Action (Rescission).

24.  Sales made in violation of the foreclosure consultant regulations are voidable, and those made with no notice of right to cancel and cancellable until 5 days after a right to cancel has been delivered. This cross complaint constitutes a demand to cancel.

 

25.  CC 1695.5 states “(d) Until the equity purchaser has complied with this section, the equity seller may cancel the contract.”. CC 2945 et seq states that contract in violation of the act are voidable.

 

26.  Thus, Cross defendants demand that the contract be voided, the transaction rescinded, and the grant deed cancelled.

            In the Statement of Decision, the Court found that that Defendant Lopez’s primary residence was 645 N. Manhattan Place, not the Subject Property, and therefore HESCA (Civil Code § 1696 et seq.) does not apply to the purchase transaction. (Statement of Decision, Pages 9 – 10.)

            The Court also found that the Foreclosure Consultant Act (Civil Code § 2945 et seq.) does not apply to Plaintiff because Plaintiff neither sought nor received compensation for any tasks performed incident to the sale of the Subject Property. (Statement of Decision, Pages 10 – 11.)

As such, the Court has already decided against the cross-claim that the sale violated foreclosure consultant regulations so as to be cancellable. This issue cannot be re-litigated. 

            As such, the motion for judgment on the pleadings is GRANTED as to the second cause of action.

3.         Third Cause of Action (Cancellation of Instrument).          

¶ 28 of the Cross-Complaint alleges:

Discussion

 

            Defendant Nevada Tobacco Distribution dba Cali King Wholesale (hereinafter “NTD”) demurs to the First Amended Complaint as follows:

 

1.         First Cause of Action (Wrongful Death—General Negligence).

 

Defendant argues that the 1AC fails to state specific facts showing any affirmative conduct constituting misfeasance on the part of NTD which contributed to plaintiffs’ injuries. Defendant argues that NTD did not control the downstream retailer, the purchaser, or the manner in which the product was ultimately used. Nor did NTD encourage, disseminate, direct, or participate in any unlawful conduct. The alleged harm outlined in the FAC instead arises from independent intervening acts namely, the retailer’s unlawful sale of nitrous oxide to a minor and the minor’s subsequent misuse of the product, while driving a vehicle intoxicated. Unlike in Weirum, where the defendant’s conduct actively created the dangerous condition, NTD’s alleged conduct consists solely of placing a lawful product into the stream of commerce.

 

            Defendant argues that to the extent Plaintiff’s claims sound in malfeasance, NTD does not owe Plaintiffs a duty. Nor does NTD have a duty to control third party conduct—such as the sale of “Gold Whip” to a minor—based on a special relationship. Defendant argues that the alleged harm stems from three forms of illegal conduct by the retailer(s) and Plaintiffs. This conduct, which Defendant argues was not foreseeable to NTD, are:

 

(1) The criminal conduct involved Retailer Parties’ alleged criminal sale of nitrous oxide to minors in violation of Penal Code, § 381(c), which is not a conduct for which NTD can be held liable;

 

(2) The criminal conduct is Plaintiffs inhaling the subject nitrous oxide Gold Whip product in violation Penal Code § 381(b);

 

(3) The criminal conduct is Plaintiffs driving while under the influence in violation of Vehicle Code, §§ 23152, 23153.

 

            Defendant also argues the foregoing breaks the chain of causation.

 

            Plaintiffs argue that their claim does not depend upon the existence of any special relationship and does not depend upon any freestanding duty to control the retailer or either decedent. Rather, as pled, the Plaintiffs’ claim for negligence rests on NTD’s own negligent distribution of the Gold Whip product to the retailer.

The foregoing is sufficient to allege that it was foreseeable that the Gold Whip products being distributed to Smoke Shop might be put to recreational purposes, and there were no warnings that foreseeable inhalation misuse could cause hazardous impairment, regardless of the age of persons who may engage in such recreational uses.

 

In addition to the concepts of fault and comparative fault as common to both negligence actions and those founded in strict products liability theory (citation omitted), the concept of foreseeability is involved in both types of actions. “In actions premised on strict products liability, just as in actions premised on negligence, an element of foreseeability is involved; liability may not be imposed unless the injury results from a use of the product which is reasonably foreseeable. (Citation omitted.)  “ (Citation omitted.) Even if an injured plaintiff’s acts constituted misuse of a product, if those acts were foreseeable, strict liability may still apply, although the plaintiff’s comparative negligence might serve to reduce any award he or she might receive proportionate to such fault. (Citation omitted.) In apportioning and comparing fault, an injured plaintiff’s conduct is to be compared with the defendant manufacturer’s product, not the manufacturer’s conduct. (Citation omitted.)

(Milwaukee Electric Tool Corp. v. Superior Court (1993) 15 Cal.App.4th 547, 558 [bold emphasis and underlining added].)

 

It explained, “ ‘[A] manufacturer owes a foreseeable user of its  [*1113]  product a duty to warn of risks of using the product.’ [Citation.] That is what appellants have alleged here.” (Ibid., italics added.) Put another way, Tellez-Cordova held that the defendant manufacturers were not being asked to warn of the risks arising exclusively from other manufacturers’ products. Instead, the plaintiffs sought to hold the defendants liable for failure to warn of risks created by the use of the defendants’ own products.

(Bettencourt v. Hennessy Industries, Inc. (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 1103, 1112-13 [bold emphasis added].)

 

Hennessy claims plaintiffs have no cause of action in negligence because no duty of care exists. In Hennessy’s view, the foreseeability of the harm is insufficient to give rise to a duty of care, and policy reasons preclude imposition of such a duty. Initially, we question Hennessy’s framing of this issue. Under established California law, a manufacturer already owes a duty of care to foreseeable users of its product. (Citations omitted.) “Because the general duty to take ordinary care in the conduct of one’s activities (Civ. Code, § 1714, subd. (a)) indisputably applies” to product manufacturers, “the issue is … whether a categorical exception to that general rule should be made …” in these circumstances. (Citation omitted.) Such an exemption is appropriate “only when foreseeability and policy considerations justify a categorical no-duty rule … .” (Citation omitted.)

(Bettencourt v. Hennessy Industries, Inc. (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 1103, 1118 [bold emphasis and underlining added].)

            As for the criminal acts which Defendants claim are superseding causes, these are questions of fact to be resolved by a trier of fact (if not on summary judgment). For purposes of demurrer, at least, there are sufficient facts pled that it was not so highly unusual or extraordinary that Gold Whip distributed to Defendant’s smoke shop would be misused recreationally by persons who would then engage in activities that are hazardous while the person is impaired:

Owners assert they are absolved from liability because Jesus’s act was an intervening or superseding cause of decedent’s death. (Citation omitted.) Under the circumstances of this case, that `superseding cause, Owners must establish as a matter of law that the intervening act was so highly unusual or extraordinary that the occurrence was not likely to happen and therefore was not foreseeable. (Citations omitted.) However, the defendant need only foresee the risk of harm, not the particular intervening act (citations omitted), and where the risk created by the breach of the duty is that the plaintiff is exposed to danger from criminal conduct, the criminal conduct is not automatically a superseding cause. (Citations omitted.)

(Vasquez v. Residential Investments, Inc. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 269, 289 [bold emphasis and underlining added].)

 

The first of these contentions is based upon the theory that Logan’s criminal conduct was a superseding intervening cause (a theory accepted by the court as an alternative ground for its ruling).  Criminal conduct which causes injury will ordinarily be deemed the proximate cause of an injury, superseding any prior negligence which might otherwise be deemed a contributing cause. (See 6 Witkin, Summary of Cal. Law (9th ed. 1988) Torts, § 992, pp. 382, 383.) However, “if the likelihood that a third person may act in a particular manner is the hazard or one of the hazards which makes the actor negligent, such an act whether innocent, negligent, intentionally tortious or criminal does not prevent the actor from being liable for harm caused thereby.” (Citations omitted.)

     (Koepke v. Loo (1993) 18 Cal.App.4th 1444, 1449 [bold emphasis added].)

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the first cause of action is sufficiently pled against demurring Defendants.

 

            The demurrer to the first cause of action is OVERRULED.

            Plaintiff tenants alleges that Defendant landlords failed to remediate uninhabitable conditions at the property leased by Plaintiffs. 

Defendant Single Room Occupancy Housing Corporation moves to strike portions of the First Amended Complaint.

TENTATIVE RULING           

Defendant Single Room Occupancy Housing Corporation’s motion to strike the requests for attorneys fees from the First Amended Complaint is DENIED in its entirety.

Defendant is to answer the First Amended Complaint within 10 days.

ANALYSIS

 

Motion To Strike

 

Meet and Confer

 

            The form Declaration of Andre L Arzoo reflects that Defendant’s counsel satisfied the meet and confer requirement set forth in Code Civ. Proc. § 435.5.

 

Discussion

 

Defendant Single Room Occupancy Housing Corporation moves to strike portions of the First Amended Complaint regarding attorney’s fees. “[A]s a general rule, attorney fees are not recoverable as costs unless they are authorized by statute or agreement.” (People ex rel. Dept. of Corporations v. Speedee Oil Change Systems, Inc. (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 424, 429.)

 

 

1.         ¶ 40, 15:11-13 – “As a proximate cause of the Defendant’s breach, Plaintiffs suffered damages in an amount to be proven at trial. Subject to the terms of Plaintiffs’ leases, Plaintiffs are entitled to an award of attorney’s fees and costs.”

 

Defendant argues that the 1AC asserts a claim for attorney fees pursuant to Plaintiffs’ lease agreements, yet fails to provide contractual language despite the Court’s Order, issued on January 7, 2026. (Arzoo Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.)

 

Plaintiffs argue that landlords have an obligation under Cal. Civil Code 1962(a)(4) to hold onto their tenant’s lease. As stated by the courts, a motion to strike attorney fees pursuant to a contract is not appropriate if the party has not had a full opportunity to determine, through discovery, whether the basis for recovery of attorney fees exists. See Camenisch v. Superior Ct. (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 1689, 1699 (denying Defendants’ contention that attorney fees be stricken because the Plaintiff did not produce a contract on the basis that discovery was still ongoing).

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiffs fail to comply with the Minute Order of January 7, 2026, for failure to plead alleged contractual terms (a lease provision) entitling them to attorney fees. (Supp. Arzoo Decl., ¶¶ 2-4.) However, these arguments are in bad faith, as Plaintiffs and their counsel are informed and aware that SRO is a non-profit, that no SRO leases provide for attorney fees, and that this is a common practice for non-profits that provide subsidized housing in Los Angeles. (Supp. Arzoo Decl., ¶ 5.) Plaintiffs have again failed to plead contractual terms and so their claims must be stricken.

 

Court’s analysis:

 

Defendant’s argument that non of SRO’s leases have attorney’s fees clauses relies on evidence extrinsic to the 1AC. While this may prove to be true, Plaintiffs will be given an opportunity to conduct discovery to verify this. There is no harm in permitting the attorney’s fees request based on a lease provision to remain in the 1AC pending discovery. If no such clause exists, then no such clause can support an eventual request for attorney’s fees if Plaintiffs prevail at trial.

 

“[E]ven though the complaint did not allege a contract allowing recovery of attorney fees by the prevailing party, the court was not required to strike the prayer before plaintiffs had a full opportunity to determine, through discovery, whether a basis for recovery existed.” (Camenisch v. Superior Court (1996)44 Cal. App. 4th 1689, 1691.)

 

The motion to strike the request for attorney’s fees at ¶ 40, 15:11-13 is DENIED.

 

2.         ¶ 50, 17:26-18:1 - “Subject to the terms of Plaintiffs’ lease and/or applicable law, Plaintiffs are entitled to an award of attorney’s fees and costs. Plaintiffs are entitled to reasonable attorney fees and costs pursuant to Cal. Civil Code 1942.4(b)(2) and Los Angeles Municipal Code 45.35(B).”

 

Re: Lease

 

Court’s analysis:

 

See discussion above at 1.

 

Re: Civil Code § 1942.4(b)(2).

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiffs are seeking attorney fees pursuant to Civil Code § 1942.4. However, a violation does not occur unless all four conditions are met: (1) “the dwelling substantially lacks any of the affirmative characteristics in Section 1941.1,” such as effective waterproofing and weather protection of roof and exterior walls and plumbing or gas facilities maintained in good working order; (2) “[a] public officer or employee who is responsible for the enforcement of any housing law, after inspecting the premises, has notified the landlord or the landlord’s agent in writing of his or her obligations to abate the nuisance or repair the substandard conditions”; (3) “[t]he conditions have existed and have not been abated 35 days beyond the date of service of the notice … and the delay is without good cause”; and (4) “the conditions were not caused by an act or omission of the tenant”. (Emphasis added; Civil Code § 1942.4(a).)

 

Defendant argues that the 1AC fails to allege a violation of Civil Code § 1942.4 relative to their unit, as opposed to common areas. The only violation alleged as to Plaintiffs’ units is a single instance for “wall damage” in BATTLE’s unit. (¶ 17, 8:5-6.) The 1AC fails to allege or provide substantive facts of any violation or citation of HALLIBURTON’s unit. In addition, throughout the 1AC, no substantive facts are pleaded regarding any other alleged conditions or violations in Plaintiffs’ own individual dwellings. (¶¶ 15-25.) Nor are there facts pled to support Plaintiffs’ claim of having provided notice to SRO, if any, it is impossible for either this Court or SRO to ascertain when the 35-day period to abate was triggered, if at all. Moreover, there is a likelihood that tenants and their guests either contributed to or created the issues complained about.

 

Plaintiffs argue that the 1AC alleges that SRO received citations regarding the Subject Property, including common area and building-wide violations, that Defendants enabled these substandard conditions throughout the building, that Defendants failed to abate cited code violations, and that Defendants demanded and collected rent from Plaintiffs despite the Subject Property’s substandard conditions. The entire context of Plaintiffs’ complaint demonstrates SRO’s deficient management, disregard for tenants, and failure to comply with direct city orders as to substandard conditions under their direct control. Plaintiffs have clearly alleged that SRO enabled, caused, and increased code violations at the premises. Plaintiffs’ FAC directly cites policies that enabled these conditions, such as SRO’s failure to comply with California Code of Regulations, Title 25, § 42, and safety issues that are entirely under SRO’s control, such as the building’s defective Fire Annunciator Panel. SRO is also an experienced operator of low-income residential buildings that voluntarily made an agreement with the city of Los Angeles to take on Section 8 tenants at the premises.

 

Court’s analysis:

 

The Court notes that the fourth cause of action is for violation of Civil Code § 1942.4. However, whether or not conditions of the common areas were caused by wear and tear or other activities of the tenants is a factual issue outside the scope of this motion to strike.

 

Civ. Proc. Code, § 437 provides:

 

(a) The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice.

 

(b) Where the motion to strike is based on matter of which the court may take judicial notice pursuant to Section 452 or 453 of the Evidence Code, such matter shall be specified in the notice of motion, or in the supporting points and authorities, except as the court may otherwise permit.

 

Indeed, the gist of Defendant’s argument is that Plaintiffs have failed to plead facts sufficient to constitute a violation of Civil Code § 1942.4. This is the function of a demurrer (or motion for judgment on the pleadings) not a motion to strike.

[A] motion to strike is generally used to reach defects in a pleading which are not subject to demurrer. A motion to strike does not lie to attack a complaint for insufficiency of allegations to justify relief; that is a ground for general demurrer. (Citation omitted.)

(Pierson v. Sharp Mem’l Hosp. (1989) 216 Cal.App.3d 340, 342-343.)

 

In this regard, the Court does not find that Defendant has presented a sufficient reason to strike the request for attorney’s fees pursuant to Civil Code § 1942.4.

 

Re: LAMC § 45.35(B).

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiffs fail to provide substantive facts to support their claims for attorney fees under the Anti-Tenant Harassment Ordinance. Expressly, the former provision of Municipal Code 45.35 in Ordinance 187109 provides that “A civil proceeding … initiated under this article alleging any violation of Section 45.33 (2) may be commenced only after the tenant provides written notice to the landlord of the alleged violation, and the landlord fails to remedy the repair or maintenance issue within a reasonable period of time.” [Emphasis added.] However, the 1AC only provides a conclusory statement that “Plaintiffs complained about the above conditions to the Defendants…” (¶ 26.) Plaintiffs provide no substantive facts, nonetheless, the dates they provided notice, how and to whom they provided notice, or even for what complaints or conditions notice was provided. Plaintiffs merely alleging conclusory statements are not only insufficient but, in context of the only facts provided in their 1AC, are improperly misleading. Plaintiffs allege at least 12 paragraphs of conditions or conduct from 2021 to 2024 yet fail to provide any facts whatsoever to support their claim of even one instance of providing notice. (¶¶ 14 25.) Given the extent of their allegations, Plaintiffs should have been able to provide such facts, if any. Therefore, Plaintiffs are not entitled to attorney fees under Section 45.35, given their failure to even allege, nonetheless, their failure to provide any substantive facts whatsoever to support, that they properly commenced this action under the conditions required by the Municipal Code.

 

Court’s analysis:

 

The only cause of action seemingly based upon a violation of LAMC § 45.35 is the sixth cause of action for tenant harassment. 

 

77. The Defendants’ actions were willful, directed at Plaintiffs, would create an apprehension of harm in a reasonable person, and served no legitimate purpose. As such, Defendants are liable for tenant harassment under L.A.M.C. Sec. 45.33 and Los Angeles County Code 8.52.130.

 

In this regard, Defendant’s argument that Plaintiffs have not pled the conditions required by the Municipal Code goes to whether Plaintiffs plead facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for violation of the LAMC § 45.35 which is the function of a demurrer (or motion for judgment on the pleadings), not a motion to strike.

 

Conclusion

 

            The motion to strike the request for attorney’s fees at ¶ 50, 17:26-18:1 is DENIED.

 

3.         ¶ 64, 21:5-6 - “Plaintiffs are entitled to an award of reasonable attorney’s fees and costs pursuant to Cal. Civil Code 1942.4(b)(2).”

 

For the reasons discussed above, the motion to strike the request for attorney’s fees at ¶ 64, 21:5-6 is DENIED.

 

4.         ¶ 78, 24:5-8 - “Plaintiffs are also entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees and costs pursuant to L.A.M.C. 45.35(B), and Los Angeles County Code 8.52.170(A).”

 

Defendant argues that the Los Angeles County Code 8.52.040, entitled “General Applicability of Chapter,” provides that the Los Angeles County Code Chapter 8.52 “shall be effective on April 1, 2020, and apply to all Landlords and Tenants in Dwelling Units within the unincorporated areas of the County, unless otherwise exempted by State law or the provisions of this Chapter.” (Ibid, emphasis added.) Here, however, the Subject Property, located at 518 San Julian Street, Los Angeles, California 90013, is not within an unincorporated area of the County of Los Angeles. Accordingly, Los Angeles County Code § 8.52.170 does not apply and does not support a claim for attorney fees. Instead, Los Angeles Municipal Code applies to properties located within the City of Los Angeles, such as the Subject Property in this case. Plaintiffs’ FAC alleges inspections and/or citations that occurred from May 28, 2021, through April 8, 2024. (¶ 17, 6:23-8:14.). However, the anti-tenant harassment ordinance only became effective in 2021, and was amended by Ordinance No. 188,416, with an effective date of December 29, 2024.

 

Court’s analysis:

 

The above argument relies upon a request for judicial notice which Defendant not properly make of the Court. Moreover, Defendant’s argument goes to whether Plaintiffs plead facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for violation of Los Angeles County Code 8.52.040, which is the function of a demurrer (or motion for judgment on the pleadings), not a motion to strike.

 

As such, the motion to strike the request for attorney’s fees at ¶ 78, 24:5-8 is DENIED.

 

5.         Page 25, line 11 - “5. For attorney’s fees pursuant to Plaintiffs’ lease agreements and/or Cal. Civil Code 1942.4(b)(2), Los Angeles Municipal Code 45.35(B), and Los Angeles County Code 8.52.170(A).”

 

For the reasons discussed above, the motion to strike the request for attorney’s fees at Page 25, line 11 is DENIED.
