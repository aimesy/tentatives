MOTION TO STRIKE AND/OR TAX COSTS

Hearing Date: June 26, 2026

CASE NO./NAME: 20STCV44313 LOUIS L. TOUTON, et al. v. KECK HOSPITAL OF USC, et al.

Moving Party: Plaintiffs Louis L. Touton and Jean Gottlieb

Responding Party: Defendants Keck Hospital of USC and John C. Lipham, M.D.

Notice: Sufficient

Ruling: GRANTED IN PART.

BACKGROUND

This action arises from alleged negligent postoperative care following a laparoscopic hiatal hernia repair performed on August 28, 2019, at Keck Hospital of USC by Dr. Lipham. Plaintiff Louis Touton alleges Defendants failed to timely recognize and treat postoperative gastric distention and obstruction and prematurely discharged him on August 31, 2019, despite ongoing symptoms. Plaintiff alleges that as a result of these failures, Plaintiff developed gastric ischemia requiring total removal of his stomach. On November 18, 2020, Plaintiffs filed the present action for professional negligence against Defendant John Lipham M.D. and Defendant Keck Hospital of USC.

On Plaintiffs’ motion, the Court ordered the trial bifurcated as to the standard of care. (Order re Mot. to Bifurcate, Feb. 13, 2026.) The standard-of-care phase was tried to a jury between February 25 and March 10, 2026. On March 10, 2026, the jury returned a unanimous special verdict finding that neither Dr. Lipham nor Keck Hospital was negligent. The Court entered judgment on the verdict on March 17, 2026. The Court has since denied Plaintiffs’ Motion for New Trial and Motion for Judgment Notwithstanding the Verdict. (Order, May 29, 2026.)

On November 2, 2022, Defendants Keck and Dr. Lipham each served offers to compromise under Code of Civil Procedure section 998, offering to waive then-accrued costs (approximately $8,975.52), with express notice that costs would increase as the matter proceeded to trial. Plaintiffs neither accepted the offers nor objected to their timeliness, and the offers lapsed by operation of law. On February 9, 2024, Plaintiffs served their own section 998 offers seeking $2,990,000.00 (Touton) and $249,000.00 (Gottlieb). Plaintiffs did not obtain a more favorable judgment.

As the prevailing parties, Defendants served and filed a memorandum of costs on April 24, 2026 in the total amount of $245,232.11, including $180,824.47 in expert witness fees. On May 12, 2026, Plaintiffs moved to strike the memorandum in its entirety or, in the alternative, to tax costs. On June 15, 2026, Defendants filed an opposition. To date, no reply has been filed.

LEGAL STANDARD

A prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (b).) “If the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary. On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.” (Ladas v. California State Automobile Assn. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 773–774.) Verification of the memorandum suffices to establish the reasonable necessity of the costs; there is no requirement that invoices be attached. (Jones v. Dumrichob (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1267.) To be recoverable, a cost must be both reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation and reasonable in amount. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(2)–(3).)

DISCUSSION

A.    Preliminary matter

Plaintiffs urged the Court to defer ruling because their post-trial motions, if granted, would place the parties as though no trial had occurred. That contingency has resolved: the Court denied the Motion for New Trial and Motion for JNOV on May 29, 2026. The judgment stands, Defendants remain the prevailing parties, and the motion is ripe for decision on the merits.

B.    Item 11 — Court Reporter Fees ($20,659.24)

Plaintiffs contend the parties agreed by written Memorandum of Understanding to share trial court reporter fees equally, with no provision permitting recovery of the apportioned fee by the prevailing party. Where parties agree to share such fees and do not reserve the right to seek recovery as a prevailing party, the agreement is enforced as written. (Anthony v. Li (2020) 47 Cal.App.5th 816, 824-825.) Defendants concede this item.

The motion is GRANTED as to Item 11, and these costs are taxed in the amount of $20,659.24.

C.    Item 1 — Filing and Motion Fees ($1,383.75)

Filing and motion fees are expressly allowable. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(1).) Plaintiffs’ principal objection that the item was unsupported is resolved by Exhibit C, which reflects filing fees for the Answer and the motion for summary judgment, incurred on behalf of the answering Defendants Keck and Dr. Lipham. Plaintiffs’ alternative argument, that some portion is attributable to dismissed Defendant Dr. Putnam (dismissed on a cost waiver), is not borne out by the documentation, which identifies no Putnam-specific filing fee.

The motion is DENIED as to Item 1.

D.    Item 4 — Deposition Costs ($34,724.15)

Section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(3)(A) expressly allows the costs of taking, video recording, and transcribing necessary depositions, and recovery does not depend on whether the deponent ultimately testifies at trial. (See Chaaban v. Wet Seal, Inc. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 49, 57; Garcia v. Tempur-Pedic North America, LLC (2024) 98 Cal.App.5th 819, 824-825.) Plaintiffs argue most depositions were not taken by Defendants or were not used at the bifurcated trial. The argument is unpersuasive. Trial use is not the governing test; the question is reasonable necessity when incurred, and the costs are documented (Exhibit D), facially proper, and reasonable in amount. Further, Plaintiffs provide “no authority for the proposition that the reasonable cost is the very lowest.” (Landwatch San Luis Obispo County v. Cambria Community Services Dist. (2018) 25 Cal.App.5th 638, 646.) Moreover, it was Plaintiffs who moved to bifurcate the standard-of-care issue, and that bifurcation is what narrowed the witnesses actually called. Having obtained that structure, Plaintiffs cannot fairly recharacterize as unnecessary the depositions that the full, unbifurcated case would have required. As the Court noted in addressing bifurcation, several defense experts, including Drs. Buckley, Dorros, Posalski, and Singh, were identified as potentially testifying on liability-related issues. The depositions were reasonably necessary as the case then stood.

The motion is DENIED as to Item 4.

E.    Item 8b — Expert Witness Fees ($177,338.51)

Expert witness fees ordinarily are not recoverable. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (b)(1).) Where a defendant serves a valid section 998 offer that is not accepted and the plaintiff fails to obtain a more favorable judgment, the court “in its discretion, may require the plaintiff to pay a reasonable sum to cover postoffer costs of the services of expert witnesses … actually incurred and reasonably necessary.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 998, subd. (c)(1), italics added.) Such an award is always discretionary, even when the statutory predicate is satisfied. (Chaaban, supra, 203 Cal.App.4th at pp. 54-55.)

The validity and timeliness of Defendants’ offers are undisputed, and Plaintiffs failed to obtain a more favorable result. Defendants may therefore seek expert fees, but only at the Court’s discretion. In exercising its discretion, the Court has considered the following:

·      The statute reaches only postoffer fees, yet the documentation includes significant preoffer work. The offers were served November 2, 2022. Defendants represent that “only those expert invoices incurred after the rejection of defendants’ 998 offers have been submitted.” The record does not bear that out. Dr. Buckley’s invoice (Exhibit E) bills work beginning June 15, 2021 and includes numerous 2021–22 entries (record review and deposition review of Drs. Lipham, Putnam, Johnson, Nakashima, Mahli, and Nakata, among others), all predating the offers. Section 998(c)(1) does not authorize recovery of those preoffer amounts, and the discrepancy between Defendants’ representation and their own exhibits leaves the burden of establishing the recoverable, postoffer figure with Defendants. (Ladas, supra, 19 Cal.App.4th at pp. 773-774.)

·      The case was tried only as to standard of care. The trial record reflects that, of the experts retained, Drs. Buckley and Dorros testified — Dr. Buckley on the standard of care and Dr. Dorros to rebut Plaintiffs’ expert Dr. Duffy on the closed-loop gastric obstruction issue as it related to standard of care. The remaining retained experts (economic, life-care-planning, infectious-disease, and other damages- or causation-oriented experts whose work fills much of Exhibit E) were not necessary to the discrete liability question actually adjudicated.

·      Plaintiffs suffered a great loss and while the jury decided in Defendants’ favor, the 998 offer that was rejected and brings this issue of expert costs to the fore essentially had little to no chance of acceptance.

·      The current record does not reflect the postoffer fees.

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED as to the expert witness fees.

F.    Item 13 — Models, Enlargements, and Photocopies of Exhibits ($2,761.60)

Models and enlargements of exhibits are allowable “if they were reasonably helpful to aid the trier of fact.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(13).) Plaintiffs contend few or no physical exhibits were used and that demonstratives were displayed electronically. The Court agrees with Plaintiffs. 

The motion is GRANTED as to Item 13.

G.    Item 14 — Electronic Filing or Service ($3,099.54)

Fees for electronic filing or service through an electronic filing service provider are allowable where the court requires electronic filing. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(14).) Electronic filing is required in this Court. Plaintiffs’ sole objection (absence of proof ) is resolved by Exhibit G, which documents the e-filing and related charges incurred over the course of the litigation. The charges are facially proper and reasonable in amount.

The motion is DENIED as to Item 14.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiffs’ Motion to Strike and/or Tax Costs is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART.

The Court taxes Item 11 (court reporter fees) in the amount of $20,659.24, Item 8b (expert witness fees) in the amount of $177,338.51, and Item 13 (models, enlargement, etc.) in the amount of $2,761.60. 

The motion is DENIED as to Items 1, 4, and 14, and those costs are allowed as claimed.

Case Number: 21BBCV00971    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: B

Hon. Lee S. Arian, Dept B 

 

MOTION NAME: GOOD FAITH SETTLEMENT DETERMINATION

Hearing Date: 06/26/26

CASE NO./NAME: 21BBCV00971/AMY R. AMIRA V. CMM, LLP, ET AL.

Moving Party: Plaintiff Amy R. Amira

Responding Party: None

Notice: Sufficient as to Defendants Agustin Gonzalez, Home Trend Developers, Inc., CMM, LLP, and Robert L. Schreiber; potentially insufficient as to Defendants Gregory Mogab and Anthony Olmedo who are in pro per, but there is no record of them consenting to electronic service per Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.253, subd. (b)(3), and Code Civ. Proc., § 1010.6, subd. (c)(1).

Ruling: DENY without prejudice.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Amy R. Amira, as Trustee of the Cynthia L. Amira Family Trust (“the Trust”), filed this fraud and negligence action on November 12, 2021. She filed her operative third amended complaint (“TAC”) on May 23, 2025. The TAC asserts twelve claims against nine defendants: CMM, LLP (“CMM”), Gregory A. Mogab (“Mogab”), OMG Construction Management, LLC (“OMG”), Agustin Gonzalez (“Gonzalez”), Anthony Olmedo (“Olmedo”), Home Trend Developers, Inc. (“Home Trend”), Ira J. Boren (“Boren”), Robert Schreiber (“Schreiber”) and derivatively and nominally defendant Hazelhurst Place Properties, L.P. (“Hazelhurst”). 

Plaintiff alleges defendants Mogab, Gonzalez, Olmedo, and OMG fraudulently induced her to invest in a rental property in North Hollywood, taking advantage of Mogab’s position as her accountant at CMM.

On May 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion for determination of good faith settlement. The motion is unopposed.

LEGAL STANDARD

       CCP section 877.6(a)(1) provides that “[a]ny party to an action wherein it is alleged that two or more parties are joint tortfeasors or co-obligors on a contract debt shall be entitled to a hearing on the issue of the good faith of a settlement entered into by the plaintiff . . . and one or more alleged tortfeasors or co-obligors . . . .” (Code. Civ. Proc., § 877.6(a)(1).) “A determination by the court that the settlement was made in good faith shall bar any other joint tortfeasor or co-obligor from any further claims against the settling tortfeasor or co-obligor for equitable comparative contribution, or partial or comparative indemnity, based on comparative negligence or comparative fault.”¿(Id., § 877.6(c).)¿Although a determination that a settlement was in good faith does not discharge any other party from liability, “it shall reduce the claims against the others in the amount stipulated” by the settlement.¿(Id., § 877(a).) 

Factors to consider in determining if a settlement was made in good faith include “a rough approximation of plaintiffs’ total recovery and the settlor’s proportionate liability, the amount paid in settlement, the allocation of settlement proceeds among plaintiffs, and a recognition that a settlor should pay less in settlement than he would if he were found liable after a trial.”¿(Tech-Bilt, Inc. v. Woodward-Clyde & Associates (1985) 38 Cal.3d 488, 499.)¿“Other relevant considerations include the financial conditions and insurance policy limits of settling defendants, as well as the existence of collusion, fraud, or tortious conduct aimed to injure the interests of nonsettling defendants.”¿(Id.)¿ 

The evaluation of whether a settlement was made in good faith is required to “be made on the basis of information available at the time of settlement.”¿(Tech-Bilt, Inc. v. Woodward-Clyde & Associates (1985) 38 Cal.3d 488, 499.)¿“[A] court not only looks at the alleged tortfeasor’s potential liability to the plaintiff, but it must also consider the culpability of the tortfeasor vis-à-vis other parties alleged to be responsible for the same injury.”¿(TSI Seismic Tenant Space, Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 159, 166.)¿ “Potential liability for indemnity to a nonsettling defendant is an important consideration for the trial court in determining whether to approve a settlement by an alleged tortfeasor.” (Id.) 

“The party asserting the lack of good faith shall have the burden of proof on that issue.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 877.6(d).)¿The party asserting the lack of good faith can establish that the proposed settlement was not a settlement made in good faith by showing the settlement is so far “out of the ballpark” in relation to the Tech-Bilt factors as to be inconsistent with the equitable objectives of the statute.¿(Tech-Bilt, Inc., supra, 38 Cal.3d at 499-500.) 

DISCUSSION                                                                    

An unopposed motion for determination of good faith of settlement need not contain a full and complete discussion of the Tech-Bilt factors (Tech-Bilt, Inc. v. Woodward-Clyde & Associates (1985) 38 Cal.3d 488) by declaration or affidavit; rather, a bare bones motion setting forth the grounds of good faith and a declaration containing a brief background of the case is sufficient. (City of Grand Terrace v. Superior Court (1987) 192 Cal.App.3d 1251, 1261.)

The motion is unopposed, and no objections to the settlement have been filed. The motion establishes that the settlement agreement was reached in good faith and includes a brief background of the case.

However, the Court notes two problems. First, the proof of service indicates electronic service on Defendants Gregory Mogab and Anthony Olmedo who are in pro per. (Motion, pp. 328-329 of pdf.) But, there is no record of these Defendants consenting to electronic service per California Rules of Court, rule 2.253, subdivision (b)(3), and Code of Civil Procedure section 1010.6, subd. (c)(1). It is therefore unclear to the Court if Defendants Gregory Mogab and Anthony Olmedo were properly served with notice of this motion.

Second, Plaintiff filed a notice of lodging of documents conditionally under seal in support of motion for determination of good faith settlement for what appears to be an unredacted version of the motion and supporting documents, but no such documents were lodged with the Court. (Notice of Lodging (5/15/26).) The motion on file with the Court contains redactions which the Court is otherwise unable to fully review and determine whether some of the settlement terms are proper.

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion for determination of good faith settlement is DENIED without prejudice. 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiff’s motion for determination of good faith settlement is DENIED without prejudice.

Plaintiff shall provide notice of this order.

MOTION FOR PREJUDGMENT INTEREST

Hearing Date: 06/26/26

CASE NO./NAME: 23BBCV00729/ARABIA v. AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR COMPANY, INC.

Moving Party: Plaintiff CAROL J ARABIA

Responding Party: Defendant AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR COMPANY, INC.

Notice: Sufficient 

Ruling: GRANT

 

BACKGROUND

This is a Song-Beverly Act (lemon law) action. On April 3, 2023, Plaintiff filed the Complaint against Defendant, alleging that Defendant failed to repair Plaintiff’s 2020 Honda Odyssey, VIN: 5FNRL6H70LB012268 (the “Vehicle”) in a reasonable number of attempts when it manifested several defects, which substantially impaired the Vehicle’s safety and use.

On May 8, 2023, Defendant filed its Answer to the Complaint.

From April 27, 2026 through May 5, 2026, the matter was tried before a jury, which returned a verdict in favor of Plaintiff for $34,043.14 in damages. Plaintiff now moves for prejudgment interest in the amount of $22,317.36 per Civil Code Section 3287, subdivision (a) or alternatively, $10,533.57 per Civil Code Section 3287, subdivision (b). Defendant opposes.

LEGAL STANDARD

“A person who is entitled to recover damages certain, or capable of being made certain by calculation, and the right to recover which is vested in the person upon a particular day, is entitled also to recover interest thereon from that day, except when the debtor is prevented by law, or by the act of the creditor from paying the debt. This section is applicable to recovery of damages and interest from any debtor, including the state or any county, city, city and county, municipal corporation, public district, public agency, or any political subdivision of the state.” (Civ. Code, § 3287, subd. (a); cf. Civ. Code, § 3287, subd. (b) [“Every person who is entitled under any judgment to receive damages based upon a cause of action in contract where the claim was unliquidated, may also recover interest thereon from a date prior to the entry of judgment as the court may, in its discretion, fix, but in no event earlier than the date the action was filed.”].)

“The purpose of prejudgment interest is to compensate plaintiff for loss of use of his or her property. [Citation.]” (North Oakland Medical Clinic v. Rogers (1998) 65 Cal.App.4th 824, 828.) “Under subdivision (a) the court has no discretion, but must award prejudgment interest upon request, from the first day there exists both a breach and a liquidated claim.” (Id.) “Under subdivision (b) the court has discretion to decide whether prejudgment interest should be awarded on an unliquidated contractual claim. It is up to the judge to determine the date from which interest runs, but in no event may the court fix a date earlier than the filing of the action.” (Id. at 829.)

DISCUSSION                                                                                                                 

As preliminary matter, the Court notes that Defendant contends the present motion is not ripe for adjudication because Plaintiff has not filed a proposed judgment on the jury verdict with Court. (Opp. at 3:4-9.) However, Defendant cites to no legal authority to support the assertion that a proposed judgment on the jury verdict must be filed before a party may move for prejudgment interest. Contrarily, “prejudgment interest should be awarded in the judgment on the basis of a specific request therefor made before entry of judgment.” (North Oakland Medical Clinic v. Rogers (1998) 65 Cal.App.4th 824, 830.) As such, the present motion is ripe for adjudication on the merits.

With respect to Plaintiff’s request for prejudgment interest under Civil Code Section 3287, subdivision (a), Defendant contends that the amount of damages were uncertain and uncapable of being made certain by calculation except by jury verdict. (Opp. at 3:11-4:26.) The Court agrees.  Although each party has cited to cases that support their view, the Court finds that the amount of damages were uncertain until the jury decided upon such damages.  Thus, the Court does not award pre-judgment interest under Section 3287(a).   

However, such interest under Section 3287(b) is appropriate.  Plaintiff, while able to use her vehicle, was unable to enjoy a vehicle consistent with what she contracted to buy, i.e., a vehicle with no significant defect.  And, Defendant failed to comply with the contractual warranty it was required to honor.  As such, the Court finds a contractual basis for interest under Section 3287(b).  In its discretion, the Court awards pre-judgment interest under that section from the date of the Complaint.

Defendant argues that lemon law actions are limited to an interest rate of seven (7) percent per annum not ten (10) percent per annum as proposed by Plaintiff. (Opp. at 9:12-24; Reply at 10:2-7.) The cases cited by Defendant do not state that the interest rate for prejudgment interest in a lemon law action is seven (7) percent. By contrast, the California Constitution states that: “For any loan or forbearance of any money, goods, or things in action, if the money, goods, or things in action are for use primarily for personal, family, or household purposes, at a rate not exceeding 10 percent per annum.” (Cal. Const., art. XV, § 1, subd. (1).) The Vehicle in this case is a “good” for the purposes set forth in the California Constitution and Song-Beverly Act. Thus, Plaintiff is entitled to prejudgment interest at the rate of ten (10) percent per annum.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiff Carol J. Arabia’s motion for prejudgment interest is granted in the amount of $10,533.57 pursuant to this Court’s discretion under subdivision (b) of Civil Code § 3287.

Plaintiff Carol J. Arabia  shall provide notice of this order.

Hearing Date: 6/26/26¿ 

CASE NO./NAME: 23BBCV01487 MAHNAZ SADANIAN, et al. vs PROVIDENCE ST. JOSEPH MED CTR EMERGENCY DEPT., et al.

Moving Party: Defendant Roger C. Lai

Responding Party: Plaintiff Mahnaz Sadanian

Notice: Sufficient¿ 

Ruling: MOOT

 

Background

         This action arises from personal injuries Plaintiff Mahnaz Sadanian allegedly suffered after a visit to Providence Saint Joseph Medical Center Emergency Department, where she was treated by Defendant Dr. Roger C. Lai. The Third Amended Complaint alleges five causes of action: (1) professional negligence; (2) battery; (3) sexual battery; (4) loss of consortium; and (5) intentional infliction of emotional distress.

Motions to Compel Initial Responses

Defendant Roger C. Lai moves to compel Plaintiff Mahnaz Sadanian to provide responses to Lai’s Requests for Production, Set Two, and Special Interrogatories, Set Three.

On May 7, 2026, Plaintiff served initial responses to the Requests for Production and to the Special Interrogatories. On May 14, 2026, Lai’s counsel sent a meet and confer letter outlining the insufficient responses to Requests for Production Nos. 8-11, 13-14, 16-17, 19-20, 22-23, 25-26, 28-29, 31-32, 34, 37-38, 42-44, 46-50, 52, 54-58 and Special Interrogatories Nos. 54, 58, 63-66, and 75. On June 3, 2026, Plaintiff served supplemental responses.

Accordingly, the motions are now moot.

Lai moves for sanctions against Plaintiff and her attorney in the sum of $2,220.00 for both motions ($1,110.00 each). Lai’s counsel declares she spent a combined 4 hours preparing the motions and will incur an additional 4 hours reviewing and replying to opposing papers and 2 hours appearing at the motions’ hearings. Counsel bills at a rate of $210/hour. Lai also incurred $120 in filing fees.

Plaintiff did not provide sufficient justification for failing to provide responses to the discovery requests before the filing of these motions (February 17, 2026). The Court finds the amount of sanctions requested reasonable and thus awards sanctions of $2,340 against Plaintiff and her attorney, jointly and severally,to be paid within 30 days.

Plaintiff also asked for sanctions against defendant in an unidentified amount based on the theory that Lai lacked substantial justification for these motions because Plaintiff substantially complied with her discovery obligations.  However, Plaintiff’s responses were only served after these motions were filed, almost 3 months after they were due.  Thus, Plaintiff’s request is denied.

DEMURRER TO SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT IS OVERRULED

 

Dept. B

8:30 a.m.

June 26, 2026

Background

On November 21, 2025, Plaintiff filed the Second Amended Complaint against Defendant Laurent Café LLC. Plaintiff alleges she is legally blind and relies on screen reading software to access websites. Plaintiff asserts that she visited Defendant's website, cafelaurent.com, on multiple occasions to review the restaurant's menu, obtain the restaurant's address, and place an online order. Plaintiff alleges that during her August 2024 and December 2024 visits to the website, she encountered multiple accessibility barriers preventing her from ordering food online or independently reviewing menu content.

Plaintiff asserts that because the website was not usable with screen reading software, she was deterred from visiting the restaurant in August 2024. Plaintiff later visited the restaurant in December 2024 with sight assistance and claims she was forced to rely on sighted assistance at the restaurant because the website had prevented her from reviewing the menu in advance.  Based on these allegations, Plaintiff alleges a violation of the Americans with Disabilities Act (42 U.S.C. § 12101 et seq.), and based on that violation, a violation of the Unruh Act (Cal Civil Code § 51 et seq.)

Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.¿ (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)¿When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context.¿ (Wilson v. Transit Authority of City of Sacramento (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 716, 720-21.)¿In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿“A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not on the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Id.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.) Before filing a demurrer, demurring party is also required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41.) 

Unruh Civil Rights Act

“All persons within the jurisdiction of this state are free and equal, and no matter what their sex, race, color, religion, ancestry, national origin, disability, medical condition, genetic information, marital status, sexual orientation, citizenship, primary language, or immigration status are entitled to the full and equal accommodations, advantages, facilities, privileges, or services in all business establishments of every kind whatsoever.”  Civ. Code, § 51(b).  “No business establishment of any kind whatsoever shall discriminate against . . . any person in this state on account of any characteristic listed or defined in subdivision (b) or (e) of Section 51 . . . because the person is perceived to have one or more of those characteristics, or because the person is associated with a person who has, or is perceived to have, any of those characteristics.”  Id., § 51.5(a).    

ADA 

        The Unruh Civil Rights Act (“Unruh Act”) prohibits discrimination on the basis of sex, race, color, religion, ancestry, national origin, disability, medical condition, genetic information, marital status, or sexual orientation. (See Civ. Code § 51.) Civil Code Section 51 provides that any violation of the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 (“ADA”) constitutes a violation of the Unruh Act. (See id., § 51(f); Jankey v. Song Koo Lee (2012) 55 Cal.4th 1038, 1044 (finding violations of the ADA are per se violations of the Unruh Act).) A plaintiff seeking damages for violation of the Unruh Act based on a violation of the ADA pursuant to section 51(f) need not prove intentional discrimination. (See Munson v. Del Taco, Inc. (2009) 46 Cal.4th 661, 664-65.)  

        The ADA prohibits discrimination on the basis of disability in the full and equal enjoyment of services, facilities, and accommodations of a place of public accommodation by any person who owns, leases, or operates a place of public accommodation. (See 42 U.S.C. § 12182(a).) Discrimination under the ADA includes “a failure to remove architectural barriers, and communication barriers that are structural in nature, in existing facilities . . . where such removal is readily achievable . . . “ and a failure to provide auxiliary aids and services, unless it would result in an undue burden. (42 U.S.C. § 12182(b)(2)(A)(iii), (iv).) Furthermore, whether a facility provides full and equal access is defined in part by the ADA Accessibility Guidelines. (See 28 C.F.R. pt. 36, app. A; Chapman v. Pier 1 Imports (U.S.) Inc. (9th Cir. 2015) 779 F.3d 1001, 1006.)  

        “[T]o prevail on a discrimination claim under Title III, a plaintiff must show that: (1) he is disabled within the meaning of the ADA; (2) the defendant is a private entity that owns, leases, or operates a place of public accommodation; and (3) the plaintiff was denied public accommodations by the defendant because of his disability.” (Arizona ex rel. Goddard v. Harkins Amusement Enterprises, Inc. (9th Cir. 2010) 603 F.3d 666, 670.)  

Discussion

Defendant’s demurrer challenges the Second Amended Complaint on two grounds: failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action and uncertainty.

Defendant argues that the SAC fails to allege facts establishing a causal connection between any identified website barrier and Plaintiff’s alleged inability to locate the restaurant, review the menu, or place an order. The Court previously held the complaint must “clearly link the specific barriers described in subsections (a) through (d) to the claimed inability to locate the restaurant, review the menu, or place an order.” An ADA violation can arise from website inaccessibility when there is a nexus between the website and the goods or services of a physical place of public accommodation. (Id. at 905–06.)

In Robles, the plaintiff, who was visually impaired, alleged that Domino’s website prevented him from ordering pizza for delivery. The Ninth Circuit held that the ADA applied to the website because it “facilitate[s] access to the goods and services of a place of public accommodation” and “connect[s] customers to the goods and services of Domino’s physical restaurants.” (Robles v. Domino’s Pizza, LLC (9th Cir. 2019) 913 F.3d 898, 905-06.)

Courts have applied the same reasoning in other website accessibility cases. (See Farr v. Hobby Lobby Stores, Inc. (C.D. Cal. Apr. 29, 2020) 2020 WL 3978078, at *2 [so long as there is a nexus between a physical location and the website, web-based barriers can create ADA injury].) A sufficient nexus exists, for example, when a website prevents a plaintiff from ordering products for pickup or delivery, as in Robles, or when web-based barriers prevent locating a physical store, as in Farr. (See also Williams v. Amazon.com Inc. (E.D. Cal. Oct. 6, 2020) 2020 WL 5909060, at *1 [plaintiff adequately alleged deterrence where inaccessible website prevented locating store information]; Haggar v. Ashley Furniture Indus., Inc. (C.D. Cal. Dec. 12, 2019) 2019 WL 8886026, at *3 [plaintiffs alleged inability to use website to locate stores and order products available at physical locations].)

Previously, the Court was persuaded by Defendant’s argument regarding the lack of a clear link between the specific website barriers alleged and Plaintiff’s claimed inability to access goods and services at a physical location. (See Whitaker v. Tesla Motors, Inc. (9th Cir. 2021) 985 F.3d 1173, 1176; see also Whitaker v. Trinitas Cellars, LLC (N.D. Cal. June 17, 2022) 2022 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 108468, at *10–11.)

Plaintiff again alleges that:

a. The home page has graphics, links, and buttons that are not labeled or are incorrectly labeled, or lack alternative text (“Alt-text”). Alt-text is invisible code embedded beneath a graphical image on a website. Web accessibility requires that Alt-text be coded with each picture so that screen-reading software can speak the Alt-text where a sighted user sees pictures. Alt-text does not change the visual presentation, but instead a text box shows when the cursor moves over the picture. The lack of Alt-text on these graphics prevents screen-readers from accurately vocalizing a description of the graphics;

b. Plaintiff encountered multiple unlabeled or mislabeled buttons and links. Without descriptive alternate text, Plaintiffs, and other screen-reader users, have no way to discern the purpose or function of the button or link;

c. Plaintiff encountered multiple pages containing insufficient navigational headings requiring Plaintiff to expend substantial additional time to access information;

d. Plaintiff encountered an inaccessible slideshow when attempting to access the Website requiring Plaintiff to expend substantial additional time to access information.

Plaintiff later alleges that she was unable to access location information, view the menu, and place an order because this information was inaccessible to screen reader software. Additionally, Plaintiff has added paragraph 53, which states,

“Due to the unlabeled buttons, lack of Alt-text, the structure of the headings and Website, cursor traps, and other barriers, Plaintiff was unable to fully and independently access the Website when visiting for the dual purpose of confirming compliance with the UCRA and ADA and to browse the menu before visiting the Venice Boulevard location.”

(Second Amended Complaint, ¶ 53.)

The Second Amended Complaint now clearly links the specific barriers described in subsections (a) through (d) to the claimed inability to locate the restaurant, review the menu, or place an order. Additionally, the Court finds that the SAC is not uncertain.

         The demurrer is OVERRULED.

MOTIONS TO COMPEL FURTHER

Hearing Date: 6/26/26¿ 

CASE NO./NAME: 25NNCV04476 BARBERIO ENTERPRISES, INC. vs SCOTT SELWAY, et al.

Moving Party: Defendant Michael Dean Duncan

Responding Party: Unopposed

Notice: Sufficient¿ 

Ruling: GRANTED

 

Background 

This action arises from allegations that Defendant Scott Selway breached his duty of loyalty to Plaintiff Barberio Enterprises, Inc. that was denoted in the collective bargaining agreement, employee handbook, and confidentiality agreement he signed at the commencement of his employment, including, but not limited to: (1) by misappropriating Plaintiff’s trade secrets; (2) obtaining his C20 license while employed with Plaintiff; (3) forming Defendants Selway Mechanical HVAC, LLC and commencing employment with Infinite Circulation in order to directly compete with Plaintiff; and (4) inducing, soliciting, stealing, and raiding key personnel, including Defendant Michael Dean Duncan (“Duncan”). Plaintiff brings nine causes of action: (1) breach of fiduciary duty; (2) breach of duty of loyalty; (3) breach of contract (confidentiality agreement); (4) breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing; (5) misappropriation of trade secrets; (6) tortious interference with prospective economic relations; (7) unfair competition; (8) conversion; and (9) injunctive relief.

 

Motions to Compel Further Responses

The instant motions concern Plaintiff’s responses to Form Interrogatories, Set One (Nos. 8.4, 8.7, 8.8, & 12.1) and Requests for Production, Set One (Nos. 1-2 & 4-54). Plaintiff asserted boilerplate responses to all of Duncan’s discovery and did not provide any substantive responses. The parties met and conferred multiple times, during which Duncan provided Plaintiff extensions to provide supplemental responses, and Plaintiff agreed to do so. Plaintiff’s counsel also argued about the necessity of an AEO-level protective order. However, Plaintiff did not provide any supplemental responses. Duncan now moves to compel further responses. Plaintiff did not file an opposition.

As to Form Interrogatory No. 8.4, which asks for Plaintiff’s monthly income at the time of the incident and how the amount was calculated, Plaintiff responded as follows: “Barberio Enterprises, Inc. dba Westco Service Company is a privately held corporation. The company does not calculate “monthly income” in the manner suggested by this interrogatory, and its revenue and expense reports vary from month to month and year to year. To the extent income or revenue is at issue in this case, Responding Party will produce relevant and responsive documents at the appropriate time and subject to a mutually agreeable protective order. Discovery and investigation are continuing, and Westco reserves the right to amend this response.”

As to Form Interrogatory No. 8.7, which asks for the total income Plaintiff has lost to date as a result of the incident and how the amount was calculated, Plaintiff responded as follows: “Responding Party contends that it has lost business opportunities and income as a result of Defendant’s conduct, including the misappropriation of proprietary information and solicitation of a key client. While the precise amount is still being calculated, Responding Party estimates that the loss of business related to The Culver Studios contract alone represents a substantial ongoing monthly loss in revenue. Additional losses will be supported by financial records, profit-and-loss statements, and customer account history, which will be produced at the appropriate time subject to a mutually acceptable protective order. Discovery and investigation are continuing, and Westco reserves the right to amend this response.” Plaintiff also objected to the interrogatory as premature.

As to Form Interrogatory No. 8.8., which asks whether Plaintiff will lose income in the future as a result of the incident and for further information like relevant fact, estimated amount, and calculation method, Plaintiff responded as follows: “Yes. (a) Responding Party anticipates future loss of income as a result of Defendants’ wrongful conduct, including but not limited to the misappropriation of trade secrets, breach of contractual and fiduciary duties, and wrongful solicitation of a key client. (b) Responding Party is continuing to assess the full scope of damages, but anticipates ongoing monthly loss of income that may exceed several hundred thousand dollars annually. (c) Responding Party is unable to determine with certainty how long the income loss will continue, but believes that unless remedial relief is granted, such losses will continue indefinitely or until the account is restored or comparable revenue is replaced. (d) The claim for future lost income will be calculated based on historical earnings from the client account at issue, projected growth, overhead, and profit margin data, and will be supported through financial records, expert analysis, and documentation of client loss. Discovery and investigation are continuing, and Westco reserves the right to amend this response.”

As to Form Interrogatory No. 12.1, which seeks personal identifying information regarding individuals who witnessed the incident or evets occurring immediately before or after the incident, who made any statements at the scene or heard any statements made about the incident by others at the scene, and who claims to have knowledge about the incident, Plaintiff responded with 5 names, two of whom are named Defendants. Plaintiff’s response did not specify which subcategory of the interrogatory the names are associated with.

As to the Requests for Production Nos. 1-4, 6-8, 10-11, 14-15, & 19-20, Plaintiff responded as follows: “Responding Party will produce non-privileged, responsive documents within its possession, custody, or control that can be located following a reasonable search. Discovery and investigation are ongoing, and Responding Party reserves the right to supplement or amend this response as additional information becomes available.” For the rest, Plaintiff responded: “Subject to and without waiving the foregoing objections, Responding Party responds as follows: Responding Party will produce non-privileged, responsive documents within its possession, custody, or control that can be located following a reasonable search. Additionally, Responding Party states that responsive documents may exist that are currently in the possession of third parties or other parties to this litigation. These documents have been or will be requested through the discovery process and will be produced upon receipt to the extent they are [non-privileged], non-confidential, and responsive. Discovery and investigation are ongoing, and Responding Party reserves the right to supplement or amend this response as additional information becomes available.”

A majority of Plaintiff’s responses contain a set of boilerplate objections and claims of privilege and a right to privacy. Such objections are improper in that they lack any specificity. Under California law, objections must be specific, “boilerplate” objections and “nuisance objections” are inappropriate and even sanctionable. (Korea Data Systems Co., Ltd. v. Superior Court, (1997) 51 Cal.App.4th 1513, 1516; Standon Co., Inc. v. Superior Court, (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 898, 902-903.) Plaintiff also bears the burden of justifying its objections in response to a motion to compel. (Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court, (2000) 22 Cal.4th 245, 355.)

Plaintiff fails to explain what sought information implicates Plaintiff’s privacy rights, prematurely seeks expert witness information, or infringes upon the attorney-client privilege and the attorney work product doctrine. Specifically, as to the RFAs, Plaintiff has not provided sufficient factual information for other parties to evaluate the merits of Plaintiff’s privilege claims as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.240. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.240, subd. (c)(1) [“If an objection is based on a claim of privilege or a claim that the information sought is protected work product, the response shall provide sufficient factual information for other parties to evaluate the merits of that claim, including, if necessary, a privilege log.”].)

Plaintiff also responded to Form Interrogatory No. 8.4 that the company does not calculate “monthly income” as suggested, but paradoxically responded to No. 8.8(b) that it “anticipates ongoing monthly loss of income that may exceed several hundred thousand dollars annually.”

Plaintiff produced boilerplate objections and promised to provide supplemental responses but failed to do so. Plaintiff also did not file an opposition and has failed to provide any justification for any of its objections. Accordingly, the motions are granted. Plaintiff is ordered to provide code-compliant responses, with verification, and without objection (although Plaintiff may assert attorney-client privilege and/or attorney work product to the extent appropriate) within 20 days of today.

Duncan moves for sanctions against Plaintiff and its attorney of record in the sum of $13,750.00 for both motions. For the motion to compel further responses to RPDs, Duncan requests $9,900.00 in sanctions: 12 hours of attorney work for preparing the motion and supporting papers and 6 hours for reviewing the opposition and preparing a reply. For the motion to compel further responses to FROGs, Duncan requests $3,850.00 in sanctions: 5 hours for preparing the motion and supporting papers and 2 hours for reviewing the opposition and preparing a reply. Counsel bills at a rate of $550/hour as an attorney with more than a decade of experience.

First, the Court reduces the request by 8 hours because no opposition was filed. Second, the Court finds 17 hours of attorney work to prepare two discovery motions to be excessive. The Court reduces the total hours to 10, 5 hours per motion. Accordingly, sanctions in the amount of $5,500 are ordered against Plaintiff and its attorney of record, jointly and severally, payable within 20 days of today.

MOTION TO QUASH

CASE NO./NAME: 26NNCV00989 JING XING FENG et al. vs. BAOLI WU et al.

Moving Party: Defendant Baoli Wu

Responding Party: None

Notice: Sufficient

Ruling: GRANTED

 

Background

 

On February 9, 2026, plaintiffs Jing Xing Feng and Rongxiang Feng (Plaintiffs) filed this action against defendant Baoli Wu (Defendant) arising out of a motor vehicle accident that occurred on November 26, 2024. Defendant was allegedly personally served as an individual, on March 31, 2026, at 7:26 p.m., at 3035 E Oak Knoll Loop, Ontario, CA 91761-3872, through a registered process server identified as Estevan Berrellez. Defendant now moves the Court for an order quashing service of summons.

Legal Standard

“‘Service of process, under longstanding tradition in our system of justice, is fundamental to any procedural imposition on a named defendant.’” (AO Alfa-Bank v. Yakovlev (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 189, 202.) “To establish personal jurisdiction, compliance with statutory procedures for service of process is essential.” (Kremerman v. White (2021) 71 Cal.App.5th 358, 371.) A defendant’s knowledge of the action does not dispense with the statutory requirements for service of summons. (Kappel v. Bartlett (1988) 200 Cal.App.3d 1457, 1466.) 

“A defendant, on or before the last day of his or her time to plead or within any further time that the court may for good cause allow,” may move “to quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over him or her.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10(a)(1).) A defendant has 30 days after the service of the summons to file a responsive pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 412.20(a)(3).) 

“When a defendant challenges the court’s personal jurisdiction on the ground of improper service of process, ‘the burden is on the plaintiff to prove the existence of jurisdiction by proving, inter alia, the facts requisite to an effective service.’” (Summers v. McClanahan (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 403, 413.) Filing of a proof of service that complies with the applicable statutory requirements creates a rebuttable presumption of proper service. (Am. Express Centurion Bank v. Zara (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 383, 390.) 

Discussion

Defendant argues that service was improperly effectuated at 3035 E. Oak Knoll Loop, Ontario, California because Defendant does not reside at this address. (Motion, p. 1.) Defendant resides in China. (Ibid.) Defendant argues that service was effectuated at the home of Baojing Wang, Defendant’s estranged wife. (Ibid.) Defendant argues that because he has not been served the court lacks jurisdiction over him. (Motion, p. 3.)

Here, personal service was allegedly effectuated on Defendant at 3035 E. Oak Knoll Loop, Ontario, California. (Nguyen Decl., Exh. A “Proof of Service”.) However, Plaintiffs have not filed the proof of service.

Defendant’s estranged wife Baojing Wang declares that Defendant abandoned her and moved to parts unknown in China approximately three years ago. (Wang Decl., ¶ 6.) Boajing Wang declares that she resides at 3035 E. Oak Knoll Loop and states that Defendant was not present at the residence on March 31, 2026, when he was allegedly served. (Wang Decl., ¶¶ 7-9.) Boajing Wang also declares that she was not personally served with the Summons and Complaint at any time. (Wang Decl., ¶10.)

When a defendant files a motion to quash, the burden is on the plaintiff to establish that service of the summons and complaint was proper. (Summers v. McClanahan (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 403, 413.) As Plaintiffs have failed to file an opposition to the motion, Plaintiffs have failed to meet Plaintiffs’ burden. As such, Defendant’s motion must be granted. Moreover, Defendant has submitted sufficient evidence to show the motion should be granted.

Accordingly, Defendant Baoli Wu’s motion to quash service of summons is granted.
