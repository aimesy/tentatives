Background

 

This is an action for contractual fraud. On November 22, 2024, Plaintiffs Eng Tan (“Eng”) and Vantage Associates, Inc. (“Vantage”) (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed this action against Defendants Vantage Associates, Inc., Employee Stock Ownership Trust (“Trust”), Kevin Wright, Mary Normand, Miguel Paredes, and Does 1 to 10.

 

On April 25, 2025, Plaintiffs filed the First Amended Complaint. At the Case Management Conference on December 17, 2025, all parties stipulated to Plaintiff’s leave to file the Second Amended Complaint (SAC). (12/17/25 Minute Order.)

 

The SAC alleges that the Trust owned all 1,361,092.218 shares of Vantage. Miguel Paredes was the trustee of the Trust. Defendant Mary Normand and Kevin Wright were Co-Chief Executive Officers of Vantage. Eng intended to purchase all shares owned by the Trust, at a purchase price of $2,750,000.00. The gravamen of the FAC is that Defendants Trust, Paredes, Wright, and Normand made certain misrepresentations to induce Eng to enter into a written Stock Sale and Purchase Agreement (“SSPA”). Pursuant to the SSPA, the final purchase price was $2,450,000.00, minus the $850,000.00 deposited in escrow. On April 24, 2024, the parties signed the SSPA, and the Trust transferred the shares to Eng. The SSPA is attached as “Exhibit D” to the SAC.

 

The parties have been unable to agree on adjustments to the purchase price associated with final accounting and post-closing expenses, and thus, the $850,000.00 remains in escrow. The SAC alleges the following causes of action: 1) Breach of Contract, 2) Breach of Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing, 3) Fraud and Intentional Deceit, 4) Negligent Misrepresentation, 5) Conversion, and 6) Breach of Fiduciary Duty.

 

Defendants Kevin Wright and Mary Normand (“Normand Defendants”) now demur to the third cause of action (brought by Eng), fifth cause of action (brought by Vantage), and sixth cause of action (brought by Vantage). Normand Defendants also move to strike portions of the SAC. In the alternative, Normand Defendants move to compel arbitration on the fifth and sixth causes of action, both brought by Vantage, pursuant to an arbitration agreement between the parties.

 

On April 28, 2026, the Court found that the Demurrer was styled as two separate requests. Thus, the Court 1) specially set the Motion to Compel Arbitration for a noticed hearing and allowed for a supplemental opposition and a reply to be filed, and 2) continued the Demurrer to today’s date. (4/28/26 Minute Order.)

 

I.             Demurrer with Motion to Strike

 

Legal Standard

 

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (“Donabedian”) (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.) 

 

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.) 

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a) and section 435.5 require that the parties meet and confer “in person,¿by telephone, or by video conference.” 

 

On December 29, 2025, Defense counsel met and conferred with Plaintiffs’ counsel via telephone regarding the issues in the Demurrer and Motion to Strike. (Farber Decls., ¶ 2.)

 

The Court finds that the meet and confer requirement has been met.

 

Discussion

 

          Third Cause of Action – Fraud and Intentional Deceit (Eng v. Trust,

          Paredes, and Normand Defendants)

 

“One who willfully deceives another with intent to induce him to alter his position to his injury or risk, is liable for any damage which he thereby suffers.” (Civ. Code § 1709.)

 

“The elements of fraud are (a) a misrepresentation (i.e., false representation); (b) scienter or knowledge of its falsity; (c) intent to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Ctr. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) The facts constituting the alleged fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.)

 

The elements for fraudulent concealment are as follows: 1) defendant concealed or suppressed a material fact, 2) defendant was under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, 3) defendant intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, 4) plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would not have acted in the same way knowing of the concealed or suppressed fact, 5) causation, and the plaintiff sustained damage. (See Blickman Turkus, LP v. MF Downtown Sunnyvale, LLC (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 858, 868; see also Lovejoy v. AT&T Corp. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 85, 96; Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 748.) The rule of specifically pleading how, when, where, to whom, and by what means, misrepresentations were communicated, applies to affirmative misrepresentations, not to concealment. (See Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn., Inc. (2009) (“Alfaro”) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384.)

 

Normand Defendants argue that the third cause of action fails to allege 1) what specific misrepresentations they made to Eng, 2) their intent to defraud, 3) justifiable reliance. To the extent that the cause of action alleges fraudulent concealment, there are no allegations regarding Defendants’ duty to disclose.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the SAC adequately alleges fraud. Specifically, Normand Defendants intentionally misrepresented and/or omitted the following material facts with the intent to induce Eng to purchase Vantage.

 

1.    Defendants intentionally misrepresented that the building in Alva, Oklahoma did not include the land but was subject to a lease agreement. (SAC, ¶ ¶ 17, 28, 29, 32, 73a, 73b, 73(c), 74, 76.)

2.    Defendants provided Eng with false financial information of Vantage. (SAC, ¶¶ 18, 22, 23 30, 31, 32(c), 73(h), 75, 76.)

3.    Defendants omitted that they paid themselves $900,000.00 in unpaid bonuses prior to the sale closing (SAC, ¶¶ 32(d) 73(d), 76, 100.)

4.    Defendants misled Eng to believe that Vantage’s employees would remain employed and omitted that the employees were going to quit after the sale closed. (SAC, ¶¶ 32(e), 73(e), 76.)

5.    Defendants omitted that Vantage was the subject of a Department of Labor investigation. (SAC, ¶¶ 32(f), 73(f), 76.)

6.    Defendants omitted that Vantage’s assets were fully insured when the Santa Fe Springs Property was not insured. (SAC, ¶¶ 32(g) 73(g), 76.)

 

The Court finds that the representations regarding the Alva facility involve both an affirmative misrepresentation and an omission. Specifically, the SAC alleges that, in a meeting with Norman and Normand’s son, Eng was informed that “VANTAGE owned the real estate where the Alva, Oklahoma facility was located.” (SAC, ¶ 16.) The Court finds that the SAC is not specific as to who exactly made this representation to Eng. As to the omission, the SAC alleges that Wright sent a PowerPoint to Eng that failed to disclose that the land that the Alva facility sits on was leased, not owned. (SAC, ¶¶ 17, 29.) The Court finds that the SAC fails to allege that Normand Defendants had a duty to disclose such information.

 

As to the false financial information provided to Eng, an affirmative misrepresentation, the Court agrees that the SAC does not specifically allege what this false information entailed or how it was communicated to Eng.

 

As to the remaining omissions, previously noted, the rule of heightened pleading for fraud does not apply to fraudulent concealment. (See Alfaro, supra, 171 Cal.App.4th at p.1384.) However, the Court finds that the SAC fails to allege that Normand Defendants had a duty to disclose such information.

 

As all alleged misrepresentations/omissions, the Court finds that the SAC fails to allege why Eng’s reliance was justifiable. The allegations regarding reliance are conclusory. (SAC, ¶¶ 79-80.) The Court finds that the SAC adequately alleges Normand Defendants’ intent to defraud. (SAC, ¶¶ 74-75, 78.)

 

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the third cause of action is SUSTAINED with 30 days leave to amend.

 

          Vantage’s Standing

 

Normand Defendants argue that Eng lacks standing to bring a derivative suit on behalf of Vantage because he was not a shareholder at the time of the alleged misconduct. Specifically, Vantage’s claims against the Normand Defendants took place before the sale closed—i.e., before Eng was a shareholder. Further, Eng did not obtain a finding from the Court, via motion, that he fits into the “continuing wrong” exception to this rule pursuant to Corporations Code section 800.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the fifth and sixth causes of action are not based on a derivative suit by Eng. Rather, they are brought by Vantage in its own capacity.

 

In reply, Normand Defendants do not address the standing issue.

 

The Court finds that Vantage has standing to bring conversion and breach of fiduciary duty claims against its corporate officers in its own capacity.

 

          Fifth Cause of Action – Conversion (Vantage v. Normand Defendants)

 

“Conversion is the wrongful exercise of dominion over the property of another. The elements of a conversion claim are: (1) the plaintiff’s ownership or right to possession of the property; (2) the defendant’s conversion by a wrongful act or disposition of property rights; and (3) damages.” (Lee v. Hanley (2015) 61 Cal.4th 1225, 1240.) 

 

Normand Defendants argue that the SAC alleges that that bonuses totaling $900,000.00 were “due” to, and later paid to, Defendants. (SAC, ¶¶ 32(d), 66(d), 73(d), 89(d).) Defendants assert that, the SAC cannot allege that Defendants converted such funds where the funds were “due” to them. In other words, the SAC admits that Vantage has no possessory interest in the funds to support conversion.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the fifth cause of action is adequately alleged. The claim alleges that there was no corporate authority or any other authorization for Normand Defendants to pay out the bonuses to themselves. (SAC, ¶ 100.) To the extent that the Court seeks clarification, Plaintiffs should be granted leave to amend.

 

The fifth cause of action alleges, in relevant part, the following:

 

“98. VANTAGE was the owner of all funds in the bank accounts of VANTAGE, but as Co-CEO’s WRIGHT and/or NORMAND had access to the VANTAGE bank accounts and other assets.

 

99. VANTAGE herein alleges that within the last year, prior to the Closing of the shares from the TRUST to ENG, WRIGHT and NORMAND directed VANTAGE property, in the form of cash, in the amount of $900,000.00 by issuing payment from VANTAGE to WRIGHT and NORMAND as individuals.

 

100. VANTAGE herein alleges that there was no corporate authority or any other authorization or legal basis for WRIGHT and/or NORMAND to direct the $900,000.00 payment.

 

101. Based upon the allegations contained herein, VANTAGE alleges that WRIGHT and/or NORMAND converted the funds of VANTAGE for their own personal benefit.

 

102. As a result of the conversion of funds from VANTAGE to WRIGHT and/or NORMAND, VANTAGE has been harmed in that it does not have right title or control of the $900,000.00 taken.

 

103. VANTAGE herein alleges that VANTAGE is entitled to a return of the funds, to wit, $900,000.00.”

 

(SAC, ¶¶ 98-103.)

 

The Court finds that SAC alleges that there were “unpaid bonuses due to” Normand Defendants (SAC, ¶¶ 32(d), 66(d), 73(d), 89(d)), while also alleging that there was no “legal basis” for them to direct such payment prior to closing. The Court finds that it is unclear how Vantage was damaged in the amount of $900,000.00—implying that the funds should not have been disbursed—where the SAC acknowledges that such amount were “due” to Defendants. The Court permits Plaintiffs the opportunity to clarify this discrepancy via amendment.

 

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED with 30 days leave to amend.

 

          Sixth Cause of Action – Breach of Fiduciary Duty (Vantage v. Normand

          Defendants)

 

The elements of a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty are the existence of a fiduciary relationship, breach of fiduciary duty, and damages.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 820.) 

 

The SAC alleges that Normand breached her fiduciary duty to Vantage because her son, an employee, received a raise when Vantage was losing money, and the Vantage Southern California operations were about to be shuttered, and there was no justification or basis […].” (SAC, ¶ 109.) Further, the SAC alleges that Wright breached his fiduciary duty by working for Normand’s company while he was employed by Vantage. (SAC, ¶ 108.) Normand Defendants assert that it is unclear how such conduct arises to the level of a breach of fiduciary duty. Further, the SAC does not allege damages stemming from this alleged breach.

 

Additionally, the SAC alleges the following: “Upon further investigation, VANTAGE believes that other facts establishing a breach of WRIGHT and NORMAND’s fiduciary duty will be discovered, and VANTAGE will amend this complaint with such facts when discovered.” (SAC, ¶ 110.) Normand Defendants argue that such allegation is insufficient to state a claim.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that it is reasonable to infer from the allegations that Normand Defendants breached their fiduciary duty and damaged Vantage.

 

The Court finds that the allegations are adequately pled. At this stage. the Court is satisfied that the alleged conduct constitutes a breach of fiduciary duty. Any specific details regarding the circumstances surrounding the alleged conduct constituting breach of fiduciary duty can be further clarified through discovery. (See Khoury v. Maly’s of Calif., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) The sixth cause of action is not so uncertain that Normand Defendants cannot reasonably understand what is being alleged against it.

 

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the sixth cause of action is OVERRULED.

“The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper:

 (a)¿Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.

 (b)¿Strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.”

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 436.)

 

Normand Defendants move to strike the following portions of the SAC:

 

1.    Conspiracy Allegations related to the Third Cause of Action: paragraphs 70, 76, 77.

2.    Punitive Damages Allegations related to the Third and Sixth Causes of Action: paragraphs 85 and 112.

3.    Attorney’s Fees Allegations: paragraph 6 of the Prayer for Damages.

 

Conspiracy Allegations

 

As the Demurrer was sustained to the third cause of action, the Motion to Strike conspiracy allegations related to that cause of action is MOOT.

 

Punitive Damages Allegations

 

As the Demurrer was sustained to the third cause of action, the Motion to Strike punitive damages allegations related to that cause of action is MOOT.

 

As to the sixth cause of action, Normand Defendants only argue that it fails to state a claim, and thus, punitive damages should be stricken. Because the Court overruled the Demurrer, the Motion to Strike is DENIED as to the punitive damages allegations in the sixth cause of action.

 

          Attorney’s Fees

 

Normand Defendants argue that the SAC cites no statute or contract that authorizes them to recover attorneys' fees against them pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that they may be entitled to attorney’s fees in relation to the fraud and conversion claims. Plaintiffs assert that plaintiffs in business fraud cases can recover attorney fees and treble damages under Penal Code section 496, subdivision (c), citing Siry Investment, L.P. v. Farkhondehpour (“Siry”) (2022) 13 Cal.5th 333.

 

In reply, Normand Defendants note that the SAC does not seek damages pursuant to Penal Code section 496, subdivision (c) as the plaintiffs did in Siry. (See Siry, supra, 13 Cal.5th 333 at p. 341.) Neither have Plaintiffs alleged sufficient facts to support an award of attorney’s fees under that provision.

 

The Court agrees with Normand Defendants. However, the Court will allow Plaintiffs to amend the SAC to establish a specific entitlement to attorney’s fees. Accordingly, the Motion to Strike the prayer for attorney’s fees is GRANTED with 30 days leave to amend.

 

II.            Motion to Compel Arbitration

 

As the Demurrer was sustained with leave to amend as to the fifth cause of action and overruled as to the sixth cause of action, the Court rules on Normand Defendants’ alternative Motion to Compel Arbitration between Vantage and Normand Defendants.


Legal Standard

 

Parties may be compelled to arbitrate a dispute upon the court finding that: (1) there was a valid agreement to arbitrate between the parties; and (2) said agreement covers the controversy or controversies in the parties’ dispute.¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; see also Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004)¿118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961.) A party petitioning to compel arbitration has the burden of establishing the existence of a valid agreement to arbitrate and the party opposing the petition has the burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, any fact necessary to its defense. (Banner Entertainment, Inc. v. Superior Court¿(1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 348, 356-57.)¿ 

 

Discussion

 

          Existence of an Arbitration Agreement

 

Under both the Federal Arbitration Act and California law, Arbitration Clauses are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a contract. (Winter v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943, 947.) The party moving to compel arbitration must establish the existence of a written arbitration agreement between the parties. (Code of Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.) In ruling on a motion to compel arbitration, the court must first determine whether the parties actually agreed to arbitrate the dispute, and general principles of California contract law help guide the court in making this determination. (Mendez v. Mid-Wilshire Health Care Center (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 534, 541.)

 

“With respect to the moving party’s burden to provide evidence of the¿existence¿of an agreement to arbitrate, it is generally sufficient for that party to present a copy of the contract to the court. (See¿Condee v. Longwood Management Corp.¿(2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 218; see also Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330 [“A petition to compel arbitration or to stay proceedings pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 1281.2 and 1281.4 must state, in addition to other required allegations, the provisions of the written agreement and the paragraph that provides for arbitration. The provisions must be stated verbatim, or a copy must be physically or electronically attached to the petition and incorporated by reference”].) Once such a document is presented to the court, the burden shifts to the party opposing the motion to compel, who may present any challenges to the enforcement of the agreement and evidence in support of those challenges. [Citation.]” (Baker v. Italian Maple Holdings, LLC¿(2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 1152, 1160.)

 

Normand Defendants move to compel arbitration as to the sixth and fifth causes of action brought by Vantage only, based on an arbitration agreement between the parties. Defendants essentially seek to sever the fifth and sixth causes of action from the remainder of the case. Defendants argue that a Vantage Employee Handbook (“Handbook”) includes an arbitration provision stating the following:

 

“Any controversy, dispute or claim between any employee and the Company, or its officers, agents, or other employees, shall be settled by binding arbitration, at the request of either party. The arbitrability of any controversy, dispute or claim under this policy shall be determined by application of the substantive provisions of the Federal Arbitration Act Title 9. Arbitration shall be the exclusive method for resolving any dispute; provided, however, that either party may request provisional relief from a court of competent jurisdiction.”

 

(Normand & Wright Decls., ¶ 4, Exh. A, p. 13.)

 

Normand Defendants argue that the Federal Arbitration Act applies because Vantages’ business operations and the Handbook evidence a transaction involving interstate commerce. Defendants argue that a valid arbitration agreement exists because Defendants signed the Handbook as employees. (Normand & Wright Decls., ¶ 4, Exh. A, p. 47.) Defendants note that the arbitration provision covers tort claims that arise between the parties—i.e., the fifth and sixth causes of action.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the Handbook is not a binding contract. Specifically, the Handbook states that “. . . this handbook is not intended to create any express or implied contract rights between the Company and the employee. . .” (Normand & Wright Decls., ¶ 4, Exh. A, p. 49.) Further, Plaintiffs assert that the fifth and sixth causes of action are not covered by the scope of the arbitration provision. Specifically, the causes of action (conversion and breach of fiduciary duty) “do not arise from the employment relationship, but instead arise from the Normand Defendants’ conduct as corporate officers exercising control over corporate assets and decision making.” (Arb. Opp., 3:20-22.)

 

Additionally, Plaintiffs argue that the Court can independently deny the Motion to Compel Arbitration based on Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2, subdivision (c). That provision provides that a court shall refuse to enforce an arbitration agreement where: (1) a party to the arbitration agreement is also involved in a pending court action with third parties; (2) the claims arise out of the same transaction or series of related transactions; and (3) there is a possibility of conflicting rulings on common issues of law or fact. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281, subd. (c).)

 

In reply, Normand Defendants argue that, since Vantage drafted the Handbook, any inconsistency in the Handbook should be construed against Vantage. The Handbook was intended to create binding conditions of employment. Further, the Handbook covers the scope of the fifth and sixth causes of action because the Normand Defendants are simultaneously officers and employees of Vantage, and the Handbook covers all disputes arising from the employment relationship.

 

Lastly, there are no potential conflicting rulings of law or fact such that the Motion to Compel Arbitration should be denied pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2, subdivision (c).

 

The Court finds that a valid arbitration agreement does not exist between the parties.

 

Firstly, upon inspection of the Handbook, Normand Defendants signed under section 16, entitled “Employee Handbook Approval.” (Normand & Wright Decls., ¶ 4, Exh. A, p. 47.) Section 17 and 19 entitled “Employee Acknowledgements” and “Handbook Acknowledgement of Receipt and Understanding” are not initialed or signed by Normand Defendants. The Court is not persuaded that Normand Defendants signed the Handbook as employees—rather, they only signed the Handbook for purposes of approval in their capacity as CCO and CFO, as indicated on page 47.

 

Further, the Court agrees with Plaintiffs that the Handbook does not cover Vantage’s claims in the fifth and sixth causes of action. The Handbook’s arbitration provision covers “Any controversy, dispute or claim between any employee and the Company, or its officers […].” (Normand & Wright Decls., ¶ 4, Exh. A, p. 13, emphasis added.) Further, the arbitration provision states that the “claims which are to be arbitrated under this policy include, but are not limited to, claims for wages and other compensation, claims for breach of contract (express or implied), claims for violation of public policy, wrongful termination, tort claims, claims for unlawful discrimination and/or harassment […].” (Ibid.) The Court reads such language to mean that the arbitration provision covers disputes between an employee and Vantage or its officers. Here, the fifth and sixth causes of action for conversion and breach of fiduciary duty arise out of Normand Defendants’ positions as corporate officers of Vantage, not as mere employees. Because the Handbook specifically covers disputes between employees and “Vantage or its officers,” the Court finds that it was not intended to cover disputes between corporate officers and Vantage, as is the case here.

 

Thus, the Court finds that there is no valid arbitration agreement that covers Plaintiff’s fifth and sixth causes of action.

 

Accordingly, Normand Defendants’ Motion to Compel Arbitration and Sever the Action is DENIED.

 


Case Number: 25NWCV00257    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: L

Defendant Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc’s Motion to Compel Plaintiff Diego Conzalez’s Deposition is GRANTED.

 

Defendant is awarded $250.00 in sanctions, payable within 60 days.

 

Defendant to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is a lemon law action regarding a 2024 Toyota Tundra. On January 22, 2025, Plaintiff Diego Conzalez (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendant Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc. (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 10. The Complaint alleges the following causes of action: 1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act - Breach of Express Warranty, 2) Violation of Song-Beverly Act - Breach of Implied Warranty, And 3) Violation of Song Beverly Act Section 1793.2.

 

On September 2, 2025, Defendant noticed Plaintiff’s deposition for October 2, 2025. (Jackson Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.) To date, Plaintiff’s deposition has not been taken. (Jackson Decl., ¶ 9.)

 

Defendant now moves to compel the deposition of Plaintiff. Defendant also requests sanctions in the amount of $750.00.


Legal Standard

 

“If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action. . .without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice, the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.450, subd. (a).)¿ 

 

Meet and Confer

 

“The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).)

 

On September 25, 2025, Plaintiff served objections to Defendant’s deposition notice. (Jackson Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. B.) On October 7, 2025, Defendant sent Plaintiff a meet and confer letter offering dates for the deposition of Defendant’s Person Most Knowledgeable and requesting Plaintiff’s availability for deposition. (Jackson Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. C.) Plaintiff’s counsel indicated that Plaintiff would provide dates for the deposition. (Jackson Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. C.) On five other occasions throughout October, Defendant attempted to schedule a date for Plaintiff’s deposition. (Jackson Decl., ¶¶ 5-8, Exh. C.) To date, Plaintiff has not provided a date for his deposition, and Plaintiff’s deposition has not been taken. (Jackson Decl., ¶ 9.)

 

The Court finds that the meet and confer requirement has been met.

 

Discussion

 

Defendant argues that it has requested Plaintiff’s availability for his deposition at least six times since October 2025, to no avail. Defendant asserts that Plaintiff’s deposition is necessary to prepare for trial, currently set for September 26, 2026.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff’s counsel represents that he intends to meet and confer further to schedule Plaintiff’s deposition. (Sanders Decl., ¶¶ 4, 11.) Plaintiff also argues that Defendant has failed to file a separate statement pursuant to California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1345(a).

 

In reply, Defendant asserts that, as of June 17, 2026, when the reply was filed, Plaintiff has made no further meet and confer efforts. (Critchlow Decl., ¶ 2.) Defendant notes that a separate statement is not required for motions to compel appearance at a deposition.

 

The Court initially finds that separate statements are not required for motions to compel initial appearances at a noticed deposition. Per California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1345(a), separate statements are only required for motions to compel answers or the production of documents at a deposition. (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1345(a)(4)-(5).) For such motions, a separate statement is used to narrow issues regarding particular issues—i.e., objections to particular questions or documents at a deposition. The instant Motion only seeks to compel Plaintiff’s presence at a noticed deposition.

 

The Court acknowledges Plaintiff’s counsel’s willingness to meet and confer further regarding a deposition date. However, without a scheduled deposition date, the Motion is not moot. To date, Plaintiff’s deposition has not been taken and no date has been scheduled.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s Motion to Compel Plaintiff’s Deposition is granted. The parties shall meet and confer to determine the date, time, and location of Plaintiff’s deposition. The deposition shall take place no later than 30 days from the date of the Court’s issuance of this Order. The date may be extended by agreement of the parties.

 

Sanctions

 

“If a motion under subdivision (a) is granted, the court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) in favor of the party who noticed the deposition and against the deponent or the party with whom the deponent is affiliated, unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.450, subd. (g)(1).)

 

Defendant requests $750.00 in sanctions against Plaintiff.

 

Plaintiff argues that the sanctions amount is unreasonable and requests that the Court reduce the amount of sanctions. (Sanders Decl., ¶¶ 6-7.)

 

The Court awards reduced sanctions in the amount of $250.00, payable within 60 days.


Case Number: 25NWCV01127    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: L

Defendants Kaiser Foundation Hospitals and Southern California Permanente Medical Group’s Demurrer to the First Amended Complaint is OVERRULED as to the first, third, fourth, and fifth causes of action and is SUSTAINED with 30 days leave to amend as to the second cause of action.

 

Defendants’ Motion to Strike Punitive Damages is GRANTED.

 

Defendants to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is a sexual assault action. On March 24, 2025, Plaintiff Jane Doe (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants Kaiser Permanente International, James P. Zalez, M.D. and Does 1 to 50. On June 27, 2025, Plaintiff amended the Complaint to identify Doe 1 as Southern California Permanente Medical Group and Doe 2 as Kaiser Foundation Hospitals.

 

On December 4, 2025, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (FAC) against Kaiser Foundation International, James P. Zalez, M.D., Nadia Pungul, R.N., (collectively, “Defendants”) and Does 1 to 60. The FAC alleges as follows: On June 26, 2024, Plaintiff visited Defendants’ facility located at 9333 Imperial Hwy., Downey, CA 90242 for a medical inspection of a lump on her labia. Defendants “failed to use reasonable care in their examination of Plaintiff’s vaginal area by failing to inform Plaintiff of their intent to manually inspect and digitally penetrate her vagina,” which was done “without her consent and without warning.” (Complaint, ¶¶ 12, 19.) Defendants also “failed to provide any privacy to allow Plaintiff to disrobe and did not provide Plaintiff with a medical gown.” (Complaint, ¶ 13.) The FAC alleging the following causes of action: 1) Negligence, 2) Negligent Hiring, Retention, Training and Supervision, 3) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress, 4) Sexual Battery (Civil Code 1708.5.5), and 5) Sexual Harassment (Civil Code 51.9).

 

On February 19, 2026, the Court granted Defendant James P. Zalez, M.D.’s Motion to Strike Punitive damages. (2/19/26 Minute Order.)

 

On January 6, 2026, Defendants Kaiser Foundation Hospitals and Southern California Permanente Medical Group, erroneously sued as “Kaiser Foundation International,” (collectively, “Defendants”) filed the instant Demurrer and Motion to Strike the FAC.

 

On May 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed an untimely opposition. On May 29, 2026, Defendants filed a reply.

 

On June 2, 2026, this matter was continued to today’s date. The Court ordered that 1) the parties must meet and confer, and 2) Plaintiff must seek authorization from the Court to proceed under a pseudonym. (6/2/26 Minute Order.)

 

The Court now considers the Demurrer and Motion to Strike, untimely opposition, and reply in its discretion.


Legal Standard

 

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (“Donabedian”) (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.) 

 

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.) 

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a) and section 435.5 require that the parties meet and confer “in person,¿by telephone, or by video conference.” 

 

On December 19, 2025, Defense counsel emailed Plaintiff’s counsel regarding its intent to file a demurrer. (Reid Decl, ¶ 9, Exh. A.) Defense counsel did not receive a response to the meet and confer letter. (Reid Decl., ¶ 10.)

 

The Court finds the meet and confer requirement has not been met, as there is no indication the parties met and conferred “in person, by telephone, or by video conference.” Nevertheless, the Court will address the merits of the Demurrer and Motion to Strike in interests of judicial efficiency.

 

Discussion

 

Defendants demur to all causes of action in the FAC.

 

          Liability as to First, Third, Fourth, and Fifth Causes of Action

 

Defendants demur to the first, third, fourth, and fifth causes of action on grounds that Defendants are not liable for the acts of Defendant James P. Zalez, M.D. based on his employment (hereinafter “Dr. Zalez”). Specifically, Defendants argue that 1) Dr. Zalez was not acting in within the course of his employment with Defendants when he allegedly sexually assaulted Plaintiff, and 2) the allegations are insufficient to show that Defendants ratified Dr. Zalez’s conduct.

 

i.     Scope of Employment

 

Defendants argue that, even accepting the allegation that Dr. Zalez was an employee of Defendants, the FAC fails to plead that Dr. Zalez was acting within the course of his employment with Defendants when he allegedly sexually assaulted Plaintiff. Defendants primarily cite to Lisa M v. Henry Mayo Newhall Memorial Hospital (“Lisa M.”) (1995) 12 Cal. 4th 291, 296 in arguing that sexual misconduct falls outside of the course and scope of Dr. Zalez’s employment as a matter of law, such that his conduct cannot be imputed to Defendants. Defendants argue that, similar to Lisa M., “[Dr. Zalez] simply took advantage of solitude with a patient to commit an assault for reasons unrelated to his work.” (Dem., 15:4-5.) Defendants argue that they should not be held vicariously liable for the actions of Dr. Zalez merely because the conditions of the examination made the alleged assault possible.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the Dr. Zalez’s alleged sexual misconduct was a foreseeable outgrowth of his employment duties. The FAC pleads that Plaintiff sought treatment for a visual-only inspection of a lump on her labia. (FAC, ¶ 11.) Dr. Zalez failed to inform Plaintiff that he would manually inspect and digitally penetrate her vagina. (FAC, ¶ 12.) Dr. Zalez exceeded the scope of Plaintiff’s informed consent. (FAC, ¶ 14.) Plaintiff asserts that “[t]here is a causal nexus between Dr. Zalez’s job duties and the tort committed since Dr. Zalez’s job duties include some degree of physical and sexual contact.” (Opp., 11:1-2.) Plaintiff asserts that Lady M. is inapplicable, because there, an ultrasound technician sexually abused the plaintiff. (Lisa M., supra, 12 Cal.4th at p. 295.) In that case, the ultrasound examination did not require inspection of the patient’s private parts. Here, the examination necessarily required such inspection, and invited the possibility that there would be a non-consensual touching.

 

“The rule of respondeat superior is familiar and simply stated: an employer is vicariously liable for the torts of its employees committed within the scope of the employment.” (Lisa M., supra, 12 Cal.4th at p. 296, citation omitted.) “Equally well established. . .is the principle that an employee's willful, malicious and even criminal torts may fall within the scope of his or her employment for purposes of respondeat superior, even though the employer has not authorized the employee to commit crimes or intentional torts.” (Id. at pp. 296-297.) “While the employee . . . need not have intended to further the employer’s interests, the employer will not be held liable for an assault or other intentional tort that did not have a causal nexus to the employee’s work.” (Id. at p. 297.) “[T]he incident leading to injury must be an ‘outgrowth’ of the employment; the risk of tortious injury must be ‘inherent in the working environment’ or ‘typical of or broadly incidental to the enterprise [the employer] has undertaken.’” (Id. at p. 298, citations omitted.) “California courts have also asked whether the tort was, in a general way, foreseeable from the employee's duties. . .The employment, in other words, must be such as predictably to create the risk employees will commit intentional torts of the type for which liability is sought.” (Id. at p. 299.) The determination of whether an employee was acting within the scope of their employment is a question of fact to be decided by a jury. (Ibid.) However, when the facts are undisputed, it becomes a question of law. (Ibid.)

 

Here, the FAC alleges that “JAMES P. ZALEZ, M.D. was operating the subject medical facility with the express or implied authority and permission of [Defendants] and within the course and scope of their agency or employment with [Defendants].” (FAC, ¶ 3.) Further, the FAC alleges that each Defendant was acting within the scope of its authority and employment with co-Defendants.” (FAC, ¶¶ 9-10.) The first cause of action alleges that “[i]t was foreseeable to Defendants” that “Plaintiff would suffer from the type of harm described herein as a result of [Dr. Zalez’s] acts and omissions.” (FAC, ¶ 1.) The third, fourth, and fifth causes of action allege that Dr. Zalez’s conduct was carried out in “the course and scope” of their agency/employment relationship with Defendants. (FAC, ¶¶ 51, 58, 63.)

 

Accepting the allegations as true, the Court finds that it is inappropriate at this stage to determine as a matter of law that Dr. Zalez was not acting within the scope of his employment with Defendants. The Court finds that the FAC, read as whole, supports a reasonable inference that Dr. Zalez’s alleged sexual assault was a foreseeable outgrowth of his employment. The digital penetration of Plaintiff’s vagina was conducted as a part of the examination of Plaintiff’s condition, although done without the consent of Plaintiff. The Court is persuaded that there is a causal nexus between Dr. Zalez’s employment and the alleged sexual assault such that Defendants could be vicariously liable.

 

The Court finds that the circumstances of Lisa M. are slightly distinguishable. There, the Supreme Court held that, just because the defendant’s “employment as an ultrasound technician provided the opportunity for him to meet plaintiff and to be alone with her in circumstances making the assault possible,” did not necessarily mean that the hospital should be held vicariously liable. (Lisa M., supra, 12 Cal.4th at p. 299-306.) Here, the subject examination did not only provide the conditions for an assault to take place—i.e., that the patient was isolated and touching was involved—as in Lisa M. Rather, the subject examination necessarily required inspection of Plaintiff’s private areas and potential touching. The Court finds that, unlike in Lisa M., it was foreseeable to Defendants that the vaginal examination might lead to non-consensual touching of Plaintiff’s private parts.

 

Thus, the Demurrer to the first, third, fourth, and fifth causes of action are OVERRULED on these grounds.

 

ii.    Ratification

 

Defendants argue that the FAC fails to plead specific material facts that Defendants ratified Dr. Zalez’s conduct. Specifically, the FAC “alleges no material facts regarding any alleged prior complaints against Dr. Zalez, or how these Defendants supposedly had any knowledge of these prior complaints.” (Dem., 16:15-16.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the ratification is adequately alleged. The FAC alleges that Defendants knew or should have known of Dr. Zalez’s propensity to commit sexual misconduct. (FAC, ¶¶ 10, 27, 40-41, 43, 71.) Further, the FAC alleges that, despite having knowledge of Dr. Zalez’s sexual abuse, Defendants did nothing to investigate. (FAC, ¶ 71.) Such allegations are sufficient to show ratification.

 

“As an alternate theory to respondeat superior, an employer may be liable for an employee's act where the employer either authorized the tortious act or subsequently ratified an originally unauthorized tort. [Citations.] The failure to discharge an employee who has committed misconduct may be evidence of ratification. [Citations.] The theory of ratification is generally applied where an employer fails to investigate or respond to charges that an employee committed an intentional tort, such as assault or battery. [Citations.] Whether an employer has ratified an employee's conduct is generally a factual question. [Citation.]” (C.R. v. Tenet Healthcare Corp. (“C.R.”) (2009) 169 Cal.App.4th 1094, 1110, as modified on denial of reh'g (Feb. 3, 2009), citations omitted.)

 

Here, the FAC alleges that all Defendants ratified the acts of the co-Defendants. (FAC, ¶¶ 9-10.) The fifth cause of action for sexual harassment alleges that, “[d]espite [Defendants’] actual or constructive knowledge of the sexual abuse and harassment against the Plaintiff, [Defendants] did nothing to investigate, supervise, or monitor [Dr. Zalez] to ensure the safety and wellbeing of the patients in its care, custody, and control.” (FAC, ¶ 71.)

 

The Court agrees with Defendants that the FAC fails to allege specifically how Defendants were put on actual or constructive notice of Dr. Zalez’s alleged misconduct, such that Defendants should have investigated. For example, there are no allegations that Plaintiff made a complaint to Defendants. Thus, the Court finds that the FAC fails to allege ratification such that Defendants are vicariously liable.

 

However, the Court may not sustain the Demurrer to ratification allegations in the fifth cause of action and other causes of action where the Demurrer is overruled as to those causes of action based on another theory (respondeat superior). (Daniels v. Select Portfolio Servicing, Inc. (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1150, 1167 [“Ordinarily, a general demurrer may not be sustained. . .as to a portion of a cause of action.”], disapproved of on other grounds by Sheen v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 905.)

 

          Second Cause of Action – Negligent Hiring, Retention, Training, and

          Supervision

 

The elements of a cause of action for negligent hiring, retention, or supervision are: (1) the employer’s hiring, retaining, or supervising an employee; (2) the employee was incompetent or unfit; (3) the employer had reason to believe undue risk of harm would exist because of the employment; and (4) harm occurs. (Evan F. v. Hughson United Methodist Church (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 828, 836-837.) 

 

Defendants argue that the FAC alleges in the conclusory fashion that Defendants had knowledge of Dr. Zalez’s unfitness. The FAC makes no specific allegation supporting such knowledge.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the allegations are sufficient. The FAC alleges that Defendants negligently “interviewed, hired, entrusted, trained, educated, supervised, retained, evaluated, reviewed, and/or disciplined …[Dr. Zalez].” (FAC, ¶¶ 36-37.) Further, the FAC alleges that Dr. Zalez was “unfit and/or incompetent to perform the work for which they were hired to do by [Defendants], including, but not limited to, providing medical treatment and services to Plaintiff.” (FAC, ¶ 39.) Defendants knew or should have known of Dr. Zalez’s “unprofessional and unlawful propensities, including but not limited to their inability to recognize and understand patient complaints, provide privacy, obtain consent for medical treatment, recognize and prevent workplace sexual assault of patients” and “had reasons to believe” that Dr. Zalez was unfit for employment. (FAC, ¶¶ 40-43.) Dr. Zalez’s unfitness proximately caused Plaintiff’s injuries. (FAC, ¶¶ 44.)

 

The Court finds that the FAC fails to allege, with specific facts, that Defendants knew of Dr. Zalez’s unfitness and had reason to believe undue risk of harm would exist because of the employment, such that he was negligently hired, trained, retained, or supervised. The Court agrees with Defendants that the allegations are conclusory as to this element. Neither are there any specific allegations demonstrating how Defendants negligently trained Dr. Zalez. For example, there are no allegations that Defendants had a duty to provide certain training and failed to do so.

 

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the second cause of action is SUSTAINED with 30 days leave to amend.

 

          Third Cause of Action – Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress (IIED)

 

“The elements of a prima facie case for the tort of intentional infliction of emotional distress are: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct. Conduct to be outrageous must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.” (Wilson v. Hynek (2012) 207 Cal.App.4th 999, 1009, citation and ellipses omitted.) 

 

Defendants argue that the FAC fails to state a cause of action for IIED. Specifically, there are no allegations that Defendants intended to cause severe emotional distress and that they engaged in extreme and outrageous conduct.

 

For reasons stated above, the Court finds that the FAC adequately alleges that Defendants are vicariously liable for the IIED committed by Dr. Zalez. The Court has also previously overruled Dr. Zalez’s Demurrer to the IIED cause of action in the Complaint, finding that his conduct was extreme and outrageous. (11/4/25 Order.)

 

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the third cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

          Uncertainty

 

Defendants demur to the entire FAC on grounds that it is uncertain and ambiguous. Specifically, the FAC fails to allege Plaintiff’s true identity, and Plaintiff has not requested the Court’s approval to proceed using a pseudonym.

 

A special demurrer for uncertainty, CCP section 430.10(f), is disfavored and will only be sustained where the pleading is so bad that defendant cannot reasonably respond—i.e., cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him/her. (Khoury v. Maly’s of Calif., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Moreover, even if the pleading is somewhat vague, “ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Ibid.)

 

The Court finds that this issue is moot, as Plaintiff has then since filed a Motion to Proceed under a Pseudonym. The Court finds that the FAC is not so ambiguous that Defendants cannot understand what it being alleged against them. Discovery procedures and protective orders may be utilized to address any uncertainties.

 

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the entire FAC based on uncertainty grounds is OVERRULED.

 

Motion to Strike

 

Code of Civil Procedure, section 436 permits trial courts to strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in a pleading:

 

“The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper:

 (a)¿Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.

 (b)¿Strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.”

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 436.)

 

Defendants move to strike paragraphs 80 to 84 in the “Prayer for Punitive Damages” section and paragraph 4 in the “Prayer for Relief.” Defendants argue that the FAC fails to meet the requirements to impose punitive damages on Defendants, as employer corporations, under Civil Code section 3294, subdivision (b).

 

Plaintiff does not oppose Defendants’ Motion to Strike in light of the Court’s ruling granting Dr. Zalez’s Motion to Strike pursuant to Section 425.13, subdivision (a) of Code of Civil Procedure [requiring a court order prior to seeking punitive damages against a medical provider]. (2/19/26 Minute Order.) Plaintiff represents that she will gather evidence and will later amend the FAC to seek punitive damages, as evidence permits.

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ Motion to Strike Punitive Damages is GRANTED.


Case Number: 25NWCV02929    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: L

Attorney Keyisha R. Anderson, Esq.’s Motions to be Relieved as Counsel are CONTINUED to July 28, 2026 at 9:30 a.m. in Dept. L.

 

Counsel to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is a tenant habitability action. Attorney Keyisha R. Anderson, Esq., counsel for Plaintiffs, now moves to be relieved as counsel as to Plaintiffs Joan Marshall (individually and as Guardian ad Litem for Eric Marshall) and Alexis Clark.

 

The Motions are unopposed as of June 24, 2026.

 

Legal Standard

 

Good cause exists to grant a motion to be relieved as counsel based on any of the grounds under California Rules of Professional Conduct Rule 1.16(b). Rule 1.16(b) provides that an attorney may withdraw based on any of the following: (1) the client insists upon presenting a claim or defense that is not warranted under existing law and cannot be supported by good faith argument for an extension, modification, or reversal of existing law, (2) the client seeks to pursue a criminal or fraudulent course of conduct, (3) the client insists that the lawyer pursue a course of conduct that is criminal or fraudulent, (4) the client by other conduct renders it unreasonably difficult for the member to carry out the representation effectively, (5) the client breaches a material term of an agreement with, or obligation, to the lawyer relating to the representation, and the lawyer has given the client a reasonable warning after the breach that the lawyer will withdraw unless the client fulfills the agreement or performs the obligation, (6) the client knowingly and freely assents to termination of the representation, (7) the inability to work with co-counsel indicates that the best interests of the client likely will be served by withdrawal, (8) the lawyer’s mental or physical condition renders it difficult for the lawyer to carry out the representation effectively, (9) a continuation of the representation is likely to result in a violation of these rules or the State Bar Act, or (10) the lawyer believes in good faith, in a proceeding pending before a tribunal, that the tribunal will find the existence of other good cause for withdrawal.

 

California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1362 requires that the following be submitted in support of an attorney’s motion to be relieved as counsel pursuant Code of Civil Procedure section 284, subdivision (2): (1) a notice of motion and motion directed to the client (made on Notice of Motion and Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel—Civil (Judicial Council Form, MC-051)); (2) a declaration stating in general terms and without compromising the confidentiality of the attorney-client relationship why a motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 284, subdivision (2) is brought instead of filing a consent under Code of Civil Procedure section 284, subdivision (1) (made on Declaration in Support of Attorney’s Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel—Civil (Judicial Council Form, MC-052)); (3) a proof of service evidencing service of the notice of motion and motion, declaration, and proposed order on the client and on all other parties who have appeared in the case; and (4) a proposed order relieving counsel (prepared on Order Granting Attorney’s Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel—Civil (Judicial Council Form, MC-053)). (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subds. (a), (c), (d), (e).)

 

The court has discretion to allow an attorney to withdraw, provided that there is no prejudice to the client. (Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 904, 915.)

 

Discussion

 

Attorney Keyisha R. Anderson, Esq. (“Counsel”) filed three Notices of Motion and Motion to be Relieved as Counsel as to each Plaintiff. (See Form MC-051.) Counsel also filed proposed orders. (See Form MC-053.) Counsel’s declarations demonstrate good cause for withdrawal, stating the following: “Withdrawal is necessary because an unwaivable conflict has arisen among jointly represented plaintiff concerning implementation of accepted settlement agreements, materially limiting counsel’s ability to continue providing competent, diligent, and loyal representation to all clients consistent with California Rules of Professional Conduct 1.7 and 1.16. In addition, the attorney-client relationship has deteriorated to the point that continued representation has become unreasonably difficult. Disagreements concerning settlement implementation, including issues raised regarding costs and allocation, have resulted in adverse positions among jointly represented plaintiffs, after plaintiffs [sic] accepted settlement.” (Anderson Decls., ¶ 2.)

 

Counsel states that the client was served copies of the Motion papers by mail at the client’s last known address. (Anderson Decl., ¶ 3.) However, counsel does not indicate that the clients’ last known addresses were confirmed as current via telephone or other means.

 

Further, the Court finds that there are no Proofs of Service filed indicating that the clients and all other appearing parties have been served the Motion papers.  

 

Accordingly, the Motions to be Relieved as Counsel are CONTINUED to July 28, 2026 at 9:30 a.m. in Dept. L.

 

Counsel is ordered to file, prior to the continued hearing, 1) amended declarations indicating whether the client’s addresses have been confirmed as current, and 2) Proofs of Service indicating that all parties, including the client, have been served.

Plaintiffs Emeritus Corporation and Brea Whitter, LLC’s Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED.

Background

 

This is an unlawful detainer action. On November 14, 2025, Plaintiffs Emeritus Corporation (“Emeritus”) and Brea Whitter, LLC, doing business as Brookdale Central Whittier, (“Brookdale”) (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed this action against Defendant Phyllis Saunders (“Defendant”). The Complaint alleges that Brookdale is a resident care facility for the elderly (RCFE) located at 8101 Painter Avenue in Whittier, California 90602 (“Premises”). Brookdale is the owner of Premises, and Emeritus Corporation is the lessee and property manager of the Premises. (Compl., ¶ 4.) On April 24, 2024, the parties entered into a Residency Agreement, wherein Defendant agreed to pay $3,291.00 in monthly rent. On August 11, 2025, Plaintiffs served a 30-day notice to pay or quit on Defendant.

 

Plaintiffs now move for summary judgment, or in the alternative, for summary adjudication.

 

This matter was continued from April 30, 2026 to May 14, 2026. (4/30/26 Minute Order.) On May 13, 2025, Defendant filed an opposition. On May 14, 2026, the matter was continued to June 3, 2026. (5/14/26 Minute Order.)

 

On June 2, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Supplemental Declaration of Chanel Sanchez in Support of Motion for Summary Judgment.

 

That same day, Defendant filed a supplemental opposition.

 

On June 3, 2026, the matter was continued to June 11, 2206 to allow Plaintiffs to file a written reply. (6/3/26 Minute Order.)

 

On June 10, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a reply.

 

On June 11, 2026, the matter was continued to today’s date allow the Court to consider the reply filed the day before. (6/11/26 Minute Order.)

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

Plaintiffs’ evidentiary objections to Exhibits D and B attached to the Declaration of Defendant are overruled.


Legal Standard

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119, internal citations omitted.) “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67, citing FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 381-382.)

 

In moving for summary judgment, a plaintiff has met its “burden of showing that there is no defense to a cause of action if that party has proved each element of the cause of action entitling the party to judgment on the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).)

 

Once a moving party has satisfied the initial burden of proof, the burden “shifts to the opposing party to show, by responsive separate statement and admissible evidence, that triable issues of fact exist.” (Ostayan v. Serrano Reconveyance Co. (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1411, 1418, disapproved on other grounds by Black Sky Cap., LLC v. Cobb (2019) 7 Cal.5th 156, 165.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiff offers the following undisputed material facts:

 

Plaintiff Brea Whittier LLC owns the Subject Property, and Plaintiff Emeritus Corporation is the lessee and property manager that oversees Brookdale Central Whittier’s day-to-day operations. (UMF Nos. 4-5.) Both Plaintiffs are licensed by State of California, Department of Social Services as a Residential Care Facility for the Elderly (RCFE). (UMF No. 6.)

 

On April 24, 2024, Defendant entered into a Residency Agreement with Plaintiffs to reside at the Subject Property. (UMF No. 7.) Defendant initially agreed to a monthly rate in the amount of $3,291.00, which was comprised of a Basic Service Rate (“BSR”) of $3,205.00 and a Personal Service Rate (“PSR”) of $86.00. Defendant was provided with a monthly market rate discount in the amount of $405.00, bringing her total monthly rent (“TMR”) to $2,886.00. (UMF Nos. 10-11.) On January 1, 2025, Defendant's BSR was increased to $3,431.00, her PSR was increased to $90.00, and her monthly market rate discount remained unchanged. As of January 1, 2025, her TMR, including her monthly discount, remained at $3,116.00. (UMF Nos. 12-13.)

 

Defendant last made a payment in July of 2024, which consisted of a partial payment of $1,000.00. (UMF No. 14.) On August 11, 2025, Plaintiffs personally served Defendant with a 30-Day Notice to Pay or Quit (“Notice”). (UMF No. 15.) Plaintiffs note that Operations Specialist Mario Preston and the prior Executive Director have been attempting to work with Defendant regarding her failure to pay rent for several months prior to serving the Notice, but they were unsuccessful. (Preston Decl., ¶¶ 5-6.)

 

The total amount due in the 30-Day Notice was $36,472.00 (“Total Amount Due”), reflecting unpaid fees for basic services since September 2024. The Total Amount Due did not include late fees, optional or ancillary fees, fees beyond the rate for basic services, or fees that were incurred more than 12 months before the 30-Day Notice was served. (UMF Nos. 16-17.) The Notice explicitly stated that Defendant had to pay the Total Amount Due or surrender possession of the Subject Property by September 10, 2025.

 

Defendant did not pay the Total Amount Due or vacate the Premises before the September 10 termination date, so Plaintiffs filed the instant lawsuit on November 14, 2025. (UMF Nos. 19-20.) As of the filing of this Motion, Defendant continues to occupy the premises despite her failure to pay past-due rent. (UMF No. 22.)

 

Plaintiffs thus assert that 1) Plaintiffs are entitled to possession of the premises, and 2) Plaintiffs are entitled to money damages. Such issues are explained in more detail below.

 

Section 87224 of Title 22 of the California Code of Regulations allows RCFEs to evict a resident for non-payment of the rate for basic services within ten days of the due date, provided the RCFE issues thirty days’ written notice to the resident. (22 C.C.R., § 87224, subd. (a)(1).)

 

Health and Safety Code section 1569.683 and Title 22 of the California Code of Regulations section 87224 set forth the requirements for a notice of eviction of a resident. The notice of eviction must state 1) the reasons relied upon for the eviction, with specific facts to permit determination of the date, place, witnesses, and circumstances concerning those reasons, 2) effective date of the eviction, 3) resources available to assist in identifying alternative housing and care options, 4) information about the resident's right to file a complaint with the Department of Social Services. (Health & Safety Code, § 1569.683, subds. (a)(1)-(3); 22 C.C.R. § 87224, subds. (d)(1)(A)-(C).).) In addition, the notice must contain the following statement:

 

“In order to evict a resident who remains in the facility after the effective date of the eviction, the residential care facility for the elderly must file an unlawful detainer action in superior court and receive a written judgment signed by a judge. If the facility pursues the unlawful detainer action, you must be served with a summons and complaint. You have the right to contest the eviction in writing and through a hearing.”

 

(Health & Safety Code, § 1569.683, subd. (a)(4); 22 C.C.R. § 87224, subd. (d)(1)(D).)

 

Plaintiffs argue that they personally served a 30-day Notice to Pay or Quit on Defendant on August 11, 2025. (UMF No. 15.) The Notice sought for Defendant to pay unpaid fees for basic services, comprising of a base monthly fee plus fees based on the resident’s specific care needs, since September 2024. (UMF No. 17.) The Notice identifies the Subject Property, the amounts due, the effective date of eviction, housing resources, and information about Defendant’s right to file a complaint. (Ferguson Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. B.) The Notice also contains the required disclosure regarding Defendant’s right to contest the unlawful detainer action. (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiffs assert that Defendant failed to pay her past-due rent by the September 10, 2025 termination date and continues to unlawfully occupy the premises. (UMF Nos. 18-20, 22.) Thus, Plaintiffs are entitled to recover possession of the Subject Property.

 

Plaintiffs further assert that they have proven entitlement to past-due rent and holdover damages. Defendant’s unpaid rent at the time the Notice was served was $36,472.00. (UMF No. 16.) Further, Defendant failed to pay the prorated rent, $1,038.70, that became due for September 1, 2025 through September 10, 2025, during the period of time that the Notice was pending. (UMF Nos. 19, 24.)

 

As to holdover damages, the fair rental value of the Subject Property is $102.44 per day. (UMF No. 25.) Such value was calculated by multiplying the monthly rate for Defendant’s monthly rent, $3,116.00, by 12 months and dividing the total by 365 days. (Ibid.)

 

Thus, Plaintiffs’ damages are summarized as follows: “past-due rent of $36,472.00, an additional $1,038.70 in unpaid rent that accrued while the Notice was pending from September 1, 2025, through September 10, 2025, and an award of holdover damages from September 11, 2025, through the date of judgment, at a rate of $102.44 per day.” (Mot., 6:2-5.)

 

The Court finds that Plaintiffs have met their burden in establishing 1) elements required for possession of the Subject Property pursuant to Health and Safety Code section 1569.683 and Title 22 of the California Code of Regulations section 87224, and 2) entitlement to unpaid rent damages.

 

Defendant’s opposition and supplemental opposition make three primary arguments: 1) a triable issue of fact exists as to Plaintiffs’ standing, 2) a triable issue of fact exists as to the habitability of the unit, and 3) Plaintiffs cannot meet all elements of their unlawful detainer claim because the underlying notice is defective.

 

The Court will address each argument in turn.

 

          Standing

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 367, “every action must be prosecuted in the name of the real party in interest, except as otherwise provided by statute.”

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1172, "the plaintiff shall only be required to show, in addition to the forcible entry or forcible detainer complained of, that he was peaceably in the actual possession at the time of the forcible entry, or was entitled to the possession at the time of the forcible detainer.”

Defendant argues that a triable issue of fact exists as to whether Plaintiffs have standing to maintain this action. Defendant offers evidence that, on April 30, 2026, Brookdale sent a letter to Defendant stating, in relevant part, that “on or around June 1, 2026, Brookdale Central Whittier will transition to Oxford Health Group.” (Saunders Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. A.) On the same date, non-party Oxford Health Group, LLC (“Oxford”) sent a letter to Defendant stating that it was the new operational manager for the facility. (Saunders Decl., ¶¶ 8-9, Exh. B.) Defendant argues that Oxford is a distinct business entity from either of the Plaintiffs. (Saunders Decl., ¶¶ 11-12, Exh. C.) Because it is unclear whether Plaintiffs retain possession of the Subject Property, a triable issue of fact exists as to their standing.

 

On June 2, 2026, Plaintiffs submitted Supplemental Declaration of Chanel Sanchez in Support of Motion for Summary Judgment. Chanel Sanchez is the Executive Director of Brookdale. (Sanchez Supp. Decl., ¶ 1.) Sanchez confirms that the April 30, 2026 letter from Brookdale was sent to Defendant. (Sanchez Decl., ¶ 3.) However, Sanchez declares that the transition has not yet occurred, and Plaintiffs expect that it will occur in July 2026. (Sanchez Supp. Decl., ¶ 4.) Plaintiffs still own and operate the facility, and Plaintiffs remain licensed by the California Department of Social Services to operate Brookdale as an RCFE. (Sanchez Supp. Decl., ¶ 5.)

 

In Defendant’s supplemental opposition, Defendant argues that the submitted Supplemental Declaration of Chanel Sanchez is insufficient. Plaintiffs have not produced any documentation proving effective date of the change in ownership. Thus, a triable issue of fact exists as to current ownership.

 

In reply, Plaintiffs have submitted a second Supplemental Declaration of Chanel Sanchez in Support of Motion for Summary Judgment stating that the transition of ownership and operation has not occurred yet and that Plaintiffs remain licensed by the California Department of Social Services to operate Brookdale as an RCFE. (Second Sanchez Supp. Decl., ¶ 3, 7.)

 

Here, the Court finds that a triable issue of material fact exists as to whether Brookdale has standing to continue with the instant suit. While Code of Civil Procedure sections 1172 indicates that the moment for establishing standing is at the time of the alleged unlawful detainer, this unlawful detainer action is brought under Health and Safety Code Section 1569.683 and Section 87224 of Title 22 of the California Code of Regulations. Under these statutes, there is no similar language to section 1172 indicating when standing is established for purposes of bringing the unlawful detainer action against a defendant in a residential care facility. Given the basis of an unlawful detainer action is right to possession, Brookdale would no longer have the right to possess the Premises if they transferred their interest or ownership in the Premises.

 

Based on the letters submitted by Defendant, Plaintiffs may have sold or transferred the Premises to Oxford, assigned its ownership and/or management responsibilities to Oxford, and/or subcontracting its management of the property. (Saunders Decl., ¶¶ 8-9, Exh. B.) While Plaintiffs submit the Supplemental Declaration of Chanel Sanchez and the second Supplemental Declaration of Chanel Sanchez to state that the transition in ownership will not take place until July, there is no documentation supporting this assertion. (Sanchez Decl., ¶ 4.) At this time, the Court is not persuaded by the evidence that Plaintiffs have standing as a matter of law. The bottom line is that Plaintiffs’ possession of the Premises is currently disputed.

 

Because the parties offer contradicting evidence regarding standing, the Court finds that a triable issue of fact exists.

 

          Habitability

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1174.2, “when a tenant raises breach of the warranty of habitability as an affirmative defense in an unlawful detainer case, the trial court is required to determine whether a substantial breach has occurred” and if it did, the court must reduce the rent to reflect the breach, give the tenant the right to possession conditioned on the tenant paying the reduced rental rate, order the rent remain reduced until the repairs are made, and award costs and attorney fees to the tenant if permitted under the law and the contract between the parties. (Hyatt v. Tedesco (“Hyatt”) (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th Supp. 62, 67.) “‘Substantial breach’ is defined to mean the ‘failure of the landlord to comply with applicable building and housing code standards which materially affect health and safety.’” (Ibid.)

 

In opposition, Defendant argues that a triable issue of fact exists related to the habitability of Defendant’s unit. In support, Defendant submits photos of roaches in her unit and work orders she has placed. (Saunders Decl., ¶¶ 14-17, Exhs. D- E.) Defendant argues that a triable issue of fact exists as to whether the condition of unit consists of a substantial breach such that the value of the rental unit should be reduced.

 

In reply, Plaintiffs assert that Defendant’s evidence is inadmissible. Defendant did not adequately authenticate Exhibits D and E—Defendant did not date those documents on the documents themselves or in the supporting declaration. Plaintiffs have objected to Defendant’s evidence. Further, if these photographs were taken after November 2025, when the Complaint was filed, they are irrelevant as to whether Defendant’s unit was uninhabitable during her residency. Lastly, a single photograph of a single insect does not rise to the level of “substantial breach.” 

 

The Court is not persuaded that a triable issue of fact exists as to whether Plaintiffs committed a “substantial breach” of the warranty of habitability. In other words, the Court finds that Defendant’s evidence—photos of a single cockroach and a single work order stating that pest control would treat the unit—is insufficient to prove that Plaintiffs failed to comply with applicable building and housing code standards such that Defendant’s’ health and safety was affected. (See Hyatt, supra, 96 Cal.App.4th Supp. at p. 67.)

 

Thus, Defendant has not demonstrated a triable issue of fact as to the defense of habitability.

 

          Validity of Notice

 

In the supplemental opposition, Defendant argues that Plaintiffs cannot meet their burden of proof because the Plaintiff’s 3-day Notice to Pay Rent or Quit (hereinafter, “Notice”) is defective. Specifically, the Notice demands rent demands rent in the amounts of $2,886 for the months from September 2024 through December 2024, and $3,116 for the months from January 2025 through August 2025. (Compl., Exh. 2.) However, the Residency Agreement (Compl., Exh. 1) reveals that “rent” is actually a Basic Services Fee that is defined to include “Accommodations,” “Dining Services,” “Utility Services,” Housekeeping Services,” “Laundry and Linen Services,” “Activities Program,” “Transportation,” “Staffing 24 hours a day,” “Observation and Consultation,” “Assistance with Access to Outside Services,” and “Personal Services.” Such fees, aside from fees for “Accommodations,” do not constitute rent. Defendant thus argues that the Complaint improperly uses summary unlawful detainer procedures to seek regular contract damages above rent damages.

 

Defendant cites cases analyzing Code of Civil Procedure section 1161, subdivision (2) regarding the elements for unlawful detainer actions based on nonpayment of rent. Defendant argues that “even a minor overstatement of rent owed [in a 3-day notice to pay rent or quit] may be sufficient to render the notice defective.” (Supp. Opp., 6:5.)

 

In reply, Plaintiffs argue that Title 22 of the California Code of Regulations section 87464, subdivision (f) requires that RCFEs must provide “basic services,” that at a minimum, includes “care and supervision,” “safe and healthful living accommodations and services,” “three nutritionally well-balanced meals and snacks made available daily,” “personal assistance and care as needed by the resident … with activities of daily living,” “[a]rrangements to meet health needs, including arranging transportation,” and “[a] planned activities program which includes social and recreational activities appropriate to the interests and capabilities of the resident.” Title 22 of the California Code of Regulations section 87224, subdivision (a)(1) states that an RCFE may evict a resident based on nonpayment of such “basic services.” Here, Plaintiffs properly seek to evict Defendant based on nonpayment of basic services. Thus, Defendant’s distinction between rent and other services is irrelevant—those other categories are included in the definition of “basic services.” Plaintiffs are entitled to evict Defendant and to recover amount owed for nonpayment of basic services. Thus, the Notice is not defective.

 

As stated previously, the Court finds that the rules regarding notices of eviction to residents of RCFEs are governed by Health and Safety Code section 1569.683 and Title 22 of the California Code of Regulations section 87224, subdivision (d). Further, as noted by Plaintiffs, Title 22 of the California Code of Regulations section 87224, subdivision (a)(1) states that an RCFE may evict a resident based on “[n]onpayment of the rate for basic services within ten days of the due date.”

 

Thus, the requirements for 3-day notices to pay or quit, based on nonpayment of rent, outlined in Code of Civil Procedure section 1161, subdivision (2) and case authority citing such Code section, are inapplicable. The Notice served on Defendant is not defective under the applicable statutes.

 

Thus, Defendant has not demonstrated a triable issue of fact as to whether the Notice is defective.

 

Conclusion

 

As Defendant has established a triable issue of fact as to standing, Plaintiffs’ Motion for Summary Judgment is denied.
