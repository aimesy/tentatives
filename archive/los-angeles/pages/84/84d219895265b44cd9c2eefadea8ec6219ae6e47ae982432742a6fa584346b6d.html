Case Number: 20SMCV02019    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: I

The court was hoping that essentially the parties would have gone their separate ways by now, but that did not occur.  The court will inquire.  The court has little or no hope that there will be the clean break envisioned by the court even given that defendants’ unit is to be sold.  But the court is not convinced that an injunction is really all that helpful.  The court is disinclined to start issuing an injunction that would apply to communications with the HOA while defendants are still the beneficiaries of the trust that owns the unit, and the likely harm beyond that will be for statements made under a pseudonym.  As to the pseudonym, other than having plaintiff speak through Armstrong, there has been no convincing proof of it, and the statements by Armstrong related to the unit.  Given that plaintiff has failed to prove the pseudonym allegation other than Armstrong, the court is disinclined to issue a prior restraint on speech.  The court is also concerned that the proposed liquidated damages provision has more to do with the cost of proof and litigation than any harm that the comment might do, and really seems to be punitive.  Therefore, the request for forward-looking injunctive relief is DENIED.  Of course, that does not bar plaintiff from bringing a new defamation action if plaintiff believes that defendants have defamed him in the future.

 

The court will therefore turn to the proposed Statement of Decision.  The court has seen no objection thereto, but the time to object might not have run.  The court notes that it is leaving California on Tuesday (and the court lacks the power to issue orders when it is not physically within the state), so the Statement of Decision time is extended to October 31, 2026 or whenever it would otherwise run, whichever is later.

This is a motion to consolidate two related cases.  In both cases, plaintiff seeks an easement over property owned by various defendants, although the defendants do not overlap.  Plaintiff contends, as best the court understands it, that an easement is needed for practical access to plaintiff’s property.  At the last hearing, the court requested that the parties submit a map.  Defendant Silver submitted the map.  The court understands that the parties were unable to agree on a map but it appears that the filing was an attempt to provide both views in a non-argumentative fashion, which the court appreciates.  All defendants share the same position.  According to defendants, the “blue line” on the map is a route sought by plaintiffs in both complaints.  Defendants further state that in both complaints, the route is defined identically.  There is also an “orange line” that is the path defendants state is sought in the Ghertner case.  Defendants also attached plaintiff’s map.  It includes an “orange line” that represents the easement plaintiff seeks in the Ghertner case.  The difference is that the “blue line” shown on defendants’ map and the “orange line” on plaintiff’s map is that the route veers a bit for what defendants’ claim is a short period at the northern part of the Clemens property.  Plaintiffs’ map also has a “green line” but defendants contend that they do not know what it is because it follows the “blue line” but then stops without reaching any public road.  According to the defense, plaintiffs’ counsel states that the “green line” is from a 1970 easement grant that is not mentioned in the Stang action or mentioned in discovery to date.

 

The court has reviewed the maps.  At least so far as the court can tell, the roads are essentially the same for all practical purposes that would relate to the adjudication of the cases.  They would go south through plaintiffs’ property to the Clemens property and roughly south from there through the Silver-Nelson property, dipping a bit into the Johnson property, then back to the Silver-Nelson property, then through the McCoy property, and then to the “Stang/Ghertner” property to Greenleaf Canyon Road, which is public.

 

Because the roads look at least very similar, the court believes that defendants have the better of it and the matters should be consolidated.  The court is therefore inclined to GRANT the motion.  The court will discuss what, if any, effect this will have on the trial date.

Case Number: 24SMCV04259    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: I

The court will inquire.  According to Arcstone, it made a loan to Kashfi—the judgment debtor’s mother—secured by property owned by her and the judgment debtor.  According to Arcstone, almost all of the loan was used to pay off a prior mortgage on the property.  In other words, there was a prior senior debt (the original lender), and that lender was repaid using the proceeds of the loan in question.  According to Arcstone, there was little or no money from the Arcstone loan used for any other purpose.  While Kashfi signed the loan documents, it appears that the judgment debtor co-signed the DOT.  Plaintiff argues that it believes that Arcstone deliberately did not do any kind of credit check on the judgment debtor even though judgment debtor co-signed the DOT not because Kashfi was the borrower, but rather so that it would have plausible deniability of the judgment.  Plaintiff served discovery to that end but contends that Arcstone has not been forthcoming in its responses or production of documents.

 

Here is the court’s question.  It seems that Arcstone got reasonably equivalent value for the DOT.  It got a DOT to secure $1.1 million in return for paying out $1.1 million to the original lender.  If Arcstone gave reasonably equivalent value, is this subject to the UVTA at all?  Civil Code section 3439.05 states that a transfer incurred by a debtor is voidable as to a creditor whose claim pre-dates the transfer if the debtor made the transfer or incurred the obligation “without receiving a reasonably equivalent value in exchange for the transfer or obligation” and the debtor was insolvent.  How does this discovery go to whether or not there was reasonably equivalent value?  If there was reasonably equivalent value, does it matter whether or not Arcstone knew or should have known of the judgment?  The court is aware that reasonably equivalent value means reasonably equivalent value FROM THE DEBTOR.  But if the same property secured the original debt, then this would seem to be met as well.

 

If the discovery would show that Arcstone did not get reasonably equivalent value in return for the DOT, that would seem to be relevant.  If there is a path to defeat the motion even if Arcstone did get reasonably equivalent value and the discovery goes to that, then that question as well would seem to be relevant.  If that can be articulated, the court would be inclined to GRANT the motion to allow plaintiff to obtain the discovery it seeks.  But if not, then the court is not sure that it makes sense to deny the motion.


Case Number: 24SMCV06153    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: I

This is a request to hear the MSJ less than 30 days before trial or continue the trial.  The motion is GRANTED and the court will exercise its discretion to hear the motion on the date for which it is currently set, November 5, 2026.  That gives plaintiff full statutory notice.

 

This is one of a number of cases brought where the MSJ is filed at just about the last possible second.  The trial date here is November 30, 2026, meaning that the MSJ must be heard no later than October 30, 2026, if it is to be heard of right.  To get a hearing date of October 30, 2026, where service is electronic, the motion must be filed no later than Friday, August 7.  (Monday, August 10 would lead to a hearing date of November 3.)  The motion here was filed on August 6, 2026.  While cutting it down to the wire, the motion is timely.  The earliest day that the motion could be heard with full notice would be October 28, which is more than 30 days before the hearing.  (That includes the two court days of additional notice for electronic service.)  Because the motion was timely filed, the moving party has the right to have it heard, notwithstanding the court’s busy calendar.  The court notes that the current date is only 5 court days beyond the minimum, but that does not change the analysis.  The court warns parties not to cut it this close.  Had there been some technical problem with service, for example, the court would be under no obligation to hear the motion at all.  But safety is not the issue.  There was not a big margin for error, but there was no error.

 

Accordingly, the moving party has the right to have the motion heard.  The court will therefore exercise its discretion to hear the motion less than 30 days before trial.  The application is GRANTED to that extent.

 

 


Case Number: 25SMCV04930    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: I

This is the continuation of a prior hearing.  Fox Sports seeks to compel responses to interrogatories and requests for the production of documents.  Fox contends that it has nothing to do with the accident that is at the heart of the controversy, but that is neither here nor there for purposes of the motion.  Fox served discovery and got no response.  When defense counsel reached out to plaintiff’s counsel, there was still no response.  Plaintiff’s counsel now states that plaintiff is not mentally competent to verify the responses, and that is why verified responses have not been forthcoming.  The court asked the parties to meet and confer over this.  Defendant seeks a total of $720 in sanctions and plaintiff opposes that.

 

The problem is this.  Plaintiff has a darn good reason for not providing verified responses assuming that a Guardian ad Litem must be appointed because plaintiff cannot verify the responses.  But there is no excuse for plaintiff (or counsel) not letting defendant know about that problem until after the motion was filed or seeking the appointment of a GAL until after the motion was filed.  Plaintiff’s counsel’s election not to meet and confer or request more time to deal with this issue is what led to the motion being filed.  So while there is a justification for the lack of verification, there is no justification for plaintiff not informing defendant of that even after defendant attempted to meet and confer.

 

Therefore, the court agrees that the motion is MOOT on the merits but will impose sanctions of $720 on plaintiff for the election to force defendant to file the motion when plaintiff and counsel did (or should have) known of the problem and not being willing to meet and confer on the subject. Plaintiff’s counsel suggests that this is a hardship on plaintiff.  That may be, but there is still no justification for plaintiff’s counsel’s failure to inform Fox of the issue thereby forcing Fox to file the motion.

Case Number: BC664654    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: I

This case is presently set for trial early next year.  The case had previously been in trial, but the court had to declare a mistrial at plaintiff’s request.  At the hearing in which the mistrial was granted, the court noted that the effect of the mistrial was to re-open discovery, including expert discovery.  However, it was still necessary.

 

At issue is a motion by the defense to compel a second day of deposition for plaintiff’s expert, Dr. Ward.  Dr. Ward was initially deposed in July 2022.  At that time, she stated that she would give her best testimony on that date but noted that she had recently received additional documents.  Although she had reviewed them, she was not confident that a closer review would not cause her to change her opinions or cause her to have new opinions.  Defendant stated that if that were to occur, defendant would want to take an additional day of deposition and asked that if Dr. Ward did change the opinion, that defendant be notified.  Dr. Ward did add to her opinion, and plaintiff did give defendant timely notice of that.  Yet defendant did not seek to re-depose Dr. Ward until right before the trial.  The court denied defendant’s motion to take the deposition so near the trial date, finding that defendant had waived that right by not seeking to take the deposition after plaintiff had given timely notice of the development.  Defendant really had no reason for not moving sooner, and the court believed that taking the deposition on the eve of trial would be prejudicial to plaintiff.  However, as stated above, that trial ended in a mistrial at plaintiff’s request in October 2025.

 

Defendant states that in February 2026, defendant renewed its request for an additional day of Dr. Ward’s deposition.  Plaintiff has refused to allow it.  Plaintiff states that she understands that discovery re-opens upon the mistrial, but she states that defendant misses the point.  Even with renewed discovery, a witness may only be deposed once without leave of court, and that one-deposition-per-person rule continues to apply after a mistrial.  Therefore, plaintiff states, the fact that discovery re-opened does not give defendant the right to another day with Dr. Ward. 

 

The court is inclined to GRANT the motion.  Plaintiff is correct as to the one day rule, but it is not inflexible.  In fact, it was waived by the parties on the first day of deposition, and even if it were not, it is subject to exception upon good cause shown.  Here, Dr. Ward is going to give new and different opinions based on materials reviewed more closely after the first day of deposition.  That is cause for a second day.  The earlier request was not denied because a second day was improper; it was denied because defendant waited until the eve of trial to request it, and given the proximity to the trial, plaintiff had demonstrated prejudice.  That is no longer the case.  The court sees no prejudice in allowing the second day of testimony.  That is especially so given that defendant must bear the costs associated with the additional day.  That includes not only the regular deposition costs, but also the expert’s fee.

 

The court also notes that it is somewhat swayed by the fact that ever since attorney Shafer came into the case, defendant has been very accommodating to plaintiff.  Defendant has agreed to extend the 5 year rule, defendant has been forthcoming in discovery, and defendant has extended numerous professional courtesies.  Had defendant been playing hard ball with a self-represented litigant, the court might still have granted the motion, but on the other hand . . . .  Where defendant has acted in a civil and cooperative manner, the court is all the more inclined to agree that when the shoe is on the other foot, similar courtesies be extended.  And, finally, there is something about avoiding trial by ambush as a policy that courts favor.  While the court recognizes that there is no excuse for the delay between 2022 and the first trial, the interests of justice strongly favor allowing defendant to know what plaintiff’s expert will say.

 

The motion is GRANTED.  The parties will meet and confer to arrive at a mutually convenient date.  If the parties cannot just agree on a date, plaintiff will offer five dates between October 30, 2026, and November 30, 2026.  The dates should all be normal working dates, and the dates ought to be provided no later than September 30, 2026.  Defendant may pick any of those five dates and will do so within 5 court days of receiving plaintiff’s list.
