I.                BACKGROUND

 

This matter arises from a motor vehicle accident that occurred on April 26, 2023, at I-101 Northbound 30 feet south of Winnetka Avenue. (Complaint p. 4.) On June 26, 2023, Plaintiff Constance Wiggins (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants Ibrahima El Hadji Cisse, Amador Solorio, Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC, and Does 1 to 50, alleging causes of action for: (1) motor vehicle; and (2) general negligence.

 

On September 19, 2023, Plaintiff filed requests to dismiss Defendant Jaguar Land Rover North America LLC and Defendant Amador Solorio and those Defendants were dismissed.

 

On July 2, 2026, Defendants Jaguar Land Rover Woodland Hills fdba Terry York Motor Cars, Ltd. dba Land Rover Encino erroneously sued and served as Jaguar Land Rover North America LLC and Ibrahima El Hadji Cisse (“Defendants”) filed a motion to quash Plaintiff’s subpoenas for workers compensation records (the “Motion”).  On July 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On July 21, 2026, Defendants filed a reply.

 

II.              LEGAL STANDARD

 

The court can make an order quashing or modifying a subpoena as necessary to protect a person from “unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right¿of privacy of the person.”¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1,¿subd. (a).) 

 

“Article I, section 1 of the California Constitution recognizes a number of inalienable rights, including the right to privacy.”  (John B. v. Superior Court (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1177, 1198.). “[C]ourts must balance the right of civil litigants to discover relevant facts against the privacy interests of persons subject to discovery.” (Id. at p. 1199.) “[E]ven where the plaintiff can establish a compelling state interest in discovery . . . precision of compelled disclosure is required so that the right of privacy is not curtailed except to the extent necessitated by the legitimate governmental objective.” (Id. at pp. 1199-1200, quotation marks and brackets omitted.) The intrusion “may only be done on the basis of ‘practical necessity’ [citation] and ‘the compelled disclosure must be narrowly drawn to assure maximum protection of the constitutional interests at stake.” (Id. at p. 1200, brackets omitted.)

“The party asserting a privacy right must establish a legally protected privacy interest, an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and a threatened intrusion that is serious. [Citation.] The party seeking information may raise in response whatever legitimate and important countervailing interests disclosure serves, while the party seeking protection may identify feasible alternatives that serve the same interests or protective measures that would diminish the loss of privacy. A court must then balance these competing considerations.” (Williams v. Superior Court (“Williams”) (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552-553.)

The right to privacy extends to medical records. (John B. v. Superior Court, supra, 38 Cal.4th at p. 1198.)

(King Declaration Ex. A pdf pp. 22-23.)

 

Defendants argue the subpoenas should be quashed because they violate Defendant Cisse’s right to privacy and seek medical records unrelated to the claims at issue here. (Motion p. 10.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues Defendants’ Motion does not satisfy the Williams factors because “[w]hen defendant makes a workers compensation claim and places his injuries at issue, and when those claims are publicly available for the world to find, there is no objectively reasonable expectation of privacy or legally protected privacy interest.” (Opposition p. 6.)

 

Plaintiff cites no authority for this statement. The court is not persuaded Defendant Cisse placed his injuries at issue in this matter by making a workers compensation claim. Nor is the court persuaded Defendant Cisse has no objectively reasonable expectation of privacy or legally protected privacy interest simply because Defendant Cisse made a worker’s compensation claim. Plaintiff also indicates Defendant Cisse has no right to privacy because the workers compensation claim is “publicly available . . .” (Opposition p. 6.) The court is not persuaded. Plaintiff appears to be arguing that because Defendant Cisse made a workers compensation claim, then Defendant Cisse has no right to privacy for anything relating to that claim. The court is aware of no authority stating a person loses their constitutional right to privacy for all records relating to a claim, simply because the fact the person filed a claim is public knowledge. If that were the case, then no plaintiff would ever have a right to privacy pertaining to any issue relating to any complaint – simply because it is public knowledge the plaintiff filed a complaint.

 

Plaintiff also cites “Davis v. Superior Court” stating, in that matter “the Court of Appeal permitted discovery of medical records where the records were directly relevant to a condition placed at issue, recognizing that privacy interests may yield when the information is directly relevant and appropriately limited.” (Opposition p. 11.)

 

Plaintiff includes only the name of the case. (Opposition p. 11.) Assuming Plaintiff is referring to Davis v. Superior Court (“Davis”) (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1008, the court is not persuaded Davis supports Plaintiff’s position.

 

In Davis, the Court of Appeal discussed whether waiver applied. The Court noted:

 

In determining whether one has waived the right of privacy by bringing suit, our Supreme Court has noted that although there may be an implicit partial waiver, the scope of such waiver must be narrowly, rather than expansively construed, so that plaintiffs will not be unduly deterred from instituting lawsuits by fear of exposure of private activities.  [Citations.] An implicit waiver of a party's constitutional rights encompasses only discovery directly relevant to the plaintiff's claim and essential to the fair resolution of the lawsuit. [Citation.] There must be a compelling and opposing state interest justifying the discovery. [Citation.] Even when discovery of private information is found directly relevant to the issues of ongoing litigation, it will not be automatically allowed; there must then be a careful balancing of the compelling public need for discovery against the fundamental right of privacy. [Citation.] The scope of any disclosure must be narrowly circumscribed, drawn with narrow specificity, and must proceed by the least intrusive manner.

 

(Davis, supra, 7 Cal.App.4th 1014, original italics.) Here, Defendant Cisse is claiming a right to privacy. Defendant Cisse did not file this action. Thus, Davis’ discussion of waiver in the context of a party bringing a suit is not applicable. Moreover, the Court of Appeal in Davis held that the trial court’s denial of a motion to quash violated the petitioner’s constitutional right to privacy. (Id. at p. 1020.) Thus, Davis does not support Plaintiff’s position.

 

Plaintiff further argues:

 

Not only have Defendants provided no reason to believe Defendant Cisse has an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy regarding these records, they have given no reason to believe that Plaintiff’s subpoenas are a serious intrusion. Defendants pepper their motion with colorful language to gloss over this absence, calling the subpoenas a “particularly stark” “constitutional violation,” a “severe invasion of Cisse’s privacy,” and describe Plaintiff “rifl[ing] through his confidential medical records and privileged attorney-client communications” like some KGB operative searching for kompromat in the dead of night, but Plaintiff’s subpoenas are none of these things. Defs.’ Mot. To Quash, p. 6, II. 6-15.

 

(Opposition p. 7.)

 

As far as the court can tell, no one is asserting Plaintiff is acting like a “KGB operative searching for kompromat in the dead of night . . .” Defendants are merely asserting Defendant Cisse’s right to privacy in his medical records. A right to privacy in medical records is established in California law. (John B. v. Superior Court, supra, 38 Cal.4th at p. 1198.)

 

Plaintiff also argues the records “may constitute party admissions, prior inconsistent statements, or impeachment evidence.” (Opposition p. 6.) Plaintiff then argues that “[f]or these reasons, Defendant Cisse has not objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in these circumstances and Defendants have provided no affirmative argument to the contrary.” (Opposition p. 6.)

 

Plaintiff is confusing the issues. The issue here is not whether statements in the records may be admissible. The issue here is whether Defendant Cisse has a right to privacy preventing Plaintiff from obtaining the records.

 

Plaintiff also argues they have no interest in humiliating Defendant Cisse and there is no possibility of chilling Defendant Cisse’s relationship with his physician because Defendant Cisse was discharged and his physician “determined that ‘no work restrictions or future medical treatment were necessary.’” (Opposition p. 7.) These arguments are not persuasive. Plaintiff cannot state with certainty that Defendant Cisse will not be humiliated by disclosure of their medical records. Moreover, if a party could defeat  this issue by simply stating they have no intention of humiliating the person whose medical records they seek to obtain, then it would cease to exist. Likewise, the court interprets the concern regarding a chilling effect to go beyond this lawsuit. Thus, the fact Defendant Cisse may not experience a chilling effect with the particular doctor is irrelevant.

Plaintiff’s subpoenas explicitly seek to obtain Defendant Cisse’s medical records. (King Declaration Ex. A.) The court finds Defendant Cisse has a legally protected privacy interest, and has an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in these circumstances. Plaintiff argues “Defendant’s workers compensation injuries and entities are records that are publicly available and therefore there is no expectation of privacy when defendant makes a claim for injuries and seeks payment from a responsible party.” (Opposition p. 2.) The court is not persuaded. If the records are publicly available, as Plaintiff proposes, then Plaintiff has no need for these subpoenas. The existence of Defendant Cisse’s worker’s compensation claim may be publicly available, but the court is not persuaded Defendant Cisse’s medical records relating to that claim are publicly available. Thus, the court concludes the threatened intrusion here is serious because Plaintiff seeks Defendant Cisse’s medical records.

 

Plaintiff argues the subpoenas are narrowly tailored because they are limited to “[a]ny and all documents . . . arising from or relating to the April 10, 2023 accident, EAMS case number ADJ18333653.” (Opposition p. 9.)

In reply, Defendants argue the language “’arising from or relating to’ does not meaningfully narrow the requests” because it “invites the subpoenaed entities to produce every document in the workers’ compensation file that mentions the accident, without regard to whether the document is privileged, confidential, employment-related, or relevant to the claims and defenses in this litigation.” (Reply pp. 2-3.)

The court agrees with Defendants. The language “[a]ny and all documents . . . arising from or relating to” does not adequately limit the subpoenas.

Plaintiff also argues Defendant Cisse placed the circumstances of the incident and Defendant Cisse’s conduct directly at issue, so Plaintiff’s subpoenas are directly relevant. (Opposition p. 9.) Plaintiff further cites Harris v. Superior Court (“Harris”) (1992) 3 Cal.App.4th 661, 665 as standing for the proposition, “[a]s stated supra, to defeat Defendants’ privacy concerns, Plaintiff need only show their request is ‘directly relevant’ to a Defendant’s claim or defense.” (Opposition p. 12.)

Plaintiff’s statement they need only show direct relevance dilutes the issue and fails to account for the Supreme Court of California’s discussion in Williams, supra, 3 Cal.5th 531, 552-553. The test here is not whether the subpoenas seek relevant information. The court understands that Defendant Cisse’s workers’ compensation file relating to the accident at issue here very likely contains some relevant information. But even if the court viewed Defendant Cisse’s filing a workers’ compensation claim constitutes a waiver, “[a]n implicit waiver of a party’s constitutional rights encompasses only discovery directly relevant to the plaintiff’s claim and essential to the fair resolution of the lawsuit” and “[t]here must be a compelling and opposing state interest justifying the discovery.” (Davis v. Superior Court (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th at p. 1014, original italics.) “The party asserting a privacy right must establish a legally protected privacy interest, an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and a threatened intrusion that is serious. [Citation.] The party seeking information may raise in response whatever legitimate and important countervailing interests disclosure serves, while the party seeking protection may identify feasible alternatives that serve the same interests or protective measures that would diminish the loss of privacy. A court must then balance these competing considerations.” (Williams , supra, 3 Cal.5th at pp. 552-553.)

Plaintiff argues they should be able to obtain the records because “Defendants are not merely disputing abstract damages; they are affirmatively relying on the circumstances of the collision, the force of impact, the parties’ physical presentation, and Cisse’s version of the event to challenge causation and injury.” (Opposition p. 10.) Plaintiff argues the requested records “are directly tied to the same incident date, the same driver, and the same factual narrative Defendants intend to use in this case.” (Opposition p. 10.) Plaintiff argues the records are “relevant because they almost certainly contain [Defendant] Cisse’s own statements about how the collision occurred, whether he claimed any injury or symptoms from the event, what mechanism of injury he reported, whether he attributed any condition to the same collision, whether he made statements inconsistent with his discovery responses or deposition testimony, and whether third-party evaluators documented facts bearing on the collision, causation, force, impairment, disability, or work restrictions.” (Opposition p. 11.)  Plaintiff also argues inconsistencies make the records “relevant to credibility, impeachment, and the completeness and accuracy of Defendants’ discovery responses.” (Opposition p. 12.) Finally, Plaintiff argues they need only show direct relevance, and Defendants made the claim “directly relevant to this matter by characterizing the collision as ‘light contact,’ the damage as ‘negligible,’ and denying Defendant Cisse’s injuries and intent to file a workers’ compensation claim to bolster those other claims.” (Opposition pp. 12-13.)

On balance the court finds the court finds the competing interests set forth by the parties weigh against disclosure. The subpoenas seek “[a]ny and all medical and billing records relating to [Defendant Cisse’s] workers’ compensation claim . . “ and,

Any and all documents, records, electronically stored information, and tangible materials in your possession, custody, or control that relate to any injuries, conditions, symptoms, treatment, evaluation, diagnosis, prognosis, disability, impairment, work restrictions, or medical billing claimed by or on behalf of [Defendant Cisse] arising from or relating to the April 10, 2023 accident, EAMS case number ADJ18333653, including but not limited to medical records, billing records, diagnostic imaging and testing, worker's compensation records, prior or subsequent injury records, employment-related medical documents, and insurance documents or claims.

(See King Declaration Ex. A.) Plaintiff argues these records may contain documents relating to matters such as the extent of the collision’s contact and whether the damage was negligible. (Opposition p. 13.) It is a reasonable assumption these records could encompass, for example, Defendant Cisse’s historical medical records that have no bearing on this matter. Moreover, although Defendant Cisse may be contesting Plaintiff’s claims by arguing the contact was light and the damage was negligible, that does not mean Defendant Cisse has affirmatively put their own injuries at issue in this matter. If Defendant Cisse is contesting that Plaintiff sustained injuries, Defendant Cisse is merely placing the burden of proving injuries on Plaintiff – where the burden lay in the first place. Finally, even if the court adopted Plaintiff’s position and found information concerning whether Defendant Cisse was injured due to the accident and the extent of his injuries was discoverable, the court is not persuaded this information cannot be obtained by less intrusive means, such as additional written discovery requests or by deposing Defendant Cisse.

Plaintiff also requests the court “modify the subpoenas rather than quash them” because “[a] complete quash order would improperly prevent Plaintiff from obtaining relevant, non-privileged evidence.” (Opposition p. 15.)  Plaintiff proposes no modifications to the subpoenas that would address the court’s privacy concerns here. The court, therefore, denies Plaintiff’s request to modify the subpoenas.

Defendants request $3,819.00 in monetary sanctions against Plaintiff. (Motion p. 2.) This reflects 7.9 hours of attorneys’ fees to prepare the Motion, and 0.5 hours of anticipated fees to review the opposition, 1.5 hours to prepare the reply, and 1.5 hours to appear at the hearing, at $335.00 per hour. (King Declaration ¶ 11.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.2, subdivision (a) states:

Except as specified in subdivision (c), in making an order pursuant to motion made under subdivision (c) of Section 1987 or under Section 1987.1, the court may in its discretion award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonable attorney's fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive.

The court finds Plaintiff failed to oppose the Motion with substantial justification. The court further finds Defendants’ request for monetary sanctions is excessive. The court will grant Defendants’ request for monetary sanctions against Plaintiff in the reduced sum of $1,842.50, reflecting 3.0 hours of attorneys’ fees to prepare the Motion, 0.5 hours to review the opposition, 1.0 hours to prepare the reply, and 1.0 hour to appear at the hearing, at $335.00 per hour. (King Declaration ¶ 11.)

Plaintiff requests monetary sanctions against Defendants and Defendants’ counsel in the sum of $3,500.00. (Opposition p. 15.) Plaintiff has not successfully opposed the Motion. Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions is denied.

IV.            CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Motion is granted. The court also grants Defendants’ request for monetary sanctions in the reduced sum of $1,842.50, to be paid by Plaintiff to Defendants’ counsel within thirty (30) days of this order.

II.              LEGAL STANDARD

 

“An agreement to settle or compromise a claim made by a minor ‘is valid only after it has been approved, upon the filing of a petition, by the superior court . . .’ in the county where the minor resides or the action could have been brought.” (Pearson v. Superior Court (2012) 202 Cal.App.4th 1333, 1337.) 

 

“[T]he protective role the court generally assumes in cases involving minors, [is] a role to assure that whatever is done is in the minor’s best interests. . . .[I]ts primary concern is whether the compromise is sufficient to provide for the minor’s injuries, care and treatment.” (Goldberg v. Superior Court (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1378, 1382.) 

 

“A petition for court approval of a compromise or covenant not to sue under Code of Civil Procedure section 372 must comply with rules 7.950 or 7.950.5, 7.951, and 7.952.” (Cal. Rules of Court 3.1384(a).) The petition “must be verified by the petitioner and must contain a full disclosure of all information that has any bearing on the reasonableness of the compromise, covenant, settlement, or disposition.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950.) “The person petitioning for approval of the compromise of the claim on behalf of the minor or person with a disability and the minor or person with a disability must attend the hearing on the petition unless the court for good cause dispenses with their personal appearance.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.952(a).) 

 

“An order for the deposit of funds of a minor or a person with a disability, and a petition for the withdrawal of such funds, must comply with rules 7.953 and 7.954.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1384(b).)

 

California Rules of Court, rule 7.955(a) requires the court to use “a reasonable fee standard when approving and allowing the amount of attorney's fees payable from money or property paid or to be paid for the benefit of a minor or a person with a disability” and requires that the court “give consideration to the terms of any representation agreement made between the attorney and the representative of the minor  . . and must evaluate the agreement based on the facts and circumstances existing at the time the agreement was made, except where the attorney and the representative of the minor or person with a disability contemplated that the attorney's fee would be affected by later events.”   

 

California Rules of Court, rule 7.955(b) sets forth fourteen nonexclusive factors the court may consider in determining a reasonable attorney’s fee. Rule 7.955(b)’s factors include,

(1)  The fact that a minor or person with a disability is involved and the circumstances of that minor or person with a disability.

(2)  The amount of the fee in proportion to the value of the services performed.

(3)  The novelty and difficulty of the questions involved and the skill required to perform the legal services properly.

(4)  The amount involved and the results obtained.

(5)  The time limitations or constraints imposed by the representative of the minor or person with a disability or by the circumstances.

(6)  The nature and length of the professional relationship between the attorney and the representative of the minor or person with a disability.

(7)  The experience, reputation, and ability of the attorney or attorneys performing the legal services.

(8)  The time and labor required.

(9)  The informed consent of the representative of the minor or person with a disability to the fee.

(10)  The relative sophistication of the attorney and the representative of the minor or person with a disability.

(11)  The likelihood, if apparent to the representative of the minor or person with a disability when the representation agreement was made, that the attorney's acceptance of the particular employment would preclude other employment.

(12)  Whether the fee is fixed, hourly, or contingent.

(13)  If the fee is contingent:

(A)  The risk of loss borne by the attorney;

(B)  The amount of costs advanced by the attorney; and

(C)  The delay in payment of fees and reimbursement of costs paid by the attorney.

(14)  Statutory requirements for representation agreements applicable to particular cases or claims.

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.955(b).)

“A petition requesting court approval and allowance of an attorney's fee under (a) must include a declaration from the attorney that addresses the factors listed in (b) that are applicable to the matter before the court.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.955(c).)

 

III.            DISCUSSION

 

The Petition is properly filed on Form MC-350 and is verified by Petitioner. (Petition pdf p. 10.) (Petition ¶ 6 pdf p. 2.) Minor Claimant received treatment, including “Vestibular autorotation test & Electronystagmography, Vestibular Rehab Session, (EEG) Electroencephalogram.” (Petition ¶ 7 pdf p. 2.) Minor Claimant has recovered completely from his injuries and there are no permanent injuries. (Petition ¶ 8 pdf p. 2.)

 

Minor Claimant’s medical expenses before any reductions total $18,750.00. (Petition ¶ 12 pdf p. 4.) After reductions, the medical expenses to be paid or reimbursed from the proceeds totals $13,175.00. (Petition ¶ 12 pdf p. 4.)

For the following reasons, the Petition is deficient.

 

In Paragraph 17, Petitioner states they have not been represented or assisted by an attorney in preparing the Petition. (Petition ¶ 17 pdf p. 7.) The Petition is signed by counsel. (Petition ¶ 23 pdf p. 10.) The court presumes Petitioner was represented or assisted by an attorney in preparing the Petition. If this is correct, Petitioner must say so.

 

In its rejection of the Second Petition, the court stated:

 

The medical records are insufficient to show Minor Claimant has recovered completely from his injuries. The report dated September 9, 2024, indicates Minor Claimant has not recovered completely from his injuries. (Petition pdf pp. 84-86.) (05/29/26 Minute Order p. 5.)

 

Petitioner has not submitted and directed the court’s attention to a report showing Minor Claimant’s current condition. Minor Claimant’s counsel states in their declaration that “Daniel has not required injury-related treatment since September 9, 2024. While he continues to experience intermittent symptoms, their frequency and severity have lessened over time, and his condition has stabilized. The settlement was negotiated with his remaining symptoms and the prospect of future care in view, and the net proceeds will be deposited in a blocked account to remain available for his future needs.” (Petition Attachment 13a, Ghevonydan Declaration ¶ 10 pdf p. 107.) Although the court appreciates Minor Claimant’s counsel’s attempt to inform the court on this matter, Form MC-350 requires “[a]n original or a photocopy of any doctor's report containing a diagnosis of the claimant's injuries or a prognosis for the claimant's recovery, and a report of the claimant's current condition . . .” (Petition ¶ 8 pdf p. 2.) Paragraph 8 further states that “[a] new report is not necessary if a previous report accurately describes the claimant's current condition.” (Petition ¶ 8 pdf p. 2.) The doctor’s report dated September 9, 2024 indicates Minor Claimant had not, at that time, completely recovered from his injuries. (Petition Attachment 8 pdf pp. 84-85.) The Petition states Minor Claimant has completely recovered. (Petition ¶ 8a pdf p. 2.) Thus, the court requires a doctor’s report on Minor Claimant’s current condition. The court will not accept Minor Claimant’s counsel’s declaration as a substitute for a doctor’s report.

 

In its rejection of the Second Petition, the court stated:

 

Petitioner is identified as “Orozco Candace Danielle” in Paragraph 1 and on page 1 of the Petition. (Petition ¶ 1 pdf p. 1.) Petitioner is identified as “Candice Dolin” on the Settlement Authorization and Release Agreement. (Petition pdf p. 107.) In the complaint, Minor Claimant’s Guardian ad Litem is identified as “Candace Dolin[.]” (Complaint p. 1.) Petitioner’s name should be consistent throughout the Petition or Petitioner should submit an explanation as to why Petitioner is identified by different names in the Petition and complaint.

 

(05/29/26 Minute Order p. 6.)

 

Minor Claimant’s counsel submitted a declaration on June 8, 2026, discussing why Petitioner is identified by different names. This declaration should be submitted with the Petition.

 

IV.            CONCLUSION

 

For the foregoing reasons, the Petition is denied.

IV.           TIMELINESS

A motion for summary judgment “may be made at any time after 60 days have elapsed since the general appearance in the action or proceeding of each party against whom the motion is directed or at any earlier time after the general appearance that the court, with or without notice and upon good cause shown, may direct.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(1).) “The motion shall be heard no later than 30 days before the date of trial, unless the court for good cause orders otherwise. The filing of the motion shall not extend the time within which a party must otherwise file a responsive pleading.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(3).)¿ 

¿ 

Per Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivisions (a)(2), (b)(2) and (4):

¿ 

Notice of the motion and supporting papers shall be served on all other parties to the action at least¿81¿days before the time appointed for hearing. If the notice is served by mail, the required¿81-day¿period of notice shall be increased by 5 days if the place of address is within the State of California, 10 days if the place of address is outside the State of California but within the United States, and 20 days if the place of address is outside the United States. If the notice is served by facsimile transmission, express mail, or another method of delivery providing for overnight delivery, the required¿81-day¿period of notice shall be increased by two court days . . . An opposition to the motion shall be served and filed not less than¿20¿days preceding the noticed or continued date of hearing, unless the court for good cause orders otherwise . . . A reply to the opposition shall be served and filed by the moving party not less than¿11¿days preceding the noticed or continued date of hearing, unless the court for good cause orders otherwise.¿ 

 

Defendant served Plaintiffs with the Motion by electronic service on December 24, 2025. (Motion pdf p. 468.) The Motion was originally set for hearing May 13, 2026. December 24, 2025, was more than 81 days, plus two court days for electronic service, before May 13, 2026. Thus, the Motion was timely served. The reply was also timely filed. Plaintiffs filed the opposition on July 9, 2026, which was only 19 days before the hearing. Thus, the opposition was untimely filed.

 

Defendant did not raise timeliness in the reply. Defendant was also able to timely file a substantive reply. Thus, the court will consider the untimely opposition.

 

V.              LEGAL STANDARD

“A party may move for summary judgment in¿an¿action or proceeding if it is contended that the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding.” (Code Civ. Proc., §¿437c, subd. (a)(1).)¿¿ 

“Code of Civil Procedure section¿437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119, original italics)

“A defendant moving for summary judgment has the initial burden of presenting evidence that a cause of action lacks merit because the plaintiff cannot establish an element of the cause of action or there is a complete defense.” (Gordon v. Continental Casualty Company (“Gordon”) (2024) 107 Cal.App.5th 89, 99.) “When the defendant moves for summary judgment, in those circumstances in which the plaintiff would have the burden of proof by a preponderance of the evidence, the defendant must present evidence that would preclude a reasonable trier of fact from finding that it was more likely than not that the material fact was true [citation], or the defendant must establish that an element of the claim cannot be established, by presenting evidence that the plaintiff ‘does not possess and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence.’” (Padilla v. Rodas (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 742, 747.)¿ 

“If the defendant satisfies this initial burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to present evidence demonstrating there is a triable issue of material fact.” (Gordon, supra, 107 Cal.App.5th at pp. 99-100.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion “must produce substantial responsive evidence[.]” (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 162-163.)¿Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Gordon, supra, 107 Cal.App.5th at p. 99.)¿

VI.           DISCUSSION

Plaintiffs allege causes of action for breach of contract and breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing against Defendant. (SAC pp. 8-13.) Plaintiffs also seek punitive damages from Defendant. (SAC Prayer for Relief ¶ 4.)

Defendant moves for summary judgment, or in the alternative, for summary adjudication as to the first cause of action for breach of contract, second cause of action for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and Plaintiffs’ claim for punitive damages. (Motion p. 2.)

  1. Breach of Contract:

The elements of a breach of contract claim are: (1) existence of a contract; (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendant’s breach; and (4) resulting damage. (Wall Street Network, Ltd. v. N. Y. Times Co. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1171, 1178.) 

Marring Exclusion:

Defendant argues that coverage for the chipped bar tile is excluded by the Policy’s marring exclusion. (Motion p. 33.) Defendant cites the Policy as excluding damage caused by “[w]ear and tear, marring, [and] deterioration[.]” (Motion p. 34.) Defendant states there is no reported California appellate case applying the marring exclusion in similar circumstances but cites two Florida cases as supporting a proposition that the damage here was “clearly excluded by the marring exclusion” because it was “caused by bottles and other items dropped on tiles[.]” (Motion p. 34.)

Florida appellate authority is not binding on this court. Further, the two Florida cases cited by Defendant are distinguishable from this matter because they did not discuss whether the matters involved burglaries. In Ergas v. Universal Property and Cas. Ins. Co. (Fla. 2013) 114 So.2d 286, 286, a tile was chipped when one of the insured’s dropped a hammer on the floor. Similarly, in Gamero v. Foremost Insurance Company (Fla. 2017) 208 So.3d 1195, 1196, the tile was damaged when a vase fell.

Defendant submits evidence the damage at issue here arose in the context of a burglary at the Subject Property. (Perez Declaration ¶ 2.) Plaintiffs’ counsel “advised that a few bottles were dropped onto the tile flooring in the bar area causing the flooring to crack . . .” (Perez Declaration ¶ 6.) Although the impact causing the damage to the tiel may be similar, the circumstances surrounding the incident – here, a burglary – differ from the Florida cases cited above.

Other than the Florida cases, Defendant’s Motion lacks authority and reasoned analysis explaining why the broken tile were excluded from the policy. (Motion pp. 33-34.) Moreover, as Plaintiffs note, Defendant also inconsistently argues it paid for the replacement of the damaged marble tiles. (Motion p. 34; Opposition p. 18.)  Thus, the court concludes this argument is meritless.

Reasonably Uniform Appearance:

Defendant argues there was no withholding of policy benefits because its obligation was to restore Plaintiffs’ flooring so there “was a reasonably uniform appearance between the replaced tiles and the pre-existing marble tiles.” (Motion pp. 34-35.) Defendant argues there are no triable issues of fact here because it paid to replace the laminate flooring and “paid for the repair or replacement of the damaged marble tiles with like, kind and quality tiles.” (Motion p. 36.)

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue there are triable issues of fact here because “Defendant limited payment for the marble damage to (1) Fitzpatrick’s preliminary and uncertain cost of single-tile replacement ($1,518.16), Material Specialist’s epoxy repair ($1,180), and unspecified box of tiles for Plaintiffs to determine whether a match is possible ($665.47, with labor included).” (Opposition p. 19.) Plaintiffs argue that the insurer may not “substitute a fundamentally different material (epoxy polymer) for the damaged material (Crema Marfil marble) and call it a day. An epoxy fill is not marble. It does not have the same physical, chemical, or optical properties as marble . . . It does not age like marble. . . . It does not produce a ‘reasonably uniform appearance’ with the surrounding marble flooring over time.” (Opposition p. 20.)

Title 10 of the California Code of Regulations, section 2695.9, subdivision (a)(2) states:

When a residential or commercial property insurance policy provides for the adjustment and settlement of first party losses based on replacement cost, the following standards apply:

[¶] . . . [¶]

(2) When a loss requires replacement of items and the replaced items do not match in quality, color or size, the insurer shall replace all items in the damaged area so as to conform to a reasonably uniform appearance.

Defendant cites evidence it received a repair estimate in the sum of $1,180 to replace the damaged marble tile. (Defendant’s Separate Statement No. 42; Perez Declaration ¶ 31) Defendant also cites evidence it paid Plaintiffs $1,180.00 based on that estimate. (Defendant’s Separate Statement No. 43; Perez Declaration ¶ 33.) The estimate is attached as Defendant’s Exhibit S. (Perez Declaration ¶ 31; Defendant Ex. S pdf p. 460.) The description of that estimate is to “REPAIR FRACTURE DAMAGE ON ONE FLOORING TILE” and “CLEAN/ PREP AFFECTED AREA, EPOXY FILL, SMOOTH, TEXTURE, CURE, COLOR-MATCH AND SEAL TO MATCH EXISTING SHEEN.” (Defendant Ex. S pdf p. 460.)

The court notes there is no evidence specifically noted in Defendant’s Separate Statement showing the described epoxy fill would conform the marble tile to “a reasonably uniform appearance”, as required by 10 California Code of Regulations, section 2695.9, subdivision (a)(2).

However, even to the extent Defendant had produced such evidence, Plaintiffs’ evidence shows there are triable issues of material fact as to whether the referenced repair would conform the marble tile to “a reasonably uniform appearance.”

Plaintiffs submit a declaration by a licensed general contractor, Christopher A. Croisdale. (Croisdale Declaration ¶ 2.) Croisdale states:

Epoxy and natural marble are fundamentally different materials with different physical, chemical, and optical properties. Natural marble ages, develops patina, and responds to environmental conditions over time; epoxy does not. Epoxy repair materials do not weather, oxidize, absorb moisture, refract light, polish, or develop patina in the same manner as natural marble. While an epoxy repair may reduce the immediate visual impact of damage, there is no reasonable basis to conclude that the repaired area will continue to remain visually consistent with the surrounding marble as the surrounding natural stone continues to age. Accordingly, the proposed repair does not restore the damaged marble to its original composition, appearance, or long-term performance characteristics and does not return the flooring system to its pre-loss condition.

(Croisdale Declaration ¶ 11.) Croisdale also states that:

The mere availability of replacement stone labeled "Crema Marfil" does not establish that replacement tiles will reasonably match the existing installed flooring. Two pieces of Crema Marfil obtained from different quarry sections, or even different production areas within the same quarry, are likely to display perceptible differences in color, veining, movement and texture. Achieving a reasonably uniform appearance requires physical comparison of replacement material to the existing floor under identical lighting conditions, which was not done here.

(Croisdale Declaration ¶ 10.)

Accordingly, Plaintiffs produce evidence Defendant’s repair estimate using epoxy would not conform the tile to “reasonably uniform appearance” because epoxy is not equivalent to marble. Plaintiffs also produce evidence that it is unknown whether replacing tile would be sufficient because two pieces of the stone “are likely to display perceptible differences in color, veining, movement and texture” and no physical comparison was done here. (Croisdale Declaration ¶ 10.)

The court finds there are triable issues of material fact relating to the breach of contract cause of action.

  1. Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing:

Defendant argues there are no triable issues of material fact relating to the second cause of action because there is no evidence Defendant engaged in misconduct, Defendant’s investigation of Plaintiffs’ claim was timely and reasonable, and a genuine dispute existed over the scope of damage. (Motion pp. 39-49.) Defendant also argues bad faith liability does not attach to delays attributable to Plaintiffs. (Motion p. 49.)

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue there are triable issues of material fact because Defendant engaged in a biased investigation. (Opposition pp. 21-24.)

In reply, Defendant argues a genuine dispute existed relating the need to remove and replace the marble flooring. (Reply p. 8.) Defendant states that here, “there is no evidence that the opinion obtained by [Defendant] from David Fitzpatrick as to whether the marble tile needed to be removed, repaired or replaced was contrived or false” and “[a]s a matter of law, the genuine dispute doctrine applies therefore precluding bad faith liability attaching to [Defendant’s] claim decision.” (Reply p. 9.)

 “If an insurer unreasonably refuses a claim, it is liable for breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing inherent in every insurance policy.” (Mariscal v. Old Republic Life Ins. Co. (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 1617, 1623.) “An insurance company may not ignore evidence which supports coverage. If it does so, it acts unreasonably towards its insured and breaches the covenant of good faith and fair dealing.” (Id. at p. 1624.)

“In insurance policies, as in all contracts, the law implies a covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and an insurer's denial or delay in paying policy benefits can give rise to tort damages if the denial or delay was unreasonable.” (Fadeeff v. State Farm General Insurance Co. (“Fadeef”) (2020) 50 Cal.App.5th 94, 101.) “As a close corollary of that principle, it has been said that ‘an insurer denying or delaying the payment of policy benefits due to the existence of a genuine dispute with its insured as to the existence of coverage liability or the amount of the insured's coverage claim is not liable in bad faith even though it might be liable for breach of contract.’” (Id.)

 “The application of the genuine dispute doctrine ‘becomes a question of law where the evidence is undisputed and only one reasonable inference can be drawn from the evidence.’” (Paslay v. State Farm General Insurance Co. (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 639, 653.)

“[A]n insurer is entitled to summary judgment based on a genuine dispute over coverage or the value of the insured's claim only where the summary judgment record demonstrates the absence of triable issues [citation] as to whether the disputed position upon which the insurer denied the claim was reached reasonably and in good faith.” (Fadeef, supra, 50 Cal.App.5th 94, 102.) “An expert's testimony will not automatically insulate an insurer from a bad faith claim based on a biased investigation.” (Id. original italics.) There are “several circumstances where a biased investigation claim should go to jury: (1) the insurer was guilty of misrepresenting the nature of the investigatory proceedings ...; (2) the insurer's employee's lied during the depositions or to the insured; (3) the insurer dishonestly selected its experts; (4) the insurer's experts were unreasonable; and (5) the insurer failed to conduct a thorough investigation.” (Id., original italics.) “Put another way, the ‘dispute . . . must be genuine. An insurer cannot claim the benefit of the genuine dispute doctrine based on an investigation or evaluation of the insured’s claim that is not full, fair and thorough.” (Id., original italics.)

Defendant states in the Motion:

The uncontroverted evidence establishes that a genuine dispute existed whether damage to a single marble tile required replacement of the marble flooring which was continuous in several rooms of the plaintiffs' home. The uncontroverted evidence establishes that the three consulting flooring experts retained by CAIC - David Fitzpatrick, Tim Howard and Material Specialist - all concluded the damaged tile can be replaced so that there is a reasonably uniform appearance between the replacement tile and the existing marble floor tiling.

(Motion p. 48.)

In the Motion, Defendant cites no evidence or portion of Defendant’s separate statement to support this paragraph. The court also cannot locate reference in Defendant’s separate statement alluding to this evidence.

Defendant submits Perez’s declaration. Perez has been employed by Mercury Insurance Services, LLC as a property adjustor since 2022. (Perez Declaration ¶ 1.) Perez states the claims representative, Byron De Leon, advised Plaintiff Fereydoun Zaman he was going to handle the dwelling portion of the claim and wanted to schedule an inspection. (Perez Declaration ¶ 3.) De Leon followed up and the inspection was later scheduled for May 15, 2023. (Perez Declaration ¶¶ 3-5.) De Leon inspected the dwelling with Plaintiffs’ counsel and Chris Croisdale present. (Perez Declaration ¶ 6.) De Leon noted there were scratches on the wood flooring and cracked tiles in the bar area. (Perez Declaration ¶ 7.) De Leon advised Plaintiffs’ counsel a flooring expert would be retained to determine whether the flooring was repairable. (Perez Declaration ¶ 7.) De Leon retained David Fitzpatrick of Xactfloors to “assess whether the flooring needed to be replaced.” (Perez Declaration ¶ 7.) De Leon received a repair estimate from Plaintiffs’ counsel from the Croisdale Group totaling $110,142.57. (Perez Declaration ¶ 8.) This estimate included the cost of replacing the laminate and tile flooring. (Perez Declaration ¶ 8.) Fitzpatrick submitted a report to De Leon, noting the marble flooring was continuous through various rooms, the laminate flooring was in other rooms, and “noted that the affected areas were a single marble tile in the bar area, a single marble tile in the primary bedroom vanity and a few laminate planks in the family room, primary bedroom and bedroom number 2.” (Perez Declaration ¶ 11.) Fitzpatrick’s opinion was the damage to the marble flooring in the bar area and the chip in the tile in the master bedroom was due to wear and tear and the damage to the laminate flooring was consistent with furniture sliding, not a burglary. (Perez Declaration ¶ 12.) Fitzpatrick also “noted in his report that like, kind and quality Crema Markil marble was available for replacement of the affected marble tiles but like kind and quality laminate flooring had not been located for replacement of the damaged laminate flooring planks.” (Perez Declaration ¶ 13.) Fitzpatrick’s estimate to repair totaled $9,892.57. (Perez Declaration ¶ 14.) De Leon then prepared a dwelling repair estimate, which adopted the Xactfloors estimate. (Perez Declaration ¶ 16.) The file was transferred to Perez and Plaintiffs’ counsel advised Perez he disagreed with Fitzpatrick’s opinion the marble could be matched. (Perez Declaration ¶¶ 18-20.) Perez asked Tim Howard from Howard’s Flooring Service to conduct an inspection. (Perez Declaration ¶ 21.) Perez inspected the Subject Property with Howard “who agreed to investigate repair options for matching the sole damaged marble tile to the existing marble floor tile.” (Perez Declaration ¶ 23.) On September 12, 2023, Perez prepared a new repair estimate. (Perez Declaration ¶ 24.) Howard’s report “agreed that the laminate wood flooring would need to be replaced.” (Perez Declaration ¶ 27.) Howard also stated the damage to the marble was limited to one damaged tile in the bar area “which could be replaced based upon an opinion received from Michael Hebermehl of Material Specialist.” (Perez Declaration ¶ 27.) Perez contacted Material Specialist by email and Hebermehl said an inspection would be needed to verify repairability. (Perez Declaration ¶ 28.) Material Specialist then provided Perez with a repair estimate for the single damaged marble tile in the sum of $1,180.00. (Perez Declaration ¶ 31.) Perez “advised plaintiffs’ counsel that an additional payment in the amount of $1,180 had been issued and [Defendant] was now closing its file in this matter.” (Perez Declaration ¶ 33.)

Based on the evidence discussed above, Perez appears to have based the $1,180.00 payment for the marble on Material Specialist’s repair estimate. (Perez Declaration ¶¶ 31-33.) This comports with Defendant’s separate statement, which states “On November 29, 2023, CAIC issued payment to the plaintiffs in the amount of $1,180, based on Material Specialist’s estimate.” (Defendant’s Separate Statement No. 133.)

Plaintiffs submit Perez’s deposition testimony. At her deposition, Perez said she agreed with the opinion the marble could be repaired. (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf pp. 46-47.) Perez stated she based this opinion on the three reports by Material Specialist, Xactfloors, and Howard. (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf p. 46.) Perez stated that she took the expert’s reports at face value and denied talking to Howard about “the process of repairing cracks and damages to marble tile flooring[.]” (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf pp. 48-49.) Perez also denied speaking on the phone or in-person with Hebermehl. (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf p. 49.) Perez denied having every seen a “finished product of a repaired marble tile with epoxy fill[.]” (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf p. 50.) Perez agreed that it was her understanding the proposal “would restore the subject home to pre-loss condition” because “it would restore the materials to reasonable uniform appearance.” (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf p. 51.) Perez was asked “[b]ut you don’t know that; right?” (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf p. 51.) Perez responded “Not until it’s completed or attempted.” (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf p. 51.) Perez was asked if any attempts to repair the tile were made. (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf p. 51.) Perez responded “[t]hat, I don’t know.” (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf p. 51.) Finally, Perez was asked “[d]id Material Specialist provide any guarantee that the repair would create a reasonably uniform appearance.” (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf p. 52.) Perez responded “[n]ot to Mercury.” (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf p. 52.)

Defendant’s payment to Plaintiffs was based on Material Specialist’s estimate. (Defendant’s Separate Statement No. 133.) Pursuant to Title 10 of the California Code of Regulations, section 2695.9, subdivision (a)(2), Defendant’s obligation was to conform the marble tile to a “reasonably uniform appearance.” Defendant states in the Motion that all three of Defendant’s experts, Fitzpatrick, Howard, and Material Specialist “concluded the damaged tile can be replaced so that there is a reasonably uniform appearance between the replacement tile and the existing marble floor tiling.” (Motion p. 48.) But the evidence submitted does not support this statement. As for Material Specialist, Perez’s declaration indicates Perez “contacted Material Specialist by email inquiring as to the status of any update, inspection, and repairability of the marble tile in the bar area” and Hebermehl said an inspection would be needed to “verify the repairability of the floor.” (Perez Declaration ¶ 28.) Perez prepared a payment to Plaintiffs based on Material Specialist’s report. (Perez Declaration ¶ 33.) Perez’s deposition testimony indicates she took the report at face value, did not talk to Howard or Material Specialist about the repair process, did not speak to Hebermehl in person or on the phone, and understood the process “would restore the subject home to pre-loss condition” but did not know whether it “would restore the materials to reasonable uniform appearance” “until it’s completed or attempted.” (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf pp. 48-51.) It is unclear to the court why Defendant states in the Motion that all three of the experts concluded the tile could be replaced so there was a reasonably uniform appearance when there is a lack of evidence in the record supporting that fact. In contrast, based on Perez’s deposition testimony, Material Specialist made no such guarantee and Perez did not know whether the process would restore the materials to “reasonable uniform appearance” until it had been completed or attempted. (Plaintiffs Ex. 1 pdf pp. 51-52.)

Plaintiffs’ evidence shows there are triable issues of material fact relating to whether Defendant’s investigation was full, fair, and thorough.

  1. Punitive Damages:

Defendant argues there is no evidence of fraud, malice, or oppression that would support a request for punitive damages. (Motion p. 50.)

In opposition, Plaintiffs state they intend to dismiss their request for punitive damages. (Opposition p. 15.)  Accordingly, the court concludes there is no dispute here for the court to resolve.

VII.         CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Motion is denied.

I.                BACKGROUND

 

Starting January 1, 2021, Plaintiff Elevated Health, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) “provided vital COVID-19 diagnostic testing and screening services to individuals insured or covered by healthcare plans administered or underwritten by Defendants.” (Third Amended Complaint [“TAC”] ¶ 12.) Plaintiff provided these services “as an independent, out-of-network, non-participating provider.” (TAC ¶ 13.) Defendants are financially responsible for paying healthcare providers and suppliers for COVID-19 testing and diagnostic services performed for their insureds and members. (TAC ¶ 13.) “Plaintiff maintained and published a uniform baseline cash price of $125.00 for its COVID-19 diagnostic testing and screening services on its publicly accessible website.” (TAC ¶ 14.) Plaintiff submitted claims to Defendants for the services rendered. (TAC ¶ 15.) Defendants did not instruct Plaintiff to cease providing services or issue notices claims would be rejected. (TAC ¶ 16.)

 

Between January 1, 2021, and February 6, 2024, Plaintiff provided over 100,000 COVID-19 diagnostic and testing services to Defendants’ insureds. (TAC ¶ 17.) Defendants failed to pay Plaintiff for these services. (TAC ¶ 17.) Between January 8, 2021, and August 22, 2023, Plaintiff provided another 14,856 COVID-10 diagnostic and testing services to Defendants’ insureds. (TAC ¶ 18.) Defendants made partial, nominal, and incomplete payments for these services. (TAC ¶ 18.) Between January 9, 2021, and November 23, 2021, Plaintiff provided an additional 7,706 COVID-19 diagnostic screening services to Defendants’ insureds. (TAC ¶ 19.) Defendants refused to pay Plaintiff for these services, bypassed the out-of-network provider, and “claims to have reimbursed their own insureds an approximate aggregate sum of $575,000.00 . . .” (TAC ¶ 19.)

II.             MEET AND CONFER

¿ 

California Code of Civil Procedure section¿430.41, subdivision (a) requires parties to “meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference” before the filing of a demurrer to attempt to resolve the objections raised in the demurrer.¿The demurring party is required to file and serve with the demurrer a declaration either confirming that parties were unable to resolve the issues raised by the demurrer despite having met and conferred or that the opposing party failed to meet and confer with the demurring party in good faith. (Code Civ. Proc., §¿430.41, subd. (a)(3).)

Defendant’s counsel submits a declaration stating they sent Plaintiff’s counsel a meet and confer email. (Ellis Declaration ¶¶ 2-4.) Defendant’s counsel states Plaintiff’s counsel did not respond to the email or agree to dismiss the complaint. (Ellis Declaration ¶ 5.) Defendant’s counsel further states “[t]he parties did not reach an agreement to resolve the pleadings dispute” so Defendant filed the demurrer. (Ellis Declaration ¶ 5.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a) requires meeting and conferring in person, by telephone, or by video conference. As the court already informed Defendant’s counsel in its April 27, 2026, Minute Order, that a meet and confer email is insufficient. (04/27/26 Minute Order pp. 3-4.)  Further, in the April 27, 2026, Minute Order, the court stated it would consider the merits, but “[i]n the future, counsels must satisfy their meet and confer obligations, or the court will consider continuing the hearing until the parties have an opportunity to properly meet and confer.” (04/27/26 Minute Order p. 4.)

 

“A determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(4).) However, “trial courts are not required to ignore defects in the meet and confer process. If, upon review of a declaration under section 430.41, subdivision (a)(3), a court learns no meet and confer has taken place, or concludes further conferences between counsel would likely be productive, it retains discretion to order counsel to meaningfully discuss the pleadings with an eye toward reducing the number of issues or eliminating the need for a demurrer, and to continue the hearing date to facilitate that effort.” (Dumas v. Los Angeles County Bd. of Supervisors (2020) 45 Cal.App.5th 348, 355, fn. 3.)

 

Defendant’s counsel failed to sufficiently meet and confer in-person, by telephone, or by video conference. As such, the court will continue the hearing to permit counsels to properly meet and confer.

 

III.           CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the court continues the hearing on the demurrer to Friday, August 28, 2026. Defendant’s counsel is ordered to file a supplemental declaration discussing meet and confer efforts that comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a) by August 21, 2026.
