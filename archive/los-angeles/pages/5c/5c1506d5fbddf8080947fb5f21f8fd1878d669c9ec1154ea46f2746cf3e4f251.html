DEPARTMENT 515 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 21STCV26958    Hearing Date: September 8, 2026    Dept: 515

The Court's tentative is to deny the proposed judgment in favor of Plaintiff submitting a corrected judgment form, for the reasons discussed below: 

I. Amount of Complaint

Plaintiff’s CIV-100 identifies the amount of complaint as $158,028.35, but that number is not supported by the Complaint.

Plaintiff requests $25,000 for the unfair competition claim and an additional $25,000 for the false imprisonment claim, neither of which request those amounts in the Complaint. Additionally, damages are not available for an unfair competition claim under Business and Professions Code §17200. (Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1134, 1144).

 

The wage claims are adequately noticed. The complaint pleads the $172.50 daily entitlement, the six-day week, the January 2020 to April 2021 period, the 30-day waiting time formula, and the wage statement penalty schedule, giving fair warning of an exposure of at least $84,451, which is more than the $66,001 in damages and penalties supported by the record. (People ex rel. Lockyer v. Brar (2005) 134 Cal.App.4th 659, 667-668).

 

II. Unsupported Figures

Additionally, five figures need reduction on the evidence.

1. The wage statement penalty was claimed at the $4,000 cap without counting pay periods; 16 monthly periods yield $1,550 (Lab. Code, § 226, subd. (e)(1)).

 

2. Interest was computed on penalties as well as wages, but Labor Code section 218.6 reaches unpaid wages only, so the figure is $29,638 rather than $34,225.50.

 

3. Attorney fees of $50,000 come with no declaration, no rates, and no hours; the Local Rule 3.214 schedule yields $2,210.02.

4. Costs are allowable as to the $838.42 in filing fees and $322.92 in process server fees, but the $4,571.43 line for court reporter, interpreter, and shipping charges must be broken out before any of it is awarded (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subds. (a)(1), (4), (b)(3)).

 

5. The $55,351.85 “Other” line on the JUD-100 double counts penalties already inside the damages figure.

The amount supported by the records is $59,276 in wages, $5,175 in waiting time penalties, $1,550 in wage statement penalties, $29,638 in prejudgment interest, $5,351.85 in discovery sanctions, $2,210.02 in attorney fees, and $1,161.34 in costs for a total of $104,362.21.

 

If counsel accepts those figures the Court can enter judgment at the hearing; otherwise, a short continuance for a corrected JUD-100, a fee declaration, and a cost itemization would be appropriate.


Case Number: 25STCV15801    Hearing Date: September 8, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Demurrer to First Amended Cross-Complaint

The Demurrer is overruled in part and sustained in part with 20 days leave to amend.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs and Cross-Defendants Emerita Celeste Aguilar Matias and Kaylani Paredes Aguilar, a minor, by and through her guardian ad litem Emerita Celeste Aguilar Matias (collectively, “Plaintiffs” or “Cross-Defendants”), filed this action against Ruben Adolfo Ajanel (“Ajanel” or “Cross-Complainant”), alleging that they were injured when an exterior stair at their apartment building collapsed. Ajanel filed a Cross-Complaint alleging that Cross-Defendants’ counsel sent his insurer a policy limits settlement demand, that the insurer accepted it, and that Cross-Defendants then refused to complete the settlement and filed this action instead.

The causes of action in the First Amended Cross-Complaint (FACC) are: (1) Breach of Oral Contract; (2) Breach of Written Contract; (3) Breach of the Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing; (4) Negligent Misrepresentation; (5) Promissory Estoppel; and (6) Declaratory Relief.

On May 18, 2026, the Court sustained Cross-Defendants’ demurrer to the original Cross-Complaint with 20 days leave to amend. Ajanel then filed the FACC.

Cross-Defendants filed a Demurrer. Cross-Complainant filed an Opposition.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Cross-Defendants request judicial notice of Exhibit 1 to the Ralidis Declaration, which the declaration describes as the Court’s ruling on the demurrer to the original Cross-Complaint. (Ralidis Decl., ¶ 2.) Exhibit 1 is instead the May 22, 2026 minute order accepting Ajanel’s peremptory challenge and reassigning the case, with the clerk’s notice and certificate of service. (Ralidis Decl., Ex. 1.) The Court grants the request as to its records, including the May 18, 2026 minute order sustaining the demurrer to the original Cross-Complaint with leave to amend and the May 22, 2026 minute order, for their existence and legal effect and not for the truth of any matter recited in them. (Evid. Code, §§ 452, subd. (d), 453.)

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer lies where “[t]he pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).) “We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law. [Citation.] We also consider matters which may be judicially noticed.” (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The Court gives the pleading “a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Ibid.) “To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)

Where a document is attached to the pleading, the Court reads the document. “[W]e assume the truth of the contents of the exhibits [attached to the complaint]. If the allegations in the complaint conflict with the exhibits, we rely on and accept as true the contents of the exhibits and treat as surplusage the pleader’s allegations as to the legal effect of the exhibits.” (Barnett v. Fireman’s Fund Ins. Co. (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 500, 505 (Barnett).)

A pleader may nonetheless allege the construction of an ambiguous contract attached to the pleading. “[W]here an ambiguous contract is the basis of an action, it is proper, if not essential, for a plaintiff to allege its own construction of the agreement. So long as the pleading does not place a clearly erroneous construction upon the provisions of the contract, in passing upon the sufficiency of the complaint, we must accept as correct plaintiff’s allegations as to the meaning of the agreement.” (Aragon-Haas v. Family Security Ins. Services, Inc. (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 232, 239 (Aragon-Haas).)

ANALYSIS

I. Meet and Confer

Ajanel argues that the Demurrer is procedurally defective because the declaration filed with it says nothing about a meet and confer. Cross-Defendants’ reply that counsel met and conferred before filing.

The declaration filed with the Demurrer contains no statement of the means by which the parties met and conferred, which Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a)(3) requires. (Ralidis Decl., ¶¶ 1-3.) However, “[a] determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(4).) The Court therefore proceeds to the merits.

II. The Prior Ruling

Cross-Defendants reply that the May 18, 2026 ruling decided these questions and that Ajanel has pleaded nothing new. As to the documents and the central allegation, that is accurate, and the Court states plainly what has and has not changed.

“‘It is well established that an amendatory pleading supersedes the original one, which ceases to perform any function as a pleading. [Citations.]’ [Citation.]” (Foreman & Clark Corp. v. Fallon (1971) 3 Cal.3d 875, 884.)The Court decides this demurrer on the pleading now before it.

III. Breach of Contract

Cross-Defendants contend that no contract was formed because the demand letter was not a time-limited demand under Code of Civil Procedure section 999.1, contained no offer to release Ajanel, lacked essential terms, and because the insurer’s response added terms and was a counteroffer that Cross-Defendants never accepted. Cross-Defendants also contend that the second cause of action fails because no written contract was signed and that the FACC pleads no breach and no damages. Ajanel argues that the demand was an offer to settle for the policy limits, that the insurer accepted it by telephone and in writing, that the parties’ agreement on the material terms was not undone by the release paperwork that was to follow, and that questions of formation are for the trier of fact.

The elements of breach of contract are “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.) A settlement agreement is governed by the principles that govern contracts generally. (Weddington Productions, Inc. v. Flick (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 793, 810-811 (Weddington).) “‘The existence of mutual consent is determined by objective rather than subjective criteria, the test being what the outward manifestations of consent would lead a reasonable person to believe.’” (Id. at p. 811.) An offer “‘must be sufficiently definite, or must call for such definite terms in the acceptance, that the performance promised is reasonably certain.’” (Ibid.)

A. Offer

Here, Ajanel sufficiently alleges an offer. Cross-Defendants’ counsel wrote the insurer “a demand for equitable settlement” of the claims arising from the fall. (FACC, Ex. A, at p. 1.) The letter names Ajanel as the party liable for the injuries, states that “we are demanding the Global Policy Limits for the settlement of this demand,” and asks for a response by April 30, 2025. (FACC, Ex. A, at pp. 3, 9.) Ajanel alleges that the letter offered to settle and release Cross-Defendants’ claims in exchange for the policy limits and that the insurer so understood it. (FACC, ¶¶ 12, 20, 25.) Cross-Defendants read the same letter as a demand for money that offered no release in return, and they reply that no one contends the letter is ambiguous, so Aragon-Haas has no application. (Reply, at p. 5:2-13.) The Court reads the letter itself. (Barnett, supra, 90 Cal.App.4th at p. 505.)

The reading that Ajanel pleads is not clearly erroneous. To settle a claim is to compromise it, and a compromise gives up the claim in return for what is paid. The letter is captioned “POLICY LIMITS SETTLEMENT DEMAND,” describes itself as “a demand for equitable settlement,” and demands the limits “for the settlement of this demand.” (FACC, Ex. A, at pp. 1, 9.) Read as Cross-Defendants read it, that language proposes Ajanel receiving nothing at all. An insurer that paid the $100,000 would purchase nothing, the claims would survive the payment, and they could be filed the next month. The rules of interpretation do not permit that reading where another is available. “A contract must receive such an interpretation as will make it lawful, operative, definite, reasonable, and capable of being carried into effect, if it can be done without violating the intention of the parties.” (Civ. Code, § 1643.) “An interpretation which gives effect is preferred to one which makes void.” (Civ. Code, § 3541.) The objective test asks the same question from the other side. A reasonable person in the insurer’s position, reading a letter that demands the limits to settle the claims and fixes a deadline for the response, would not understand it to propose a payment that left the claims intact. (Weddington, supra, 60 Cal.App.4th at p. 811.)

Additionally relevant is that an insurer may not simply hand the money over when faced with a demand. “‘[A]n insurer is authorized to settle lawsuits, not to pay unilaterally the policy limits to a plaintiff.’” (Lehto v. Allstate Ins. Co. (1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 60, 74 [insurer did not act in bad faith by declining to pay its limits without a release protecting all of its insureds]; see FACC, ¶ 9.) A demand for the limits directed to an insurer is read against a background in which the payment and the end of the claim move together.

That a release document was to follow does not show that the demand proposed no release. A signed release records the exchange and fixes its scope. It is the ordinary paperwork of a settlement rather than the settlement itself, and the insurer’s letter treats it that way, stating that a release “will be forwarded for signatures” once the apportionment is supplied. (FACC, Ex. B, at p. 1.) A demand that proposes an exchange the parties expect to document is still a demand that proposes the exchange.

Cross-Defendants contend that the letter is not a time-limited demand because it does not offer the “complete release” that Code of Civil Procedure section 999.1, subdivision (c) requires, and that no contract could form without one. The chapter states the consequence of a noncompliant demand: “In any lawsuit filed by a claimant, or by a claimant as an assignee of the tortfeasor or by the tortfeasor for the benefit of the claimant, a time-limited demand that does not substantially comply with the terms of this chapter shall not be considered to be a reasonable offer to settle the claims against the tortfeasor for an amount within the insurance policy limits for purposes of any lawsuit alleging extracontractual damages against the tortfeasor’s liability insurer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 999.4, subd. (a).) Whether the letter substantially complies with section 999.1 therefore does not decide whether it was an offer that could be accepted

B. Acceptance

Here, Ajanel sufficiently alleges mutual assent. Ajanel alleges that the insurer accepted on April 17, 2025, first by telephone and then by letter. (FACC, ¶¶ 15, 17, 26, 32.) The letter states, “we are accepting your global policy limit demand,” and continues, “We are prepared to resolve your client’s Bodily Injury claims for the policy limit of $100,000.00 (One Hundred Thousand Dollars), inclusive of all liens and bystander claims.” (FACC, Ex. B, at p. 1.) The letter encloses the declarations page, asks for the final lien amounts and the apportionment between the two claimants, notes the possible need for a court-approved minor’s compromise, and states that a release will be forwarded for signature once the apportionment is received. (Ibid.)

Cross-Defendants contend that the release, lien, apportionment, and minor’s compromise steps were new material terms that made the letter a counteroffer. “[T]erms proposed in an offer must be met exactly, precisely and unequivocally for its acceptance to result in the formation of a binding contract [citations]; and a qualified acceptance amounts to a new proposal or counteroffer putting an end to the original offer.” (Apablasa v. Merritt & Co. (1959) 176 Cal.App.2d 719, 726 (Apablasa).) However, “when parties agree on the material terms of a contract with the intention to later reduce it to a formal writing, failure to complete the formal writing does not negate the existence of the initial contract. [Citations.] If the parties do not agree on the content of the formal writing (for example because one party wants to include something not agreed on in the first place, as Hodroj says happened here), the proposed writing is not a counteroffer; rather, the initial agreement remains binding and a rejected writing is a nullity.” (CSAA Ins. Exchange v. Hodroj (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 272, 276 (Hodroj).) In Hodroj, the claimant’s counsel demanded the policy limits, the insurer wrote “We accept your demand for settlement of this claim” and enclosed a release, and the claimant repudiated the settlement because the release carried terms beyond the offer. (Id. at pp. 274-275.) The court held the settlement enforceable and the claimant in breach for filing suit on the claims he had agreed to settle. (Id. at pp. 277-278.)

That Hodroj was decided on undisputed facts at summary judgment does not diminish its relevance here. On demurrer the Court assumes the truth of the FACC’s allegations. Thus, Ajanel’s position at this stage is the more favorable one. Likewise, an insurer’s written acceptance of a demand for its available policy limits formed a settlement. Because the demand there had not made a declaration a condition of the offer, the parties’ later exchange of one “did not equate to a novation or unwinding of the settlement.” (Farmers Ins. Exchange v. Superior Court (2026) 122 Cal.App.5th 333, 339.)

Cross-Defendants distinguish Hodroj on the ground that the demand there invited a release and the demand here did not. Ajanel meets that distinction in two ways. First, the demand, read as Ajanel pleads it, proposed a settlement of the claims for the limits, and a settlement of the claims includes their release, as discussed above. Second, Ajanel alleges that on April 17, 2025, Cross-Defendants’ counsel told the insurer’s representative that executed releases, with the final lien amounts and the apportionment for a possible minor’s compromise, would be provided. (FACC, ¶ 16.) The insurer’s letter records the same exchange: “You agreed to forward the distribution amounts upon receipt of the offer,” and “Your office confirmed your clients are non Medicare recipients but are DHCS recipients and agreed to forward the final liens.” (FACC, Ex. B, at p. 1.) On these allegations the parties agreed on April 17 that the insurer would pay its $100,000 limits and that Cross-Defendants would release their claims against Ajanel, with the release document and the lien and minor’s compromise mechanics to follow. That is an agreement on the material terms with a formal writing to come. Under Hodroj, the writing that never came does not undo it.

Cross-Defendants argue that the insurer’s letter was a counteroffer because it added terms. The letter opens by thanking counsel for the call that day and states, “As discussed, we are accepting your global policy limit demand.” (Ibid.) The inquiry into mutual assent asks what those outward manifestations would lead a reasonable person to believe. (Weddington, supra, 60 Cal.App.4th at p. 811.) The acceptance letter also records the sequence of the call. The representative “requested the apportionment of the policy limit,” “discussed the potential need for a court approved minor’s compromise,” and counsel “agreed to forward the distribution amounts.” (FACC, Ex. B, at p. 1.) The letter’s remaining terms state that the payment is “inclusive of all liens and bystander claims” and that checks issue on receipt of the executed release, the final liens, and any minor’s compromise. (FACC, Ex. B, at p. 1.) Those terms are the mechanics of payment and release. Under Hodroj, they do not make the acceptance conditional.

Alternatively, if the requests made on the call turned the insurer’s acceptance into a counteroffer, then the counteroffer was the call, not the letter. Ajanel alleges that Cross-Defendants’ counsel agreed to provide “executed release(s) with information as to any final lien amounts and/or apportionment for a possible minor’s compromise” before the letter confirmed the exchange. (FACC, ¶ 16.) A counteroffer “amounts to nothing” only “if not accepted by the original offeror.” (Apablasa, supra, 176 Cal.App.2d at p. 726.) On either framing, the Court concludes that Ajanel sufficiently alleges mutual assent.

Cross-Defendants reply that the allegation is false and that it, like the allegation of the medical payments disclosure, does not identify who spoke, to whom, or how. (Reply, at p. 3:21-28.) The Court accepts properly pleaded facts as true. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318.) Ajanel identifies the parties to the conversation by role, its date, and its substance. Ajanel need not plead the evidentiary detail behind that conversation or behind the disclosure alleged in the general allegations. (FACC, ¶¶ 6, 16; C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist., supra, 53 Cal.4th at p. 872.) Uncertainty is a separate ground of demurrer that the notice does not raise. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).)

C. Essential Terms

Cross-Defendants contend that the “policy limits” were never defined, that the insurer withheld the medical payments coverage, that the demand did not say whether the limits were joint or separate as to the two claimants, and that the parties never agreed on who would be released, when payment was due, or attorney fees. “[A] contract will be enforced if it is sufficiently definite (and this is a question of law) for the court to ascertain the parties’ obligations and to determine whether those obligations have been performed or breached. [Citations.] Stated otherwise, the contract will be enforced if it is possible to reach a fair and just result even if, in the process, the court is required to ‘fill in some gaps.’” (Ersa Grae Corp. v. Fluor Corp. (1991) 1 Cal.App.4th 613, 623.)

Here, the obligations are ascertainable. The demand asked for the limits of Ajanel’s liability policy, and the acceptance identified them as $100,000 and enclosed the declarations page showing that figure as the business liability limit. (FACC, ¶ 17, Ex. B, at pp. 1, 3.) The demand for Ajanel’s policy limits is definite because his policy fixes the amount. As to medical payments coverage, Ajanel alleges that Cross-Defendants’ counsel was told that coverage was unavailable to residents of the property. (FACC, ¶ 6.) Cross-Defendants’ unexpressed expectation of $200,000 or $300,000 does not enter the objective inquiry. (Dem., at p. 12:7-9.) Nor does Cross-Defendants’ speculation about what the redacted portions of the declarations page conceal; Ajanel alleges no coverage beyond the $100,000 limit. The demand names Ajanel as the person liable. (FACC, Ex. A, at p. 3.) He is therefore the party to be released. The division of a single global payment between the two claimants is a matter of distribution, and Ajanel alleges that Cross-Defendants’ counsel stated that the apportionment would be provided. (FACC, ¶ 16.) A payment date and a fee provision are not essential to a settlement whose exchange, $100,000 for a release, is fixed. Where no time for performance is specified, “a reasonable time is allowed.” (Civ. Code, § 1657.) The absence of a fee clause means only that each side bears its attorney fees. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1021.) Civil Code section 1654 applies “[i]n cases of uncertainty not removed by the preceding rules” of interpretation. On demurrer the Court accepts the pleader’s construction and does not construe it against him. Thus, Ajanel sufficiently alleges a contract with reasonably certain terms.

D. Written Contract

Cross-Defendants contend that the second cause of action fails because no written contract was signed by the parties.

Here, Ajanel pleads the written demand and the written acceptance as the contract. (FACC, ¶¶ 31-32, Exs. A, B.) Cross-Defendants cite no authority requiring a signature before an exchange of letters can be a written contract. The writings here are the two letters, and neither is “unattached” or “undescribed.” (Dem., at p. 13:18.) Whether the contract is characterized as oral, formed on the April 17 call, or written, formed by the exchange of letters, Ajanel may plead both in the alternative. The label does not decide a general demurrer. The second cause of action is sufficiently alleged.

E. Breach and Damage

Cross-Defendants contend that the FACC identifies no breach and pleads no facts supporting damages, and they point out that the first cause of action alleges a breach “by not reneging on the written offer.” (FACC, ¶ 28; Dem., at p. 10:5.)

Here, the first cause of action incorporates the general allegations, which allege that Cross-Defendants supplied neither the release nor the lien and apportionment information and instead filed this action on the claims they had agreed to settle. (FACC, ¶¶ 21-24.) Ajanel alleges the breach in the same terms in the second cause of action, as a failure to release him. (FACC, ¶ 36.) Against those allegations, the word “not” in the phrase “not reneging” is an evident drafting error. The Court reads the pleading “as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318.) The claimant in Hodroj “breached the contract here by filing suit on the bodily injury claims he had agreed to settle.” (Hodroj, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th at p. 278.) Ajanel alleges resulting damage in an amount according to proof. (FACC, ¶¶ 29, 37.) That is the ultimate fact the element requires. Whether particular items in the prayer, including attorney fees, are recoverable is not a question decided on general demurrer, because “[a] demurrer does not lie to a portion of a cause of action.” (PH II, Inc. v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1680, 1682.)

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the first and second causes of action is overruled.

IV. Implied Covenant

Cross-Defendants contend that the third cause of action fails because it depends on the contract claims.

“The prerequisite for any action for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing is the existence of a contractual relationship between the parties, since the covenant is an implied term in the contract.” (Smith v. City and County of San Francisco (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 38, 49.) Here, Ajanel sufficiently alleges the contract, as discussed above, and Cross-Defendants raise no other ground. The Demurrer to the third cause of action is overruled.

V. Negligent Misrepresentation

Cross-Defendants contend that the fourth cause of action identifies no misrepresentation of fact, pleads a promise instead of a representation, and alleges no justifiable reliance or cognizable damage. Ajanel argues that Cross-Defendants represented that the policy limits would settle their claims and then did not settle.

“To be actionable, a negligent misrepresentation must ordinarily be as to past or existing material facts. ‘[P]redictions as to future events, or statements as to future action by some third party, are deemed opinions, and not actionable fraud.’” (Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 158 (Tarmann).) A promise made without the intent to perform is intentional fraud, and “[t]he specific intent requirement also precludes pleading a false promise claim as a negligent misrepresentation.” (Id. at p. 159.) The court in Tarmann declined “to establish a new type of actionable deceit: the negligent false promise.” (Ibid.)

Here, Ajanel insufficiently alleges a misrepresentation of existing fact. The representations alleged are that Ajanel was liable for the injuries and that the policy limits “needed to be offered to settle and release” him. (FACC, ¶ 45.) The first is the claimants’ contention of liability in a demand letter. The second is a promise of future performance. Ajanel alleges that Cross-Defendants “had no intention of releasing and settling” when the promise was made. (FACC, ¶ 48.) That allegation describes a false promise. The reliance Ajanel pleads is on that promise. (FACC, ¶ 50.) Under Tarmann, a false promise is not a negligent misrepresentation. The Court declines to develop Cross-Defendants’ remaining arguments on reliance and damage. Ajanel requests leave to amend. (Opp., at p. 10:11-15.) Accordingly, the Court sustains the Demurrer to the fourth cause of action with leave to amend.

VI. Promissory Estoppel

Cross-Defendants contend that promissory estoppel is inapplicable because the claim rests on the alleged contract alone, and that the FACC pleads no clear promise, no reasonable reliance, and no injury. Ajanel argues that the allegations supporting negligent misrepresentation support this claim as well, and that alternative theories on the same facts are proper.

“Under this doctrine a promisor is bound when he should reasonably expect a substantial change of position, either by act or forbearance, in reliance on his promise, if injustice can be avoided only by its enforcement.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 249 (Youngman).) “The purpose of this doctrine is to make a promise binding, under certain circumstances, without consideration in the usual sense of something bargained for and given in exchange.” (Ibid.) “If the promisee’s performance was requested at the time the promisor made his promise and that performance was bargained for, the doctrine is inapplicable.” (Ibid.) “Under such circumstances, the only reliance which can make the promisor’s failure to perform actionable is the promisee’s doing what was requested.” (Healy v. Brewster (1963) 59 Cal.2d 455, 463 (Healy).) “In other words, where the promisee’s reliance was bargained for, the law of consideration applies; and it is only where the reliance was unbargained for that there is room for application of the doctrine of promissory estoppel.” (Ibid.)

Here, Ajanel insufficiently alleges promissory estoppel. The promise alleged is Cross-Defendants’ promise to release and settle their claims “in exchange for” the policy limits, and the reliance alleged is that Ajanel “relied on the promise and agreed to provide” those limits. (FACC, ¶¶ 53-54.) The performance Ajanel alleges is the performance that the promise requested. That reliance was bargained for. Thus, the law of consideration governs it. The claim it supports is the breach of contract claim pleaded in the first and second causes of action. Ajanel also alleges that he relied on the promise by not inspecting the premises, gathering evidence, or consulting counsel before the Complaint was filed. (FACC, ¶ 56.) Nobody requested that forbearance, and Ajanel does not allege that Cross-Defendants bargained for it. However, where the promise was made in exchange for a requested performance, “the only reliance which can make the promisor’s failure to perform actionable is the promisee’s doing what was requested.” (Healy, supra, 59 Cal.2d at p. 463.) Ajanel’s forbearance from investigating the claim therefore does not make Cross-Defendants’ promise actionable outside the contract.

Accordingly, the Court sustains the Demurrer to the fifth cause of action with leave to amend.

VII. Declaratory Relief

Cross-Defendants contend that the sixth cause of action pleads no actual controversy, that the statute requires a written instrument, that the disputes are factual, that the claim depends on the failed contract claims, and that the FACC seeks attorney fees without a basis. Ajanel argues that declaratory relief is a recognized means of settling disputes over contractual rights.

“Any person interested under a written instrument, excluding a will or a trust, or under a contract ... may, in cases of actual controversy relating to the legal rights and duties of the respective parties, bring an original action or cross-complaint in the superior court for a declaration of his or her rights and duties in the premises, including a determination of any question of construction or validity arising under the instrument or contract.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1060.) “‘A complaint for declaratory relief is legally sufficient if it sets forth facts showing the existence of an actual controversy relating to the legal rights and duties of the parties under a written instrument or with respect to property and requests that the rights and duties of the parties be adjudged by the court.’” (Ludgate Ins. Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 592, 606.)

Here, Ajanel sufficiently alleges an actual controversy. Ajanel alleges that he contends Cross-Defendants are obligated to release him from all liability for the fall in exchange for the $100,000 limits, and that Cross-Defendants dispute it. (FACC, ¶ 59.) Cross-Defendants deny that any agreement exists and are prosecuting the claims Ajanel says were settled. The statute covers rights “under a contract.” No separate written instrument is required, and the contract alleged is in any event the exchange of letters. That the parties dispute the facts is a reason to try the controversy, not to dismiss it at the pleading stage. The contract claims on which the sixth cause of action depends survive, as discussed above. The prayer for attorney fees is a portion of the relief sought. Thus, a demurrer does not lie to it. (PH II, Inc. v. Superior Court, supra, 33 Cal.App.4th at p. 1682.)

Cross-Defendants also say the claim duplicates the contract counts. The Court may “refuse to exercise the power granted by this chapter in any case where its declaration or determination is not necessary or proper at the time under all the circumstances.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1061.) Declaratory relief “‘operates prospectively,’” and “‘there is no basis for declaratory relief where only past wrongs are involved.’” (Osseous Technologies of America, Inc. v. DiscoveryOrtho Partners LLC (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 357, 366, quoting 5 Witkin, Cal. Procedure (5th ed. 2008) Pleading, § 869, p. 284.) That the claim has accrued and that another remedy exists do not bar it. (Id. at p. 370.) Where the Court may entertain the count or dismiss it, “there is little support for the proposition that a trial court abuses its discretion by refusing to dismiss a declaratory relief action that amounts to a mere breach of contract action.” (Id. at pp. 374-375.) The declaration Ajanel seeks is not confined to the past. It would determine whether Cross-Defendants may continue to prosecute the claims now pending in this action. The Court therefore entertains the sixth cause of action.

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the sixth cause of action is overruled.

 

CONCLUSION

The Demurrer is sustained as to the fourth and fifth causes of action with 20 days leave to amend and is otherwise overruled.


Case Number: 25STCV30618    Hearing Date: September 8, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion to Tax Costs

The Motion to Tax Costs is granted in part and denied in part, and costs are taxed in the amount of $1,392.15. Plaintiffs are awarded costs of $2,590.99.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Bryan Albright and Tracy Germain (Plaintiffs) filed this action against Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (Defendant), alleging that a vehicle Defendant warranted was delivered with defects that Defendant’s authorized repair facilities failed to correct.

The causes of action are: (1) Breach of Express Warranty; (2) Breach of Implied Warranty; and (3) Violation of Civil Code Section 1793.2.

Plaintiffs served a statutory offer to compromise under Code of Civil Procedure section 998, which Defendant accepted on February 3, 2026. Plaintiffs dismissed the action on March 24, 2026, and the Court retained jurisdiction under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6. Plaintiffs then filed a Memorandum of Costs. The Court granted Plaintiffs’ Motion to Enforce Settlement on July 1, 2026, and Plaintiffs’ Motion for Attorney Fees is set for hearing on October 16, 2026.

Defendant filed a Motion to Tax Costs. Plaintiffs filed an Opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivisions (a) and (b), list the items allowable and not allowable as costs. Subdivision (b) excludes the items it lists “except when expressly authorized by law.” Costs “are allowable if incurred, whether or not paid,” must be “reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation,” and must be “reasonable in amount.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(1)-(3).) “Items not mentioned in this section and items assessed upon application may be allowed or denied in the court’s discretion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(4).)

A memorandum of costs must be “verified by a statement of the party, attorney, or agent that to the best of his or her knowledge the items of cost are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).)

“If items on their face appear to be proper charges, the verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of their propriety, and the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show they were not reasonable or necessary. ‘On the other hand, if items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.’” (Jones v. Dumrichob (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1266.) “There is no requirement that copies of bills, invoices, statements, or any other such documents be attached to the memorandum. Only if the costs have been put in issue via a motion to tax costs must supporting documentation be submitted.” (Id. at p. 1267.)

Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), part of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (the Act), provides: “If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.”

 

ANALYSIS

I. Costs and Expenses

Defendant contends that every item in the Memorandum of Costs is prohibited, unsupported, unnecessary, or unreasonable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5. Plaintiffs argue that Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), governs and extends to costs and expenses beyond the section 1033.5 list. Defendant replies that section 1794, subdivision (d), contains its own limitation and leaves each item to be tested for necessity and reasonableness.

Here, Plaintiffs are the prevailing party. Plaintiffs dismissed this action after Defendant accepted their statutory offer to pay $85,000 and repurchase the vehicle. (Mot., at p. 1:3-6; Rotman Decl., ¶ 16.) “When a defendant pays money to a plaintiff in order to settle a case, the plaintiff obtains a ‘net monetary recovery,’ and a dismissal pursuant to such a settlement is not a dismissal ‘in [the defendant’s] favor.’” (DeSaulles v. Community Hospital of the Monterey Peninsula (2016) 62 Cal.4th 1140, 1144.) Plaintiffs are therefore entitled to costs as a matter of right. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (b).)

Settling parties remain free to allocate costs as they choose, “although they must do so in language specifically addressing such allocation.” (DeSaulles, supra, 62 Cal.4th at p. 1147.) These parties addressed costs, and they did so in Plaintiffs’ favor. Defendant agreed to pay “attorney fees, costs, and expenses actually and reasonably incurred in the commencement and prosecution of this action, including post-offer acceptance attorney fees, costs, and expenses incurred in performing on the settlement and/or completing the case pursuant to Civil Code section 1794(d) and Cal. Rule of Court 3.1700.” (Rotman Decl., Ex. 1, ¶ 7.) Defendant also agreed that Plaintiffs are deemed the prevailing party under section 1794, subdivision (d), for purposes of the fee motion. (Ibid.) The Court retained jurisdiction to enforce that agreement.

“Section 1794, subdivision (d), permits the prevailing buyer to recover both ‘costs’ and ‘expenses.’” (Jensen v. BMW of North America, Inc. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112, 137, disapproved on another ground in Rodriguez v. FCA US, LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189.) The court examined the language of the statute and concluded that “it is clear the Legislature intended the word ‘expenses’ to cover items not included in the detailed statutory definition of ‘costs.’” (Ibid.) The Legislature added that language in 1978, and an Assembly committee analysis explained that awards of “costs and expenses” would “cover such out-of-pocket expenses as filing fees, expert witness fees, marshall’s fees, etc.” (Id. at p. 138.)

However, section 1794, subdivision (d), allows only the sum “determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” A prevailing buyer must show that each claimed item was incurred and that incurring it was reasonable.

II. Filing Fees

Defendant contends that the $555 in filing and motion fees should be taxed because Plaintiffs supplied no invoices and because the action and the two motions were unnecessary.

Plaintiffs argue that the fees are allowable and submit proof of payment.

Defendant replies that Plaintiffs must still show that each fee was incurred, necessary, and reasonable.

Filing and motion fees are allowable costs. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(1).)

Here, Plaintiffs incurred all three fees. First, Plaintiffs paid $435 to file the Complaint. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. 2.) Second, Plaintiffs paid $60 to file the Motion to Enforce Settlement, which the Court granted on July 1, 2026. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. 3.) Finally, Plaintiffs incurred the $60 fee for the Motion for Attorney Fees when they filed that motion on August 24, 2026. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 7, Ex. 4.)

Defendant’s objection to the fee for the Motion for Attorney Fees was well taken when Defendant made it. Plaintiffs claimed that fee in the Memorandum of Costs before they filed the motion it would pay for. However, Plaintiffs have since filed the motion, and the fee was incurred before this hearing.

Moreover, Plaintiffs reasonably incurred each fee. Defendant’s contention that Plaintiffs “should have never filed this lawsuit in the first place” contradicts the terms Defendant accepted, which reach the expenses of commencing the action. (Mot., at p. 2:23-24; Rotman Decl., Ex. 1, ¶ 7.) Even if the agreement permitted Defendant to dispute whether the action itself was reasonable, Defendant has not shown that their offer was unconditional and fully compensable, such that there was nothing to be gained by filing this action. Accordingly, the Motion to Tax Costs is denied as to the filing and motion fees.

III. Service of Process

Defendant contends that the $95 in service costs should be taxed because Plaintiffs explained nothing and documented nothing, and because service by mail under Code of Civil Procedure section 415.40 would have cost less.

Plaintiffs argue that the cost is allowable and submit the proof of service.

Defendant replies that the question is whether personal service was necessary in this action, and that Plaintiffs, having mailed their pre-litigation demand, cannot show that it was.

Where service is made by a registered process server, “the recoverable cost is the amount actually incurred in effecting service, including, but not limited to, a stakeout or other means employed in locating the person to be served, unless those charges are successfully challenged by a party to the action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(4)(B).)

Here, Plaintiffs incurred $95 for personal service on Defendant’s authorized agent for service of process. A process server personally served the summons and complaint on that agent. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 8, Ex. 5.) The proof of service identifies the server as a registered California process server working as an independent contractor through InfoTrack US, Inc., P000634, and gives the registration number and county. (Ibid.) Defendant does not dispute that the server was registered. Section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(4)(B), therefore allows the amount actually incurred.

Personal service was also reasonably necessary. Defendant is a Delaware limited liability company that maintains an agent for service of process in this state. Personal service on that agent is an ordinary means of serving a foreign limited liability company. Section 415.40 permits mail service on a person outside this state, but nothing in section 1033.5 obliges a plaintiff to choose the least expensive lawful method. Service by that means is complete only on the tenth day after mailing and depends on a return receipt. (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.40.)

That Plaintiffs mailed their pre-litigation repurchase demand does not establish that personal service of the summons was unnecessary. The demand and the summons are governed by different provisions. Code of Civil Procedure section 871.24, subdivision (d), prescribes how a consumer notifies a manufacturer before suing, and the service statutes prescribe how a summons reaches a defendant.

Accordingly, the Motion to Tax Costs is denied as to the service of process costs.

IV. Court Reporter Fees

Defendant contends that the $625 in court reporter fees should be taxed because Plaintiffs supplied no invoice and because Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (b)(5), prohibits recovery for transcripts not ordered by the court.

Plaintiffs argue that the cost is allowable under section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(11), and under the Act, and they support it with an invoice from a reporter retained for a fee motion hearing in another action.

Defendant replies that the cost has not been incurred.

Costs “are allowable if incurred, whether or not paid.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(1).)

Here, Plaintiffs have not incurred the reporter fee. The hearing on the Motion for Attorney Fees is set for October 16, 2026, and Plaintiffs call the charge “anticipated” and “estimated.” (Opp., at p. 6:3-5.) The invoice they submit is dated August 30, 2023, and bills a court appearance in an unrelated action. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 9, Ex. 6.) An estimate drawn from another matter establishes neither that this cost has been incurred nor what it will be.

Plaintiffs alternatively ask the Court to defer ruling on this item or to continue the hearing on this Motion to the date of the fee hearing. (Opp., at p. 6:7-9.) The Court declines both requests. A memorandum of costs claims what the claiming party has incurred when the memorandum is filed, and a motion to tax tests the items that memorandum claims. Waiting until October would not change what Plaintiffs claimed in June.

Rule 3.1700 also limits how long a cost claim stays open. Absent the parties’ agreement, the Court may extend the time for serving and filing a memorandum of costs by no more than 30 days. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(b)(3).) Plaintiffs ask the Court to hold a cost claim open for a charge arising 114 days after they filed their memorandum.

Plaintiffs’ own arrangements produced the timing issue they now ask the Court to accommodate. Plaintiffs drafted the offer Defendant accepted. That offer waives any deadline for Plaintiffs’ fee motion, and it preserves the deadlines that California Rules of Court, rule 3.1700, sets for the recovery of costs and expenses. (Rotman Decl., Ex. 1, ¶ 7.) Plaintiffs then dismissed the action on March 24, 2026, which started the period that rule allows. Plaintiffs noticed the fee motion for October 16, 2026, a date outside the 180-day outer limit of rule 3.1700(a)(1).

Accordingly, the Motion to Tax Costs is granted as to the court reporter fees, and $625 is taxed.

V. Electronic Filing

Defendant contends that the $364.50 in electronic filing fees should be taxed because Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(14), covers only electronic filing a court requires or orders, because the Memorandum of Costs identifies no filings, and because Plaintiffs documented nothing.

Plaintiffs argue that this Court requires electronic filing, and they submit an itemization and the vendor invoices.

Defendant replies that several of the entries are projections and that Plaintiffs have not shown the rest were necessary.

Fees for the electronic filing or service of documents through an electronic filing service provider are allowable “if a court requires or orders electronic filing or service of documents.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(14).) A court may require represented parties to file and serve documents electronically. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.253(b).)

Here, this Court requires represented litigants to file civil documents electronically. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 11, Ex. 8.) The fees therefore fall within section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(14). Plaintiffs’ itemization lists 15 entries totaling $364.50. The vendor invoices, the vendor’s order records, and Plaintiffs’ case management records document the charges. (Rotman Decl., ¶¶ 7, 10, Exs. 4, 7.)

Plaintiffs have incurred 11 of those entries, the last of them the electronic filing of the Opposition on August 25, 2026. (Rotman Decl., ¶¶ 3, 7, 10, Exs. 4, 7.) Each of the 11 corresponds by document, and within days by date, to a paper that the register of actions shows was filed. Those papers are the pleading that commenced the action, the notices and proofs of service that the rules require, and the papers by which Plaintiffs dismissed the action, enforced the settlement, and claim and defend their costs and fees. Each was necessary to conduct or to complete this action, and this Court required each to be filed electronically.

Two of the entries, $49.40 in all, are the fees to file the Memorandum of Costs and the Opposition to this Motion. The accepted offer covers expenses incurred in “completing the case.” (Rotman Decl., Ex. 1, ¶ 7.) Those expenses include the papers by which Plaintiffs claim and defend the costs that the offer promised them.

Plaintiffs mark the remaining 4 entries as anticipated and date them from October 9 through December 31, 2026. (Rotman Decl., Ex. 7.) Plaintiffs concede that those entries “have not yet been incurred.” (Opp., at p. 6:18-19.) Each is $24.70, for a total of $98.80.

Accordingly, the Motion to Tax Costs is granted in part as to the electronic filing fees, and $98.80 is taxed.

VI. Research

Defendant contends that the $668.35 in research charges should be taxed because legal research is not recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5 and because Plaintiffs documented nothing.

Plaintiffs argue that the charges are recoverable under the Act because they were incurred in the commencement and prosecution of this action, and they submit the vendor invoices.

Defendant replies that section 1794, subdivision (d), does not convert attorney work or overhead into a separately recoverable charge, and that the invoices identify only a client and an amount.

“Fees for legal research, computer or otherwise, may not be recovered under section 1033.5.” (Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 776.) The charges are therefore recoverable only if they are an “expense” within Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d).

The Court concludes they are not. Rotman states that counsel researched Code of Civil Procedure sections 871.20 through 871.30 after this action was filed. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 12.) Those sections had taken effect before Plaintiffs filed. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 871.24, subd. (k), 871.26, subd. (l).) Generic research into the area of law counsel practices is overhead not chargeable to any one case.

Accordingly, the Motion to Tax Costs is granted as to the research charges, and $668.35 is taxed.

VII. Mediation

Defendant contends that the $1,668.75 in mediation charges should be taxed because the Court never ordered mediation, because the parties agreed to mediate without discussing a fee split or reserving a right to shift the fee, and because Plaintiffs documented nothing.

Mediation charges appear on neither the allowable list nor the prohibited list of Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5. The Court may therefore allow them in its discretion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(4).) “We conclude mediation fees incurred for mediation that was not ordered by the court are not categorically nonrecoverable as ‘not reasonably necessary to the conduct of litigation.’ The question whether mediation fees should be awarded as costs in a particular matter must be determined based on the facts and circumstances of the particular action.” (Berkeley Cement, Inc. v. Regents of University of California (2019) 30 Cal.App.5th 1133, 1143.)

Both parties cite Gibson v. Bobroff (1996) 49 Cal.App.4th 1202. The court there held “that when an unsuccessful mediation has been court-ordered, reasonably necessary expenses incident thereto may, in the sound discretion of the trial court, be awarded after trial to a prevailing party.” (Id. at p. 1209.) The mediation in Gibson was court-ordered, and the opinion expressly reserved the question of a voluntary mediation. (Id. at p. 1209, fn. 7.) The court in Berkeley Cement resolved that question, rejecting any bright line rule. Neither decision bars an award here.

Here, the mediation charges were reasonably necessary to the conduct of this action rather than merely convenient to its preparation. Both sides agreed to mediate and selected a neutral together. They held two sessions, on January 14 and January 26, 2026, and the mediator billed each side for half. (White Decl., ¶ 5; White Reply Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. B; Rotman Decl., ¶ 13, Ex. 10.) The action then resolved 8 days after the second session. A mediation that both parties chose and that produced the settlement resolving the action was necessary to conducting this litigation to its end.

Defendant’s reliance on Anthony v. Li (2020) 47 Cal.App.5th 816 is misplaced. The parties there signed a written mediation agreement providing that they would “divide the professional fees and additional fees” equally and that each would pay its share in advance. (Id. at p. 819.) The Court of Appeal affirmed an order taxing the prevailing party’s half, holding that “[b]ecause the parties agreed to share mediation and court reporter fees equally, without providing for the later recovery of those shared fees by a prevailing party, we see no error or abuse of discretion in the trial court’s taxing those fees as items of costs.” (Id. at p. 825.) The allocation there appeared in a writing the parties signed.

Similarly, in Carr Business Enterprises, Inc. v. City of Chowchilla (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 25, the parties’ stipulation for a general reference provided that “compensation for the referee shall be paid 50% by [the city] and 50% by [the company].” (Id. at p. 27.) The Court of Appeal held that section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(13), “does not authorize the trial court to ignore a valid contractual agreement.” (Id. at p. 30.) In Howard v. American National Fire Ins. Co. (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 498, 540, the parties stipulated to divide equally the fees of a privately compensated judge pro tempore. The court held the award of those fees error. In each, the court enforced an allocation the parties themselves wrote.

Defendant identifies no comparable writing here. On the contrary, White states that “[t]he parties did not discuss any fee split with respect to the mediation.” (White Decl., ¶ 5.) Defendant repeats in the Motion that the parties “agreed to mediate but did not discuss the fee split for the mediation.” (Mot., at p. 5:9-10.) The equal division came from the mediator’s confirmation letter, which stated that the fee “will be equally divided between the parties.” (White Reply Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. B.)

Defendant’s evidence shows that the mediator stated the fee terms to both sides and invoiced each side separately. Plaintiffs paid the invoices addressed to them. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 3.) That payment performed an obligation to the mediator. It was not a promise to Defendant about who would ultimately bear the charge.

Accordingly, the Motion to Tax Costs is denied as to the mediation charges.

VIII. Postage and Printing

Defendant contends that the $6.04 in postage and the $0.50 printing charge are prohibited by Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (b)(3), and unsupported.

Plaintiffs argue that they incurred both charges to print and mail the pre-litigation repurchase demand, and they submit the envelope.

Defendant replies that Code of Civil Procedure section 871.24, subdivision (d), permits notice by email, that postage and printing are overhead, and that Plaintiffs identify no law expressly authorizing the charges.

Section 1033.5, subdivision (b)(3), excludes “[p]ostage, telephone, and photocopying charges, except for exhibits.” As discussed above, Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), is the express authorization that subdivision (b) requires. The court in Ladas held that “[s]ection 1033.5, subdivision (b)(3) forbids reimbursement of expenses for postage, telephone and photocopying.” (Ladas, supra, 19 Cal.App.4th at p. 775.) The party claiming costs there invoked no statute authorizing an award of expenses. Therefore, no statute displaced the exclusion there.

The court in Jensen applied the introductory clause of subdivision (b) to paragraph (1). That clause draws no distinction among the paragraphs that subdivision (b) governs. The committee analysis quoted in Jensen names expert witness fees, an item that paragraph (1) excludes. The Legislature therefore understood “expenses” to include charges that the cost statutes place outside allowable costs. (Jensen, supra, 35 Cal.App.4th at p. 138.) Nothing in the Act confines that authorization to paragraph (1).

Here, Plaintiffs incurred $6.04 in postage and $0.50 in printing to send Defendant a written repurchase demand before filing suit. (Rotman Decl., ¶¶ 14-15, Ex. 11; White Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. A.) Defendant received the demand on August 27, 2025, approved a repurchase on September 17, 2025, and sent a revised repurchase offer and release on October 20, 2025. (White Decl., ¶ 3.) The demand opened the negotiation that preceded this action. Plaintiffs therefore reasonably incurred the cost of sending it.

In reply, Defendant argues that Code of Civil Procedure section 871.24, subdivision (d), permits notice by email. Notice may be sent “either by email to the email address prominently displayed on the manufacturer’s website for this purpose or by certified or registered mail, return receipt requested.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.24, subd. (d).)

Rotman states that the $6.04 covered first-class and certified mail, and the envelope bears a meter stamp in that amount. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 14, Ex. 11.) Defendant offers evidence that much of the pre-litigation correspondence went by email. That evidence does not establish that Defendant maintained the address that the subdivision describes. (White Reply Decl., ¶ 2.)

Defendant also argues that postage and printing are overhead, relying on Bussey. The test there allowed counsel’s disbursements only where they are ordinarily billed to a client and fall outside the overhead component of the hourly rate. (Bussey, supra, 225 Cal.App.3d at pp. 1163-1164.) That test construes a contractual fee provision under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(10). Section 1794, subdivision (d), does not import it. In any event, Rotman states that the firm records printer charges to a matter number at $0.25 per page, and that the $0.50 claimed is the cost of printing the single-page demand and its envelope. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 15.)

Accordingly, the Motion to Tax Costs is denied as to the postage and printing charges.

IX. Total Costs

Plaintiffs’ Memorandum of Costs claims $3,983.14. The Court taxes $625 in court reporter fees, $98.80 in electronic filing fees, and $668.35 in research charges, for a total of $1,392.15. The Court awards Plaintiffs the remaining $2,590.99.

 

CONCLUSION

The Motion to Tax Costs is granted in part and denied in part, and costs are taxed in the amount of $1,392.15. Plaintiffs are awarded costs of $2,590.99.
