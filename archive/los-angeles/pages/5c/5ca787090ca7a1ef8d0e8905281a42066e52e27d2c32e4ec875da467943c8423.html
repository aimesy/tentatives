CITY OF MONTEBELLO.

Defendants.

22STCV24991

Hearing Date:

September 14, 2026

[TENTATIVE] ORDER CONTINUINING MOTION FOR ORDER RECIEVER'S FINAL REPORT AND ACCOUNTING AND DISCHARGING RECIEVER

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff City of Montebello (“City”) filed this nuisance abatement and receivership complaint against Defendants regarding the real property at 836 North Vail Avenue, Montebello, California 90640 (“subject property”), on the basis that the subject property had fire hazards, overgrown and dead vegetation, and hoarder-type accumulation of debris, among other issues. Causes of action included receivership, public nuisance, and nuisance per se.

As of November 22, 2022, all named Defendants are in default.

On December 12, 2022, the Court appointed non-party Eric P. Beatty (“Beatty”) as the receiver in this action.

On April 22, 2026, Beatty filed the instant motion for order approving receiver's final report and final accounting and discharging receiver.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

California Rules of Court rule 3.1184, subdivision (a),[1] provides that a receiver must present, by noticed motion or stipulation of all parties: (1) a final account and report, (2) a request for the discharge, and (3) a request for exoneration of the receiver’s surety. Rule 3.1184, subdivision (c), further provides that “[n]otice of the motion or of the stipulation must be given to every person or entity known to the receiver to have a substantial, unsatisfied claim that will be affected by the order or stipulation, whether or not the person or entity is a party to the action or has appeared in it.” 

 

 

 

III.       DISCUSSION

Rule 3.1184, subdivision (c), requires that notice of motion be served on all persons or entities known to the receiver to have a substantial, unsatisfied claim, that will be affected by the order, whether or not the person or entity is a party to the action or has appeared in it.  Here, non-party Rumi Yamaguchi (“Rumi”), as intestate heir to Defendants Kazuhiko Yamaguchi and Mitsuru Yamaguchi, has a claim to the remaining assets that will result in payment to Rumi upon approval of the order.  Beatty has provided a proof of service of the motion and proposed order only on the City.  Beatty has also provided a proof of service on both the city and Rumi of his declaration in lieu of reply and her declaration indicating review of the Sale Approval Order and waiving an appeal of the Sale Approval Order.  Rumi appears to have a substantial, currently unsatisfied claim, that will be affected by the order and so Rumi should have been served with notice of this motion prior to the court issuing a ruling on the merits.  Although there is no standard proof of service of the motion, Rumi’s and Beatty’s declarations indicate her knowledge of the issues.  Under these circumstances, if Rumi appears at the hearing (telephonically is sufficient), confirms her knowledge of the motion and her declaration, waives formal service of motion, and has no other objections, the Court will deem Rule 3.1184 satisfied and grant the motion.

Case Number: 24NWLC05362    Hearing Date: September 14, 2026    Dept: 730

Superior Court of California

SOCAL LIEN SOLUTIONS LLC

Plaintiff,

vs.

COUNTRYWIDE DEVELOPMENT & CONTRACTING, INC., et al.,

Defendants.

Case No.:

24NWLC05362

Hearing Date:

September 14, 2026

ORDER DENYING MOTION TO EXPUNGE MECHANIC’S LIEN AND GRANTING MOTION TO EXPUNGE LIS PENDENS

 

I.          BACKGROUND

This is real property action arising out of alleged failure to pay for drywall work done to the 1298 W 37th St, Los Angeles, CA 90007, property ("subject property”) by Plaintiff SoCal Lien Solutions, LLC’s (“SoCal”) assignee. SoCal sued Defendants for five causes of action, including breach of contract.

Defendants Campus Rentals LLC (“Campus”) and Countrywide Development & Contracting Inc. (“CDC”) filed a Cross-Complaint against SoCal and New Concept Drywall, Inc. (“NC”) for four causes of action, including breach of contract.

Named defendants were dismissed on April 15, 2026. Doe Defendants have not been dismissed.

On November 15, 2023, NC filed a mechanic’s lien against the subject property.

On March 8, 2024, SoCal filed a lis pendens for the subject property.

On July 2, 2026, Campus filed the instant motion to expunge NC’s mechanic’s lien.

On August 19, 2026, Campus filed the instant motion to expunge lis pendens.

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

California Civil Code section 8480 provides that the owner of property may petition the court for an order to release the property from the claim of lien if the claimant has not commenced an action to enforce the lien within the time provided in section 8460. (Civ. Code § 8480(a).) Section 8460 provides that “[t]he claimant shall commence an action to enforce a lien within 90 days after recordation of the claim of lien.” (Id., § 8460(a).) Section 8460 further provides that “[i]f the claimant does not commence an action to enforce the lien within that time, the claim of lien expires and is unenforceable.” It also provides that the 90-day time limit to commence an action to enforce a lien does not apply if there was an agreement to extend credit and a notice of that fact was recorded within 90 days after recordation of the claim of lien or more than 90 days after recordation of the claim of lien but before a purchaser or encumbrancer for value and in good faith acquires rights in the property.

A property owner may not petition for a release order until he or she gives the claimant notice demanding that the claimant execute and record a release of lien claim at least ten days before filing the petition. (Civ. Code § 8482.) A petition for a release order must be verified and include all of the following: (1) the date of recordation of the claim of lien, with a certified copy of the lien attached; (2) the county in which the claim of lien is recorded; (3) the book and page or series number of the place in the official records where the claim of lien is recorded; (4) the legal description of the property subject to the claim; (5) whether an extension of credit has been granted and if so to what date and that the time for commencement of an action to enforce the lien has expired; (6) that the owner has given the claimant notice under section 8482 demanding that the claimant execute and record a release of the lien and that the claimant is unable or unwilling to do so or cannot with reasonable diligence be found; (7) whether an action to enforce the lien is pending; and (8) whether the owner of the property or interest in the property has filed for relief in bankruptcy or there is another restraint that prevents the claimant from commencing an action to enforce the lien. (Civ. Code § 8484.)

A lis pendens is an instrument recorded in the office of the county recorder that gives constructive notice of a pending lawsuit affecting title to the real property described in the lis pendens.  (Gale v. Superior Court (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 1388, 1395.)  The pleadings in the lawsuit to which the lis pendens refers must describe a specific parcel of real property and must state a “real property claim.”  (Ibid.; Code of Civil Procedure § 405.1[1].)  For purposes of expunging a lis pendens, a real property claim is a cause of action that, if successful, would affect title to or the right of possession of specific real property or the use of an easement identified in a pleading.  (§§ 405.2, 405.4.) 

Where a motion to expunge a lis pendens has been filed, the proponent of the lis pendens carries the burden of showing evidence that the lis pendens is supported by a real property claim which is probably valid.  (§§ 405.30, 405.32; Hunting World, Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 22 Cal.App.4th 67, 70-71 (Hunting World).)  Where a party does not establish the probable validity of their claim underpinning a lis pendens by a preponderance of the evidence, the lis pendens shall be expunged.  (§ 405.32.)   

III.       DISCUSSION

A.         Service

At the previous hearing on the motions, the Court continued the hearing due to a lack of proof of service of the Cross-Complaint on Cross-Defendant Balam Arturo Cardenas Zotelo dba New Concepts Drywall (“NC”). As of August 28, 2026, Campus filed proof of service on NC for the Cross-Complaint, resolving service issues.  

B.          Motion to Expunge Mechanic’s Lien

Campus requests the Court expunge NC’s mechanic’s lien filed on November 15, 2023, as Instrument No. 20230789692 in the Official Records of Los Angeles County against the subject property. Campus also requests an award of attorney’s fees and in the amount of $2,992.80 for bringing this motion.

However, Campus only requests the Court expunge the mechanic’s lien under sections 405.31 and 405.32, both of which only grant the Court the right to expunge a lis pendens. These sections do not apply to mechanic’s liens. Campus has provided no legal basis for the Court to expunge the subject lien. Therefore, the Court denies the motion and attorney fees as to the mechanic’s lien.

C.          Motion to Expunge Lis Pendens

Campus requests the Court expunge SoCal’s Notice of Pendency of Action [lis pendens] filed on March 8, 2024, in this case, against the subject property. Campus also requests an award of attorney’s fees and in the amount of $2,992.80 for bringing this motion. “A court shall order a notice of lis pendens expunged if it determines (1) that the pleading on which the notice is based does not contain a real property claim (§ 405.31); (2) that the claimant has not established, by a preponderance of the evidence, the probable validity of a real property claim (§ 405.32); or (3) that adequate relief can be secured by an undertaking. (§ 405.33.).” (Shoker v. Superior Court of Alameda County (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 271, 277.)

Campus asserts SoCal cannot maintain a lis pendens because SoCal dismissed their complaint, which was the only pleading that supported a real property claim in regard to the subject property. The pleadings in the lawsuit to which the lis pendens refer must describe a specific parcel of real property and must state a “real property claim.” (§ 405.1.) For purposes of expunging a lis pendens, a real property claim is a cause of action that, if successful, would affect title to or the right of possession of specific real property or the use of an easement identified in a pleading.  (§§ 405.2, 405.4.) To determine whether the complaint involves a real property claim, the court considers the allegations of the complaint. (Urez Corp. v. Superior Court (1987) 190 Cal.App.3d 1141, 1149.) [“The issue is simply whether the action as pleaded is one that affects title or possession of the subject property.”] 

Here, it is undisputed only the underlying complaint concerned a real property claim. Campus’s Cross-Complaint only has causes of action for breach of contract, specifically alleging NC failed to complete work under subcontract as outlined by contract. Although the relevant contract relates to work performed on the subject property, it does not affect title or possession of the subject property. Therefore, only SoCal’s complaint is potentially relevant to the lis pendens claim. SoCal’s complaint has been dismissed. Therefore, there are no currently pending pleadings that state a “real property claim” against the subject property; notice is proper for expungement pursuant to section 405.31. It is also proper for expungement under section 405.32, as SoCal has not established, by preponderance of the evidence, the probable validity of the real property claim as SoCal has dismissed its claims. Therefore, the Court grants the motion.

D.         Attorney Fees

The Court “shall direct that the party prevailing on any motion under this chapter be awarded the reasonable attorney’s fees and costs of making or opposing the motion unless the court finds that the other party acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of attorney’s fees and costs unjust.” (§ 405.38.)

Given the fact no party opposes this motion, the Court does not find substantial justification in SoCal maintaining the lis pendens following the request for dismissal, as, at that point, SoCal was aware they had no viable property claims. Therefore, the Court awards attorney fees.

Campus requests attorney’s fees totaling $2,992.80, based upon $2,730.00 in attorney fees and $262.80 in filing costs. The Court first notes, outside of identifying 1 hour will be spent at the hearing on the motion, Campus did not provide information as to time spent on the motion; it is unclear if the allotted time is based upon attorney time or paralegal time. Absent this information, the Court finds the total requested attorney’s fees unwarranted. (In re Marriage of Huntington (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1513, 1524 [“When the trial court is informed of the extent and nature of the legal services rendered, it may rely on its own experience and knowledge in determining their reasonable value.”].) The Court also finds the motion filing fees, nearly $200.00 over the usual request for filing fees, to be unsupported by the evidence. Instead, the Court imposes sanctions of $960.00, based upon 3 hours of attorney’s work at a rate of $300.00 per hour and 1 $60.00 filing fee.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, Campus’s motion to expunge NC’s mechanic’s lien is DENIED.

Campus’s motion to expunge lis pendens is GRANTED.

SoCal may not file another lis pendens regarding the subject property without first seeking leave of court.

SoCal and counsel of record are ordered to pay attorney’s fees in the amount of $960.00 within 30 calendar days of this order.

Moving Party is ordered to give notice.

GAGE DEVELOPMENTS I LLC, et al.

Plaintiffs,

vs.

BHS, INC., et al.,

Defendants.

Case No.:

24STCV05248

Hearing Date:

September 14, 2026

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING, IN PART, AND DENYING, IN PART, MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Gage Developments I, LLC (“Gage I”), Gage Developments II, LLC (“Gage II”), and Gage Developments III, LLC (“Gage III”)’s predecessor-in-interest entered into a contract with Defendants BHS, Inc. (“BHS”) and Kidder Matthews of California, Inc. (“KM”) for purchase of the subject property, prior to the rights to the property being assigned to Plaintiffs. Allegedly, even after singing of the contract, Defendants allowed water damage to incur to the property and hid a locked room full of chemical fumes to attempt to obtain a higher price from other buyers. These issues were allegedly concealed, breaking the terms of the purchase contract. Plaintiffs sue Defendants for breach of written contract, specific performance, fraud and deceit, negligent misrepresentation, and unfair business practices.

On June 22, 2026, BHS filed the instant motion for summary judgment, or, in the alternative, summary adjudication. Plaintiffs filed an opposition. BHS filed a reply.

II.         OBJECTIONS

California Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (q) provides: “[i]n granting or denying a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication, the court need rule only on those objections to evidence that it deems material to its disposition of the motion. Objections to evidence that are not ruled on for purposes of the motion shall be preserved for appellate review.”

A.    BHS's Objections

All objections other than those articulated below are overruled. The Court rules on BHS's objections to Plaintiffs' evidence as follows:

1.     Neman Decl. ¶ 6 is SUSTAINED as to lack of foundation and personal knowledge.

2.     Neman Decl. ¶ 8 is SUSTAINED as to lack of foundation.

3.     Neman Decl. ¶ 12 is SUSTAINED as to lack of foundation and personal knowledge.

4.     Neman Decl. ¶ 14 is SUSTAINED as conflicting with previous testimony, lack of personal knowledge.

5.     Neman Decl. ¶ 15 is SUSTAINED as irrelevant.

6.     Neman Decl. ¶ 16, “This conducted indicated to me that Sellers had intentionally concealed the Concealed Room during the initial site visits.” is SUSTAINED as argumentative.

7.     Vivoli Decl. ¶ 11, “Following the site visit, and personally resigned to the notion that Plaintiffs had, in fact, been misled” is SUSTAINED as argumentative.

 

III.       LEGAL STANDARD

A party may move for summary judgment “if it is contended that the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (a)[1].) “[I]f all the evidence submitted, and all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law,” the moving party will be entitled to summary judgment. (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

The moving party bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact, and if he does so, the burden shifts to the opposing party to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850, citing to § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence.  (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

Neither a moving nor responding party may rely on the mere allegations or denials of its pleadings. A moving party must submit specific admissible evidence showing that the responding party cannot establish at least one element of his, her or its cause of action or defense. The responding party, to defeat the motion, must submit specific admissible evidence showing that a triable issue of material fact does exist as to that element of the cause of action or defense. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

The “Golden Rule” on a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication is that “if [a fact] is not set forth in the separate statement, it does not exist.” (Zimmerman, Rosenfeld, Gersh & Leeds LLP v. Larson (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 1466, 1477, citing United Community Church v. Garcin (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 327, 337.).

 

IV.        DISCUSSION

BHS requests the Court grant summary judgment as to the entire complaint. BHS requests the Court grant summary judgment as to the first cause of action for breach of contract, as there is no evidence of breach by BHS. BHS requests the Court grant summary judgment as to the second cause of action for specific performance as Plaintiffs were not ready, willing, or able to perform. Judgment is requested as to the third cause of action for fraud and deceit as Plaintiffs do not have sufficient evidence to demonstrate actual knowledge, intent, justifiable reliance, or damages. BHS requests the Court grant judgement as to the cause of action for negligent misrepresentation as Plaintiffs cannot identify any actionable misrepresentation. BHS finally requests the Court grant summary judgment as to the cause of action for unfair business practices because it is derivative of Plaintiffs’ other failed claims. In order to prevail on a motion for summary judgment, the moving party must demonstrate there exists no triable issues of material fact; this is established by showing one or more elements of a cause of action cannot be established or by proving there is a complete defense to the cause of action. (§ 437c, subd. (p)(2).) 

A.         Statement of Facts

On December 9, 2021, BHS and Cross-Defendant NewGen Capital, LLC (“NewGen”), Plaintiffs’ predecessor, entered into a written Purchase and Sale Agreement (“PSA”) for purchase of the property at 7222 Gage Avenue, Commerce, California 90040 (“subject property”). (UMF 2.) On or about September 20, 2022, NewGen assigned its interests in the PSA to Plaintiffs. (UMF 4.)

The PSA stated that any environmental reports, surveys, or similar documents provided by BHS to the buyer were delivered without any representation or warranty as to sufficiency, accuracy, completeness, and/or validity of said documents; the buyer was advised to retain their own consultants to review the documents and investigate the property. (UMF 14-16.) Any duty to disclose was limited to matters within BHS’s actual knowledge. (UMF 18.) The PSA also stated the buyer was purchasing the property in existing condition. (UMF 16.) The maintenance clause limited BHS’s obligation to maintaining the property in present condition. (UMF 17.) In the event of post-agreement damage or loss, the buyer either could terminate the agreement or proceed with the purchase without adjustment to purchase price. (Ibid.)

The PSA also required the buyer to deposit all remaining funds to close escrow/show proof of funds no later than 2:00 p.m. on the business day immediately preceding the scheduled closing date. (UMF 20.) If BHS was in default or indicated it would not perform, the buyer was not obligated to deposit funds and instead may show evidence of available funds to preserve the buyer’s rights. (UMF 21.) Pursuant to contract, the buyer wired the first non-refundable deposit of $500,000.00 on December 16, 2021. (UMF 5.)

BHS provided disclosures to the buyer on December 10, 2021. (UMF 22.) The Property Information Sheet expressly disclosed the use of paint on the premises as a stored hazardous substance. (UMF 22.) On December 21, 2021, BHS provided the buyer with a copy of BHS’s Phase I environmental Site Assessment Report, which noted “wood staining and painting” were on-site operations. (UMF 23.) During the contingency period, the buyer investigated the property by retaining a contractor and environmental consultant to conduct a physical inspection and Phase I environmental site assessment on January 6, 2022. (UMF 25.) The contractor’s report identified roof conditions, including water damage and cracks, that warranted roof replacement. (UMF 27-28.) The environmental site assessment report identified the presence of drums of paint and spray paint booths but did not expect they posed any environmental concern. (UMF 29-30.) Neither report alleged there were any concealed or inaccessible areas. (UMF 34.)

Following these assessments, the buyer sought to renegotiate purchase price, requesting a reduction of $500,000.00 based on the roof conditions. (UMF 7.) BHS declined to modify the price; the buyer elected to proceed and removed all contingencies on January 24, 2022. (UMF 8-9.) The buyer’s representative deposited an additional non-refundable $500,000.00 into escrow. (UMF 9.)

From November 2021 to December 2023, Plaintiffs conducted at least five walkthroughs of the subject property; during that time, Plaintiffs never objected that any area was concealed, restricted, or inaccessible. (UMF 34.) On December 20, 2023, Plaintiffs attempted to commence a second environmental site assessment without notice, almost two years after the buyer had waived all contractual contingencies. (UMF 9, 32, 37.)

Pursuant to Plaintiffs’ request, the closing date was set for January 18, 2024. (UMF 10.) Therefore, Plaintiffs were required to demonstrate the ability to close escrow no later than 2:00 p.m. on January 17, 2024. (UMF 20-21.) Plaintiffs did not deposit the purchase price or provide proof of funds to BHS on this date. (UMF 12.) BHS subsequently issued a five-business-day notice to close escrow on March 5, 2024, pursuant to PSA. (UMF 11.) Plaintiffs did not cure nonperformance within the notice period. (UMF 12.) Therefore, the PSA was terminated on March 12, 2024. (UMF 13.)

B.          Lack of Proper Opposition Separate Statement

The Court cannot weigh any evidence presented by Plaintiffs outside of the scope of dispute as Plaintiffs have failed to present a proper separate statement in support of Plaintiffs’ offered evidence.

California Rules of Court rule 3.1350, subdivision (e), provides that an opposition motion for summary judgment or adjudication must include a separate statement in opposition to the motion. California Rules of Court rule 3.1350 subdivision (f)(3) requires that is an opposing party contends additional material facts are pertinent to the motion, these facts must be set forth in a separate statement, complete with evidentiary cites.

            Plaintiffs’ separate statement is in violation of California Rules of Court rule 3.1350, subdivision (f), as it does not provide a separate statement outlining any of the additional material facts allegedly pertinent to the motion. Throughout Plaintiffs’ opposing papers, Plaintiffs cite directly to declarations that are not affirmatively included in Plaintiffs’ separate statement, only as vague ‘disputes’ to BHS’s own separate statement. For example, Plaintiffs relied in part upon a visit to the property by Michael Vivoli (“Vivoli”), Plaintiffs’ attorney, who allegedly discovered a concealed room: “Vivoli, then counsel for Plaintiffs, personally observed at a joint site visit attended by BHS's counsel and Robert An that the door to that area was obstructed from view and locked; that there was no outward indication of any separate space behind it; that he could not open it; and that no paint or industrial odor was detectable from close range until the doors were opened. (Vivoli Decl. ¶ 10.)” (Opp., p. 4 ¶¶ 20-24.) Although Vivoli Decl. ¶ 10 is cited multiple times within the disputed portion of the separate statement, nowhere within those sections is there any mention of a lack of paint or odor until it was opened. Plaintiffs failed to provide a separate statement that accurately contains all facts alleged.

“The purpose of the law of summary judgment is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute. [Citation.]”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) To that end, the rules dictating the content and format for separate statements submitted by moving and responding parties “permit trial courts to expeditiously review complex motions for . . . summary judgment to determine quickly and efficiently whether material facts are disputed.” (United Community Church v. Garcin (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 327, 335.)  “The realization of this goal is so important that the Legislature has determined ‘[f]ailure to comply with this requirement of a separate statement may constitute a sufficient ground, in the court’s discretion, for granting the motion.’ (§ 437c, subd. (3).)”  (Collins v. Hertz Corp. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 64, 73 [citing to former numbering of §437c, subd. (b)(1); see §437, subdivision (b)(1) “The failure to comply with this requirement of a separate statement may in the court’s discretion constitute a sufficient ground for denying the motion.”].)

Therefore, the Court only evaluates evidence explicitly offered in the separate statement in dispute of BHS’s proffered undisputed material facts.

C.         First and Second Causes of Action – Breach of Written Contract / Second Cause of Action – Specific Performance

BHS argues Plaintiffs’ cause of action for breach of contract fails, as a matter of law, as BHS performed the terms of the contract. “To prevail on a cause of action for breach of contract, the plaintiff must prove (1) the contract, (2) the plaintiff’s performance of the contract or excuse for nonperformance, (3) the defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damage to the plaintiff.” (Richman v. Hartley (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1182, 1186.)

BHS argues that both the alleged roof condition and hazardous paint condition were pre-existing and fully disclosed. The roof condition was preexisting, not the result of failure to maintain the property in its present condition. Similarly, the paint condition existed at the time of contract formation. The evidence shows the buyer was on notice of both the roof condition and paint condition at the latest by January 7, 2022. BHS explicitly disclosed the existence of paint and wood-staining substances, later confirmed by the buyer’s own investigators. Substantial roof damage was noted and accepted by the buyer after BHS refused to alter the terms of the contract. BHS argues any later identified roof conditions are ‘untenable’ as proof of BHS’s failure to maintain, as a full roof replacement was already required.

Plaintiffs do not dispute Plaintiffs’ contractors identified the roof conditions and recommended replacement. However, Plaintiffs argue that this was only indicative of the state of the roof at the time, and not at close of escrow. Plaintiffs allegedly relied upon statements by BHS’s agent that BHS had regularly maintained the roof and that the roof did not leak; it is unclear when these statements were made. This is irrelevant for purposes of determining breach of written contract, as it is uncontested Plaintiffs were allowed to perform their own investigation and accepted the terms of the agreement again after this investigation.

Plaintiffs also do not dispute that information regarding paint and paint-staining was made available to them by both BHS’s report and Plaintiffs’ own investigation. Plaintiffs only assert that the investigation report was “completed without the benefit of inspecting the door to the area adjacent to the cabinet shop that was locked and inaccessible to Plaintiffs.” (Opp., Neman Decl. ¶ 6.) However, Neman provided no explanation as to how he was aware the investigation did not include the allegedly ‘concealed’ area. Neman did not cite the report or conduct the investigation himself; he is unable to attest to the breadth of the report. There is no evidence that establishes Plaintiffs were prevented from completing a full investigation prior to acceptance of the contract, in full.

Furthermore, BHS argues that Plaintiffs, not BHS, failed to perform contractual obligations, as Plaintiffs failed to deposit all required funds or demonstrate it had funds available to preserve its rights, as required by contract. Plaintiffs also do not dispute this fact.

Based upon admissible evidence, the Court does not find any dispute of material fact as to the cause of action for breach of written contract. All evidence demonstrates BHS disclosed known information and allowed Plaintiffs to conduct thorough investigations prior to complete acceptance of the contract. Plaintiffs understood the property was being offered as-is and agreed to continue with the purchase, even after these investigations. There is no evidence of additional damage accruing throughout the escrow period that would violate contract. Therefore, the Court grants summary judgment on this cause of action.

Similarly, this argument applies also to the cause of action for specific performance. Specific performance is an equitable remedy only available for a party who has fully performed all conditions precedent, has only partially failed to perform and said failure is immaterial or capable of being fully compensated, or can otherwise show it was ready, willing, and able to perform its contractual obligations. (Civ. Code § 3392; Behniwal v. Mix (2005) 133 Cal.App.4th 1027, 1044–1045.) As Plaintiffs failed to make the required payment or otherwise demonstrate it was capable of making the required payment, Plaintiffs did not satisfy the requirements for specific performance. The Court grants summary judgment as to this cause of action.

D.         Third Cause of Action – Fraud / Fourth Cause of Action – Negligent Misrepresentation

BHS first argues that Plaintiffs’ fraud claim improperly repackages Plaintiffs’ contractual claim as a fraud claim, despite there being no evidence of independent duty or intentional misrepresentation independent from contract. “When evaluating whether the parties' expectations and risk allocations [in contract] bar tort recovery, the court must consider the alleged facts.  First, applying standard contract principles, it must ascertain the full scope of the parties' contractual agreement, including the rights created or reserved, the obligations assumed or declined, and the provided remedies for breach.  Second, it must determine whether there is an independent tort duty to refrain from the alleged conduct.  Third, if an independent duty exists, the court must consider whether the plaintiff can establish all elements of the tort independently of the rights and duties assumed by the parties under the contract.”  (Rattagan v. Uber Techs., Inc., (2024) 17 Cal. 5th 1, 26.)

BHS argues that all alleged misrepresentation and failure to disclose stem from the requirements of the contract to disclose. “California decisions ... have long recognized that a wrongful act committed in the course of a contractual relationship may afford both tort and contractual relief, and in such circumstances the existence of the contractual relationship will not bar the injured party from pursuing redress in tort.”  (Rattagan, 17 Cal. 5th at p. 24, quoting Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 174-175.)  “[T]he public policy of ensuring certainty in contract relations must give way when a party engages in affirmative misrepresentation fraud during contractual performance because ‘[n]o rational party would enter into a contract anticipating that they are or will be lied to.’ ”  (Rattagan, 17 Cal.5th at p. 42, quoting Robinson Helicopter Co. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 993.) As BHS allegedly intentionally misrepresented the conditions of the property during contractual performance, exceeding the bounds of contract, the Court must evaluate whether Plaintiffs have a viable intentional misrepresentation claim.

“The elements of fraud that will give rise to a tort action for deceit are: “ ‘(a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.’ ” (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 974.)

Here, the evidence does not show any misrepresentation, as BHS timely disclosed the existence of paint and wood-staining material on the property. The evidence also does not show any intent to defraud, as there is no evidence demonstrating BHS knew of any undisclosed condition. Finally, the evidence does not show Plaintiffs justifiably relied upon BHS’s alleged misrepresentation. The buyer conducted their own investigations which confirmed the existence of paint and roof damage; The buyer discussed these issues with BHS and requested a lower price. When BHS refused and stated the buyer could abandon the contract, the buyer opted to proceed with the contract. In total, the evidence indicates Plaintiffs cannot present a viable cause of action for fraud. Therefore, the Court grants summary judgment as to this cause of action.

Similarly, the cause of action for negligent misrepresentation also fails as there is no evidence of any misrepresentation or justifiable reliance. “Negligent misrepresentation requires an assertion of fact, falsity of that assertion, and the tortfeasor’s lack of reasonable grounds for believing the assertion to be true. It also requires the tortfeasor’s intent to induce reliance, justifiable reliance by the person to whom the false assertion of fact was made, and damages to that person. An implied assertion of fact is ‘not enough’ to support liability.” (SI 59 LLC v. Variel Warner Ventures, LLC (2018) 29 Cal.App.5th 146, 154.) As there is no evidence of misrepresentation or justifiable reliance, the Court also grants summary judgment as to this claim.

E.          Fifth Cause of Action - Unfair Business Practices

BHS next argues that Plaintiffs’ cause of action for violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200, et seq. (“UCL”), is subject to summary judgment as it is entirely derivate of other claims, does not cite any specific underlying statutory violation, and does not identify any specific recoverable restitution or unjust enrichment. The UCL identifies unfair competition as any unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business act or practice. (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200.) Each category represents a separate and distinct basis for liability. (Id.; Beaver v. Tarsadia Hotels (9th Cir. 2016) 816 F.3d 1170, 1177.)

BHS argues that Plaintiffs’ UCL claim is entirely derivative of the other causes of action and therefore fails as a matter of law. While it is true that if the predicate statutory claims fail as a matter of law, the UCL claim may also, that is only if that is the sole alleged basis of the UCL claim. (Aleksick v. 7-Eleven, Inc. (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 1176, 1185.) Here, the complaint also argues that the business practices were unfair and fraudulent, not simply unlawful. (SAC ¶¶ 46-47.) BHS offers no analysis tailored to the unfair or fraudulent prongs of the claim, therefore failing BHS’s burden to demonstrate that Plaintiffs’ UCL claims are barred by law.

BHS also argues that Plaintiffs’ complaint failed to allege any particular benefit which BHS supposedly acquired that must be returned. However, failure to state these allegations in the complaint is not a sufficient basis for the Court to grant summary judgment. BHS has not demonstrated that Plaintiffs are incapable of presenting evidence upon which restitution can be derived, making Plaintiffs’ claims barred as a matter of law.

Therefore, the Court denies summary judgment as to this cause of action.

V.         CONCLUSION

Defendants BHS, Inc.’s motion for summary judgment is GRANTED as to the first four cases of action and denied as to the last.

BHS is to give notice.

ELENA M. ROSCHDI, et al.,

Plaintiff,

vs.

AGAM PROPERTIES, INC., et al.,

Defendant.

Case No.:

24STCV27912

Hearing Date:

September 14, 2026

ORDER GRANTING MOTION FOR ORDER PERMITTING DISCOVERY OF DEFENDANTS’ FINANCIAL CONDITION

 

I.           BACKGROUND

During Plaintiffs Elena M. Roschdi (“Elena”), Nasser Roschdi (“Nasser”), Sara Raquel Roschdi (“Sara”), and Sanam Rebecca Roschdi’s (“Sanam”) tenancy with landlord Defendants Agam Properties, Inc. (“Agam”) and Pacifica Mortgage Fund, LLC (“PMF”), Defendants allegedly failed to make requested repairs, violating Plaintiffs’ implied warranty of habitability. Plaintiffs sued Defendants for seven causes of action, including tortious breach of warranty of habitability.

On May 21, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion for order permitting discovery of Defendants’ financial condition. Defendants filed an opposition. Plaintiffs filed a reply.

II.         Objections

A.         Defenants’ Objections

All of Defendants’ objections are OVERRULED, unless otherwise indicated below:

1.          The objection to Plaintiff’s Exhibit 6 is SUSTAINED as irrelevant.

2.          The objection to Plaintiff’s Exhibit 7 is SUSTAINED as irrelevant.

3.          The objection to Plaintiff’s Exhibit 8 is SUSTAINED as hearsay, and lack of authentication.

B.          Plaintiffs’ Objections

All of Plaintiffs’ objections are SUSTAINED on the basis of lack of personal knowledge and foundation.

 

 

III.       LEGAL STANDARD

A plaintiff may not conduct pretrial discovery into a defendant’s financial condition absent a court order permitting such discovery. (Civ. Code § 3295, subd. (c.)[1].) In making the order, the court must find, on the basis of the supporting and opposing affidavits presented, that the plaintiff has established a “substantial probability” of prevailing on a claim for punitive damages. (Ibid.) A court must weigh the evidence submitted in favor of and in opposition to the motion and find that it is “very likely” that the plaintiff will prevail on his or her claim for punitive damages. (Jabro v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 754, 758.) It is not sufficient for a plaintiff to simply demonstrate a prima facie showing that they have a reasonable probability of prevailing sufficient to defeat summary judgment.  (Ibid.) 

 

IV.        DISCUSSION

Plaintiffs request the Court issue an order, pursuant to section 3295, allowing Plaintiffs to conduct discovery into Defendant’s financial condition. The Court may only enter such an order upon a showing that Plaintiffs have a substantial probability of prevailing on their section 3294 punitive damages claims via noticed motion and affidavits. (§ 3295, subd. (c).)

Plaintiffs’ complaint alleges Plaintiffs are entitled to punitive damages as Defendants oppressively and/or fraudulently refused to repair, maintain, and exterminate Plaintiffs’ leased home, despite multiple requests by Plaintiffs, on the basis that the rental agreement contained an illegal ‘as-is’ clause making Plaintiffs responsible for all repairs. (Complaint ¶¶ 9, 38, 32, Ex. B.) Plaintiffs specifically alleged that, during their tenancy, the subject property experienced a cockroach infestation, rodent infestation, widespread water leaks and chronic mold, a lack of functional heaters, and physical defects, all of which Defendants failed to fix. (Complaint ¶ 10.)

All residential leases contain an implied warranty of habitability requiring that the landlord maintain and fix the property to comply with statutory and regulatory requirements, regardless of other provisions of the lease agreement. (Green v. Superior Court (1974, 10 Cal.3d 616, 629.) Case law holds punitive damages are authorized for tortious breach of the warranty of habitability, one of Plaintiffs’ articulated causes of action, so long as the plaintiff meets the threshold for establishing punitive damages. (See Smith v. David (1981) 120 Cal.App.3d 101, fn 3.) Punitive damages may only be imposed upon a showing a defendant is guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. (§ 3294, subd. (a).)

In support of their claims, Plaintiffs provided affidavits and evidence demonstrating that Defendants knowingly relied upon the lease contract’s illegal ‘as-is’ clause to avoid making requested repairs pursuant to their obligations under the warranty of habitability. According to Elena, issues arose as early as 2013 and ranged from inoperable appliances to sewage overflows to vermin/mold infestations. (Mot., Elena Decl. ¶ 4.) Elena provided a 2016 e-mail between Elena and Defendants’ agent, Scott Radin (“Radin”), in which Elena notified Defendants that Plaintiffs lacked hot water, violating section 1941.1, subdivision (3); Defendants stated Plaintiffs were responsible for all repairs and maintenance. (Id. at ¶ 5, Ex. 2.) Sara contacted Radin via e-mail in August 2024, identifying plumbing issues, mold growth, and rainwater seepage (violating Health and Safety Code § 17920.3, subd. (a)); although Radin initially expressed interest in reviewing the issues, Radin later responded that Defendants would not make any repairs to the home unless Plaintiffs agreed to a new rental rate (approximately $1,000.00 more per month), relying upon the ‘as-is’ clause of the initial lease. (Mot., Sara Decl. ¶¶ 3-5, Ex. 4-5.) At bare minimum, Plaintiffs demonstrated that as early as 2016, Defendants refused to perform requested and necessary repairs by relying upon an illegal contract provision contrary to public policy.

Defendants rely upon the existence of the ‘as-is’ provision and argues that, at best, this reflects a dispute as to the terms of the contract. Defendants also argue that Plaintiffs testified at their deposition that the initial lease was only intended to apply for a short term while Plaintiffs looked for alternate, more affordable housing. However, neither of these arguments address the fact a residential lease agreement that waives or modifies the implied warranty of habitability is void as a matter of public policy, unless the provision agrees that the tenant takes on the obligation to repair in consideration for a reduced rental rate. (§ 1942.1.) Nothing within the agreement specifies Plaintiffs were paying a reduced rate in return for undertaking repair responsibilities. (Mot., Elena Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. 1.) Defendants have provided no evidence that otherwise demonstrates this obligation came with a reduced rental rate. Therefore, the evidence shows any reliance upon the ‘as-is’ provision was without merit; failure to repair when provided with notice while relying upon void portions of a contract is substantially likely to establish Defendants were acting oppressively and/or fraudulently, providing a basis for punitive damages.

The Court finds Plaintiffs have sufficiently established a substantial probability of prevailing on a claim for punitive damages as to tortious breach of warranty of habitability. Defendants request that any order permitting discovery into financial condition be narrowly tailored to information reasonably necessary for purposes of punitive damages. Section 3295 already outlines the limits of financial discovery: profits Defendants gained by virtue of wrongful course of conduct and the financial condition of the defendant. (§ 3295, subd. (a)(1-2).) The Court adopts the statutory limits of discovery.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Plaintiffs’ motion for order permitting financial discovery is GRANTED. Plaintiffs are entitled to serve discovery concerning section 3295, subdivision (a)(1-2).

Plaintiffs are ordered to give notice.

ORDER DEEMING DEFENDANTS' DEMURRER MOOT

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Okwii Agbo (“Agbo”), while represented by Defendants Chad Biggins (“Biggins”) and Bigging Law, Inc. (“BL”), was unsuccessfully sued by a non-party and awarded $18,000.00 in costs and fees. Defendants placed a lien on non-party's home to obtain the award, but allegedly never informed Agbo or distributed the amount to Agbo. Agbo sued Defendants for six causes of action, including breach of fiduciary duty.

On June 18, 2026, Defendants filed the instant amended demurrer.

On July 23, 2026, Agbo filed a proposed amended complaint.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10[1] states: “The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) The person who filed the pleading does not have the legal capacity to sue; (c) There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) There is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) The pleading is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, uncertain includes ambiguous and unintelligible; and (g) In an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct.”

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.  (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.”  (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.)  As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations.  (Ibid.)  The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.”  (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

Pursuant to section 472, a party may amend its pleading once without leave of court. If the amendment is filed after a demurrer or motion to strike is filed, it must be filed and served no later than the date for filing an opposition to the demurrer or motion to strike. (Ibid.)

III.       DISCUSSION

Pursuant to section 472, a party may amend its pleading once, without leave of court, following the filing of a demurrer or motion to strike, so long as it is filed and served by the date for filing an opposition to those pleadings. The date for filing an opposition to demurrer is nine court days prior to the hearing on the pleadings. (§ 1005, subd. (b).)

Agbo’s initial complaint was filed on March 25, 2026. On May 28, 2026, and June 12, 2026, Defendants filed special motions to strike (or “anti-SLAPP motion”). Both motions were heard and denied on July 15, 2026.

Defendants also filed a demurrer on June 11, 2026, amending the demurrer on June 18, 2026.  Upon filing of a demurrer, Agbo was entitled to file one amended complaint pursuant to the timeline outlined in section 472, so long as an anti-SLAPP motion was not pending. As this demurrer was always set to be heard on September 14, 2026, the amended complaint needed to be filed by August 31, 2026, to be deemed operative without leave of court. Agbo filed the FAC on July 23, 2026, far before the deadline, and after the anti-SLAPP motions were resolved.

As Agbo’s FAC was filed and served prior to the opposition due date, it is the new operative complaint. Defendants’ demurrer to the original complaint is MOOT.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Defendants’ demurrer to the initial complaint is MOOT.

Agbo is to give notice.
