I.        MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT/ADJUDICATION

 

Defendants move for summary judgment, or alternatively, summary adjudication of the four causes of action remaining in this litigation on the premise that Plaintiff cannot demonstrate that she is a shareholder of Houston Salem. (Mot., at p. 9.)

 

In the FAC, Plaintiff repeatedly alleges that she was a minority shareholder and/or owner of Housten Salem d.b.a. Saltwater Luxe pursuant to the LOI and the APA. (FAC, ¶¶ 16, 20, 43, 85, 88-89, 127, 130-131, 174.) It is Defendants’ position in this motion that Plaintiff was not a shareholder of Houston Salem, and thus, Defendants did not have a fiduciary relationship with Plaintiff and Plaintiff did not have the right to inspect Houston Salem’s corporate records. (Mot., at pp. 10-12; Rep., at pp. 2-3.) Defendants support this factual premise by relying on their interpretation of the terms of the LOI, the APA, and the board/shareholder consents, as well as the fact that Plaintiff does not appear on the stock ledger produced in this action by Houston Salem. (Kayne Decl., ¶¶ 4-8; Exhs. A, B, C, D, E.)

 

The contents of the operative transaction documents are undisputed. The LOI contemplates the Buyer’s purchase of “all or substantially all of the assets of Seller” for a purchase price of one dollar, as well as the “Participation Right” and the “Commissions” as outlined in the LOI. (Def. Exh. A, § 1.) The LOI describes the “Participation Right” as Seller’s 20% participation interest in any disposition of the Saltwater Luxe brand. (Id. § 4.) The “Participation Right” also entitles Seller to receive 20% of the “net purchase price” paid for the Saltwater Luxe brand in the event that the brand is sold by Buyer. (Ibid.) The APA has largely consistent terms. (Def. Exh. B, §§ 2.1, 2.3.) The APA confirms that the sale transaction consistent of an asset transfer from Seller to Buyer, including Saltwater Luxe’s intellectual property, inventory, equipment, books and records, vendor and customer information, goodwill, and “all other properties, rights, titles, interests and other assets owned, licensed or leased by Seller as of the Closing, or in which Seller has an interest, which are related to or used or useful in the Business and not otherwise Excluded Assets.” (Id. § 2.1(a).) The Seller’s “corporate character” and the “maintenance and existence of Seller as a corporation” was among the Excluded Assets. (Id. § 2.1(b).) By its plain language, the APA transferred the Saltwater Luxe “brand” to Houston Salem while preserving the corporate existence of the Saltwater Luxe company. With respect to the consideration received by Seller for the asset transfer, the “Commission Payments” in the APA indicate that Buyer would pay Commissions to Plaintiff based on revenues received by the Buyer from sales of Saltwater Luxe Brand goods, so long as Plaintiff is employed on a full-time basis by Buyer. (Id. § 2.4(b)(i).) The “Participation Right” in the APA also grants Seller a 20% “participation interest” in “any disposition of the [Saltwater Luxe] Brand.” (Id. § 2.5.) This provision is limited in scope: there is no language purporting to give Seller a participation interest in the profits of all commercial activities associated with the Saltwater Luxe Brand— only a 20% interest in the net purchase price of any “disposition” (i.e. the transfer or disposal) of the Brand, whether by a sale of the Brand itself or the sale of the Buyer. (Ibid.) There is no ambiguity in this language.

 

Defendants argue that the Participation Right is not an ownership interest in the Houston Salem corporation itself, only a contractual right to share in the proceeds of the Saltwater Luxe brand. (Mot., at p. 12; Rep., at p. 4.) Plaintiff does not point to any provisions of the LOI or the APA which expressly contemplate the transfer of ownership or stock in either Saltwater Luxe (the company) or Houston Salem. (Def. Exhs. A, B.) In fact, Plaintiff appears to concede that Defendants’ shareholder records, as currently available to Plaintiff, negate Plaintiff’s status as a record-shareholder. (Opp., at p. 12:3; see Corp. Code, § 185 [“‘Shareholder’ means one who is a holder of record of shares.”]; see also Corp. Code, § 701, subd. (d).) Instead, Plaintiff argues that she is entitled to shareholder rights and privileges under a theory of equitable ownership based on her participation interest under the APA, the parties’ continuing relationship, and Kayne’s written acknowledgement that Plaintiff would “maintain [her] 10%” interest in the Saltwater Luxe brand. (Id. at p. 12; Bischofberger Decl., ¶¶ 6, 28-30; Khatib Decl., ¶ 15; Exh. 1.)

 

Even so, Plaintiff fails to submit any legal authority to support her position that a mere contractual participation interest in the disposition of a limited asset either creates a fiduciary relationship or confers equitable shareholder status in the entire corporation to the interest holder. California courts have rejected the premise that fiduciary duties are owed to individuals with a mere “expectancy interest” in a corporation through unexercised stock options. (Daly v. Yessne (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 52, 63 (Daly).) Plaintiff refutes the applicability of Daly to this action by characterizing her interest as a “vested participation interest” paid in consideration for the asset transfer, while the Daly decision involved a mere expectancy interest in stock. However, Plaintiff ignores the plain language of the LOI and the APA, which state that Plaintiff’s “participation interest” only applies to the disposition of the Saltwater Luxe Brand. (Def. Exhs. A, B.) Thus, Plaintiff’s “participation interest” can reasonably be characterized as an expectancy interest in the disposition of the Saltwater Luxe Brand— if no such disposition occurs, the “participation interest” will never be triggered. Similarly, stock options, which are a purely contractual right to purchase shares, are more comparable to the “participation interest” described in the LOI and the APA than recorded shares. As Daly confirms, “[t]he holder of an option to purchase stock is not an equitable stockholder of the corporation.” (Daly, supra, 131 Cal.App.4th at p. 61.) By analogy, the holder of a contractual participation interest in the disposition of a specific asset has a mere expectancy or contractual entitlement to proceeds, not a vested equitable ownership stake.

 

Based on the unambiguous language of the LOI and the APA, the Court finds that Plaintiff cannot demonstrate that she has a present shareholder status, whether recorded or equitable, in Houston Salem as a matter of law. As Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty and tenth cause of action for violation of her shareholder inspection rights necessarily turn on Plaintiff’s shareholder status, Defendants are entitled to summary adjudication on these claims. For the same reasons, the “unlawfulness” prong of Plaintiff’s UCL claim also fails because it is predicated on Defendants’ violations of the Corporations Code, which could not have occurred as a matter of law if Plaintiff is not a shareholder.

 

At minimum, Plaintiff argues that the “unfair” and “fraudulent” prongs of her UCL cause of action and her equitable accounting cause of action do not turn on a shareholder relationship between Plaintiff and Houston Salem. (Opp., at p. 14-19.) With respect to the UCL claim, the allegations of unfair and fraudulent conduct that do not involve rights derived from Plaintiff’s shareholder status are limited to the theory that Defendants have, in bad faith, “manipulated commission calculations and withheld material information to avoid paying Plaintiff the full commission and royalty payments she was due under the APA and LOI” and by “misrepresenting and concealing material facts about Saltwater Luxe’s sales to reduce the payouts owed to Plaintiff under the APA/LOI” while Defendants “knowingly mislabeled full-price sales as ‘discounted’ and provided false justifications for these discounts…” (FAC, ¶¶ 131-132.) Relatedly, with respect to Plaintiff’s equitable accounting claim, Plaintiff alleges that Defendants engaged in “fraud, self-dealing, abuse, illegality, gross mismanagement, commingling, and misappropriation of corporate assets” to “deprive [Plaintiff] of the full value of her [] interest,” for example, by “purposefully mislabel[ing] items as discounted when they were in fact sold at full price.” (Id. ¶ 174.) Plaintiff seeks an accounting to determine the “total commission and royalty payments that should have been paid to Plaintiff under the APA and LOI…” (Id. ¶ 175.)

 

Of course, Plaintiff’s contractual rights to commissions and her participation interest in the Saltwater Luxe brand give her standing to pursue these claims apart from any shareholder interest in Houston Salem. Accordingly, Defendants cannot demonstrate that they are entitled to summary adjudication of these claims in their favor on the basis of Plaintiff’s non-shareholder status alone. Even so, Plaintiff concedes that the allegations regarding this compensation structure and “commission dispute” are arbitrable. (Opp., at pp. 14, 16; Rep. at p. 4.) As summarized by the above allegations, Plaintiff’s “unfair” and “fraudulent” claims under the UCL and her accounting claim are unrelated to her contractual interest in the disposition of the Saltwater Luxe Brand itself, as contemplated by her Participation Right under the APA. Instead, these claims concern Defendants’ misrepresentation of the sales revenue related to the Saltwater Luxe Brand, which impacts Plaintiff’s right to commissions and royalty payments, but does not implicate a disposition of the Brand. Plaintiff is not alleging that the Brand asset itself was sold, which would trigger Plaintiff’s Participation Right under the APA. Thus, any such claim is beyond the scope of the pleadings. Accordingly, these remaining claims fall within the scope of the Court’s previous ruling compelling employment and commission-related matters to arbitration. (See Second Am. Answ., ¶ 1; Minute Order, Oct. 30, 2025; Minute Order, Nov. 7, 2025.)

 

The Court will hear oral argument as to whether Plaintiff’s remaining claims should be transferred into the ongoing arbitration proceedings or stayed in this litigation pending resolution of the arbitration.

 

Finally, Plaintiff seeks a continuance for further discovery pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subd. (h). However, “[t]he trial court need not grant a continuance where the proposed discovery is focused on matters beyond the scope of the dispositive issues framed by the pleadings.” (Ace American Ins. Co. v. Walker (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 1017, 1023.) In this case, the Court’s interpretation of the LOI and the APA precludes, as a matter of law, any finding that the underlying asset sale conferred shareholder or equitable ownership status in Houston Salem upon either Saltwater Luxe (or Plaintiff as its successor). Accordingly, even if Plaintiff were permitted to conduct further discovery into the shareholder records of Houston Salem, Plaintiff cannot show that there is a reasonable likelihood that she will discover documents reflecting her status as a record shareholder. As discussed above, Plaintiff fails to provide any legal authority to support her theory that she is entitled to be treated as an equitable owner of Houston Salem (or even the Saltwater Luxe Brand) based on her contractual participation interest alone. Thus, Plaintiff has not overcome her burden of demonstrating that she is entitled to a continuance under Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subd. (h).

·         [2] DEFENDANTS SBDTLA 1, LLC, SBDTLA 2, LLC, SBDTLA, 3, LLC, and SBDTLA 4, LLC’S DEMURRER TO THE COMPLAINT is SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND. DEFENDANTS SBDTLA 1, LLC, SBDTLA 2, LLC, SBDTLA, 3, LLC, and SBDTLA 4, LLC’S MOTION TO STRIKE is DENIED as MOOT in part and DENIED in part.

 

I.        DEMURRER OF DEFENDANT 111 W 7 OWNER, LLC – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

111 W 7 demurs to the second through fifth and the seventh through ninth causes of action in the Complaint pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subds. (e) and (f). 111 W 7 also demurs to the causes of action arises out of habitability issues and the right to possession of the Property on the grounds that such claims are barred by the preclusion doctrines.

 

A.           2nd Cause of Action: Specific Performance/Equitable Conveyance – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s second cause of action is inadequately pled. Plaintiff alleges that the Lease contained a contractual right of first offer (“ROFO”) to purchase the Unit if Main SB decided to sell or transfer the Unity. (Compl., ¶¶ 17-18, 48, Exh. A.) The Right to Purchase is set forth in Section 29 of the Lease, and states: “Tenant has been informed that the subject building has been approved for condominium. If seller should sell the unit upon the expiration of this lease, Tenant will have the right to purchase the unit at market price or vacate the unit 30 days from receiving written request.” (Id. Exh. A, § 29.) However, the Property was subsequently sold to both SBDTLA and later 111 W 7 without compliance with the ROFO, despite these purchasers’ notice of the ROFO. (Id. ¶¶ 25, 28-29, 51.) As a result, Plaintiff seeks specific performance and equitable conveyance requiring the two transfers to be set aside and transferring title of the Unit to Plaintiff under the ROFO. (Id. ¶ 53.) 111 W 7 is the current record owner of the Property. (Id. ¶¶ 10, 23, Exh. B.)

 

111 W 7 argues that Plaintiff has not demonstrated a basis for contractual remedies from 111 W 7. The Lease was executed between the original owner, Main SB, and Plaintiff. (Compl., ¶¶ 16-18, Exh. A.) 111 W 7 was not a party to the Lease. (Id. Exh. A.) Plaintiff does not specifically allege that 111 W 7 assumed the Lease upon its purchase of the Property. “Normally, the right is enforceable against third persons entering into a contract to buy the property with notice of the holder's right.” (Hartzheim v. Valley Land & Cattle Co. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 383, 389, italics added.) Thus, the inquiry turns on whether 111 W 7 had adequate notice of Plaintiff’s ROFO.

 

Based on the plain language of the Lease, the Court finds that the “Right to Purchase” offered in the Lease is not a traditional “right of first offer.” (Compl., Exh. A, § 29.) The clause does not state that the Property owner promised to convey the Unit to Plaintiff in the event of a competing offer, only that an opportunity to purchase the Unit at market price would be given. (Ibid.) The Lease exhibit controls over Plaintiff’s characterization of this provision as a “ROFO” in his Complaint. Even if this clause can be construed as a traditional “ROFO,” the terms of the provision are not sufficiently certain to give notice of the “precise act which is to be done.” (Civ. Code, § 3390, subd. (e).) The clause includes no price-setting method in the event that the Property owner received competing offers and no response period or expiration date. (Compl., Exh. A, § 29.) In fact, Plaintiff effectively concedes that the provision is missing material terms, including the price-setting method, the response period, contingencies for bad-faith offers, or contingencies if the sale was already committed to a third party. (Compl., ¶ 24.) Further, Plaintiff does not allege that the Unit was converted to a stand-alone condominium unit (an apparent condition precedent of the right to purchase), only that ownership of the entire Property was transferred between owners. (Id. ¶¶ 21-31.) If even Plaintiff lacked notice of a reasonably certain ROFO, it is unreasonable to expect the buyers, including 111 W 7 to have such notice.

 

B.            3rd Cause of Action: Constructive Trust – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s third cause of action is inadequately pled. 111 W 7 argues that Plaintiff’s request for a constructive trust cannot be pled as a separate basis for liability. Indeed, a constructive trust is not a¿cause¿of action,¿but a remedy. (Stansfield v. Starkey¿(1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 75 (Stansfield); see¿Monastra v. Konica Business Machines, U.S.A., Inc. (1996) 43 Cal.App.4th 1628, 1645 [“A constructive trust plainly is a proper remedy” for a fraudulent transfer claim.]; see Habitat Trust for Wildlife, Inc. v. City of Rancho Cucamonga (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1306, 1332 [“constructive trust is an equitable remedy….”]; PCO, Inc., supra, 150 Cal.App.4th at p. 398 [constructive trust is a remedy]; see Glue-Fold, Inc. v. Slautterback Corp. (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 1018, 1023 [same]; see Olson v. Toy (1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 818, 823 [same].) Plaintiff effectively concedes this point. (Opp., at p. 7.) Accordingly, the third cause of action is properly subject to demurrer. Plaintiff is granted leave to amend to the limited extent that he can appropriately replead his request for a constructive trust as a remedy in the Prayer for Relief.

 

C.           4th Cause of Action: Cancellation of Instruments – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action is inadequately pled. “A written instrument, in respect to which there is a reasonable apprehension that if left outstanding it may cause serious injury to a person against whom it is void or voidable, may, upon his application, be so adjudged, and ordered to be delivered up or canceled.” (Civ. Code, § 3412.)¿“To prevail on a claim to cancel an instrument, a plaintiff must prove (1) the instrument is void or voidable due to, for example, fraud; and (2) there is a reasonable apprehension of serious injury including pecuniary loss or the prejudicial alteration of one's position.” (U.S. Bank National Assn. v. Naifeh¿(2016) 1 Cal.App.5th 767, 778.)

 

111 W 7 argues that Plaintiff has not adequately alleged that the grant deed from SBDTLA to 111 W 7 is either void or voidable. Indeed, Plaintiff’s allegations that the grant deed was “void or voidable” is merely conclusory. (Compl., ¶ 62.) Plaintiff indicates that the grant deed was “voidable” because it was executed in violation of Plaintiff’s ROFO. (Id.; see Opp., p. 8.) Plaintiff appears to concede that the grant deed was not “void.” A deed that is merely voidable “can be relied upon and enforced by a bona fide purchaser,” that is, a purchaser who took title of the Property for value and without notice of a prior equitable claim to title. (Fallon v. Triangle Management Services, Inc. (1985) 169 Cal.App.3d 1103, 1106; Schiavon v. Arnaudo Brothers (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th 374, 376 [“Under California law, a bona fide purchaser for value takes title free and clear of an improperly reconveyed deed of trust, so long as the reconveyance is voidable and not void.”]; see also Civ. Code, § 1107 [“Every grant of an estate in real property is conclusive against the grantor, also against every one subsequently claiming under him, except a purchaser or incumbrancer who in good faith and for a valuable consideration acquires a title or lien by an instrument that is first duly recorded.”].) As described above, Plaintiff fails to adequately allege factual support for his conclusion that 111 W 7 had notice of Plaintiff’s Right to Purchase, let alone that Plaintiff had an equitable claim to title adverse to 111 W 7. (See supra, Section I.A.) The plain language of the Lease controls over Plaintiff’s contradictory allegations related to the ROFO. Plaintiff must plead additional specific facts demonstrating a basis for notice.

D.           5th Cause of Action: Quiet Title – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action is inadequately pled. “[O]rdinarily an action to quiet title will not lie in favor of the holder of an equitable title as against the holder of a legal title.” (Bacon v. Bacon (1937) 21 Cal.App.2d 540, 544; G.R. Holcomb Estate Co. v. Burke (1935) 4 Cal.2d 289, 296 [same]; Stafford v. Ballinger (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 289, 294–295 [same].) Plaintiff admits that 111 W 7 holds record legal title to the Property. (Compl., ¶ 28, Exh. B.) Plaintiff also concedes that his claim to title is equitable in nature. (Opp., at p. 9.) While narrow exceptions to this rule exist, such as in the event of fraud or breach of fiduciary duty, Plaintiff does not allege facts to support that any exception applies here. (See Liberty National Enterprises, L.P. v. Chicago Title Ins. Co. (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 62, 81.) Plaintiff also fails to submit any legal authority to support his contentions disputing the categorical nature of the general rule. Additionally, Plaintiff’s notice-based arguments fail for the same reasons described above. (See supra, Sections I.A, I.C.) 

 

E.            7th Cause of Action: Constructive Eviction – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s seventh cause of action is inadequately pled. Plaintiff’s constructive eviction claim is based on generalized allegations that the Property suffered from various inhabitable conditions, including electrical problems, negligent security, and other unspecified safety issues and unsanitary conditions and that the Defendants collectively refused to cure these conditions. (Compl., ¶¶ 80-81.) Plaintiff vacated the Unit in September 2024 as a result of Defendants’ collective failure to abate these issues. (Id. ¶ 81.)

 

111 W 7 argues that liability for Plaintiff’s habitability-related allegations, which form the entire basis for Plaintiff’s constructive eviction claim, is precluded by the judgment favoring 111 W 7 in the UD Action, entered on 4/6/2026, under the doctrines of both res judicata and collateral estoppel. (Mot., at p. 15.) Both doctrines require a showing that the prior proceeding was resolved by a final adjudication on the merits. (Zevnik v. Superior Court¿(2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 76, 82; Mycogen¿Corp. v. Monsanto Co.¿(2002) 28 Cal.4th 888, 896.) “California law is settled that pending appeal a trial court judgment is not final and will not be given res judicata effect.” (Sandoval v. Superior Court (1983) 140 Cal.App.3d 932, 936.) However, “once the appeal is settled … and thereafter dismissed, … the trial court judgment reemerges with sufficient finality to permit the application of collateral estoppel.” (Id. at p. 937.)

 

Here, the UD Action was adjudicated in favor of 111 W 7 by jury trial on 4/6/2026. (RJN, Exh. 1.) Judgment was entered in favor of 111 W 7 on the same day, awarding 111 W 7 possession of the Unit, past due rent, and forfeiture of the lease. (RJN, Exh. 2.) The jury concluded (in a vote of 9 to 3) that 111 W 7 had not failed to provide substantially habitable premises during the time period for which Plaintiff failed to pay the rent due on the Property. (RJN, Exh. 1, at p. 4.) Plaintiff concedes that this factual question was directed to Plaintiff’s affirmative defense of habitability. (Opp., at p. 14.)

 

However, Plaintiff argues that the judgment in the UD Action is not “final” for the purposes of the preclusion doctrines because the judgment is still pending the resolution of his appeal. (Opp., at p. 14.) The Court, on its own motion, takes judicial notice of the court docket in the UD Action and finds that Plaintiff is indeed pursuing his appellate rights. As of the date this tentative ruling was prepared, on 7/27/2026, the most recent appellate activity represented on the trial court’s docket was the Clerk’s Notice of Default on Appeal (dated 7/15/2026), notifying Plaintiff that he was required to cure the default on or before 7/30/2026. (See Notice of Default, Case No. 25STUD09542.) Because the appeal remains pending as of this date, 111 W 7’s preclusion defense is premature. However, the Court will reconsider the defense in a subsequent demurrer. (See Gombiner v. Swartz (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1365, 1371 [“The res judicata effect of an unlawful detainer proceeding is narrow, but is not nonexistent.”].)

 

Notwithstanding, the issue with Plaintiff’s constructive eviction claim, as currently pled, is that it utilizes improper group pleading and fails to clearly differentiate the acts of 111 W 7 from the acts of the other Defendants. (Complaint, ¶¶ 38, 80-84.) Indeed, Plaintiff’s allegations regarding the habitability concerns on the Property are impermissibly vague. “A constructive eviction occurs when the acts or omissions to act of a landlord, or any disturbance or interference with the tenant's possession by the landlord, renders the premises, or a substantial portion thereof, unfit for the purposes for which they were leased, or which has the effect of depriving the tenant for a substantial period of time of the beneficial, enjoyment or use of the premises.” (Groh v. Kover's Bull Pen, Inc. (1963) 221 Cal.App.2d 611, 614, italics added.) In the Complaint, Plaintiff does not identify any specific dates of the electrical problems or unsanitary conditions or the specific dates on which he made complaints to 111 W 7. While Plaintiff identifies a single safety incident on May 31, 2024, he admits that that the fire department concluded that no crime occurred and he does not identify any specific acts or omissions attributing fault to 111 W 7 for this incident. (Id. ¶ 81; see infra, Section I.F.) Plaintiff must plead more specific factual details clarifying how the conduct by 111 W 7 (as opposed to the other defendants) was causally connected to the alleged habitability issues and Plaintiff’s constructive eviction.

 

F.            8th Cause of Action: Premises Liability – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s eighth cause of action is inadequately pled. “The elements of a negligence claim and a premises liability claim are the same: a legal duty of care, breach of that duty, and proximate cause resulting in injury.” (Kesner v. Superior Court (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1132, 1158.) “A landowner owes a duty to exercise reasonable care to maintain his or her property in such a manner as to avoid exposing others to an unreasonable risk of injury.” (Barnes v. Black (1999) 71 Cal.App.4th 1473, 1478.) “Ordinarily, there is no duty to protect others from third party criminal activity. Courts, however, have recognized exceptions to the general no-duty-to-protect rule, one of which is the ‘special relationship’ doctrine. […] Based on the special relationship, commercial proprietors... are required to maintain land in their possession and control in a reasonably safe condition and... this general duty includes taking reasonable steps to secure common areas against foreseeable criminal acts of third parties that are likely to occur in the absence of such precautionary measures.” (Ericson v. Federal Express Corp. (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 1291, 1300, internal quotations and citations omitted.)  

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that 111 W 7 “negligently allowed an assailant to gain access to the [] Property and access the sixth floor where Plaintiff [] lived.” (Compl., ¶ 85.) The same morning, Plaintiff woke up to the smell of gas, unable to breath. (Id. ¶ 86.) Plaintiff traced the smell to a bottle left outside his front door. (Id. ¶ 87.) Plaintiff admits that the Los Angeles Fire Department concluded that no crime had occurred before there was no fire. (Id. ¶ 91.) The issue with this cause of action is that Plaintiff identifies no factual basis to conclude that 111 W 7 was negligent in allowing the unidentified “assailant” into the Property, or that the “assailant’s” conduct was foreseeable. Plaintiff does not identify a failure to take precautionary measures, such as a security failure, he does not allege that 111 W 7 had notice of prior similar incidents, and he does not otherwise describe what 111 W 7 did or failed to do. Plaintiff also identifies no injury, particularly as LAFD confirmed that he was not the victim of a crime. Plaintiff’s mere speculation regarding 111 W 7’s role in causing this incident is insufficient to overcome his pleading burden.

 

G.           9th Cause of Action: Violations of Tenant Harassment Law – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s ninth cause of action is inadequately pled. Civil Code section 1942.5 provides tenants with the right to bring a civil action against landlords who engage in retaliatory conduct. “Under section 1942.5 it is unlawful for a lessor to ‘bring an action’ against a lessee to ‘recover possession ... for the purpose of retaliating against the lessee because he or she ... has lawfully and peaceably exercised any rights under the law.’” (Banuelos v. LA Investment, LLC (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 323, 327–328.) Additionally, a landlord cannot cause the lessee to quit involuntarily, increase the rent, or decrease services in retaliation for the tenant’s exercise of his rights. (Civ. Code § 1942.5, subd. (a).) 

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that Defendants retaliated against Plaintiff after he complained about safety issues at the Property by “filing frivolous unlawful detainer proceedings” against Plaintiff “based on a fraudulent notice [to] quit.” (Compl., ¶ 104.) Plaintiff further alleges that Defendants have harassed Plaintiff by refusing to make repairs and discriminating against Plaintiff by attempting to evict him. (Id. ¶¶ 105-106, 109.)

 

111 W 7 argues that the litigation privilege bars liability for filing the UD proceedings and notices to quit in preparation of litigation. However, California courts have found that “the scheme of retaliatory eviction remedies in section 1942.5 … would be rendered significantly inoperative if the litigation privilege were to apply.” (Winslett v. 1811 27th Avenue, LLC (2018) 26 Cal.App.5th 239, 254.) Thus, section 1942.5 “create[s] an exception to the litigation privilege.” (Ibid.; Banuelos v. LA Investment, LLC (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 323, 333–334.)

 

Further, to the extent that 111 W 7 argues that this cause of action is barred by the preclusion doctrines, the Court adopts its above analysis and finds that this defense is premature based on the still-pending appeal. (See supra, Section I.E.)

 

Nevertheless, the issue with Plaintiff’s tenant harassment/retaliation claim is that the allegations pertaining to the timeline of Plaintiff’s complaints, the habitability concerns at the Property are impermissibly vague. (See supra, Section I.E.) Other than identifying two UD actions filed by 111 W 7, Plaintiff does not identify the specific acts (or dates) of the conduct by 111 W 7 in particular, as opposed to the other Defendants. Plaintiff also fails to identify any specific exercise of his own statutory rights under Civ. Code Div. 3, Pt. 4, T. 5, Ch. 2 or any formal complaint to an appropriate agency about either the conditions of the Property or 111 W 7’s conduct. (Civ. Code, § 1942.5, subd. (a) [“If the lessor retaliates against the lessee because of the exercise by the lessee of the lessee's rights under this chapter or because of the lessee's complaint to an appropriate agency as to tenantability of a dwelling…”].) Plaintiff must allege the harassing conduct by 111 W 7 and the causal relationship to Plaintiff’s protected conduct to state a claim under the applicable tenant harassment statutes.

 

II.       MOTION TO STRIKE OF DEFENDANT 111 W 7 OWNER, LLC – DENIED as MOOT

 

111 W 7 moves for an order striking Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages from the Complaint. In light of the above ruling on 111 W 7’s demurrer, the Complaint no longer states any claim to support an award of punitive damages. Accordingly, the Court finds the motion to strike is MOOT.

 

III.      DEMURRER OF DEFENDANT SBDTLA – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

SBDTLA demurs to the second through sixth causes of action pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subds. (e) and (f).

 

A.           2nd Cause of Action: Specific Performance/Equitable Conveyance – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s second cause of action is inadequately pled. SBDTLA argues that Plaintiff has not demonstrated that the “Right to Purchase” provision in the Lease constitutes an enforceable “Right of First Offer” (“ROFO”) as a matter of law, let alone that the Unit was ever developed as a separately conveyable condominium as contemplated by the Right to Purchase. (Mot., at pp. 5-6.) For the same reasons described above with respect to 111 W 7, Plaintiff has not sufficiently alleged facts demonstrating that the Right to Purchase was enforceable against SBDTLA due to a lack of adequate notice and a lack of certainty in the terms of the provision. (See supra, Section I.A.)

 

B.            3rd Cause of Action: Constructive Trust – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s third cause of action is inadequately pled. Pursuant to the above-identified legal authorities, a constructive trust is an equitable remedy, not an independent cause of action. (See supra, Section I.B.) Plaintiff concedes this point. (Opp., at p. 12.) Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to this cause of action, but permits Plaintiff leave to the limited extent that Plaintiff can properly recharacterize his request for a constructive trust as a remedy.

 

C.           4th Cause of Action: Cancellation of Instruments – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action is inadequately pled. Plaintiff appears to concede that he is alleging that the grant deeds involving SBDTLA are merely “voidable.” (Opp., at p. 12.) For the same reasons discussed supra, the Court finds that Plaintiff fails to allege sufficient facts to dispute that SBDTLA was not a bona fide purchaser when purchasing the Property from Main SB or that 111 W 7 was not a bona fide purchaser when purchasing the Property from SBDTLA. A deed that is merely voidable “can be relied upon and enforced by a bona fide purchaser.” (see Fallon v. Triangle Management Services, Inc. (1985) 169 Cal.App.3d 1103, 1106.) Accordingly, the Court incorporates the above analysis regarding this cause of action against 111 W 7 by reference herein. (See supra, Section I.C.)

D.           5th Cause of Action: Quiet Title – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action is inadequately pled. Plaintiff concedes that his alleged claim to title is merely equitable in nature. (Opp., at p. 13.) However, an action to quiet title to property will ordinarily not lie in favor of an equitable title holder against the legal title holder. (See e.g. Bacon v. Bacon (1937) 21 Cal.App.2d 540, 544.) Accordingly, the Court incorporates the above analysis regarding this cause of action against 111 W 7 by reference herein. (See supra, Section I.D.)

 

E.            6th Cause of Action: Declaratory Relief – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s sixth cause of action is inadequately pled. Plaintiff’s declaratory relief claim relies on the premise that the Right to Purchase set for in the Lease is a valid and enforceable ROFO in favor of Plaintiff, that SBDTLA took ownership with notice of the ROFO, and that the subsequent transfers of the Property were made in violation of Plaintiff’s ROFO. (Compl., ¶¶ 75, 77.) As described above, the plain language of the Right to Purchase in the Lease contradicts Plaintiff’s characterization of this provision as a “ROFO” and Plaintiff does not state sufficient facts to demonstrate either that Defendants, including SBDTLA, had sufficient notice of this alleged “right” based on the uncertain terms of the provision or that the “right” was ever triggered. Plaintiff must allege more specific factual details demonstrating that the SBDTLA and 111 W 7 were not bona fide purchasers, that SBDTLA had sufficient notice of a clear and definite ROTO, and that SBDTLA nevertheless knowingly violated Plaintiff’s vested ROTO. Without more evidence of an enforceable, vested purchase right and notice to SBDTLA, Plaintiff has not demonstrated an actual present controversy requiring declaratory relief.

 

IV.      MOTION TO STRIKE OF DEFENDANT SBDTLA – DENIED as MOOT in part and DENIED in part

 

SBDTLA moves to strike Plaintiff’s requests for equitable relief (specific performance, conveyance of title, injunctive relief, constructive trust, quiet title, cancellation of instruments) from the Complaint. In light of the above ruling on SBDTLA’s demurrer, the Complaint no longer states any claim to enforce Plaintiff’s alleged equitable interest in the Unit to support a request for these equitable remedies. Accordingly, the Court finds the motion to strike Plaintiff’s requests for equitable remedies is MOOT.

 

SBDTLA moves to strike Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages. Plaintiff only appears to be seeking punitive damages in connection with his ninth cause of action, which is only asserted against 111 W 7, not SBDTLA. In the event that Plaintiff is seeking punitive damages in connection with his constructive eviction claim, SBDTLA has offered no legal authority or argument pertaining to this request and therefore has not overcome its burden in moving to strike punitive damages request. Likewise, SBDTLA also moves to strike Plaintiff’s request for attorneys’ fees. SBDTLA entirely omits any analysis on the basis for this request from its motion, and thus, fails to overcome its burden in moving to strike Plaintiff’s attorneys’ fees request. Accordingly, SBDTLA’s motion to strike is DENIED with respect to punitive damages and attorneys’ fees.
