             For the reasons stated below, the Court DENIES Defendant’s Motion to Tax Costs in its entirety.

Background

This is a lemon law case. Plaintiff Aykui Sarkavagyan alleges that on October 16, 2021, she purchased a vehicle manufactured by Defendant General Motors, LLC for $76,835.49. Plaintiff alleges that the vehicle exhibited numerous defects, which could not be remedied after a reasonable number of repair attempts.

On October 3, 2024, Plaintiff filed suit against Defendant, alleging two violations of the Song Beverly Act, and two violations of the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act.

On January 30, 2026, the parties settled this case. Defendant agreed to pay Plaintiff $133,000.00 in exchange for dropping their suit.

On May 8, 2026, Plaintiffs brought a Motion for Attorney Fees, requesting $20,117.34 in reasonable attorney fees and costs. On July 10, 2026, this Court granted this motion in part, awarding a total of $7,500.00 in reasonable fees. At the time, the Court declined to rule on the issue of costs, as there was already a pending and partially briefed Motion to Tax Costs scheduled to be heard in August.

Now, the Court hears Defendant’s Motion to Tax Costs.

            Discussion

Defendant moves to tax the entirety of Plaintiff’s requested $1,545.84 in costs. (Mot. to Tax, p. 3:24-27.)

i)                   Costs are Awardable

First, the Court must determine whether costs are awardable. Plaintiff in this case is the prevailing party in a Song-Beverly action. Moreover, under the terms of the January 30, 2026, settlement agreement, Plaintiff is deemed the prevailing party and is entitled to recover reasonable attorney fees and costs by noticed motion. (Bowton Decl. ¶ 2.) On May 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed a memorandum of costs on form MC-010, requesting $1,545.84 in costs. (Memorandum of Costs, p. 1.)

ii)                 Analysis

Next, the Court will analyze the motion itself. Typically, in a ruling on a motion to tax costs, the trial court’s first determination is whether the statute expressly allows the particular item and whether it appears proper on its face; if so, the burden is on the objecting party to show the costs to be unnecessary and unreasonable. Where costs are not expressly allowed by statute, the burden is on the party claiming the costs to show that the charges were reasonable and necessary. (Foothill-De Anza Comm College Dist. v Emerich (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 11, 29.) In other words, the Court has the power to disallow costs that are “allowable as a matter of right” when they are not “reasonably necessary.” (Perko’s Enterprises Inc. v. RRNS Enter. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 238, 245.)

However, the case at hand is a Song-Beverly case. This means the controlling statute with respect to costs is CIV § 1794(d). Under CIV § 1794(d), “[i]f the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs . . . determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” (CCP § 1794(d).) Thus, the only analysis this Court must engage in is whether the costs requested were “reasonably incurred”.

Here, Defendant argues that all of the following costs were not reasonably incurred by Plaintiff: 1) $170.27 in jury fees; 2) $1,245.57 in filing and service fees; and 3) $130 in mediation costs. (Mot. to Tax, p. 5:2-18.)

1)      Jury Fees

First, Defendant moves to tax Plaintiff’s request for $150 in costs for jury fees. (Id. p. 5:2-6.) Defendant cites to Perko’s Enterprises Inc. v. RRNS Enterprises for the proposition that “[u]nder California law, jury fees are only recoverable when a trial occurs, as they are intended to cover expenses associated with empaneling a jury.” (Mot. to Tax, p. 5:3-4.) Defendant misstates the law. Perko’s does not hold that jury fees are recoverable only when a trial actually occurs. In fact, the only mention of “jury fees” in Perko’s is in a footnote that quotes the following language from CCP § 1033.5(a): “[t]he following items are allowable as costs under Section 1032: "(1) Filing, motion, and jury fees.” (Perko’s Enterprises Inc. v. RRNS Enterprises (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 238, fn. 5.) Thus, Defendant’s misleading citation holds no weight here.

Moreover, this Court, like others, holds that jury fees are a reasonably incurred cost, even when a case does not go to trial. Under CCP § 631, jury fees must be paid by the first case management conference in the action. (CCP § 631.) Thus, if Plaintiff wished to be entitled to a jury trial, they had to pay this fee early in the case’s lifespan. Thus, the fee was reasonably necessary for the litigation of the case.

Additionally, the Court notes that jury fees are specifically enumerated as recoverable costs under CCP § 1033.5(a). While this statute is not the relevant authority in this Song-Beverly case, it still provides persuasive authority for the position that California courts believe that jury fees are a reasonably incurred cost.

For these reasons, the Court declines to strike these costs.

2)      Filing and Service Fees

Next, Defendant moves to strike $1,245.57 in costs related to filing fees and service of process fees. (Mot. to Tax, p. 5:7-14.) Here, Defendant argues that these costs were simple administrative filings, and therefore not reasonably necessary for the prosecution of this case. (Mot. to Tax, p. 5:7-14.) In support of their argument, Defendant once again cites to the Perko’s case, as well as Ladas v. California State Auto Assn. for the general proposition that Plaintiff can only recover costs “reasonably necessary” to the litigation. (Id. p. 5:9-11.) The Court disagrees. Administrative filings are required of every litigant in a case, as documents need to be filed with this Court. Thus, these are standard fees that should be expected of litigation and are therefore reasonably incurred. Additionally, the Court once again notes that filing costs are allowable under CCP § 1033.5(a)(1), suggesting that California courts presume these costs as reasonably incurred.

For these reasons, the Court declines to strike these costs.

3)      Mediation Costs

Finally, Defendant moves to strike $130 in costs related to mediation costs. (Id. p. 5:15-19.) Here, Defendant points out that Plaintiff’s memorandum of costs provides no explanation or supporting documents for these costs. (Mot. to Tax, p. 5:16-17.) Additionally, the Court notes that mediation costs are not specifically enumerated as recoverable costs under CCP § 1033.5(a). (See generally, CCP § 1033.5(a).) While this is not the relevant cost statute, it does suggest that these costs are not typically seen by courts as being reasonably necessary for the litigation.

That being said, the Court disagrees that mediation costs are not reasonably incurred. Informal attempts to resolve claims are crucial to the litigation process. This case itself was resolved out of court. Moreover, as Plaintiff points out in their Opposition, Defendant was an active participant in setting up this mediation. (Opp. p. 5:20-24.) Thus, the actions of both parties suggest that this was a reasonably incurred cost.

For these reasons, the Court declines to strike these costs.

4)      Summary

In summation, the Court declines to strike any of the costs requested in this Motion to Tax.

Conclusion

The Court DENIES Defendant’s Motion to Tax Costs in its entirety. 

            IT IS SO ORDERED, Plaintiff TO GIVE NOTICE

             For the reasons stated below, the Court SUSTAINS Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ Complaint without leave to amend. 

Background

            This is a lemon law case. Plaintiffs Oscar and Lilian Rivera allege that on September 4, 2023, they purchased a new Ford F-150 manufactured by Defendant Ford Motor Company for $81,323.76. Plaintiffs allege that the vehicle exhibited numerous defects that could not be cured after a reasonable number of repair attempts.

            On April 1, 2026, Plaintiffs filed suit against Defendant, alleging three violations of the Song-Beverly Act. Plaintiffs are represented by the firm Quill and Arrow. However, this is not the first time Plaintiffs filed suit for this vehicle. Two years ago, on June 3, 2024, Plaintiffs filed suit against Defendant Ford Motor Company, alleging multiple violations of the Song-Beverly Act, based on the purchase of this same vehicle. This suit (Case No. 24VECV02584) currently sits in the Van Nuys Dept. A. Plaintiffs are represented in that case by Strategic Legal Practices.

            Now, Defendant demurs to Plaintiff’s Complaint.

Meet and Confer

Before filing a demurrer, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (CCP § 430.41(a) [emphasis added].) A declaration must be filed with a demurrer regarding the results of the meet and confer process. (CCP § 430.41(a)(3).)

Here, Defendant provides the declaration of Sydney Sloas, who outlines an extensive meet and confer effort between her and Plaintiffs’ counsel. Ms. Sloas requested that Plaintiffs’ counsel either drop this duplicative case or dismiss the original action. (Sloas Decl. ¶¶ 3-7.) Plaintiffs’ counsel was initially communicative and stated that they planned to contact Plaintiffs’ original counsel to get the case dismissed. (Id. ¶ 5.) Defendant was then granted a 30-day extension for filing a responsive pleading to allow Plaintiffs to resolve this issue. (Id. ¶ 7.) Defendant clearly communicated to Plaintiffs that they would be forced to file a demurrer if neither suit was dismissed. (Id. ¶ 6.) Plaintiffs have yet to dismiss either of these claims. (Id. ¶ 8.) Thus, even though Defendant did not meet and confer in person or by call, the Court finds that Defendant has met their meet and confer obligations.

            Discussion

Defendant demurs to Plaintiffs’ entire Complaint, arguing that Plaintiffs already have a pending action against Defendant for identical Song-Beverly claims for the same vehicle.

Under CCP § 430.10, “[t]he party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds: . . .There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action.” (CCP § 430.10(c).)

Here, Defendant plainly shows that Plaintiffs have a pending action as to all three of these Song-Beverly claims on this same vehicle. On June 6, 2024, Plaintiffs filed suit against Defendant, alleging three violations of the Song-Beverly Act as brought in this case, as well as claims for breach of implied warranty, negligent repair, and fraudulent concealment. (See, Sloas Decl. Ex. 3.) The case at hand also alleges the same three violations of the Song-Beverly Act. (See generally, Compl.) Both cases involve the same vehicle – a 2023 Ford F-150, VIN # 1FTFW1E50PFB59417. (Sloas Decl. Ex. 3, ¶ 7; see also, Compl. ¶ 8.) Thus, there is a duplicative pending action.

In opposition, Plaintiffs do not dispute that this duplicative pending action exists. (Opp. p. 2:6-13.) Instead, Plaintiffs state that, “Plaintiffs unequivocally elected to have Quill & Arrow continue representing them in this action” and that dismissal of this claim would force Plaintiffs, “to continue litigating through counsel unequivocally discharged or risk losing their claims entirely.” (Id. p.  2:6-7; Id. p. 2:19-20.) Plaintiffs further argue that dismissal would deprive them of their right to dismiss and choose their own counsel. (Id. p. 2:14-17.)

As Defendant points out in their Reply, that is incorrect. (Reply, p. 1:13-21.) Plaintiffs still maintain their right to be represented by whichever counsel they choose. If Plaintiffs wish to be represented by Quill and Arrow, rather than Strategic Legal Practices, Plaintiffs should substitute their new counsel into the original 2024 case. Nothing this Court does here today will prevent Plaintiffs from doing this. Thus, Plaintiffs’ preference of counsel is irrelevant to the issue at hand, and the dismissal of this second duplicative suit will do nothing to prevent Plaintiffs from being represented by whomever they prefer.

Ultimately, it is uncontested that Plaintiffs filed a suit two years ago that fully encompasses the three claims alleged in this case. Thus, a demurrer under CCP § 430.10(c) is appropriate. Additionally, there are no grounds to grant leave to amend, as the issue with this Complaint is the existence of the duplicative action from 2024. For these reasons, the Court SUSTAINS Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ Complaint without leave to amend.

Conclusion

The Court SUSTAINS Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ Complaint without leave to amend.
