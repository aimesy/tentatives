DEPARTMENT 529 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV21486    Hearing Date: July 13, 2026    Dept: 529

MARCUS GABRIEL WHITTEMORE vs RAMY BARAMILY

 

motion for judgment on the pleadings

 

Date of Hearing:        July 13, 2026                          Trial Date:       None set   

Department:              529                                         Case No.:        24STCV21486 

 

Moving Party:            Defendant Ramy Baramily

Responding Party:     No opposition  

 

BACKGROUND 

 

On August 23, 2024, Plaintiff Marcus G. Whittlemore filed a complaint against Defendant Ramy Baramily for (1) Assault; (2) Battery; (3) Gender Violence in Violation of Civil Code §52.4; (4) Ralphs Civil Rights Act (Violation of Civ. Code §51.7); (5) Interference with Exercise of Civil Rights (Civ. Code §52.1); and (6) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress. Plaintiff alleges Defendant punched Plaintiff when Plaintiff, acting on behalf of his client in another civil matter, arranged for the return of approximately 200 electric scooters to that client and the client’s business associate. Plaintiff alleges this was after Plaintiff observed Defendant and his employees attempting to take the scooters and confronted Defendant on the issue.  

 

On January 28, 2025, the request for entry of default was entered against Defendant.  The default was set aside on May 8, 2025.

 

On January 15, 2026, Plaintiff named Oleh Hlladun as Doe 1.

 

[Tentative] Ruling

 

Defendant Ramy Baramily’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is GRANTED.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Defendant Ramy Baramily moves for judgment on the pleadings pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 438 as to the third, fourth and fifth causes of action on the grounds that each of the causes of action fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.

 

A motion for judgment on the pleadings is the functional equivalent to a general demurrer.  (Lance Camper Mfg. Corp. v. Republic Indemnity Co. of Am. (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 194, 198).  Like demurrers, motions for judgment on the pleadings challenge the legal sufficiency of the allegations, not their veracity.  (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994). Any defects must either appear on the face of the pleading, or else be taken by judicial notice.  (Bezirdjian v. O'Reilly (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 316, 321-22).  The parties’ ability to prove their respective claims is of no concern.  (Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 99.)  Though the Court must accept the allegations of the complaint and answer as true (Gerawan Farming, Inc. v. Lyons (2000) 24 Cal.4th 468, 515), it will not do so for “conclusions of law or fact, opinions, speculation, or allegations contrary to law or [judicially noticed] facts…” (Stevenson Real Estate Servs., Inc. v. CB Richard Ellis Real Estate Servs., Inc. (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1219-20). 

 

Third Cause of Action

 

Civil Code section 52.4, subdivision (a), allows “[a]ny person who has been subjected to gender violence” to “bring a civil action for damages against any responsible party,” and “[t]he plaintiff may seek actual damages, compensatory damages, punitive damages, injunctive relief,” and attorney’s fees and costs. “‘[G]ender violence’ is a form of sex discrimination” and means either an act “that would constitute a criminal offense under state law that has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person or property of another, committed at least in part based on the gender of the victim, whether or not those acts have resulted in criminal complaints, charges, prosecution, or conviction,” or “[a] physical intrusion or physical invasion of a sexual nature under coercive conditions, whether or not those acts have resulted in criminal complaints, charges, prosecution, or conviction.” (Civ. Code, § 52.4(c).) 

 

Defendant argues liability under section 52.4 requires factual allegations plausibly supporting that the force or threat was committed at least in part “based on” the plaintiff’s sex/gender, not merely that the plaintiff is of a sex or gender. Defendant contends, here, the complaint’s asserted gender nexus is not supported by conduct to Plaintiff’s gender as required by the statute. At most, the allegations of a “derogatory remark” referencing Plaintiff as a “tough guy” is not enough to establish the statutory gender-based element.

 

The court finds Plaintiff has not sufficiently alleged a claim for violation of Civil Code section 52.4. The allegations that Defendant referred to Plaintiff as a “tough guy” do not amount to physical conduct — either criminal acts or invasion of a sexual nature under coercive conditions. The court also notes the allegations that Defendant attempted to punch Plaintiff, but missed do not sufficiently allege that the attempted punch was motivated in part by Plaintiff’s gender.  

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion for judgment on the pleadings is GRANTED.  If plaintiff does not have additional facts to allege, it will be GRANTED  WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Fourth Cause of Action

 

Civil Code section 51.7 states “[a]ll persons within the jurisdiction of this state have the right to be free from any violence, or intimidation by threat of violence, committed against their persons or property because of political affiliation, or on account of any characteristic listed or defined in subdivision (b) or (e) of Section 51, or position in a labor dispute, or because another person perceives them to have one or more of those characteristics.” (Civ. Code, § 51.7(a).) Section 51 characteristics include sex, race, color, religion, ancestry, national origin, disability, medical condition, genetic information, marital status, sexual orientation, citizenship, primary language, or immigration status.  (Civ. Code § 51(a).)  To plead a claim for violation of section 51.7, a plaintiff must allege (1) the defendant threatened or committed violent acts against the plaintiff; (2) the defendant was motivated by his perception of plaintiff’s protected characteristic; (3) the plaintiff was harmed; and (4) the defendant’s conduct was a substantial factor in causing the plaintiff’s harm.  (See Austin B. v. Escondido Union School Dist. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 860, 880-81.)

 

Defendant argues Plaintiff’s attempt of tying the alleged incident to Plaintiff’s gender are conclusory and not supported by specific facts indicating that gender was a motivating factor for any alleged violence or intimidation. Mainly, the complaint does not allege facts showing that any alleged conduct occurred because Plaintiff is male (or because of any protected characteristic), as required under section 51.7.

 

The court finds Plaintiff has not sufficiently alleged a claim for violation of Civil Code section 51.7. As noted above, the complaint does not sufficiently allege the attempted punch was motivated by Plaintiff’s gender. It does not allege that the speech (“tough guy”) was a threat of violence motivated by Plaintiff’s gender.

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion for judgment on the pleadings is GRANTED.  If plaintiff does not have additional facts to allege, it will be GRANTED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Fifth Cause of Action

 

A claim for violation of the Bane Act can be brought by an individual against a private person, where that person interferes with the Plaintiff’s legal rights by threat, intimidation, or coercion. (Civ. Code § 52.1(a), (b).) To allege a cause of action under Civil Code section 52.1, the plaintiff must allege that “the defendant interfered with or attempted to interfere with the plaintiff’s legal right by threatening or committing violent acts.” (Doe v. State (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 832, 842.) “[A] plaintiff need not allege the defendant acted with discriminatory animus or intent; a defendant is liable if he or she interfered with the plaintiff’s constitutional rights by the requisite threats, intimidation, or coercion.” (O’Toole v. Superior Court (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 488, 502.) “Coercion inherent in the alleged constitutional violation, i.e., an over detention in jail, is insufficient to meet the statutory requirement of ‘threat, intimidation, or coercion.’” (Doe, supra, 8 Cal.App.5th at 842 (quoting Shoyoye v. County of Los Angeles (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 947, 959).) “The statute requires a showing of threatening conduct independent from the alleged interference or violation of a civil right.” (Id. at 842-43.) “‘[A] wrongful arrest and detention, without more, cannot constitute ‘force, intimidation, or coercion’ for purposes of section 52.1.’” (Id. at 843 (quoting Shoyoye, supra, 203 Cal.App.4th at 960).)¿¿ 

 

Defendant argues the complaint does not plead facts showing that Defendant’s alleged conduct interfered with, or attempted to interfere with, the exercise or enjoyment of a distinct constitutional or statutory right through threats, intimidation, or coercion as required by section 52.1. Instead, the fifth cause of action relies on conclusory recitations of statutory language and incorporates the same alleged physical-contact narrative underpinning the common-law tort claims.

 

The court finds Plaintiff has not sufficiently alleged a claim for violation of Civil Code section 52.1. It is unclear from the complaint what the constitutional right or statutory right Defendant interfered with through the threats, intimidation or coercion.

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion for judgment on the pleadings is GRANTED.  If plaintiff does not have additional facts to allege, it will be GRANTED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND.

 


Case Number: 25STCV15642    Hearing Date: July 13, 2026    Dept: 529

SAM SAYYAH VS FCA US LLC, ET AL.

 

MOTION TO COMPEL INITIAL DISCLOSURES PURSUANT TO CALIFORNIA CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE §871.26

 

Date of Hearing:        July 13, 2026                                      Trial Date:       None set. 

Department:              529                                                                 Case No.:        25STCV15642

 

Moving Party:            Plaintiff Sam Sayyah

Responding Party:     Defendant FCA US LLC

 

BACKGROUND

 

This is a lemon law action. On May 29, 2025, Plaintiff Sam Sayyah filed a complaint against Defendants FCA US LLC AND Santa Monica Chrysler Jeep Dodge Ram for various violations of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act as well as negligent repair and fraudulent inducement. On October 30, 2025, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint.  

 

[TENTATIVE] RULING:

 

Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures Pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure §871.26 is DENIED.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Plaintiff Sam Sayyah moves this court for an order compelling Defendant FCA US LLC to comply with California Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 by producing all documents required under subdivisions (h)(6), (h)(7), (h)(8), (h)(9), (h)(10), (h)(12), and (h)(13) and imposing mandatory sanctions against FCA US LLC and its counsel of record in the amount of $2,500.00 pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure § 871.26(j)(1).

 

Effective January 1, 2025, in an action seeking restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle pursuant to Section 871.20 (above), the parties shall: “Within 60 days after the filing of the answer or other responsive pleading, … without awaiting a discovery request, provide to all other parties an initial disclosure and documents pursuant to subdivisions (f), (g), and (h) [of Section 871.26].” (CCP § 871.26(b).)¿ 

 

Plaintiff argues FCA has failed to provide complete, verified, and unfiltered disclosures pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26(h).  As noted above, pursuant to Section 871.26(b), FCA was required to provide all mandatory initial disclosures within 60 days of filing its Answer — by October 14, 2025. However, as of the date of this motion, FCA has failed to produce any documents whatsoever under section 871.26(h). Plaintiff also maintains FCA has not provided written responses accompanied by verifications of defense counsel to the mandatory section 871.26(h) disclosures.

In opposition, FCA argues it served its initial disclosure production on October 14, 2025, in compliance with the due date and roughly seven months before Plaintiff’s motion was served on June 13, 2026. That production included over 1,000 pages of materials, including the available repair records, warranty claims records, sales brochures, vehicle information detail report, glove box documents, the Monroney label, Technical Service Bulletins, and recalls applicable to the Subject Vehicle. (Gahan Decl. ¶ 4.) In advance of the production, FCA provided Plaintiff with a proposed Stipulation and Protective Order. (Gahan Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. 1.) The parties eventually entered a protective order on June 3, 2026. (Gahan Decl. ¶6, Exh. 4.) FCA also notes it has now served written disclosures (although it disagrees that they are mandatory).

 

The court finds Plaintiff’s requests for DealerConnect Report, AutoPay Claims, Complaint Claims as well as LOP records exceeds the scope of discovery required under Section 871.26. Moreover, Plaintiff contends FCA did not produce ISBs but FCA has stated there are none. The court further notes Plaintiff has not sufficiently demonstrated FCA must do an further search for these records.

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion to compel initial disclosures pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure §871.26 is DENIED.

 

Sanctions

 

Plaintiff seeks $2,500 in monetary sanctions pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26(j)(1). Plaintiff’s request for sanctions pursuant to Section 871.26(j)(1) is DENIED.

 


Case Number: 25STCV17552    Hearing Date: July 13, 2026    Dept: 529

gabrielle ceja, et al. v. american honda motor co., inc.

 

PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL THE DEPOSITION OF DEFENDANT’S PERSON MOST KNOWLEDGEABLE AND PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS

 

Date of Hearing:        July 13, 2026                                      Trial Date:       None set.   

Department:              529                                                      Case No.:        25STCV17552

 

Moving Party:            Plaintiffs Gabrielle Ceja and Victor Manuel Sanchez-Sanchez

Responding Party:     Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.

Meet and Confer:      Yes. (Ardi Decl.)

 

BACKGROUND

 

This is a lemon law action. On June 17, 2025, Plaintiffs Gabrielle Ceja and Victor Manuel Sanchez-Sanchez filed a complaint against Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. for various violations of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act.

 

[Tentative] Ruling

 

Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel the Deposition of Defendant’s Person Most Knowledgeable and Production of Documents is GRANTED.

 

discussion

 

Plaintiffs Gabrielle Ceja and Victor Manuel Sanchez-Sanchez move for an order striking Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.’s objections and to compel (1) the testimony from Defendant’s Person Most Knowledgeable (“PMK”) pertaining to Matters for Examination 1 through 34, and (2) responses and production of documents to Plaintiffs’ associated Request for Production of Documents, numbers 1-18 at the time of the PMK deposition.

 

Pursuant to CCP §2025.450, if after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action, without having served a valid objection, fails to appear for examination, or proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document, the party giving notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document.  (CCP §2025.450(a).)  A motion to compel the deposition of a party to the action must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration, or, when the deponent failed to attend the deposition, a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.  (CCP §2025.450(b)(2).)   

 

Plaintiffs argue Defendant failed to provide a witness to a properly noticed deposition and failed to provide adequate responses to Plaintiffs’ document requests, which seek documents relevant to the asserted Song-Beverly Act claims. Plaintiffs note Defendant, instead, asserted objections to Plaintiff’s discovery. Defendant objected to a vast majority of matters for examination and requests for production of documents without substantive responses or any clear indication as to which matters for examination it is willing to produce a PMK witness upon.

 

AHM filed a declaration on June 22, 2026 attesting they have now agreed to a deposition date of August 28, 2026.

 

The court grants the motion to compel AHM’s PMK’s deposition, to take place on August 28, 2026.  With respect to the request for production, the court finds some of the objections to be boilerplate or inappropriate, while some are well taken.  The court orders defendant to produce further responses and responsive documents within 30 days to the following requests: 3, 6, 7, 8-12, 17 and 18.  The remaining requests are unreasonably overbroad or vague.  Defendant does not need to provide any further response to the following requests: 1, 2, 13-16.  For any documents withheld on the ground of attorney client or attorney work product privilege, defendant must list those documents on a code-compliant privilege log within 30 days.

 

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel the Deposition of Defendant’s Person Most Knowledgeable and Production of Documents is GRANTED in part.

 

 


Case Number: 25STCV37297    Hearing Date: July 13, 2026    Dept: 529

CHRISTOPHER ORLANDO ESCOBAR V. HENKELS & MCCOY WEST, LLC, ET AL.

 

DEFENDANT HENKELS & MCCOY WEST, LLC’S MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION AND STAY ACTION

 

Date of Hearing:        July 13, 2026                                      Trial Date:       None set  

Department:              529                                                     Case No.:        25STCV37297

 

Moving Party:            Defendants Henkels & McCoy West LLC

Responding Party:     Plaintiff Christopher Orlando Escobar

 

BACKGROUND

 

On December 19, 2025, Plaintiff Christopher Orlando Escobar filed a complaint against Defendants Henkels & McCoy West, LLC, Mastec North America, Inc., Mark Partida, and Lizeth Marcial for various violations of the Labor Code, wrongful termination, violation of Business and Professions Code, and violation of FEHA based on sex/gender based discrimination and failure to take all reasonable steps to prevent and/or remedy discrimination and/or retaliation. On February 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint adding a PAGA cause of action.

 

[TENTATIVE] RULING

 

Defendant Henkels & McCoy West LLC’s Motion to Compel Arbitration and Stay Action is GRANTED.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

Defendant Henkels & McCoy West, LLC requests this court take judicial notice of Plaintiff Ismael Cruz’s Class Action Complaint, filed on November 7, 2024, and Joint Report Regarding Outcome of Mediation, filed on April 23, 2026, in Ismael Cruz v. Henkels & McCoy West, LLC; Henkels & McCoy, Inc.; and Does 1 through 100, inclusive, Case No. 24STCV29321 (Exhs. A and B) and Plaintiff Ismael Cruz’s Representative Complaint for Violation of the Private Attorneys General Act, filed on November 5, 2024, in Ismael Cruz v. Henkels & McCoy West, LLC; Henkels & McCoy, Inc.; and Does 1 through 100, inclusive, Case No. 24STCV29074 (Exh. C).

 

The court GRANTS the request for judicial notice. (Evid. Code §§452.)

 

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

 

In opposition, Plaintiff submits objections to the declaration of Rene Cedillo. The court OVERRULES the objections.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Defendant Henkels & McCoy West, LLC[1] move this court for an order compelling arbitration of Plaintiff Orlando Escobar’s first through thirteenth causes of action in his First Amended Complaint pursuant to the collective bargaining agreement (“CBA”) governing his employment with Defendant: the 2022–2026 Master Labor Agreement between the Southern California District Council of Laborers and signatory contractors (the “MLA”). Defendant also seeks to dismiss, stay or otherwise abate Plaintiff’s fourteenth cause of action for violation of PAGA, pursuant to Labor Code § 2699.6 and the overlapping Cruz v. Henkels & McCoy West, LLC et al., Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 24STCV29074 representative settlement proceedings.

 

Parties may be compelled to arbitrate a dispute upon the court finding that: (1) there was a valid agreement to arbitrate between the parties; and (2) said agreement covers the controversy or controversies in the parties’ dispute.¿ (CCP §1281.2; Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004)¿118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961.) Because the right to arbitration depends upon contract, the party seeking arbitration bears the initial burden of proving that the parties actually agreed to arbitrate the instant dispute.¿ (Hotels Nevada v. L.A. Pacific Center, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 754, 761.)¿ If the moving party does so, the burden shifts to the opposing party to show that the subject agreement is unenforceable.¿ (Id. at 761.)¿ The court “sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination.”¿ (Engalla v. Permanente Med. Grp., Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 972.)¿¿¿ 

 

Defendant argues Plaintiff is bound by the MLA and its arbitration provisions. The MLA is a CBA between the Associated General Contractors of California, Inc., the Building Industry Association of Southern California, the Southern California Contractors Association, Inc., and the Southern California District Council of Laborers. (MLA p. 1.) As part of the MLA, Appendix C broadly requires arbitration of claims arising under Wage Order 16, Labor Code § 2699.5 et seq., derivative Business and Professions Code § 17200 claims, and other wage-hour disputes. (MLA app. C.) The MLA also requires arbitration of discrimination, retaliation, harassment, and wrongful-termination claims arising under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (“Title VII”), Age Discrimination in Employment Act (“ADEA”), Americans with Disabilities Act (“ADA”), Family and Medical Leave Act (“FMLA”), California Fair Employment and Housing Act (“FEHA”), California Family Rights Act (“CFRA”), and related statutes. (MLA arts. I.B.1, I.P, art. XV.A, art. XVI.F–G, app. C.) Because Plaintiff’s claims fall squarely within the MLA’s arbitration provisions, Defendant maintains Plaintiff should be compelled to arbitration. Defendant also notes it is a basic principle of collective bargaining law that an employee within the bargaining unit is bound by the terms of a valid CBA even if he is not a formal signatory to the agreement. (Florio v. City of Ontario (2005) 130 Cal. App. 4th 1462, 1466.) And where a CBA clearly requires arbitration of covered statutory claims, represented employees may be compelled to arbitrate those claims. (14 Penn Plaza LLC v. Pyett (2009) 556 U.S. 247, 257–58, 274; Vasquez v. Superior Court (2000) 80 Cal. App. 4th 430, 434.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues the motion should be denied because Defendant did not properly authenticate the MLA as Defendant failed to show that it is the writing that Defendant claims to be. Even if the court finds Defendant did properly authenticate the MLA, Plaintiff argues the motion should still be denied because Plaintiff’s claims are outside the scope of the MLA. Specifically, Section B of Appendix C provides that “[n]o Statutory Dispute subject to this Appendix C shall be recognized unless called to the attention of and, in the event it is not resolved, confirmed in writing by the individual employee to the individual Contractor . . . The employee and Contractor shall give written notice to the Union of the existence of a Statutory Dispute.” Plaintiff maintains they filed the instant case against Defendant, Mastec North America, Inc., Mark Partida, Lizeth Marcial, and DOES 1-20, inclusive, not against the Defendant alone as “the individual Contractor,” as expressly required by the MLA. Thus, it is equally undisputed that Plaintiff’s claims in the instant case fall outside the scope of the MLA. Similarly, Plaintiff argues numerous cases have held arbitration provisions in a collective bargaining agreement cannot prevent an individual union member from pursuing his or her statutory and/or common-law claims in a court of law.

 

First, the court finds the MLA has been properly authenticated. As noted by Defendant, authentication may be shown by direct or circumstantial evidence, including the document’s contents and the circumstances of its production. (People v. Goldsmith (2014) 59 Cal. 4th 258, 267–68.) Mr. Cedillo’s declaration provides that Mr. Cedillo is a Director, Human Resources with Henkels & McCoy West, LLC and as part of his job duties, he has knowledge over the employment, payroll, dispatch, and job records relating to Plaintiff Christopher Escobar along with access to Defendant’s personnel files, payroll records, union dispatch records, daily work reports, timesheets, and labor-agreement records. (Cedillo Decl. ¶¶1-3.) The declaration further states he is “familiar with the 2022–2026 Master Labor Agreement between the Southern California District Council of Laborers and signatory contractors (the “MLA”), including the portions of the MLA governing covered work, wages, hours, working conditions, and grievance/arbitration procedures.” (Cedillo Decl. ¶3.) Plaintiff does not dispute that he is a member of the union mentioned in the MLA. The court finds this sufficient to show that the parties are subject to the MLA. Thus, Defendants have carried the burden to show the existence of an arbitration agreement with Plaintiff. Moreover, the California Supreme Court has directly addressed the issue of whether employees within the bargaining unit are bound by the terms of a valid CBA even if they are not individual signatories. (See San Lorenzo Education Assn. v. Wilson (1982) 32 Cal.3d 841 holding that “[i]t is a fundamental principle of such a system that a member of a bargaining unit is bound by the terms of a valid collective bargaining agreement, though he is not formally a party to it and may not even belong to the union which negotiated it.”) Accordingly, it does not matter that Plaintiff had never seen the MLA, never heard of the MLA, and was unaware that such an agreement existed. (See Wilson-Davis v. SSP America, Inc. (2021) 62 Cal.App.5th 1080.)

 

Next, the court finds Appendix C of the MLA covers Plaintiff’s claims. Appendix C expressly states that the agreement to arbitrate covered discrimination, harassment, retaliation, and related claims includes claims asserted against the Employer’s employees, officers, or owners. It also expressly identifies the categories and statutes at issue: Wage Order 16, Labor Code wage-hour claims, derivative section 17200 claims, meal and rest periods, wage payment, FEHA, Title VII, ADEA, ADA, FMLA, CFRA, retaliation, wrongful termination in violation of public policy, and Labor Code section 1102.5. (Cedillo Decl., Exh. A, app. C.) The provision Plaintiff relies on, “[n]o Statutory Dispute subject to this Appendix C shall be recognized unless called to the attention of and, in the event it is not resolved, confirmed in writing by the individual employee to the individual Contractor . . . The employee and Contractor shall give written notice to the Union of the existence of a Statutory Dispute[]” does not narrow the claims subject to arbitration as Plaintiff suggests. The Appendix C as a whole allows the arbitrator to combine related disputes involving multiple contractors or entities for efficiency while maintaining the individual nature of each claim.

 

As for Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2(c), the court stays the remaining proceedings while Henkels and Plaintiff go to arbitration.

 

PAGA

 

Defendant argues Appendix C expressly waives PAGA and requires covered statutory disputes to proceed through the MLA’s arbitration procedure. (MLA, app. C.) It provides:

 

Pursuant to California Labor Code Section 2699.6, the Parties hereby expressly and unambiguously waive the provisions of the California Private Attorneys General Act (PAGA), Labor Code Section 2698, et seq., and agree that none of the provisions of that statute apply to any of the employees covered by the collective bargaining agreement between the undersigned Parties (the ‘Agreement’).

 

The Parties further agree that this Agreement prohibits any and all violations of the California Labor Code sections identified in Labor Code §§ 2699.5 and 2699(f) as well as any others that would be redressable by PAGA, and that such claims shall be resolved exclusively through the Arbitration procedures in this Appendix C and shall not be brought in a court of law or before any administrative agency such as the California Labor Commissioner.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues the MLA does not qualify for Section 2699.6 because it fails to “[a]uthorize[ ] the arbitrator to award any and all remedies otherwise available under” the PAGA statute. Plaintiff also argues the MLA is procedurally and substantively unconscionable because employees had no way to opt out of the MLA or otherwise not to be covered by it if they work in the construction industry in Southern California; Plaintiff never received the American Arbitration Association Employment Arbitration Rules and Mediation Procedures; it waives all representative PAGA actions and is not a valid collective bargaining agreement under Labor Code Section 2699.6; it does not authorize the arbitrator to award any and all remedies that would otherwise be available in trial court; and it does not contain a severability clause.

 

The court disagrees on the points raised by Plaintiff. First, the MLA language here tracks the requirements of California Labor Code Section 2699.6 which creates a narrow, industry-specific exemption from PAGA for employees in the construction industry. Moreover, the court finds the MLA is not unconscionable. As noted by Defendant, the mere failure to provide AAA rules is insufficient to establish procedural unconscionability. Appendix C itself discloses the governing arbitral forum, requires a neutral arbitrator, requires Henkels to pay AAA and arbitrator fees, preserves statutory fee-shifting, and authorizes statutory remedies and therefore, Plaintiff’s substantive challenges relate to terms clearly stated in the agreement itself. (See Peng v. First Republic Bank (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1462; Baltazar v. Forever 21, Inc. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 1237.) Similarly, none of Plaintiff’s contentions establish substantive unconscionability.

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion to compel arbitration and stay the pending proceeding is GRANTED.


[1] Defendants note Defendant Mastec North America, Inc. (“Mastec”) contends it never employed Plaintiff and was erroneously named in this action.
