Manning v. Taylor

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff's Motion to Strike Defendants' Demurrer and to Enforce Entry of Default, with Sanctions, is DENIED.

Plaintiff's Motion for Sanctions Against Defendants and Counsel is DENIED.

Introduction

On July 18, 2024, Plaintiff filed the Complaint in this action.

On October 4, 2024, Defendants filed a Demurrer and Motion to Strike the

Complaint.

On October 7, 2024, Plaintiff filed a Request for Entry of Default.

On October 22, 2024, the Clerk rejected the Request for Entry of Default on the ground that Defendants had filed responsive pleadings.

On August 1, 2025, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint.

On November 4, 2025, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion to Strike Defendants' Demurrer and to Enforce Entry of Default, with Sanctions.

On January 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion for Sanctions Against Defendants and Counsel.

On February 13, 2026, Defendants filed a Demurrer and Motion to Strike the First Amended Complaint.

On September 9, 2026, Defendants filed Oppositions to both Motions.

On September 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed Responses to both Oppositions.

Legal Standard

Strike

“The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: (a) Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. [or] (b) Strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) An “immaterial allegation” means “irrelevant matter” as that term is used in Section 436. (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10(c).) “An immaterial allegation in a pleading is any of the following: (1) An allegation that is not essential to the¿statement of a¿claim or defense. (2) An allegation that is¿neither¿pertinent to¿nor¿supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense. (3) A demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint or cross-complaint.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10(b).)

Sanctions

A motion for sanctions can be brought against an attorney or a party for presenting to the court a frivolous paper or a paper for an improper purpose. (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7.)

To prevail on a motion for sanctions under section 128.7, the movant must establish that (1) a challengeable paper (2) was presented to the court and (3) the paper was frivolous (i.e., without legal or factual merit) or brought for an improper purpose. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 128.7(b), (c).)

Section 128.7 is modeled after, and nearly identical to, the 1993 amended version of FRCP 11. (Guillemin v. Stein (2002) 104 Cal.App.4th 156, 167 (Guillemin); see Bucur v. Ahmad (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 175, 190 (Bucur).) When the California Legislature enacted § 128.7, it also adopted the Advisory Committee’s notes to Rule 11 as a statement of legislative intent. (Barnes v. Department of Corr. (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 126, 132 (Barnes).) Thus, cases interpreting the amended FRCP 11 and the Advisory Committee’s notes to Rule 11 can be used as persuasive authority. (See Bucur, supra, at p. 190; see, e.g., Barnes, supra, at p. 136 [court relied on Advisory Committee’s notes to conclude that formal, rather than informal, notice is required under § 128.7].)

“As with Rull 11 (28 U.S.C.) sanctions, Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7 sanctions should be ‘made with restraint’ [citation], and are not mandatory even if a claim is frivolous. [Citations.] Further, when determining whether sanctions should be imposed, the issue is not merely whether the party would prevail on the underlying factual or legal argument. Instead, courts should apply an objective test of reasonableness, including whether ‘any reasonable attorney would agree that [the claim] is totally and completely without merit.’ [Citations.] Thus, the fact that a plaintiff fails to provide a sufficient showing to overcome a demurrer or to survive summary judgment is not, in itself, enough to warrant the imposition of sanctions. [Citations.]” (Peake v. Underwood (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 428, 448.)

CCP section 128.5 provides that “[a] trial court may order a party, the party’s attorney, or both, to pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by another party as a result of actions or tactics, made in bad faith, that are frivolous or solely intended to cause unnecessary delay.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.5(a).) “‘Actions or tactics’ include, but are not limited to, the making or opposing of motions or the filing and service of a complaint, cross-complaint, answer, or other responsive pleading.” (Id., § 128.5(b)(1).) “‘Frivolous’ means totally and completely without merit or for the sole purpose of harassing an opposing party.” (Id., § 128.5(b)(2).)

Motion to Strike Defendants' Demurrer and to Enforce Entry of Default

Plaintiff Stacy L. Manning moves to strike Defendants' October 4, 2024 Demurrer as "procedurally void," to compel entry of default nunc pro tunc as of October 7, 2024, to vacate Defendants' pending motions, and to set a default prove-up hearing. Plaintiff contends that Defendants failed to respond to the Complaint, that the Clerk had a ministerial duty to enter default upon receipt of her request, and that Defendants' pleadings are void because they were filed on behalf of a dissolved corporation.

Procedural Defects

As an initial matter, the relief Plaintiff seeks has already been sought and resolved. Plaintiff's Request for Entry of Default was rejected on October 22, 2024, and the Court thereafter considered and ruled upon Defendants' Demurrer on July 25, 2025 — a disposition that necessarily proceeded from the determination that Defendants were not in default and that their responsive pleading was properly before the Court.

The motion appears to be an improper motion for reconsideration. A motion asking the Court to undo those determinations is an application to reconsider, whatever its label. (Passavanti v. Williams, supra, 225 Cal.App.3d at pp. 1605-1606.) Plaintiff identifies no new or different facts, circumstances, or law. The motion re-argues the same October 2024 chronology on the same authorities previously considered. Section 1008, subdivision (e) makes these requirements jurisdictional, and a party may not evade them by recaptioning a reconsideration request as a motion to strike.

Independently, the motion seeks relief the Court can no longer grant. The pleading Plaintiff asks the Court to strike is Defendants' Demurrer to the original Complaint. That Complaint was superseded when Plaintiff filed her First Amended Complaint on August 1, 2025, and it ceased at that point to have any effect as a pleading. (State Compensation Ins. Fund v. Superior Court, supra, 184 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1130-1131.) The Demurrer directed to it was likewise spent; there is nothing remaining to strike. (Sylmar Air Conditioning v. Pueblo Contracting Services, Inc., supra, 122 Cal.App.4th at p. 1054.) The operative challenge to the operative pleading is Defendants' Demurrer and Motion to Strike the First Amended

Complaint, filed February 13, 2026, which this motion does not address and which was filed months after this motion was served.

Nor may default be entered against parties who have appeared and are actively litigating the operative pleading.

The motion is DENIED on these grounds.

Motion for Sanctions

Plaintiff moves for sanctions "in the maximum amount permitted by law" against Defendants and counsel Elsa Larsen under Code of Civil Procedure sections 128.5, 128.7, and 177.5, and the Court's inherent authority, on the ground that counsel's meet and confer declaration mischaracterized service as "purported," invoked sections 430.41 and 435.5 on behalf of defaulted parties, and omitted material facts concerning corporate dissolution.

Notice

The notice of motion does not state the amount of monetary sanctions sought, and seeks sanctions against "Defendants" collectively without identifying the particular persons or entities targeted. Defendants invoke Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.040, which by its terms governs sanctions under the Civil Discovery Act. Whether or not that section applies, basic notice principles require that a party facing a monetary sanction be told what amount is sought against whom, supported by a declaration establishing the factual basis for that amount. Neither is supplied here.

Sections 128.7 and 128.5

Sanctions under section 128.7 require compliance with the twenty-one day safe harbor, which is mandatory and may not be excused. (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7, subd. (c)(1); Li v. Majestic Industry Hills LLC, supra, 177 Cal.App.4th at pp. 591, 595.) Plaintiff concedes she has not complied, stating in her motion that she "will comply with the 21-day safe harbor requirement prior to seeking an order under CCP § 128.7." The request is premature.

Plaintiff's alternative reliance on section 128.5 does not avoid the requirement. Where, as here, the asserted misconduct is a filing that could have been withdrawn or corrected, section 128.5 incorporates the same safe harbor. (Code Civ. Proc., §128.5, subd. (f)(1)(B); Nutrition Distribution, LLC v. Southern SARMs, Inc., supra, 20 Cal.App.5th at pp. 127-131.)

Substantively, section 128.5 requires bad faith conduct that is frivolous or undertaken solely to cause unnecessary delay. The declaration Plaintiff challenges was expressly authorized by section 430.41, subdivision (a)(2), and the responsive pleading it extended was filed within the statutory period. Availing oneself of a statutory extension and then filing a timely demurrer is not sanctionable conduct. That Defendants characterized service as "purported" reflects a disputed position on a contested issue, not a knowing misrepresentation.

Section 177.5

Section 177.5 authorizes sanctions for violation of a lawful court order without good cause. Plaintiff identifies no order that was violated. The section is inapplicable.

Inherent Authority

Sanctions under the Court's inherent authority for fraud upon the court require a showing of egregious misconduct that corrupts the judicial process itself. (See Stephen Slesinger, Inc. v. Walt Disney Co. (2007) 155 Cal.App.4th 736, 758-765.) Plaintiff's showing consists of her disagreement with Defendants' litigation positions and with rulings adverse to her. That does not approach the standard.

Mootness of the Underlying Premise

Finally, the premise of the motion — that Defendants stood in default when counsel filed her declaration — fails for the reasons stated above. No default was entered, and a defendant may file a responsive pleading at any time before entry of default. Defendants' Demurrer and Motion to Strike the First Amended Complaint was filed February 13, 2026, accepted for filing, and remains pending.

The motion for sanctions is DENIED.

Conclusion

Plaintiff's Motion to Strike Defendants' Demurrer and to Enforce Entry of Default is DENIED. The request for sanctions is DENIED.

Plaintiff's Motion for Sanctions Against Defendants and Counsel is DENIED.

Case Number: 24STCV34548    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: 224

Jackson v. General Motors LLC 

TENTATIVE RULING

The demurrer is SUSTAINED in part, with leave to amend. The Motion to Strike is GRANTED, with leave to amend. Plaintiff shall file her Amended Complaint within 20 days. 

The Case Management Conference is continued to February 4, 2027 at 8:30 a.m.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On December 30, 2024, Plaintiff filed a Complaint arising out of Plaintiff’s warranty contract with Defendant General Motors, LLC (“GM”) regarding a 2016 Chevrolet Silverado 2500, VIN 1GC1CWE8XGF154784 (“Subject Vehicle”).

On December 29, 2025 Plaintiff filed their First Amended Complaint.

On January 15, 2026 Defendants filed the instant Demurrer and Motion to Strike to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint (“FAC”)

On September 9, 2026 Plaintiff filed an Opposition to the instant motion.

On September 10, 2026 Defendants filed a Reply.

Meet and Confer

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (CCP §§ 430.41)

The Declaration of Xylon Quezada, executed on January 14, 2026, sets forth Defendant’s counsel meet and confer efforts and attempts to resolve the disputes informally by telephone. The Court finds that the parties conferred as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41

Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.¿ (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)¿When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context.¿ (Wilson v. Transit Authority of City of Sacramento (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 716, 720-21.)¿In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿“A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not on the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Id.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Fifth Cause of Action: Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment

Duty to Disclose

Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not pled a transactional relationship between GM and Plaintiff which gives rise to a duty to disclose.

The California Supreme Court “has described the necessary relationship giving rise to a duty to disclose as a ‘transaction’ between the plaintiff and defendant.” (BiglerEngler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 311.) Specifically, “[i]n¿transactions¿which do not involve fiduciary or confidential relations, a cause of action for non-disclosure of material facts may arise in at least three instances: (1) the defendant makes representations but does not disclose facts which materially qualify the facts disclosed, or which render his disclosure likely to mislead; (2)¿the facts are known or accessible only to defendant, and defendant knows they are not known to or reasonably discoverable by the plaintiff; (3) the defendant actively conceals discovery from the plaintiff.” (Ibid.)¿For example, “a duty to disclose may arise from the relationship between seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement.” (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336.)¿

Here, Plaintiff has alleged that Defendant issued warranties directly on Plaintiff’s vehicle, and the Subject Vehicle was purchased at GM's authorized retail dealership Community Chevrolet in Burbank, California (FAC, ¶¶ 6-7.) Thus, a transactional relationship exists pursuant to Dhital, wherein the court found that the plaintiffs' allegations were sufficient to establish a relationship requiring the manufacturer to disclose known defects. There, plaintiffs bought the car from a Nissan dealership, and Nissan backed the car with an express warranty. The court noted that Nissan's authorized dealerships were its agents for the purposes of selling Nissan vehicles to consumers (Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828).

Further, the claim is based on both Defendant’s superior knowledge and Defendant’s knowing and intentional concealment (Id. ¶¶ 84-96). Plaintiff has alleged Defendant had exclusive knowledge of, failed to disclose and concealed the existence of the transmission defect in order to ensure Plaintiff’s purchase of the Subject Vehicle. (Id.)

A duty to disclose has been pled.

Defendant’s demurrer is OVERRULED on this ground.

Specificity

However, even where a duty exists, a plaintiff must still plead the circumstances of fraudulent concealment with particularity. (Rattagan v. Uber Techs., Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 43–44.)

“‘The required elements for fraudulent concealment are (1) concealment or suppression of a material fact; (2) by a defendant with a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff; (3) the defendant intended to defraud the plaintiff by intentionally concealing or suppressing the fact; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he or she did if he or she had known of the concealed or suppressed fact; and (5) plaintiff sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact. [Citation.]’ [Citation.]” (Hambrick v. Healthcare Partners Medical Group, Inc. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 124, 162.)

Ordinarily, “fraud must be pleaded specifically; general and conclusory allegations do not suffice.” (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.). “This particularity requirement necessitates pleading facts which show how, where, to whom, and by what means” the alleged fraud occurred. (Id.) The purpose of the particularity requirement is to “separate meritorious and nonmeritorious cases, if possible in advance of trial.” (Small v. Fritz Companies, Inc. (2003) 30 Cal.4th 167, 184.)¿¿¿¿

California courts apply the same specificity standard to evaluate the factual underpinnings of a fraudulent concealment claim at the pleading stage, even though the focus of inquiry shifts to the unique elements of the claim. (Rattagan v. Uber Techs., Inc. (2024) 17 Cal. 5th 1, 43–44, citing Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 347; Boschma v. Home Loan Center, Inc. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 230, 248; Cansino v. Bank of America (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1462, 1472.) In Rattagan, the court held that it had to determine whether the plaintiff alleged a sufficient factual basis for establishing a duty of disclosure on the part of the defendant independent of the parties' contract. If the duty allegedly arose by virtue of the parties' relationship and defendant's exclusive knowledge or access to certain facts, as the plaintiff alleged there, the Court found that the complaint also had to include specific allegations establishing all the required elements, including (1) the content of the omitted facts, (2) defendant's awareness of the materiality of those facts, (3) the inaccessibility of the facts to plaintiff, (4) the general point at which the omitted facts should or could have been revealed, and (5) justifiable and actual reliance, either through action or forbearance, based on the defendant's omission. “[M]ere conclusionary allegations that the omissions were intentional and for the purpose of defrauding and deceiving plaintiff[ ] ... are insufficient for the foregoing purposes.” (Goodman, at p. 347.) (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal. 5th at 43–44).

Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not pled this cause of action with the requisite specificity. Namely, Defendant argues that Plaintiff failed to allege the specific “facts” that GM allegedly failed to disclose; allegations establishing that GM knew of those “facts” at the time Plaintiff purchased the vehicle; what advertisements, brochures, or other materials where GM could have disclosed the allegedly omitted “facts” that Plaintiff reviewed and relied upon in purchasing the Subject Vehicle; how long prior to purchasing the vehicle she viewed them; and whether those materials, if any, were prepared by GM or someone else (such as a dealership). Defendant further argues that Plaintiff failed to allege Defendant’s intent to defraud.

Here, the Court agrees that Plaintiff does not plead the fraudulent concealment claim with the requisite particularity. Although Plaintiff alleges that GM had superior knowledge of the defect through pre-production and post-production testing data; early consumer complaints about the Transmission Defect; aggregate warranty data; testing conducted in response to these complaints; as well as warranty repair and part replacements data, (FAC ¶ 88) the pleading does not specify when, where, or how GM’s alleged omissions occurred, nor does it identify which advertisements, marketing materials, or representations Plaintiff reviewed, when she reviewed them, or how those materials were prepared or disseminated by GM as opposed to the dealership. Further, Plaintiff does not allege the general point at which disclosure should or could have been made.

Further, while Plaintiff alleges that GM’s concealment induced her to purchase the vehicle and that she would not have done so had she known of the defect (FAC ¶ 94), the FAC does not allege any facts establishing Defendant’s intent to defraud.

The demurrer is SUSTAINED on this ground, with leave to amend.

Statute of Limitations

Finally, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s Fraudulent Concealment claim is time-barred.

To sustain demurrer to the complaint based on a statute of limitations defect, “the defect must clearly and affirmatively appear on the face of the complaint.... A demurrer will not lie where the action may be but is not necessarily barred.” (Citizens for a Responsible Caltrans Decision v. Department of Transportation (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 1103, 1117.)

The fraudulent concealment claim is not time-barred on the face of the complaint. Under Code of Civil Procedure § 338(d), a claim for fraud accrues when the aggrieved party discovers, or reasonably should have discovered, the facts

constituting the fraud. Here, Plaintiff alleges that the defect became apparent only after repeated failed repair attempts, and that she was unaware of GM’s concealment until shortly before filing the complaint. (FAC ¶ 48.) Plaintiff also invokes several tolling doctrines, including the discovery rule, fraudulent concealment, the repair doctrine, and Emergency Rules Related to COVID-19. (FAC ¶¶ 47-63). These allegations are sufficient at the pleading stage, as a demurrer based on statute of limitations can only be sustained where the defect clearly and affirmatively appears on the face of the complaint.

Defendant’s demurrer is OVERRULED on this ground.

Motion to Strike

Defendants move to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages from the FAC on the grounds the FAC fails to state facts sufficient to support the recovery of punitive damages.

Civil Code § 3294(a) authorizes the recovery of punitive damages in non-contract cases “where it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice…

(1) “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.

(2) “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.

(3) “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the party of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.

(Civ. Code § 3294(c)(1)-(3).)

As reviewed above, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s fraud-based claim is not sufficiently pled to survive general demurrer.

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s Motion to Strike Plaintiff’s punitive damage request based on Plaintiff’s Fraudulent Concealment claim. Because the Court has allowed Plaintiff leave to amend to address the issues in the fifth cause of action and punitive damages may be awarded upon a properly pled fraud repair claim (See Civil Code § 3294), the ruling as to Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action is without prejudice – Plaintiff may plead punitive damages upon amendment of her fifth cause of action.

Defendant’s Motion to Strike Punitive Damages from Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint is GRANTED as to the fifth cause of action for Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment, with leave to amend.

Conclusion

The demurrer is SUSTAINED in part, with leave to amend. The Motion to Strike is GRANTED, with leave to amend.Plaintiff shall file her Amended Complaint within 20 days.

Case Number: 25STCV03769    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: 224

Reyes v. Strategic Legacy Management Corp. et al.,

Below, the Court posts two tentative rulings.

1. APPLICATION FOR NOTICE BY PUBLICATION

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff’s motion to serve Defendant Strategic Legacy by publication is DENIED W/OUT PREJUDICE.

ANALYSIS

Legal Standard

If service of a summons by other means proves impossible, service may be effected by publication, upon the trial court's approval. (Watts v. Crawford (1995) 10 Cal.4th 743, 748–749 & fn. 5 (Watts).) Code of Civil Procedure (CCP) section 415.50 provides for service by publication “if upon affidavit it appears to the satisfaction of the court in which the action is pending that the party to be served cannot with reasonable diligence be served in another manner specified in this article and that either: [¶] (1) [a] cause of action exists against the party upon whom service is to be made or he or she is a necessary or proper party to the action [or] [¶] (2) [t]he party to be served has or claims an interest in real or personal property in this state that is subject to the jurisdiction of the court or the relief demanded in the action consists wholly or in part in excluding the party from any interest in the property.” (CCP § 415.50, subd. (a).)

The plaintiff must establish reasonable diligence via the testimony of an individual with personal knowledge of the efforts to serve the defendant. (Olvera v. Olvera (1991) 232 Cal.App.3d 32, 42; Donel, Inc. v. Badalian (1978) 87 Cal.App.3d 327, 333 [“the question is simply whether [plaintiff] took those steps which a reasonable person who truly desired to give notice would have taken under the circumstances”].)¿“The term ‘reasonable diligence’ denotes a thorough, systematic investigation and inquiry conducted in good faith by the party or his agent or attorney.¿A number of honest attempts to learn defendant's whereabouts or his address by inquiry of relatives,...and by investigation of appropriate city and telephone directories, [voter registries, and assessor's office property indices situated near the defendant's last known location], generally are sufficient. These are the likely sources of information, and consequently must be searched before resorting to service by publication.¿ Before allowing a plaintiff to resort to service by publication, the courts necessarily require him to show exhaustive attempts to locate the defendant, for it is generally recognized that service by publication rarely results in actual notice.”¿(Watts, supra, 74310 Cal.4th at p. 749, fn. 5 [internal citations omitted].)¿

Discussion

Plaintiff seeks to serve Strategic Legacy Management Corp. (“Strategic Legacy”) by publication in the Los Angeles Daily Journal, a newspaper of general circulation in Los Angeles County.

In support of the motion, Plaintiff submits: (1) the declaration of Plaintiff’s counsel Onica V. Cole (Cole Decl.) and (2) Defendant’s registered address with the California Secretary of State (Exh. A).

CCP § 415.50, subd. (a), requires the moving party to show by affidavit that “[a] cause of action exists against the party upon whom service is to be made or he or she is a necessary or proper party to the action.” (CCP § 415.50, subd. (a).) Verified pleadings, standing alone, are insufficient to satisfy this jurisdictional requirement; the existence of a cause of action must be proved by an affidavit containing a statement of facts constituting legal evidence with a tendency to prove the existence of a cause of action against the defendant to be served. (Olvera, supra, 232 Cal.App.3d at p. 42, fn. 9.) Here, Plaintiff’s counsel declaration does not set forth that a cause of action exists against the Defendant. Therefore, the Court finds that Plaintiff has not satisfied this requirement pursuant to CCP § 415.50, subd. (a). (Application, Cole Decl.)

Turning to reasonable diligence, Plaintiff’s counsel obtained Defendant’s last known address from the California Secretary of State. Defendant’s last known address is 221 E. 12th St., Los Angeles, CA. Plaintiff’s counsel states that a process server attempted and failed to personally serve the Defendant at this address because they no longer have offices at this location. Plaintiff provides no corroboration about said attempts, such as a declaration of diligence from the process server. As a result, the Court finds that Plaintiff has not established a sufficient showing of reasonable diligence. (Application, Exh. A, Cole. Decl.)

Finally, Plaintiff requests publication in the Los Angeles Daily Journal on the grounds that it is most likely to give notice to Defendant because “the newspaper covers the area of Defendant’s last known residence…” (Application) Defendant’s last known address is located in Los Angeles County, and Los Angeles Daily Journal is circulated in said county. Thus, the Court agrees that this newspaper is likely to give Defendant actual notice. (Application)

Conclusion

The applicant must demonstrate that service cannot reasonably be accomplished by any of the methods specified in CCP § 415.10 through 415.40. Here, the application explains why personal service, substituted service, or service by mail could not be accomplished. However, Plaintiff’s counsel fails to provide evidence regarding the attempts to serve the Defendant. The Court cannot make a decision on the incomplete information provided by Plaintiff. Thus, The Court finds that Plaintiff has not made a sufficient showing of reasonable diligence as required by CCP § 415.50. Plaintiff also failed to establish, by affidavit, the existence of a cause of action against the Defendant to be served as required by CCP § 415.50, subd. (a). Plaintiff explains why the Los Angeles Daily Journal, a county circulated newspaper, is most likely to give Defendant actual notice as required by CCP § 415.50, subd. (b).

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff’s motion to serve Defendant Strategic Legacy by publication is DENIED without PREJUDICE.

2. PLAINTIFF'S MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff's Motion for Summary Adjudication of the First Cause of Action is DENIED.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On February 11, 2025, Plaintiff Angelica Reyes filed the Complaint in this action against Defendants Strategic Legacy Management Corp., Jose Lopez, and Patricia Lopez, arising out of her tenancy at 233 West 42nd Street, Los Angeles. The First Cause of Action alleges violation of the Los Angeles Rent Stabilization Ordinance (unregistered unit).

On October 28, 2025, the Court heard and denied Plaintiff’s Motion for Summary Adjudication of the First Cause of Action, on the grounds that the Motion was procedurally defective.

On April 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed another Motion for Summary Adjudication of the First Cause of Action, originally noticed for hearing on July 9, 2026.

On April 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion for Summary Adjudication of the First Cause of Action, noticed for hearing on July 16, 2026.

No opposition has been filed.

MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

Plaintiff Angelica Reyes moves for summary adjudication of the First Cause of Action for violation of the RSO (unregistered unit), Los Angeles Municipal Code ("LAMC") sections 151.05(A) and 151.10(A).

As an initial matter, the Court notes that two identical Motions for Summary Adjudication have been filed by Plaintiff, with separate reservation numbers. The first was filed April 6, 2026 and originally reserved for hearing on July 9, 2026 (Reservation No. 607220223287). The instant motion was filed April 14, 2026 and reserved for hearing on July 16, 2026 (Reservation No. 060627727227). The two motions are the same in substance and rest on the same memorandum of points and authorities, the same Declaration of Angelica Reyes, and the same Exhibits 101 through 106. The Court addresses only the motion reserved for hearing on July 16, 2026 (Reservation No. 060627727227) and takes the other motion off calendar as moot.

Legal Standard

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial.¿(Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843). In analyzing such motions, courts must apply a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent's claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.”¿(Hinesley¿v.¿Oakshade¿Town Center¿(2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294).¿ Thus, summary judgment or summary adjudication is granted when, after the Court’s consideration of the evidence set forth in the papers and all reasonable inferences accordingly, no triable issues of fact exist and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.¿(CCP § 437c(c);¿Villa v.¿McFarren¿(1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 733, 741).¿

As to each claim as framed by the complaint, the party¿moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate an essential element.¿(Scalf¿v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc.¿(2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520). Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.”¿(Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc.¿(2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389). A motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication must be denied where the moving party's evidence does not prove all material facts, even in the absence of any opposition or where the opposition is weak.¿(See¿Leyva v. Superior Court¿(1985) 164 Cal.App.3d 462, 475;¿Salesguevara¿v. Wyeth Labs., Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 379, 384, 387.)¿¿¿

Once the¿moving¿party has met the burden, the burden shifts to the opposing party¿to show via specific facts that a triable issue of material facts exists as to a cause of action or a defense thereto.¿(CCP § 437c(o)(2)). When a party cannot establish an essential element or defense, a court must grant a motion for summary adjudication.¿(CCP § 437c(o)(1)-(2)).¿

A moving plaintiff meets his or her burden of showing that there is no defense to a cause of action “if that party has proved each element of the cause of action entitling the party to judgment on the cause of action. Once the plaintiff or cross-complainant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the defendant or cross-defendant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto. The defendant or cross-defendant shall not rely upon the allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists but, instead, shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (CCP § 437c(p)(1).)

First Cause of Action: Violation of LAMC §§ 151.05(A) and 151.10(A)

LAMC section 151.05(A) provides in relevant part that "no landlord shall demand or accept rent for a rental unit without first serving a copy of a valid [rental unit] registration or annual [rental unit] registration renewal statement on the tenant of that rental unit." LAMC section 151.11(B) permits a tenant to withhold rent otherwise lawfully due and owing where the landlord has failed to comply with section 151.05(A). Once the landlord complies, the tenant becomes obligated to pay current rent and any back rent withheld. (Lyles v. Sangadeo-Patel (2014) 225 Cal.App.4th 759, 765-766.)

LAMC section 151.10(A) supplies the damages remedy. It provides that any person who demands, accepts, or retains any payment of rent in excess of the maximum rent or maximum adjusted rent in violation of the chapter is liable in a civil action for three times the amount by which the payments exceed the maximum rent or maximum adjusted rent, together with reasonable attorney's fees and costs as determined by the court.

Plaintiff moves for summary adjudication on the grounds that (1) the unit was unregistered throughout the limitations period, (2) no registration or annual renewal statement was ever served on her, (3) Defendants nevertheless demanded and accepted approximately $52,879.00 in rent between February 2022 and February 2024, and (4) $5,931.56 of that amount constituted rent in excess of the maximum lawful rent, entitling her to treble damages of $17,794.68, restitution, and fees.

Plaintiff provides the following evidence in support. Plaintiff testifies that she has resided at the property since 2007, that the unit is subject to the RSO, that she paid approximately $52,879.00 in rent between February 2022 and February 2024, that she was never served with a registration or annual renewal statement in 2021, 2022, 2023, or 2024, that no such notice was posted at the premises, and that she first observed a registration statement only after May 30, 2025. (Reyes Decl., ¶¶ 2-10.) Plaintiff further relies on the 2025 Statement of Registration issued by LAHD (Exh. 104), an LAHD declaration regarding the property's registration status (Exh. 105), Defendants' Answer (Exh. 103), and an LAHD rent investigation, letter, and ledger (Exh. 106).

Evidence of Excess Rent

Plaintiff asserts that LAHD "conclusively determined" $5,931.56 in overcharges during 2022 to 2024, consisting of $34.72 for 2022, $5,518.72 for 2023, and $378.12 for 2024. (Exh. 106.)

The $5,931.56 figure does not appear in Exhibit 106. It is the sum of three annual entries on the "Total Overpaid by Year" line of the enclosed ledger. The ledger tracks the difference between legal rent plus SCEP fee and amounts actually paid, month by month, from 2016 through February 2024. It reflects both positive and negative entries. The ledger's bottom line is "Total Rent Reimbursement Due $2,316.72," and the accompanying letter states that "the maximum amount owed by the tenant between January 2020 and February 2024 is $2316.72." (Exh. 106.) On its face, Exhibit 106 does not conclusively establish $5,931.56 in recoverable excess rent. At most it raises questions requiring resolution by a trier of fact.

Plaintiff asks the Court to adjudicate the First Cause of Action, establish Defendants' liability and her entitlement to statutory remedies, and defer the amount of further overcharges to trial or post-judgment proceedings.

Plaintiff contends that “the precise calculation of any such further unlawful amounts can be addressed at trial or in post-judgment proceedings; it does not create a triable issue as to Defendants’ liability.” However, Plaintiff fails to cite to, nor is the Court aware of, any legal authority supporting that proposition. Section 437c(f)(1) permits summary adjudication of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for punitive damages as specified in Civil Code section 3294, or an issue of duty, and only where the motion completely disposes of that cause of action, defense, claim, or issue. Summary adjudication cannot be granted in favor of a plaintiff on liability alone, with damages left for later determination, as damages are an element of the cause of action. (Paramount Petroleum Corp. v. Superior Court (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 226, 238, 242-243.)

A plaintiff moving for summary adjudication must therefore establish the amount of damages as a matter of law. (Id. at p. 243.)

Plaintiff has not done so. She concedes that amounts beyond the ledger period remain to be proven, and the ledger itself does not establish the excess rent she claims.

Plaintiff has not met her initial burden of proving each element of the First Cause of Action. The evidence she submits does not establish that Defendants demanded, accepted, or retained rent in excess of the maximum lawful rent in the amount claimed. The relief she seeks would not completely dispose of the cause of action in any event. Further, Plaintiff’s supporting exhibits appear to be unauthenticated.

The burden therefore does not shift to Defendants to show that a triable issue of material fact exists, although Defendants fail to oppose the motion. (CCP § 437c(p)(1).) The motion must be denied even in the absence of opposition, because Plaintiff's evidence does not prove all material facts. (Leyva, supra, 164 Cal.App.3d at p. 475; Salasguevara, supra, 222 Cal.App.3d at pp. 384, 387.)

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff's Motion for Summary Adjudication of the First Cause of Action is DENIED.

Case Number: 25STCV11038    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: 224

Maxwell Philip Kagan v. Kevin Chang

TENTATIVE RULING

The motion to quash is DENIED. The alternative request to modify is GRANTED IN PART, and the Court MODIFIES the three July 9, 2026 subpoenas as set forth below.

The request for sanctions is DENIED.

Introduction

On April 15, 2025, Plaintiff filed this action arising from an automobile collision that occurred on February 3, 2024. The Complaint alleges motor vehicle negligence and general negligence.

On June 4, 2026, Defendant served subpoenas on three Kaiser Permanente custodians of records.

On August 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion to Quash.

On September 2, 2026, Defendant Kevin Chang filed an opposition.

On September 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

Legal Standard

CCP section 1987.1 states that “[i]f a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b), or upon the court’s own motion after giving counsel notice and an opportunity to be heard, may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1(a).) “In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.” (Id.) Subdivision (b) provides that a party may make a motion pursuant to subdivision (a). (Id., § 1987.1(b).)

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1345 provides that “[a]ny motion involving the content of a discovery request or the responses to such a request must be accompanied by a separate statement. The motions that require a separate statement include a motion . . . To compel or to quash the production of documents or tangible things at a deposition[.]” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(a)(5).)

Discussion

Scope of the Requests

The three subpoenas at issue were all issued July 9, 2026. Item 3 of each directs the custodian to Attachment 3 and states that the records to be produced pertain to Plaintiff "from 02/03/2014 to present."

The subpoenas request the following categories of documents:

· All documents and records of whatever character, and all reproductions, including reports, records, ledgers, notes in written or electronic form, charts, correspondence and communications in written or electronic form, letters, handwritten notes, diaries, contracts, agreements, brochures, maps, diagrams, and photographs;

· All video, radiological examinations and imaging studies, scans, myelograms, ultrasounds, films, MRIs and CTs, statistical charts, illustrations, publications, and mechanical, electronic, or photographic transcriptions;

· All invoices, invoice receipts, books of account, bills, assignments of bills, invoices and accounts receivable, liens, payments, loan and lending agreements, cash advance documents, fee schedules, third-party lien holders, factoring companies, purchasers of accounts receivable, financing companies, and documents evidencing the negotiation of medical charges;

· All treatment authorizations, diagnosis and prognosis records, informed consent and pain diagrams, admission sheets, hospital charts, and emergency room records;

· All communications from any of the patient's other health care providers who have treated or are treating the patient;

· All operative and anesthesia log notes, vitals monitoring, supply records, patient sign-in sheets, progress reports and notes, lab reports, physicians' charts, nurses' charts, and records of any other medical personnel that refer, relate, or otherwise pertain to the examination, diagnosis, treatment, and care of Plaintiff.

Attachment 3 then supplies three qualifiers. Under "TIMEFRAME," it states: any and all records for the past ten years. Under "Treated at," it lists six Kaiser facilities in Panorama City, North Hollywood, and Woodland Hills. And under a heading reading "BODY AREA LIMITATION," it asks the custodian to provide a breakdown of films and studies reflecting body parts, dates taken, number of films or studies, and associated cost, for approval prior to production.

The Subpoenas Are Limited in Time

Plaintiff's Motion describes the subpoenas as "overbroad, unlimited in subject matter and time." As to time, the record does not support that characterization.

Item 3 of each subpoena limits production to records "from 02/03/2014 to present." The collision occurred February 3, 2024. The subpoenas therefore reach back ten years and no further. Attachment 3 states the same limitation in narrative form.

That limitation is the one Plaintiff himself requested. His counsel's July 24, 2026 meet-and-confer letter asked that the subpoenas be "limited to records generated within 10 years of the subject incident." His separate statement describes the subpoenas as requesting "broad ten-year medical records." And his Motion asks this Court to permit production "only of records dated February 3, 2014 through the present."

The Court finds that the subpoenas are limited in time, that the ten-year range is the range Plaintiff proposed, and that no further temporal limitation is warranted.

Relevance

For discovery purposes, information is relevant if it might reasonably assist a party in evaluating the case, preparing for trial, or facilitating settlement. (Gonzalez v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1539, 1546.) Admissibility is not the test, and information, unless privileged, is discoverable if it might reasonably lead to admissible evidence. (Id.) These rules are applied liberally in favor of discovery and, contrary to popular belief, fishing expeditions are permissible in some cases. (Id.)

The conditions Plaintiff has placed at issue are head pain and traumatic brain injury, neck pain, cervical spine pain, lumbar spine pain, right wrist pain, anxiety, and depression. By denying Request for Admission No. 4, Plaintiff affirmatively asserts a claim for psychological and emotional injury. Records concerning those conditions are relevant, and Plaintiff does not contend otherwise.

Privacy Objections

Medical records fall within the right of privacy protected by article I, section 1 of the California Constitution. (John B. v. Superior Court (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1177, 1198.) In ruling on a privacy objection in the context of discovery, the party asserting a privacy right must establish a legally protected privacy interest, an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and a threatened intrusion that is serious. (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552.) If the Court reaches the fourth step, it balances the competing considerations: the party seeking information may raise whatever legitimate and important countervailing interests disclosure may serve, and the party seeking protection may identify feasible alternatives that serve the same interests or protective measures that would diminish the loss of privacy. (Id.) Courts may not require the party seeking discovery to demonstrate a "compelling need" simply because discovery of facially private information is sought. (Id. at pp. 556-557.)

Plaintiff satisfies the threshold showing as to records outside the conditions he has tendered. He has a legally protected privacy interest in his medical records.

However, balancing the competing interests, the Court finds that disclosure is warranted as to the conditions Plaintiff has placed in controversy and matters bearing on their causation, aggravation, and apportionment. A plaintiff who seeks recovery for physical and mental injuries places his condition in controversy and waives the privilege and the privacy interest as to the conditions he has tendered. (Britt v. Superior Court (1978) 20 Cal.3d 844, 862-864; Vinson, supra, 43 Cal.3d at p. 839.) That waiver, however, is measured by the injuries the patient-litigant has brought before the court, and does not expose his entire medical history. (Britt, supra, 20 Cal.3d at pp. 862-864; Davis v. Superior Court (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1008, 1014-1017.).

Plaintiff’s Proposed Limitation

Plaintiff's separate statement proposes that the subpoenas be rewritten to seek "[o]nly those records pertaining to" the listed body parts, together with "all charges/billings made therefore" and only those films and studies "taken during the course of treatment to the aforementioned body parts only."

The Court declines to impose that limitation.

The Court finds that a condition-based limitation is not practicable. As set forth by Defendants, determining which progress notes concern the lumbar spine rather than another complaint documented at the same visit, and which charges are attributable to a given body part, requires clinical judgment a records custodian is not positioned to exercise. An order directing the custodian to make those determinations would not yield a narrower production so much as an unreliable one.

That the condition-based limitation is unavailable does not mean that no limitation is available. Kaiser identified three it could apply: date range, department, and provider.

Accordingly, the Court is inclined to modify the subpoenas as follows:

1. Within twenty (20) days of this order, Defendants shall serve amended subpoenas that, in addition to the existing date and facility limitations, limit production by Kaiser department and, where applicable, by provider. The amended subpoenas shall identify the departments from which records are sought, or alternatively the departments from which records are excluded, in terms the custodian can apply without review of record content.

2. Within twenty (20) days thereafter, Plaintiff shall serve responses to the subpoenas.

3. The February 3, 2014 to present date range stated in each subpoena shall remain unchanged.

Sanctions

Pursuant to Code of Civ. Proc. section 1987.2, subd. (a), the court may award sanctions “if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive.”

Here, because the Court is only inclined to modify the subpoena rather than quash it, the Court cannot say that Plaintiff has opposed the motion without substantial justification. The request for sanctions is DENIED.

Conclusion

The motion to quash is DENIED. The alternative request to modify is GRANTED IN PART. The Court hereby MODIFIES the subpoena to seek documents as follows:

1. Within twenty (20) days of this order, Defendants shall serve amended subpoenas that, in addition to the existing date and facility limitations, limit production by Kaiser department and, where applicable, by provider. The amended subpoenas shall identify the departments from which records are sought, or alternatively the departments from which records are excluded, in terms the custodian can apply without review of record content.

2. Within twenty (20) days thereafter, Plaintiff shall serve responses to the subpoenas.

3. The February 3, 2014 to present date range stated in Item 3 of each subpoena shall remain unchanged.

The request for sanctions is DENIED.
