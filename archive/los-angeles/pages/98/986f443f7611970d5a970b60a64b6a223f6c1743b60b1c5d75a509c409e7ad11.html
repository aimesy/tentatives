I.               BACKGROUND 

This case arises out of the alleged construction defects of a residence located at 17100 Rancho Street, Encino, California (“Property”). (See Fourth Amended Complaint (“4AC”), ¶¶ 1-2.) Plaintiff Evan Jehle, Trustee of the 17100 Rancho Trust, alleges he purchased the Property from Defendant developer Rancho Villa, LLC (“Rancho Villa”), on October 25, 2019, but that the Property was “replete with latent defects.” (See 4AC, ¶¶ 2, 27.) III.         LEGAL STANDARD

A defendant may move for judgment on the pleadings when the “complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against the defendant.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 438(b)(1) and (c)(1)(B)(ii).) The standard for ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings is essentially the same as that applicable to a general demurrer: that under the state of the pleadings, together with matters that may be judicially noticed, it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (See Schabarum v. California Legislature (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1216.) The court “must accept as true all material facts properly pleaded, but does not consider conclusions of law or fact, opinions, speculation, or allegations contrary to law or facts that are judicially noticed.” (Stevenson Real Estate Services, Inc. v. CB Richard Ellis Real Estate Services, Inc. (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1219-1220, citing Shea Homes Limited Partnership v. County of Alameda (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 1246, 1254.)

¿Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court a pleading can be amended successfully. (See id.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.)

IV.          MEET AND CONFER

Before filing a motion for judgment on the pleadings pursuant to this chapter, the moving party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 439(a).)

Oded Z.’s counsel attests he met and conferred by email and was unable to resolve his dispute. (See Declaration of Timothy R. Hanigan filed August 10, 2026, ¶ 3.) Plaintiff argues Moshe Z. failed to comply with his meet and confer obligation. (See Opp. at 3:4-5.)

The Court finds an exchange of emails is insufficient to satisfy the obligation to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference. Parties are expected to meet and confer in good faith prior to seeking judicial intervention. The Court will consider the merits of the instant Motion but will continue future hearings – or even deny the relief sought without reaching the merits – if the parties fail to comply with their obligations again.

V.             ANALYSIS

Oded Z. moves for judgment on the pleadings on the 4AC’s five causes of action. As to the first claim, Oded Z. argues the 4AC lacks allegations he was involved in the construction improvements or responsible for any resulting defect. (See Mot. at 3:5-7.) Oded Z. demurs to the second through fifth claims on grounds they are not asserted against him. (See id., at 3:17-20.)

In opposition, Plaintiff contends the first cause of action is sufficiently alleged against Oded Z. as the 4AC pleads he is the alter ego of IO Lifetime Builders, Inc. (“Lifetime”) (See Opp. at 3:1-3.) Plaintiff argues paragraphs 51, 53, and 55 allege Defendants breached their duty to Plaintiff by improperly constructing improvements to the Property. (See Opp. at 3:4-17.)

In reply, Oded Z. argues there are no factual allegations showing he or Lifetime were involved in the construction of the Property or owed Plaintiff a duty in connection therewith. (See Reply at 3:6-9.) Oded Z. argues a general allegation that all Defendants owed a general duty of care to Plaintiff is fatally defective as to him. (See id. at 3:22-23.)

As an initial matter, the Court denies the Motion as to the second through fifth causes of action because they are not asserted against him to begin with. The Court next turns to the first cause of action as to Oded Z., which the Court finds is insufficiently alleged against Oded Z.

A claim under the Right to Repair Act is the exclusive way to recover property damage resulting from construction defects. (See Civ. Code §§ 895-945.5; see McMillin Albany LLC v. Superior Court (2018) 4 Cal.5th 241, 252-53.) “[T]o make a claim for violation of the standards set forth in Chapter 2 (commencing with Section 896), a homeowner need only demonstrate… the home does not meet the applicable standard….” (Civ. Code § 942.) Civil Code § 896 provides “a builder…a general contractor, subcontractor, material supplier, individual product manufacturer, or design professional, shall…be liable…” under the Act. (Civ. Code § 896.)

The 4AC alleges Oded Z. was sole managing member and alter ego of Lifetime. (See 4AC, ¶¶ 9, 12-13.) Plaintiff alleges Lifetime’s assets “have been commingled with those of Defendants David Zemach, Rancho Villa LLC, Urbino Construction Services, LLC and Moshe Zemach.” (See id., ¶ 12.) The 4AC alleges “Plaintiff’s counsel sent the Developer [Rancho Villa LLC] a formal demand for repairs in accordance with…the Right to Repair Act.” (Id., ¶ 4, 42-43.) Plaintiff alleges Developer sold the property. (See id., ¶ 54.) These allegations do not allege that either Oded Z. or Lifetime held any of the roles—builder, developer, general contractor, contractor, or original seller of the subject property—that could be liable under the Act. (See Civ. Code §§ 896, 911(a).)

Instead, the 4AC appears to rely on its alter ego allegation to hold Oded Z. liable under the first cause of action, as the 4AC alleges “Defendants owed a duty to Plaintiff either directly or through Defendant Rancho Villa LLC to properly develop and construct the improvements on the Property…” (4AC, ¶ 51.) “Generally, alter ego liability is reserved for the parent-subsidiary relationship.” (Las Palmas Associates v. Las Palmas Center Associates (1991) 235 Cal.App.3d 1220, 1249.) “However, under the single-enterprise rule, liability can be found between sister companies.” (Id.) “The theory has been described as follows: ‘‘In effect what happens is that the court, for sufficient reason, has determined that though there are two or more personalities, there is but one enterprise; and that this enterprise has been so handled that it should respond, as a whole, for the debts of certain component elements of it.’” (Id. at 1249-50 (quoting 2 Marsh’s Cal. Corp. Law (3d ed. 1990) § 16.23, p. 1416).) 

The elements under the alter ego doctrine and single enterprise doctrine are the same. (See Las Palmas, 235 Cal.App.3d at 1249-51.) “Two conditions are generally required for application of the doctrine to two related corporations: (1) such a unity of interest and ownership that the separate corporate personalities are merged, so that one corporation is a mere adjunct of another or the two companies form a single enterprise; and (2) an inequitable result if the acts in question are treated as those of one corporation alone” (Tran v. Farmers Group, Inc. (2002) 104 Cal.App.4th 1202, 1219.) Factors to consider in applying the doctrine include the commingling of funds and other assets, the holding out by one entity that it is liable for the debts of the other, identical equitable ownership in the two entities, inadequate capitalization, and disregard of corporate formalities. (See Sonora Diamond Corp. v. Superior Court (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 523, 538-39.) 

Here, the 4AC’s alter ego allegations are only sufficient as to Oded Z. and Lifetime. However, this is insufficient to show Oded Z. could be liable for Rancho Villa’s conduct. The conclusory allegation that Lifetime’s assets were commingled with Rancho Villa’s are insufficient to show that Lifetime and Rancho Villa exhibited such a unity of interest and are one enterprise such that Oded Z. could be held liable under the alter ego doctrine by virtue of being the alter ego of Lifetime, which would essentially be the same enterprise as Rancho Villa. Thus, the claim fails as to Oded Z.

The Court GRANTS IN PART Oded Z.’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings to the 4AC to the First Cause of Action for violation of the Right to Repair Act WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

The Court DENIES the Motion as to the Second through Fifth Causes of Action.

VI.          CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS IN PART Oded Z.’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings to the 4AC to the First Cause of Action for violation of the Right to Repair Act WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

The Court OVERRULES the Motion as to the Second through Fifth Causes of Action.

Plaintiff is ordered to file an amended pleading on or before October 2, 2026.

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On March 8, 2024, Petitioner filed this action alleging negligence.

On May 22, 2026, Petitioner filed a Notice of Settlement.

On August 19, 2026, Petitioner filed the instant Petition, which is unopposed.

III.         LEGAL STANDARD

An enforceable settlement of a minor’s or incompetent’s claim requires court approval. (See Prob. Code, §§ 2504, 3500, 3600 et seq.; Code Civ. Proc., § 372.) [1]

          Probate Code § 3600, et seq., governs how the settlement proceeds are to be paid. Pursuant to Probate Code § 3601, the order shall approve payment of reasonable expenses from the settlement as follows:¿  

The court making the order or giving the judgment referred to in Section 3600, as a part thereof, shall make a further order authorizing and directing that such reasonable expenses (medical or otherwise and including reimbursement to a parent, guardian, or conservator), costs, and attorney’s fees, as the court shall approve and allow therein, shall be paid from the money or other property to be paid or delivered for the benefit of the minor or incompetent person.

 

(See Prob. Code § 3601(a).)¿

          In cases pursuant to Probate Code § 3601, “unless the court has approved the fee agreement in advance, the court must use a reasonable fee standard when approving and allowing the amount of attorney’s fees payable from money or property paid or to be paid for the benefit of a minor or a person with a disability.” (See Rules of Court 7.955(a)(1).) In doing so, “[t]he court must give consideration to the terms of any representation agreement made between the attorney and the representative of the minor or person with a disability and must evaluate the agreement based on the facts and circumstances existing at the time the agreement was made, except where the attorney and the representative of the minor or person with a disability contemplated that the attorney's fee would be affected by later events.” (See id., rule 7.955(a)(2).) 

          There are several nonexclusive factors a Court may consider in determining a reasonable attorney’s fee. (See id., rule 7.955(b).) Rule 7.955(c) requires that a petition requesting Court approval and allowance of an attorney’s fee under 7.955(a) must include a declaration from the attorney that addresses the factors listed in 7.955(b) that are applicable to the matter before the Court.

“In any case in which the court orders that funds to be received by a minor or a person with a disability must be deposited in a financial institution and not disbursed without further order of the court, the order must include a provision that a certified or filed endorsed copy of the order must be delivered to a manager at the financial institution where the funds are to be deposited, and that a receipt from the financial institution must be promptly filed with the court, acknowledging receipt of both the funds deposited and the order for deposit of funds.” (See id., rule 7.953(a).)

IV.          ANALYSIS

Claimant has agreed to settle his claims against Defendants in exchange for $85,000.00. (See Pet., ¶ 10.) Claimant sustained injuries, including “[t]wo broken teeth, one loosened/damaged tooth.” (See id., ¶ 6.) Claimant has recovered completely from his injuries. (See id., ¶ 8(a), Attach. 8.) No medical expenses incurred are to be paid or reimbursed from the proceeds. (See id., ¶ 12a(4).)

If approved, $21,250.00 will be used for attorney fees and ¿$3,703.25 for non-medical expenses, leaving a balance of $60,046.75¿ to be deposited in an insured account at Morgan Stanley 2725 Sand Hill Road, Suites 100 & 200, 1st Floor Menlo Park, CA 94025, subject to withdrawal only by authorization of the court. (See id., ¶ 16, 18a(2).)

Petitioner’s counsel, David B. Bobrosky, requests attorney fees which comprise 25% of the settlement amount. (See Attachs. 13(a) at ¶ 4, 17(a).) Counsel was able to obtain a favorable settlement for Claimant. (See id.) This case settled a little over two years after it commenced. After reviewing counsel’s declaration, the Court finds the 25% fee is proportionate to the value of the services performed. (See Rules of Court, rule 7.955(b).)

The Petition appears to set forth information bearing upon the reasonableness of the compromise pursuant to Rules of Court, rule 7.950. Given the age of the minor Claimant, the Court will only require attendance at the hearing by the Petitioner to grant the petition if the parties wish to be heard further on the Court’s tentative ruling granting the Petition; otherwise, Petitioner’s attendance is not necessary. (See Rules of Court, rule 7.952(a).) Thus, the Court GRANTS Petitioner’s Petition for Minor’s Compromise.

III.         LEGAL STANDARD

A.             Judgment on the Pleadings

A plaintiff may move for judgment on the pleadings on grounds “the complaint states facts sufficient to constitute a cause or causes of action against the defendant and the answer does not state facts sufficient to constitute a defense to the complaint.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 438(c)(1)(A).) The standard for ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings is essentially the same as that applicable to a general demurrer: that under the state of the pleadings, together with matters that may be judicially noticed, it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (See Schabarum v. California Legislature (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1216.)

¿Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court a pleading can be amended successfully. (See id.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.)

B.             Motion to Dismiss

The court may dismiss the complaint as to a defendant when “after a demurrer to the complaint is sustained with leave to amend, the plaintiff fails to amend it within the time allowed by the court and either party moves for dismissal.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 581(f)(2).)

The Court grants Upgrade’s request for judicial notice of (1) Upgrade’s Requests for Admission (“RFAs”) propounded on Defendant (filed in the supporting declaration to the Motion to Deem Admitted) and (2) this Court’s July 6, 2026, Order granting Upgrade’s Motion to Deem RFAs Admitted pursuant to Evidence Code § 452(d).

V.             MEET AND CONFER

Before filing a motion for judgment on the pleadings pursuant to this chapter, the moving party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 439(a).)

Upgrade’s counsel attests she made numerous phone calls and sent meet and confer letters to Smalls but has received no response. (See Declaration of Laura D’Anna filed August 13, 2026, ¶¶ 4-5.) Thus, the Court finds Upgrade satisfied its meet and confer obligation.

VI.          ANALYSIS

A.             Upgrade’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is Granted.

Upgrade moves for judgment on the pleadings on the grounds that the deemed admissions admit all elements of the Complaint’s single cause of action for breach of contract. Accordingly, Upgrade states it is entitled to a total judgment of $66,125.87, comprising $64,301.66 in principal plus $435.00 in sanctions and $1,389.21 in costs.   

As Upgrade moves for judgment on the pleadings, the Court first addresses whether the Complaint states sufficient facts to constitute a breach of contract cause of action. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 438.) A cause of action for breach of contract must plead “(1) the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for non-performance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) damage to plaintiff therefrom.” (Acoustics, Inc. v. Trepte Constr. Co. (1971) 14 Cal.App.3d 887, 913.) If the action is based on an alleged breach of a written contract, the terms must be set out verbatim in the body of the complaint or a copy of the written agreement must be attached and incorporated by reference.” (Harris v. Rudin, Richman & Appel (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 229, 307.)   

Here, the Complaint alleges Smalls entered into a Borrower Agreement, with the loan to “be administered and serviced by Upgrade, Inc.’s national online consumer loan marketplace platform.” (Compl., ¶¶ 1, 4-, 6, Ex. A.) The Complaint alleges the Borrower Agreement was assigned to Upgrade who is now the current holder of the Loan. (See id., ¶ 10.) The Complaint alleges “[Upgrade], its predecessor in interest, and Upgrade, Inc. have performed all conditions, covenants and promises required of them under the Loan Document” and Smalls has “defaulted on their obligations to [Upgrade] under the Loan Documents…despite demand... There is now due, owing and unpaid to Plaintiff the principal sum of $64,301.66.” (Id., ¶¶ 21-23.) The Court finds the Complaint states a claim for breach of contract. Next, the Court addresses whether the Answer states sufficient facts to constitute a defense to the Complaint. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 438.) 

Smalls’ Answer asserts the general denial, “Pursuant to the provisions of Section 431.30(d) of the California Code of Civil Procedure, Defendant generally denies each and every allegation contained in Plaintiff's Complaint, and further denies that Plaintiff has sustained damages in the sums alleged, or in any other sum, or at all.” (Answer at 2.) The Answer asserts four affirmative defenses for fraud in the inducement attributed to the contractor, failure of consideration due to contractor performance, unconscionability, and breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. (See id.)

Upgrade argues Smalls’ admissions in the noticed RFAs, Set One, warrant judgment against him. The Court has taken judicial notice that the truth of the matters in Upgrade’s RFAs (Set One) served on Smalls were deemed admitted against him. (See RJN, Exs. A-B; Code Civ. Proc. § 2033.280(b).) The RFAs were previously filed in this action in support of Upgrade’s Motion to Deem Admitted and the Court ordered the RFA’s admitted on July 6, 2026. (See id.) These documents are already properly part of the record before the Court. They may therefore serve as a basis for this Court’s ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings. 

There is authority providing that in ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings, a court may consider matters that may be judicially noticed, including a party’s admissions or concessions which cannot be reasonably controverted. In Pang v. Beverly Hospital, Inc. (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 986, the Court of Appeal affirmed the trial court’s treatment of a motion for summary adjudication as a motion for judgment on the pleadings, and the granting of that motion based on the sworn evidence presented by plaintiff in connection with the motion, noting that on a motion for judgment on the pleadings: “In addition to the facts pleaded, we may consider matters that may be judicially noticed, including a party’s admissions or concessions which cannot reasonably be controverted. (Evans v. California Trailer Court, Inc. (1994) 28 Cal. App. 4th 540, 548-549 [33 Cal. Rptr. 2d 646].)” (Pang, 79 Cal.App.4th at 989-990; see also Tucker v. Pacific Bell Mobile Services (2012) 208 Cal.App.4th 201, 218, fn. 11 [a court may take judicial notice of a party’s admissions in cases where the admission cannot reasonably be controverted, such as requests for admission].) 

Pursuant to this Court’s July 6, 2026, Order, which is judicially noticed, Smalls admitted: (1) As a member of the Upgrade platform, he electronically signed its “Terms of Use” thereby agreeing and consenting to do business with Upgrade online electronically; (2) On or about December 27, 2023, upon completion of the loan application process online, he posted a home improvement loan request to the Upgrade platform; (3) he is the Borrower as addressed in the Borrower Agreement; (4) he promised to pay the principal amount together with interest and any additional fees or charges incurred under the Borrower Agreement; (5) Upgrade disbursed one or more separate advances to the contractor to his your home improvement project; (6) the Initial Advance occurred; (7) he approved Substantial Completion causing the Final Advance to be disbursed to the contractor; (8) the Final Advance occurred; (9) he received the Transition Notice and Final Payment Schedule; (10) he did not dispute any Advance within 60 calendar days from the date the Advance request was made; (11) he received a copy of the Truth in Lending Act Disclosure; (12) Upgrade is the owner of the subject loan; (13) All payments submitted to Upgrade have been credited to the subject loan balance; (14) he is in default on the subject loan in that he has failed to pay Upgrade pursuant to the terms of the Loan Agreement; and (15) Upgrade has performed all conditions required of it under the-Loan Agreement.

The Court finds the Answer does not state facts sufficient to constitute a defense to the Complaint. The RFAs undermine Smalls’ general denial of every allegation in the Complaint and third affirmative defense, as the RFAs establish Smalls voluntarily requested a loan through Upgrade, he voluntarily entered into to an agreement with Upgrade for provision of a loan, Upgrade performed all conditions under the parties’ agreement, and Smalls is in default of the agreement.

Additionally, the first, second, and fourth affirmative defenses are insufficiently stated. To sufficiently allege an affirmative defense in an Answer, the same pleading of “ultimate facts” rather than evidentiary matter or legal conclusions is required as when pleading the Complaint. (See FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashimi (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 384.) In other words, the Answer must “‘aver facts as carefully and with as much detail as the facts which constitute the cause of action and which are alleged in the complaint.’” (Id.) Here, the first affirmative defense for fraud in the inducement is based on a third party’s misrepresentations. This affirmative defense lacks specificity and fails to state ultimate facts establishing that Upgrade would be liable for this party’s alleged misrepresentations under its loan agreement with Smalls. The second affirmative defense for failure of consideration based on insufficiently performed services by a third party suffers from the same defect; it lacks ultimate facts establishing how the terms of the agreement would be voided based on the third party’s performance. The fourth affirmative defense for breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing also fails to allege ultimate facts establishing Upgrade’s duty to ensure quality control over the third party. Accordingly, these affirmative defenses are unclear as to how they would be applicable to Upgrade’s Complaint and thus they fail to sufficiently allege an affirmative defense against the Complaint.

Thus, all elements of a breach of contract have been established based on the judicially noticed documents, and no defenses articulated in the answer can be advanced at this juncture as a matter of law given the RFAs deemed as admitted.

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS the Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings without leave to amend, and awards plaintiff damages of $64,301.66.

B.             Upgrade’s Motion to Dismiss is Granted.

Upgrade also moves to dismiss Smalls’ First Amended Cross-Complaint on grounds that Smalls did not amend his pleading after the Court sustained Upgrade’s demurrer to the FAXC in its entirety and gave him leave to amend until July 6, 2026. (See Mot. at 2:20-25.)

On June 22, 2026, the Court sustained Upgrade’s demurrer to all causes of action in the FAXC with leave to amend. (See June 22, 2026, Order.) Smalls failed to file an amended pleading and the time to amend has expired. Thus, the Court GRANTS Upgrade’s Motion to Dismiss. The First Amended Cross-Complaint is accordingly dismissed with prejudice. (See Cano v. Glover (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 326, 329-330 [“Section 581, subdivision (f)(2) ‘... gives the defendant the right to obtain a court order dismissing the action with prejudice…’”].)

VII.       CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS the Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND, and awards Upgrade damages of $64,301.66. In addition, the Court GRANTS Upgrade’s Motion to Dismiss. Accordingly, the Court dismisses the First Amended Cross-Complaint with prejudice.

          IT IS SO ORDERED.

ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANT BRUNO M. UNDERWOOD’S MOTION TO SET ASIDE DEFAULT

 

I.               BACKGROUND 

Plaintiff Simon Mikail bring this motor vehicle tort action against Defendants Secural Security Corp. (“Security”) and Bruno Underwood (“Underwood”) (jointly “Defendants”). Plaintiff alleges Underwood acting within the course and scope of his employment for Security, drove a marked security vehicle onto Plaintiff’s driveway, engaged with Plaintiff, and then opened his vehicle’s door, striking Plaintiff’s body.

Underwood moves to set aside the default entered against him.

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On October 27, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Complaint.

On November 18, 2025, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging (1) Motor Vehicle Negligence; (2) General Negligence; (3) Assault; (4) Battery; and (5) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress.

On July 8, 2026, the Court entered default against Underwood.

On July 23, 2026, Underwood filed the instant Motion.

On September 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

On September 10, 2026, Underwood filed a Reply.

III.         LEGAL STANDARD

A.             Discretionary or Mandatory Relief – Code of Civil Procedure § 473(b)

“The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken…” (Code Civ. Proc. § 473(b).) [1] 

Code of Civil Procedure § 473(b) “provides for both discretionary and mandatory relief. [Citation.]” (Pagnini v. Union Bank, N.A. (2018) 28 Cal.App.5th 298, 302.) For discretionary relief, “[t]he court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.”¿(Code Civ. Proc. § 473(b).)  

A moving party need only establish “mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect” by a preponderance of the evidence. (Luz v. Lopes (1960) 55 Cal.2d 54, 62.) Excusable neglect exists where counsel or the party acted as a reasonably prudent person would under the circumstances. (See Hearn v. Howard (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1206; See also Zamora v. Clayborn Contracting Group, Inc. (2002) 28 Cal.4th 249, 258.)

An application for discretionary or mandatory relief must be made no more than six months after entry of the judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding from which relief is sought. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 473(b); see also English v. IKON Bus. Solutions (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 130, 143.)

IV.          ANALYSIS

Underwood moves to set aside default. Underwood attests he was served at his home on April 30, 2026, though he would not be home for another two months. (See Declaration of Bruno Underwood filed July 23, 2026 (“Underwood Decl.”), ¶ 3.) At this time, Underwood contacted his employer regarding the documents left at his residence. (See id., ¶ 4.) Underwood understood his employer would be covering him on this case until July 9, 2026, when he learned he needed to obtain counsel. (See id., ¶¶ 5-6, 8.) Underwood attests he obtained counsel on July 16, 2026. (See id., ¶ 9.) Underwood argues he reasonably relied on his employer’s assurances they would cover him in this action and was surprised to learn on July 9, 2026, that they were not. (See Mot. at 4:12-25.) Underwood argues there is no prejudice to Plaintiff as default was recently entered. (See id., at 5:6-13.) Underwood attaches a copy of his proposed answer. (See Mot., Ex. 3.)

Plaintiff argues Underwood has not shown grounds for relief under § 473(b) based on his voluntary inaction. Plaintiff argues Underwood’s claimed reliance on his employer is insufficient to establish surprise or excusable neglect because he was expressly advised that he could still be sued personally and his declaration does not identify any person who affirmatively told him that an attorney had been retained for him, that an insurer had accepted his individual defense, or that he was free to disregard the Summons. (See id., at 6:25-7:19, 9:15-27.) Alternatively, if the Motion is granted, Plaintiff requests relief be conditioned upon Underwood’s payment of $4,560.00 in reasonable attorney’s fees and costs pursuant to § 473(c)(l)(C).[2] (See Opp. at 3:21-4:1, 12:2-12.)

In reply, Underwood argues he reasonably relied on his employer’s representations that they would cover him in this action and given that he was not in receipt of the physical documents. (See Reply at 4:21-24.) Underwood argues he diligently obtained counsel after learning he was not covered by his employer and that default had been entered against him. (See id.)

The Court will grant the Motion. “Because the law favors disposing of cases on their merits, ‘any doubts in applying section 473 must be resolved in favor of the party seeking relief from default…’” (Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 980.) “‘Where a default is entered because defendant has relied upon a codefendant or other interested party to defend, the question is whether the defendant was reasonably justified under the circumstances in his reliance or whether his neglect to attend to the matter was inexcusable...With regard to whether the circumstances warranted reliance by the defendant on a third party, the efforts made by the defendant to obtain a defense by the third party are, of course, relevant.... Even if the mistake were caused by some negligence on defendant's part, this negligence might be excused if it in no way prejudiced the opposing party.’” (Fasuyi v. Permatex, Inc. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 681, 697 citing Weitz v. Yankosky (1966) 63 Cal.2d 849, 855-56.)

Here, it appears Underwood conflated his employer’s affirmative confirmation to his question that the company’s insurance would be overseeing him as an employee with confirmation they would be representing him on an action brought against him individually. (See Underwood Decl., ¶¶ 5-6.) The Court finds Underwood acted reasonably in assuming his employer was legally covering him in this action upon relying on his employer’s confirmation that their insurance would be “overseeing him as an employee”. Underwood promptly obtained counsel upon returning to his residence and learning from a mailed letter that default had been entered against him and his employer was not representing him. (See id., ¶¶ 7-9.) Further, there is no showing plaintiff was in any way prejudiced by defendant's failure to answer on time. Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Underwood’s Motion to Set Aside Default. The Court denies Plaintiff’s request for attorney fees pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 473(c)(1)(C).

V.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS Underwood’s Motion to Set Aside Default. The Court denies Plaintiff’s request for attorney fees pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 473(c)(1)(C).

Underwood is ordered to file his Answer on or before September 24, 2026.
