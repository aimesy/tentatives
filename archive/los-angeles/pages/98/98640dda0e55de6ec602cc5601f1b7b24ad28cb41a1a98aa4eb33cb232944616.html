Case Number: 22NWCV00183    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: L

Background

 

This is a wrongful termination action. On March 15, 2022, Plaintiff Estela Espinosa (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants Feniks Funding Incorporated, Feniks Funding, Inc., Ricardo Talamantes, Steven Lopez, and Does 1 to 100. The Complaint alleges that Plaintiff was a Senior Loan Processor working for Feniks Funding Incorporated and Feniks Funding, Inc. (hereinafter, “Feniks Defendants”) beginning on September 15, 2020, until Plaintiff was wrongfully terminated on February 26, 2021. Defendant Talamantes was the owner of Feniks Defendants and Plaintiff’s supervisor. The Complaint alleges that, during her employment, Plaintiff suffered from 1) disability discrimination and 2) retaliation after she complained that the company was violating the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act. The Complaint allege the following causes of action:

 

1.    Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy;

2.    Retaliation (Gov. Code§ 12940(h));

3.    Employment Discrimination Based on Disability (Gov. Code § 12940(a));

4.    Hostile Work Environment and Harassment in the Workplace;

5.    Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress;

6.    Failure to Accommodate Disability (Gov. Code§§ 12940(m) and 12926(p);

7.    Failure to Engage in the Interactive Process (Gov. Code § 12940(n));

8.    Violation of Labor Code § 1102.5(b);

9.    Violation of Labor Code § 1102.5(c);

10.        Violation of Labor Code § 1102.5(d);

11.        Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing;

12.        Negligent Supervision and Retention

13.        Failure to Pay Wages in a Timely Manner (Labor Code§ 204);

14.        Failure to Pay Earned Wage (Labor Code §§ 204, 206 and 210)

15.        Failure to Pay Meal Period Compensation (Labor Code §§ 226.7 and 512);

16.        Failure to Pay Rest Period Compensation (Labor Code§§ 226.7; IWC Wage Order No. 4-2001);

17.        Waiting Time Penalties (Cal. Labor Code§§ 201, 202 and 203); and

18.        Unfair Business Practices (Bus. & Prof Code§ 17200).

 

On March 6, 2026, counsel for Feniks Defendants and Defendant Talamantes was relieved as counsel. (3/6/25 Minute Order.) Defendants remain self-represented.

 

On March 20, 2026, Defendant Steve Lopez was dismissed without prejudice.

 

Plaintiff now moves for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication as to the first, second, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh, eighth, ninth, tenth, eleventh, and eighteenth causes of action as to Defendant Talamantes.

 

The Court notes that the Notice of Motion does not include the fourth cause of action—however, the memorandum and point of authorities and separate statement include the fourth cause of action.

 

The Court further notes that the Notice of Motion indicates that the twelfth to seventeenth causes of action “will be dismissed.” However, no request for dismissal have been filed as to these causes of action. The Court clarifies that the Motion may only seek summary adjudication, not summary judgment.

 

No opposition has been filed as of August 3, 2026.


Request for Judicial Notice

 

The Court grants Plaintiff’s Request for Judicial Notice of the Court’s March 5 2025 order granting Plaintiff’s Motion to have Requests for Admission, Set Two, Deemed Admitted as to Ricardo Talamantes, with respect to RFAs 21-56 only (Exhibit A).

 

Legal Standard

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119, internal citations omitted.) “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67, citing FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 381-382.)

 

In moving for summary judgment, a plaintiff has met its “burden of showing that there is no defense to a cause of action if that party has proved each element of the cause of action entitling the party to judgment on the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).)

 

Once a moving party has satisfied the initial burden of proof, the burden “shifts to the opposing party to show, by responsive separate statement and admissible evidence, that triable issues of fact exist.” (Ostayan v. Serrano Reconveyance Co. (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1411, 1418, disapproved on other grounds by Black Sky Cap., LLC v. Cobb (2019) 7 Cal.5th 156, 165.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

 

Discussion

 

In support of the Motion, Plaintiff primarily relies on Requests for Admissions deemed admitted by the Court on March 5, 2025. (Nigro Decl., ¶¶ 3-4, Exhs. B-C; RJN Exh. A.) Plaintiff’s Separate Statement lists twenty (20) undisputed material facts based on these admissions (“UMF Nos. 1 to 20”). The Separate Statement first sets forth UMF Nos. 1 to 20 under a section entitle “General Background Facts.” The Separate Statement then sets forth sections based on each cause of action, and subsections labeled by issue numbers. Under almost every issue subsection, Plaintiff sets forth the following: “Plaintiff incorporates by reference Undisputed Material Facts and the Supporting Evidence pertaining to Undisputed Material Facts, Nos. 1 -20 as though set forth fully herein.”

 

The only other evidence Plaintiff relies upon is a Right to Sue letter issued by the Department of Fair Employment and Housing on March 22, 2021. (Nigro Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.)

 

In the memorandum of points and authorities, Plaintiff essentially states the relevant legal standard for each cause of action and cites to UMF Nos. 1 to 20. For example, as to the third cause of action, Plaintiff states the legal standard, followed by a statement that “Plaintiff established all of the elements by virtue of the deemed admitted Requests for Admission (UF 1 - 20).” (Mot., 11-12.) The same formula is followed by the discussion on the fourth cause of action.

 

As to the fifth, sixth, seventh, eighth, ninth, tenth, eleventh, and eighteenth causes of action, Plaintiff states in the conclusory manner that each element of the cause of action is met in this case without citing to any specific undisputed material fact.

 

The remainder of Plaintiff’s memorandum states that Plaintiff is entitled to non-$2,000,000 in economic damages, $1,500,000.00 in special damages, and $100,000.00 in restoration and restitution damages by virtue of the admissions, citing to UMF Nos. 17-19.

 

No opposition has been filed.

 

The Court initially finds the memorandum of points and authorities fails to specifically explain how a particular undisputed fact supports each element of a cause of action or admits Defendant Talamantes’s liability as to the entire cause of action. Rather, Plaintiff cites to all twenty undisputed facts or cites to no facts.

 

The Court acknowledges that a moving party is not required to repeat specific citations to the record in its memorandum that are properly noted in the party's separate statement. (See Basich v. Allstate Ins. Co. (2001) 87 Cal.App.4th 1112, 1118 [finding that the moving party need not restate specific citations in the memorandum where the moving party “submitted a comprehensive Statement of Undisputed Material Facts, which separately identified the [claim] and each supporting material fact claimed to be without dispute with respect to that claim”].)

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff’s Motion is procedurally deficient based on the inadequate separate statement.

 

A motion for summary judgment “shall include a separate statement setting forth plainly and concisely all material facts that the moving party contends are undisputed. Each of the material facts stated shall be followed by a reference to the supporting evidence. The failure to comply with this requirement of a separate statement may in the court’s discretion constitute a sufficient ground for denying the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (b)(1).) “The Separate Statement of Undisputed Material Facts in support of a motion must separately identify…[e]ach supporting material fact claimed to be without dispute with respect to the cause of action, claim for damages, issue of duty, or affirmative defense that is the subject of the motion.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1350, (d)(1)(B), emphasis added.)

 

“The separate statement is not merely a technical requirement, it is an indispensable part of the summary judgment or adjudication process. ‘Separate statements are required not to satisfy a sadistic urge to torment lawyers, but rather to afford due process to opposing parties and to permit trial courts to expeditiously review complex motions for ... summary judgment to determine quickly and efficiently whether material facts are disputed.’ [Citation.]” (Whitehead v. Habig (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 896, 902.)

 

Here, the Separate Statement fails to specifically identify the specific undisputed facts that support each cause of action or underlying issue. Each cause of action or underlying issue merely cites to all twenty (20) undisputed facts. It is not the Court’s job to determine which facts support which cause of action or underlying issue. That is the purpose of a separate statement.

 

In effect, Plaintiff’s Motion puts the burden on the Court to identify which undisputed facts support which cause of action, element, or issue. This is improper. “A motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication will be defective if the moving party fails to (1) accurately identify the facts that are material to the legal theory upon which the motion is based; (2) actually include those material facts in the separate statement; and (3) reference evidence establishing, either directly or by inference, each material fact the moving party claims is undisputed.” (Pierson v. Helmerich & Payne Internat. Drilling Co. (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 608, 617, emphasis added.)

 

Without a proper separate statement, the Court is unable to determine whether Plaintiff “has proved each element of the cause of action entitling [Plaintiff] to judgment on the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).)

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the alternative, for Summary Adjudication is denied without prejudice.

Case Number: 23NWCV00097    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: L

Plaintiffs Angie Mejia Antunez and Darwin Zambrano’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED, in part.

 

Plaintiffs are awarded $20,462.00 in attorney’s fees and $6,868.42 in costs.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is a lemon law action regarding a 2021 Honda Civic. On January 11, 2023, Plaintiff Mabel Lopez Garcia (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (“AHM”) and Does 1 to 10. The Complaint alleges two cause of action for 1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act - Breach of Express Warranty, and 2) Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment.

 

On January 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Notice of Settlement (Conditional).

 

On March 17, 2026, the Court dismissed the case without prejudice. (3/17/25 Minute Order.)

 

Plaintiff now move for attorney’s fees in the amount of $31,419.50, with a 1.5 loadstar multiplier in the amount of $15,709.75, for a total fee award of $47,129.25 in attorneys’ fees. Plaintiff also seeks $8,291.67 in costs and expenses.


Legal Standard

 

 

The Song-Beverly Act provides for the award of attorneys’ fees to prevailing plaintiffs as follows: 

 

If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney's fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action. 

 

(Civ. Code § 1794, subd. (d).) 

 

The matter of reasonableness of a party’s attorney fees is within the sound discretion of the trial court. (Bruckman v. Parliament Escrow Co. (1989) 190 Cal.App.3d 1051, 1062.) When assessing the amount of any attorney’s fee award, courts typically determine what is reasonable through the application of

the “lodestar” method. Under the lodestar method, a base amount is calculated from a compilation of (1) time reasonably spent and (2) the reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 48); see also Meister v. Regents of University of California (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 437, 448-449 [holding that the lodestar method applies to statutory attorney fees award unless the underlying statute provides for another method of calculation].)

 

Discussion

 

Here, it is undisputed that Plaintiff is the prevailing party in this action and is entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees. The only issue pending before this Court is

the amount of the award.

 

Reasonable Hourly Rates

 

A “reasonable” hourly rate is the prevailing rate charged by attorneys of similar skill and experience in the relevant community. (PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.) “The experienced trial judge is the best judge of the value of professional services rendered in his court […].” (Ibid.) “[R]ate determinations in other cases, particularly those setting a rate for the plaintiffs' attorney, are satisfactory evidence of the prevailing market rate.” (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1009.)

Plaintiff asserts that counsel’s rates are reasonable based on 1) the hourly rates as set forth above, and 2) numerous trial court orders in and around Los Angeles affirming that such rates are reasonable. (Kirnos Decl., ¶ 29, Exhs. W-HH.)

 

In opposition, AHM argues that Plaintiff counsel’s requested rates are excessive and seek premium rates for non-attorney work. AHM asserts that Plaintiff’s examples of orders approving the rates in other cases are not dispositive in this case. AHM suggests that the rates should be reduced based on a straightforward nature of this case.

 

Based on counsel’s declaration and the Court’s own experience with the billing rates of Los Angeles-area counsel in similar lemon law cases, the Court finds Plaintiff’s counsel’s rates are reasonable. The Court addresses any issue of hours billed for non-legal work at attorney rates in the “reasonable fees” section.

 

          Reasonable Fees

                                  

The Court is vested with discretion to determine which claimed hours were reasonably spent, and what an attorney’s reasonable hourly rate is. (Dover Mobile Estates v. Fiber Form Products, Inc. (“Dover”) (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 1494, 1501); see also Flannery v. California Highway Patrol (1987) 61 Cal.App.4th 629, 644 [“We readily acknowledge the discretion of the trial judge to determine the value of professional services rendered in his or her court.”].)

 

Plaintiff asserts that the total hours spent on this case is reasonable. Plaintiff has submitted a billing invoice identifying all hours spent on this case. (Kirnos Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.) Further, the billing entries were audited to reduce or eliminate “any time deemed excessive, duplicative, or otherwise unreasonable under the circumstance […].” (Kirnos Decl., ¶ 21.) The major tasks in this case included the following: 1) filing and attending the hearing on Plaintiff’s Motion for Summary Adjudication to AMH’s fifth and eighth affirmative defenses, which the Court granted, 2) conducting Plaintiff’s deposition, 3) filing and attending the hearing on Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel AMH’s Person Most Qualified (PMQ), which the Court granted, 4) attending the mandatory Settlement Conference, and 5) filing and attending the hearing on Plaintiff’s ex parte application to continue trial, which the Court granted, 5) attending the deposition of AHM’s PMQ, 6) opposing and attending the hearing on AHM’s Motion for Summary Adjudication, which was continued, and 6) taking a non-appearance of AHM’s Custodian of Records.

 

In opposition, AHM argues that the Court should exclude unreasonable and unnecessary time billed. AHM notes that this case was resolved by AHM’s Code of Civil Procedure section 998 offer, which AHM presented on September 29, 2025 and Plaintiff accepted on October 28, 2025. (Tolmasoff Decl., ¶ 12, Exh. D.) AHM submits a billing entry table suggesting specific entries to be stricken or reduced. (Tolmasoff Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. A.)

 

AHM first argues that Plaintiffs cannot recover fees incurred in relation to Plaintiffs’ fraud claim. Specifically, the Court should strike entries related to AHM’s Motion for Summary Judgment, which was directed only to Plaintiff’s fraud cause of action. That Motion was continued and was ultimately taken off-calendar pursuant to the settlement.

 

Further, AHM argues that Plaintiff should not recover for premature trial preparation where trial was continued twice and never proceeded. For example, Plaintiff filed nine motions in limine on April 30, 2025, when trial was then set for June 11, 2025. (Tolmasoff Decl., ¶ 14.) Work related to the motions in limine should be stricken. On May 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed an ex parte application to continue trial, which the Court granted. Trial was continued to September 22, 2025. (Ibid.) On July 22, 2026, trial was continued again to March 9, 2026 pursuant to Joint Stipulation filed by the parties. (Tolmasoff Decl., ¶¶ 13, 15, Exh. E.) AHM argues that entries related to the ex parte to continue trial (including preparation, travel, appearance) should be stricken where the continuance was 1) unopposed, and 2) not reasonably necessary to the result achieved.

 

AHM additionally argues that entries for administrative, clerical, routine case-management, vague, or template-based work should be stricken. For example, AHM objects to Plaintiff’s October 14, 2025 entry for taking a certificate of non-appearance at a deposition of AHM’s custodian of records. AHM served an objection indication that no witness would be produced on that date, and Plaintiff has not shown that the nonappearance procedure was reasonably necessary to the litigation. (Tolmasoff Decl., ¶ 10, Exh. B.)

 

Lastly, AHM objects to the work spent (and anticipated work) on the instant fee motion as excessive, “post-settlement” work. Overall, Plaintiff should not be awarded more than $14,842.15 in attorney’s fees.

 

The Court first addresses AHM’s request to strike entries related to the fraud cause of action. In reply, Plaintiffs cite to Santana v. FCA US, LLC (“Santana”) (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 334 to assert that apportionment is not required here. In that case, the court declined to apportion fees between Song-Beverly Act claims and fraud claims on the grounds that the causes of action encompassed a single set of facts. (Id. at p. 349.) However, the procedural posture of Santana is distinguishable, as there was jury verdict on both claims in that case. (Id. at p. 338.) The Santana court noted there was no practical method of apportioning fees spent on fraud issues and Song-Beverly Act issues. (Id. at p. 350.) Such issue is not present here where the fees at issue clearly involve AHM’s Motion for Summary Judgment, which only targeted the fraud cause of action. The Court is not persuaded by Plaintiff’s argument that “[w]hile the MSA opposition work did address fraud, the substance of the work was primarily arguing the common nexus of facts: [AHM] sold Plaintiff a lemon and willfully failed to promptly repurchase or replace it.” (Repl., 4:6-8.) The Court thus finds the time spent on this matter was not reasonably incurred in furtherance of Plaintiffs’ Song-Beverly causes of action. The Court accordingly strikes the relevant billing entries.

 

Next, the Court addresses AHM’s objections regarding the motions in limine and the Plaintiff’s May 6, 2026 ex parte application to continue trial. In reply, Plaintiff argues that AHM’s assertion that the motions in limine and other trial preparation did not contribute to eventual settlement is speculative. As to the ex parte application, Plaintiff argues that it is irrelevant that ex parte was unopposed by AHM. Plaintiff was required to prepare and argue that ex parte application if the Court disagreed. Plaintiff states that travel time was reasonably incurred because “the Court was requiring an in-person appearance at this hearing and Ms. Smith was the only attorney at Knight Law that was available to attend.” (Reply, 7:15-17.) The Court finds that Plaintiff is entitled to fees incurred to prepare motions in limine, reasonably billed at .3 hours per motion, and to prepare and attend the hearing on the ex parte application. It is only in retrospect that such work appears unnecessary. As to travel for the ex parte, Plaintiff has provided no evidence that an in-person appearance was required, and the Court finds that the $8,562.50 (13.7 total hours) incurred for travel is excessive and non-legal work.

 

The Court next discusses the costs incurred in relation to taking the non-appearance of AHM’s Custodian of Records. The Court finds that these costs were reasonably incurred. Again, it is only in retrospect that such work appears unnecessary.

 

Lastly, the Court addresses the work to prepare the instant fee motion, prepare the reply, and attend the hearing. The Court finds that Plaintiff is entitled to costs incurred for this Motion, but finds that the amount is unreasonable. Incorporating Plaintiff counsel’s reply declaration, the total amount incurred in relation to this fee motion is $3,712.00 (7.2 hours). (See Cohen Reply Decl., ¶¶ 7-10.) The Court finds that this amount is around 12 percent of the entire amount of base attorney’s fees sought. The Court reduces the time spent on this Motion by 1.7 hours.

 

The Court has also conducted an independent review of the billing table and exercises its discretion to strike certain entries as vague, excessive, duplicative, or related to inter-office communications. (See Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 253 [affirming reduced fee award of $95,900 from the proposed amount of $344,639 where trial court found counsel sought fees for “dual billing” entries and for tasks that “should not have required anything more than [a] slight factual modification to [an] existing boilerplate”].)

 

The Court ultimately finds Plaintiffs are entitled to $20,462.00 in attorney’s fees.

 

          Lodestar Multiplier

 

The lodestar amount “may be adjusted by the court based on factors including (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Bernardi¿v. County of Monterey¿(2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1379, 1399 [citing Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132].)¿ The purpose of any lodestar and the increase thereto “is intended to approximate market-level compensation for such services” and is entirely discretionary.¿(Ibid.)¿“The purpose of a fee enhancement is not to reward attorneys for litigating certain kinds of cases, but to fix a reasonable fee in a particular action.”¿ (Weeks v. Baker & McKenzie¿(1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1128, 1171-72.)¿

 

Plaintiffs assert a 1.5 multiplier is justified based on the contingent nature of lemon law cases, the risk associated with taking on the case, and the delay in payment. The Court notes that, in the moving papers, Plaintiff inconsistently seeks “a 1.3 enhancement,” “a 1.2 enhancement,” and “a multiplier of 1.5.” (Mot., 12L28, 13:4-6.)

 

In opposition, AHM asserts that a lodestar multiplier is unwarranted where this case involved no novel legal questions and no exceptional result was achieved.

 

Plaintiffs’ request to award a 1.5 multiplier is denied. The Court finds there is no basis to award a multiplier considering the time and skill of counsel are compensated with a mandatory fee award. Also, there is nothing before the Court to suggest this relatively straightforward case required extraordinary legal skill and/or required counsel to bear unnecessary risk. Nothing before this Court suggests that special skill was displayed, or an extraordinary result was obtained by Plaintiffs’ counsel.

 

          Costs

 

Plaintiff seeks $8,291.67 in costs pursuant to the Memorandum of Costs filed on March 19, 2026.

 

In opposition, AHM particularly objects to costs sought in relation to the Memorandum of Costs “Item 13: Other” category, which consists of attorney services and messenger court filings and service, court appearance professionals, travel, and sanctions. AHM asserts that Plaintiff provided no supporting documentation showing those amounts were reasonable or necessary to the litigation. Further, AHM objects to costs incurred for depositions or non-appearance fees that did not advance the litigation.

 

In reply, Plaintiff submits its itemized internal worksheet used to draft the Memorandum of Costs, in addition to supporting invoices for the “Item 13: Other” category costs. (Cohen Reply Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A.)

 

The Court generally accepts Plaintiff’s documentation in support of the “Item 13: Other” category costs. However, the Court finds that travel costs, unless for an authorized witness, are not recoverable per Ladas v. California State Automotive Assoc. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 775-76 ["Routine expenses for local travel by attorneys or other firm employees are not reasonably necessary to the conduct of litigation."].) The Court thus strikes the $1,423.25 sought for “Travel” costs in the Memorandum of Costs.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff is awarded $6,868.42 in costs and expenses.

Case Number: 24NWCV02213    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: L

Defendants Bellflower Self Storage, LLC and Kimberly Cabrera’s Demurrer to the Second Amended Complaint is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

 

Defendants to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is a wrongful eviction action. On July 18, 2024, Plaintiff Firpo Wycoff Carr, PhD, in pro per, (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants Bellflower Self Storage, LLC (“Bellflower”), Kimberly Cabrera (“Cabrera”), (collectively, “Defendants”) and Does 1 to 50.

 

The Complaint alleges Plaintiff filed a lawsuit against Defendants, LASC Case No. 24NWCV00571. Defendants allegedly “retaliated” against him by filing two unlawful detainer suits, LASC Case Nos. 24NWUD00730 and 24NWUD00738. The Complaint alleges that Defendants wrongfully sued STIR Inc., Plaintiff’s organization, as a California corporation, where it is actually a 501(c)(3) non-profit organization. The crux of the Complaint is that “Defendants breached the contract or Rental Agreement between Plaintiff and Defendants when they illegally accessed Plaintiff's storage unit before he vacated the premises, violating California Code, Civil Code - CIV § 1954, and after that, fulfilled their threat.” (Compl., ¶ 15.) The Complaint alleges the following causes of action: 1) Violation of California Civil Code § 1942.5 (Retaliatory Eviction), 2) Wrongful Eviction, 3) Breach of Contract, and 4) Violation of California Code, Civil Code - CIV § 1954 (Landlord’s Unlawful Entry). On June 4, 2024, the Court sustained Defendants’ Demurrer to the Complaint without leave to amend as to the first, second, and fourth causes of action. (6/4/24 Order.)

 

The Court sustained the third cause of action with 20 days’ leave to amend. (See 6/4/25 Minute Order.) On June 12, 2025, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (FAC). The FAC alleges one cause of action for “Breach of Contract, Violation of California Civil Code § 1954.”

 

On February 19, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to File Second Amended Complaint (SAC). (2/19/26 Minute Order.) The SAC became the operative complaint.

 

The SAC alleges one cause of action for breach of contract. The breach of contract action is primarily based on an incident in which Defendant Kimberly Cabrera noticed the subject unit had no lock on it during daily inspections, checked to see if anyone was in the unit, and placed a temporary lock on the unit. (See Compl., ¶¶ 73-74.) Plaintiff asserts that four provisions of the rental agreement between the parties were breached.

 

Defendants now demur to the SAC.


Legal Standard

 

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (“Donabedian”) (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.) 

 

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.) 

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that the parties meet and confer “in person, by telephone, or by video conference.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).)

 

Defense counsel “took all reasonable steps to meet and confer with Plaintiff” regarding the issues in the Demurrer, but the parties could not reach a resolution. (Ferraz Decl., ¶10.)

 

The Court notes that Defense counsel fails to indicate the means by which the parties met and conferred. Nevertheless, the Court will address the merits of the Demurrer in interests of judicial efficiency.

 

Discussion

 

Defendants demur on grounds that the first cause of action fails to state fact to constitute a cause of action and is uncertain.

 

          Standing/Existence of a Contract

 

First, Defendants argue that Plaintiff was not party to the subject rental agreement attached to the SAC as Exhibit A. Further, Exhibit J to the SAC is a photograph of a debit card issued to STIR, which Plaintiff alleges was used to pay the rent for the storage units. (SAC, ¶ 26, Exhs. J-K.) Thus, Plaintiff has no standing to sue for breach of contract under the rental agreement.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that, once STIR was terminated, Plaintiff became the operative tenant on the rental agreement by virtue of the fact that he occupied the unit for over two decades. (SAC, ¶¶ 23-28.) Plaintiff cites to “California Code Section 1940” and “California Code Section 1940.1.”

 

The SAC alleges that “Plaintiff signed a Rental Agreement with Defendant Bellflower Self Storage, 14822 Lakewood Blvd, Bellflower, California, 90706 (“Property” or “Facility”) around 1997. On August 22, 2020, Plaintiff, an agent of STIR Inc., a California corporation, who is not a party in the instant case, replaced himself with STIR Inc. on the Rental Agreement as the tenant for tax purposes.” (Compl., ¶ 14.) When STIR Inc. (hereinafter, “STIR”) was terminated, Plaintiff “became a live- in tenant in practice. . .” (Compl., ¶ 15.)

 

The Court may rely on exhibits as an extension of the allegations in determining that a defense exists on the face of the complaint. (See Barnett v. Fireman's Fund Ins. Co. (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 500, 505 [“[T]o the extent the factual allegations conflict with the content of the exhibits to the complaint, we rely on and accept as true the contents of the exhibits and treat as surplusage the pleader's allegations as to the legal effect of the exhibits.”].)

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff is not party to the subject rental agreement. (SAC, Exh. A.) The rental agreement names STIR as the tenant, while Plaintiff is only listed as the person “in care of” STIR. Further, Civil Code section 1940 and 1940.1 are inapplicable. Section 1940 pertains to tenancy regulations generally and section 1940.1 pertains to residential hotels. No legal authority supports Plaintiff’s position that he automatically became the tenant on the agreement by virtue of the parties’ transactional history.

 

Thus, Plaintiff lacks standing to enforce the rental agreement as a non-party.

 

          Breach of Contract

 

Defendants further argue that, even if Plaintiff has standing, the SAC fails to allege any breach of the rental agreement. The SAC alleges that Defendant Cabrera placed a lock on Unit 159 after observing that the unit was unsecured. Defendant Cabrera emailed Plaintiff stating that a lock had been installed that morning on unit 159 “to secure the tenant’s belongings” (SAC, ¶ 74), The lease provides that the landlord requires the tenant to provide a disc lock and key for the Premises which tenant deems sufficient. (SAC, ¶ 76). Further, the SAC alleges that, the next day, Defendant Cabrera emailed Plaintiff that the lock had been removed pursuant to his request. (SAC, ¶ 80.) The SAC fails to allege how exactly Defendant Cabrera’s actions constitute a breach of the rental agreement. Further, the SAC fails to allege that Plaintiff suffered damages as a result of the temporary lock installation.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the SAC adequately describes how Defendant Cabrera breached the following provisions of the rental agreement:

 

-      [Paragraph 7] INSURANCE: Tenant shall bear the risk of loss or damage which would have been covered under the above mentioned fire and extended coverage insurance policy. Tenant hereby releases Landlord and its agents, authorized representatives and employees. . .from any and all claims for damages or loss to the personal property in, on, or about the Premises.

-      [Paragraph 12] DENIAL OF ACCESS: If rent or other charges are ten (10) or more days past due Landlord may deny Tenant access to the rented self-storage space.

-      [Paragraph 15] LOCK: PADLOCKS AND COMBINATION TYPE LOCKS ARE NOT PERMITTED. We require that tenant shall provide at tenants expense a disc lock and key for the Premises which Tenant, in Tenant's sole discretion. Tenant shall not provide Landlord or Landlord's agents with a key to Tenant's lock.

-      [Paragraph 16] RIGHT TO ENTER, INSPECT AND REPAIR PREMISES: Tenant shall grant Landlord [sic] Landlord's agents or the representatives of any governmental authority, including police and fire officials, access to the Premises upon three days prior written notice to Tenant.

 

(SAC, ¶¶ 75-83.)

 

The Court finds that the SAC fails to allege any cognizable breach of the rental agreement. According to the rental agreement, all tenants were required to use a disk lock. (See SAC, ¶ 76, Exh A.) Noticing that there was no lock on the subject unit, Defendant Cabrera put a disc lock on the unit on May 22, 2024, and removed the lock at Plaintiff’s request the next day. (SAC, ¶¶ 74, 80.) The Court is not persuaded by Plaintiff’s interpretation of breach—i.e., that Plaintiff was denied access by the temporary lock, that Defendants entered/inspected the unit without notice, etc. There is no indication that Defendant Cabrera entered the unit, but only that she opened the door to check if anyone was inside before securing the unit.

 

Lastly, even if a breach occurred, the Court finds that the SAC only alleges in a conclusory manner that “Defendants' actions have caused Plaintiff's detriment and harm, and damages legally and proximately caused by Defendants' actions, in an amount to be shown according to proof at trial.” (SAC, ¶ 95.) As noted by Defendant, there are no allegations that Plaintiff “was trapped inside the premises or that he incurred any actual expense or inconvenience” as a result of the temporary locking, for example. (Dem., 5:11-12.)

 

Thus, the Court finds that the breach of contract cause of action fails to state a claim.

 

          Defendant Cabrera

 

Defendants further argue that Defendant Cabrera is an improper defendant because she was not a party to the rental agreement. Defendant Cabrera cannot be sued in her individual capacity for alleged acts she committed as an agent for Defendant Bellflower.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendant Cabrera both acted in her own individual capacity and in the capacity of an agent of Defendant Bellflower, pursuant to language in her email. Plaintiff asserts that Defendant Cabrera committed independent acts constituting trespass and violation of Labor Code section 2865.

 

“[A]gents are protected from vicarious liability for the torts of their principals, but are held responsible for their own actions that constitute a tort […].” (Peredia v. HR Mobile Services, Inc. (2018) 25 Cal.App.5th 680, 693.)

 

The Court finds that Defendant Cabrera cannot be held personally liable where 1) she was acting as an agent for Defendant Bellflower, and it is found that Bellflower committed no breach of contract, and 2) the SAC contains no allegations that Defendant Cabrera committed any tort. The Court may not consider allegations or theories offered in the opposition, as they are not within the four corners of the SAC. (See Donabedian, supra, 116 Cal.App.4th at p. 994.)

         

Conclusion

 

The Court is not persuaded the outlined deficiencies can be cured with amendment.

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendants’ Demurrer to the SAC is sustained without leave to amend.

  III.        Defendant’s Motion for Request for Admissions, Propounded on Plaintiff Miguel Castillo, be Deemed Admitted (brought as Trustee) is DENIED.

 IV.        Defendant’s Motion for Request for Admissions, Propounded on Plaintiff Elizabeth Castillo, be Deemed Admitted (brought as Trustee) is DENIED.

   V.        Defendant is awarded $750.00 in sanctions in total for all Motions, payable within 60 days.

 

Defendant to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is a tenants’ rights action. On October 10, 2024, Plaintiff Miguel Castillo and Elizabeth Castillo (“Plaintiffs”) filed this action against Defendant Jacob Ibarra, individually and Trustee of the Ibarra Living Trust, (“Defendant”), and Does 1 to 100.

 

On November 10, 2025, Plaintiffs’ prior counsel was relieved as counsel. (See 11/10/25 Order.)

 

On April 18, 2026, Defendant served Requests for Admissions on Plaintiffs. (Fuentes Decls., ¶¶ 4-5, Exhs. 4-5.)

 

On June 10, 2026, Attorney Dalton B. Phillips was substituted as counsel for Plaintiffs.

 

The following matters are on calendar:

 

-      Defendant’s Motion for Request for Admissions, Propounded on Plaintiff Miguel Castillo, be Deemed Admitted (brought as an individual)

-      Defendant’s Motion for Request for Admissions, Propounded on Plaintiff Elizabeth Castillo, be Deemed Admitted (brought as an individual)

-      Defendant’s Motion for Request for Admissions, Propounded on Plaintiff Miguel Castillo, be Deemed Admitted (brought as Trustee)

-      Defendant’s Motion for Request for Admissions, Propounded on Plaintiff Elizabeth Castillo, be Deemed Admitted (brought as Trustee)

 

The Court will address the Motions concurrently as they are substantially similar.


Legal Standard

 

Where there has been no timely response to a request for admission under Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.010, the propounding party may move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the requests be deemed admitted. (Code of Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (b).) The court “shall” grant a motion to deem admitted requests for admissions, “unless it finds that the party to whom the requests for admission have been directed has served, before the hearing on the motion, a proposed response to the requests for admission that is in substantial compliance with Section 2033.220.”¿ (Code of Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).)¿¿

 

Discussion

 

On April 18, 2026, Defendant served Requests for Admissions on Plaintiffs via mail and e-mail, as well as by priority mail on April 20, 2026. (Fuentes Decls., ¶¶ 4-5, Exhs. 4-5.) Plaintiffs failed to respond by the deadline of May 20, 2026. (Fuentes Decls., ¶ 8.) Defendant asserts that he granted Plaintiffs an extension to respond by June 3, 2026 via a meet and confer email. (Fuentes Decls., ¶¶ 8-9, Exh. 3.) Plaintiffs later retained current counsel, Mr. Phillips. Mr. Phillips called Defense counsel requesting an extension to respond, and Defense counsel orally granted an extension for Plaintiffs to respond by June 30, 2026. (Fuentes Decls., ¶ 12.) Defendants note that, in open court on June 16, 2026, Mr. Phillips agreed to comply with the June 30, 2026 extension. (Fuentes Decls., ¶ 13.) However, Plaintiffs did not comply with the deadline. (Ibid.)

 

Defendant requests $4,500.00 in sanctions in total for all Motions.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs note that current counsel was retained on June 1, 2026. (Phillips Decls., ¶ 2.) On June 2, 2026, Plaintiffs’ counsel called Defense counsel requesting a 30-day extension from the June 3, 2026 deadline previously offered. (Phillips Decls., ¶ 5.) After a lengthy discussion, Defense counsel agreed to a 30-day extension—however, Defense counsel did not state that the extension expired June 30, and Mr. Phillips did not request or agree to a June 30 deadline. (Phillips Decls., ¶ 7.) Plaintiffs’ counsel thus believed that the operative deadline was July 2, 2026, 30 days after the June 2, 2026 call.

 

On July 2, 2026, Plaintiffs served substantive responses, without objections, to the four subject discovery requests. (Phillips Decl., ¶ 9, Ex. B.) Plaintiffs argue that Defendant filed these Motions approximately one hour after the responses were served. (Phillips Decl., ¶ 11.) After meeting and conferring, Defense counsel refused to withdraw the Motions. (Phillips Decl., ¶¶ 12-13.) In an abundance of caution, Plaintiffs re-served verified, objection-free, substantive responses on July 22, 2026. (Phillips Decl., ¶ 14, Exh. C.)

 

Plaintiffs thus argue that the Motions should be denied where Plaintiffs provided substantially compliant responses to the subject discovery requests.

 

In reply, Defense counsel claims that he never agreed to a July 2 or July 3 deadline. (Fuentes Reply Decls., ¶ 3.) Defense counsel asserts that the burden is on Plaintiffs to demonstrate that an extension agreement existed, noting that Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.260 requires that the responding party give notice of any such agreement. Defendant argues that Plaintiffs’ July 2, 2026 responses were not substantially compliant because they were not verified. Further, on that date, Plaintiffs only responded to Defendant’s Requests for Admissions propounded as Jacob Ibarra as an individual, not as a Trustee. (See Fuentes Reply Decls., ¶  7, Exh. 1.) Defendant asserts that Plaintiffs failed to serve verified responses until July 22, 2026, 20 days after these Motions were filed. Thus, sanctions are warranted.

 

The court shall grant a motion to deem requests for admissions, admitted, “unless it finds that the party to whom the requests for admission have been directed has served, before the hearing on the motion, a proposed response to the requests for admission that is in substantial compliance with Section 2033.220.” (Code Civ. Proc., 2033.280, subd. (c).) “It is mandatory that the court impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) on the party or attorney, or both, whose failure to serve a timely response to requests for admission necessitated this motion.” (Ibid.)

 

The Court finds that the parties’ disagreement regarding the operative deadline

for Plaintiffs to provide responses is not dispositive. The bottom line is that Plaintiffs’ July 22, 2026 responses to all subject discovery requests were substantially compliant with the Code—i.e., the responses were substantive and verified.

The Court notes that Plaintiffs’ July 2, 2026 responses were not in “substantial compliance” because they were unverified. (See St. Mary v. Superior Court (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 762, 778 [finding that unverified responses to requests for admissions are not in “substantial compliance” with the Code].) Further, those responses only responded to two of the four discovery requests. (See Fuentes Reply Decls., ¶  7, Exh. 1.) Thus, even if the operative deadline was July 2, 2026, the responses would not defeat the mandatory sanctions associated with the Motions.

 

Because Plaintiffs have provided substantially compliant, albeit untimely, responses before the hearing on these Motions, the Motions are DENIED as MOOT.

 

Sanctions are “mandatory” even if untimely responses are provided. (Code Civ. Proc., 2033.280, subd. (c); see also Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1348

[“The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though. . .the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.].)

 

Defendant is awarded $750.00 in sanctions in total for all Motions, payable within 60 days.

Case Number: 25NWCV00368    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: L

Defendant Monterey Lakewood Owner, LLC’s Demurrer to the Complaint is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

 

Defendant’s Motion to Strike is MOOT.

 

Defendant to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is a wrongful death action. On January 29, 2025, Plaintiff Brian Anakin Hill-Alamina, a minor, by and through his guardian ad litem, Angela Hill, individually and as wrongful death beneficiary and successor-in-interest to decedent Brian Alamina,(“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants City of Lakewood, City of Cerritos, County of Los Angeles, State of California, Greystar California, Inc., and Does 1 to 100.

 

Various amendments, cross-pleadings, and dismissals have been filed. As amended, the Complaint asserts claims against Defendants the City of Lakewood, the City of Cerritos, The People of the State of California, acting by and through the Department of Transportation, Greystar California, Inc., Yasseerarafat G. Shaikh, Mirza Mahejabin, B9 MF 12350 Del Amo LLC, and Monterey Lakewood Owner, LLC (collectively, “Defendants”). The Defendants have filed a total of five cross-claims directed amongst themselves.

 

The Complaint alleges that Brian Alamina (“Decedent”) was killed in a motor vehicle accident on roadways that were negligently owned, designed, and maintained by Defendants such that there was an unreasonable risk of harm to Decedent. Specifically, in June 2024, Decedent “was traveling eastbound on Del Amo Boulevard between Norwalk Boulevard and Claretta Avenue when a vehicle operated by Yaseerarafat G. Shaikh attempted a U-turn and collided with [him].” (Compl., ¶ 1.) The Complaint alleges causes of action for 1) Negligence, 2) Premises Liability, 3) Negligence - Dangerous Condition of Public Property, 4) Survival Action, and 5) Wrongful Death.

 

Defendant Monterey Lakewood Owner, LLC (hereinafter, “Monterey”) now demurs to all causes of action in the Complaint. Monterey also moves to strike portions of the Complaint.

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

Monterey’s Request for Judicial Notice of a recorded Grant Deed, dated January 15, 2025, transferring real property from Monterey to Towne Center Fee Owner, LLC, (Exhibit A) is granted.


Legal Standard

 

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (“Donabedian”) (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.) 

 

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.) 

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a) and section 435.5 require that the parties meet and confer “in person,¿by telephone, or by video conference.” 

 

The parties met and conferred via telephone regarding the issues in this Demurrer and Motion to Strike but were unable to come to a resolution. (Olson Decl., ¶ 2.) The Court finds that the meet and confer requirement has been met.

 

Discussion

 

Monterey provides the following context for this Demurrer: The Complaint alleges that the public roadway and the gate at the adjacent apartment complex created a dangerous condition because of roadway configuration, U-turn space, signage, markings, visibility, insufficient signals or signs and an adjacent apartment-complex gate. (Compl., ¶¶ 3, 25.) The Complaint alleges that Defendant Greystar California, Inc. owned the gate at the adjacent apartment complex (hereinafter, “Private Premises”). (Ibid.) Monterey was later named as Doe 4.

 

Monterey argues that the first and second causes of action for negligence and premises liability fail because Plaintiff cannot plead duty, control, or causation against it. Specifically, a recorded grant deed shows Monterey conveyed its interest in the property to Towne Center Fee Owner, LLC in January 2015, nine years before the subject incident. (RJN, Exh. A.) Thus, Monterey had no duty because it did not own, possess, or control the Private Premises. Further, Monterey had no duty because the incident occurred on a public roadway, and a private landowner has no duty to maintain adjacent public property. The Complaint does not allege that Monterey created a hazard condition or had significant control over the public roadway. Because the tort causes of action fail, the fourth and fifth causes of action for wrongful death and survival action, which are derivative claims, also fail.

 

As to the third cause of action for dangerous condition of public property pursuant to Government Code sections 830 and 835, such claim does not apply to private entities such as Monterey.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff cites to the following allegations as they pertain to Monterey:

 

-      “[T]he gate at the adjacent apartment complex. . .is designated for exiting vehicles only, not left turning vehicles to enter. So vehicles in the left-turn lane. . .were forced to make U-turns only.” (Compl., ¶ 3.)

-      “[Defendant] planned, designed, built, constructed, maintained, operated, controlled, repaired, and/or monitored the roadway and adjacent apartment complex at the collision site, and failed to make the roadway and complex safe for drivers. . .” (Compl., ¶ 15.)

-      “The entrance to the adjacent apartment complex at this location created a hazardous condition.” (Compl., ¶ 35(c).)

-      “The subject area and the adjacent property created inadequate visibility, including lacking a reasonable line of sight.” (Compl., ¶ 35(k).)

 

The Complaint thus alleges that Monterey designed, implemented, and maintained the hazardous condition that caused the subject incident, at the time it owned the Private Premises. Plaintiff asserts that the fact that Private Premises were transferred in 2015 is not dispositive. The general rule on non-liability for dangerous conditions created by landowners does not apply to commercial developers like Monterey. Further, the Complaint’s theory of liability rests on negligent design of the Private Premises, which does not rely on ownership at the time of the subject incident. Because Monterey contributed to dangerous condition of the public roadway, via the Private Premises, it can be held liable regardless of where the incident took place.

 

In reply, Monterey reiterates its arguments. Monterey notes that there are no allegations in the Complaint that Monterey was a designer, architect, or developer.

 

The general rule is the prior owners are not are not liable for harm that occurs to third-parties after they relinquish ownership and control, even if their negligence created the hazard and it existed at the time of sale. (Preston v. Goldman (“Preston”) (1986) 42 Cal.3d 108, 112, 125-127 [adopting Rest.2d Torts § 352]; see also Lewis v. Chevron U.S.A., Inc. (“Lewis”) (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 690, 692.) However, an exception to this rule is that prior owners may be liable if they actively conceal known defective conditions to the buyer. (See Lewis, supra, 119 Cal.App.4th at p. 698 [“The Restatement provides for no liability unless a seller knows of a hidden defect and conceals it. “].)

 

This case is similar to the Lewis case. In that case, the plaintiff was working on premises when a copper water pipe burst, injuring the plaintiff. (Lewis, supra, 119 Cal.App.4th at p. 692.) The plaintiff substituted in Chevron U.S.A., Inc. (“Chevron”) as a doe defendant. (Ibid.) Chevron sold the property to the current owner eight years prior to plaintiff’s accident. (Ibid.) The Court of Appeal affirmed the trial court’s granting of summary judgment based on the general rule barring liability for predecessor owners, finding that the “known condition” exception did not apply based on plaintiff concessions. (Id. at pp. 699-700.) The Court notes that the Lewis case involved a motion for summary judgment—however, the application of law regarding duty is relevant here.

 

Here, the judicially-noticed grant deed (RJN, Exh. A) reveals that Monterey transferred its interest in the Private Premises to another entity in 2015, nine years prior to the subject incident. The Court thus finds that, as a prior owner, Monterey had no duty for Plaintiff’s harm, even if their negligence created the hazard. Further, there are no allegations in the Complaint suggesting that Monterey actively concealed any known hazard from the buyer such that the exception to the general rule should apply.

 

Further, as to Plaintiff’s argument that Monterey can be held liable for negligent design of the Private Premises, the Court acknowledges that “builders, contractors, and architects owe a duty of care to third parties.” (Beacon Residential Community Assn. v. Skidmore, Owings & Merrill LLP (2014) 59 Cal.4th 568, 573.) However, there are no allegations in the Complaint that Monterey was a builder, contractor, or architect of the Subject Premises. The Complaint only contains conclusory statements that Defendants negligently “planned, designed, built, constructed, maintained” the subject area. (Compl., ¶¶ 15, 16, 18, 27, 29, 34.) Plaintiff offers no explanation in the opposition suggesting how the Complaint can be amended to specifically allege that Monterey negligently designed the Private Premises. Further, even if it could be established that Monterey created the defect, Monterey would still be shielded by the non-liability for former owners rule. (See Lewis, supra, 119 Cal.App.4th 690, 692.)

 

Thus, the Court finds that Complaint fails to state a cause of action for negligence and premises liability, as well as the derivative causes of action for wrongful death and survival action.

 

As to the third cause of action for dangerous condition of property, the Court finds that Monterey cannot be held liable as a private entity. The third cause of action is brought under “Government Code Sections 815.2, 815.4, 815.6, 820, 830, 830.8, 835, and 835.2 et seq.” (Compl., ¶ 37.) Government Code section 830 defines property of a public entity” and “public property” as used in section 835 [regarding dangerous conditions of public property] to mean “real or personal property owned or controlled by the public entity.” (Gov. Code, § 830.) Plaintiff does not address this point in the opposition. Thus, the Court finds that the Complaint fails to state a cause of action for dangerous condition of property against Monterey.

 

Accordingly, Monterey’s Demurrer to the Complaint is sustained without leave to amend as to all causes of action.

 

Motion to Strike

 

As the Demurrer was sustained in full without leave to amend, the Motion to Strike is moot.
