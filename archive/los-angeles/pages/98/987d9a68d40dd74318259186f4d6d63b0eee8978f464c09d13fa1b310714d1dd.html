APPLICATION TO BE ADMITTED PRO HAC VICE

 

Application filed July 6, 2026.

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiffs Kenneth Lawson and Monique Jourdain-Lawson

RESPONDING PARTY: N/A

NOTICE: OK.

RELIEF REQUESTED: Admission of James D. Montgomery, Jr., Esq. to appear as counsel pro hac vice.

RULING: Granted.

 

BACKGROUND/DISCUSSION

 

On July 6, 2026, Plaintiffs Kenneth Lawson and Monique Jourdain-Lawson filed an application to admit James D. Montgomery, Jr., Esq. to appear pro hac vice in this action. On July 29, 2026, the Court continued the hearing because the proof of service did not comply with California Rules of Court, rule 9.40, subdivision (c)(1). The Court directed Plaintiffs to serve the application and notice of continued hearing on all appearing parties and the State Bar and file a signed proof of service. On July 29, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a renewed application and proof of service.

 

A non-licensee of the State Bar may appear as counsel pro hac vice in the Court's discretion. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40, subd. (a).) An active State Bar licensee must be associated as attorney of record. (Ibid.) The applicant must file a verified application together with proof of service by mail of the application and notice of hearing on all appearing parties and on the State Bar at its San Francisco office. (Id., subd. (c)(1).) The application must contain the information required by subdivision (d). (Id., subd. (d).) The applicant must pay the application fee set by the State Bar. (Id., subd. (e).)

 

Here, Montgomery resides in Illinois, maintains his office in Illinois, and states that he is not a California resident and does not regularly engage in practice or other business in California. (Montgomery Decl., ¶ 2.) He has been admitted to practice before the Supreme Court of Illinois since November 1, 1982, is a member in good standing, and has never been suspended or disbarred. (Id., ¶¶ 3-4.) He has not filed an application to appear pro hac vice in California during the preceding two years. (Id., ¶ 5.) California attorney John C. Allen IV is associated as attorney of record. (Id., ¶ 6; Allen Decl., ¶¶ 1, 3.) Montgomery agrees to comply with the California Rules of Professional Conduct and consents to the jurisdiction of California courts and disciplinary authorities. (Montgomery Decl., ¶ 7.)

 

The deficiencies identified at the prior hearing have been substantially cured. The renewed proof of service is signed and identifies the appearing parties and the State Bar at its San Francisco office as recipients. (Proof of Service, pp. 3-4.) Allen declares that the application and proposed order were delivered to the State Bar with payment of the required $50 application fee. (Allen Decl., ¶ 4.) Under these circumstances, the Court finds that the renewed filing substantially complies with rule 9.40 and cures the deficiencies identified at the prior hearing.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The application to admit James D. Montgomery, Jr., Esq. to appear as counsel pro hac vice is GRANTED.

PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION FOR PRE-TRIAL DISCOVERY OF DEFENDANT’S FINANCIAL CONDITION PURSUANT TO CIVIL CODE SECTION 3295(c)

 

Motion filed on June 15, 2026.

MOVING PARTY: Kenneth Lawson and Monique Jourdain-Lawson (Plaintiffs)

RESPONDING PARTY: Cash McElroy Jones (Jones), 5th Amendment Management Corp., and 5th Amendment Entertainment, Inc. (collectively, Defendants)

NOTICE: OK.

RELIEF REQUESTED: Plaintiffs seek an order permitting pretrial discovery of Jones’s financial condition pursuant to Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c).

RULING: Denied.

 

BACKGROUND

 

On September 1, 2021, Plaintiffs filed the First Amended Complaint. On June 15, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the present Motion for Pre-Trial Discovery of Defendant’s Financial Condition. On August 20, 2026, the parties filed a Joint Trial Setting Conference Statement. On August 28, 2026, Defendants filed an Opposition supported by the declarations of Jones and Justin R. Trauben. No reply was filed.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2024.050, subdivision (a), provides that “[o]n motion of any party, the court may grant leave to complete discovery proceedings, or to have a motion concerning discovery heard, closer to the initial trial date, or to reopen discovery after a new trial date has been set.” In exercising its discretion, the Court considers the necessity and reasons for the discovery, the requesting party’s diligence and reasons the discovery was not completed earlier, any effect on the trial calendar or prejudice to another party, and the time between the former and present trial dates. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.050, subd. (b).)

 

Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c), provides that no pretrial discovery by a plaintiff into the financial condition of the defendant shall be permitted unless the Court enters an order permitting such discovery. “Upon motion by the plaintiff supported by appropriate affidavits and after a hearing, if the court deems a hearing to be necessary, the court may at any time enter an order permitting the discovery otherwise prohibited by this subdivision if the court finds, on the basis of the supporting and opposing affidavits presented, that the plaintiff has established that there is a substantial probability that the plaintiff will prevail” on a claim for punitive damages. (Ibid.)

 

“[B]efore a court may enter an order permitting discovery of a defendant’s financial condition, it must (1) weigh the evidence submitted in favor of and in opposition to motion for discovery, and (2) make a finding that it is very likely the plaintiff will prevail on his claim for punitive damages.” (Jabro v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 754, 758.) “In this context, we interpret the words ‘substantial probability’ to mean ‘very likely’ or ‘a strong likelihood’ just as their plain meaning suggests.” (Ibid.) It is not sufficient for a plaintiff to merely make a prima facie showing such as would avoid summary judgment. (Id. at p. 759.)

Here, the Court’s July 9, 2026 order directed the parties to identify any fact discovery they sought to reopen, the reasons supporting reopening, and whether limited reopening would suffice. The Court ordered that discovery remain closed unless otherwise ordered. (Trauben Decl., Ex. 7.) Plaintiffs thereafter requested reopening for three witness depositions and discovery of Jones’s financial condition. (Joint TSC Statement, ¶ 19.) The Court addresses here only the request for financial-condition discovery. The parties’ remaining discovery requests are addressed separately at the Trial Setting Conference.

 

Here, the Court’s July 9, 2026 order directed the parties to identify any fact discovery they sought to reopen, the reasons for reopening, and whether limited reopening would suffice. The Court ordered discovery to remain closed unless otherwise ordered. (Trauben Decl., Ex. 7.) Plaintiffs thereafter requested reopening for three witness depositions and discovery of Jones’s financial condition. (Joint TSC Statement, ¶ 19.) The Court addresses here only whether fact discovery should be reopened to permit pretrial discovery of Jones’s financial condition. The parties’ remaining discovery requests are addressed separately at the Trial Setting Conference.

 

The factors under Code of Civil Procedure section 2024.050, subdivision (b), weigh against reopening fact discovery to permit pretrial discovery of Jones’s financial condition.

 

As to necessity, Plaintiffs contend that Jones’s financial information is important to presentation of their punitive damages claim and can be readily produced. (Joint TSC Statement, ¶¶ 14, 19.) Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c), however, permits Plaintiffs to subpoena financial documents and witnesses for trial. That statutory procedure preserves Plaintiffs’ ability to present evidence of Jones’s financial condition if punitive liability is established and reduces the need to reopen discovery for pretrial production. The necessity factor therefore provides only limited support for reopening. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.050, subd. (b)(1).)

 

The diligence factor weighs substantially against reopening. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.050, subd. (b)(2).) Plaintiffs alleged punitive damages in September 2021 but did not seek authorization under section 3295(c) until June 15, 2026, approximately four years and nine months later. During that period, the parties conducted extensive written discovery and eight depositions, litigated a summary judgment motion, and participated in mediation. (Trauben Decl. ¶¶ 6-9.) Plaintiffs explain that they did not propound financial discovery because section 3295(c) prohibited it absent a court order. (Allen Decl. ¶¶ 6-7.) That explains why the discovery itself was not propounded. It does not explain why Plaintiffs waited until June 2026 to seek the order required to conduct it.

 

The Joint Trial Setting Conference Statement does not account for that delay. Plaintiffs generally assert that discovery was diligently pursued and provide a discovery timeline. (Joint TSC Statement, ¶ 7, Ex. A.) They also detail their efforts to locate and schedule the depositions of Polsky, Marzuca, and Ross-Jones. (Id., ¶¶ 10, 12.) As to Jones’s financial condition, however, Plaintiffs identify only their efforts to advance the hearing after this motion was filed. (Id., ¶ 14.) Those efforts do not address why authorization under section 3295(c) was not sought earlier.

 

The Court previously identified the same deficiency when Plaintiffs sought ex parte relief to advance this motion or extend the discovery deadline. On July 2, 2026, the Court found that Plaintiffs had not adequately explained why the section 3295(c) motion could not reasonably have been brought earlier in light of the trial date and discovery deadlines. The Court also found that conclusory assertions of diligence were insufficient. (Trauben Decl., Ex. 5.)

 

The trial-calendar factor does not weigh against reopening. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.050, subd. (b)(3).) The September 14, 2026 trial date has been vacated, and no new trial date is set. Reopening discovery therefore would not interfere with a presently scheduled trial. The factor under subdivision (b)(4) likewise carries little weight because there is no presently set trial date to compare with the former September 14, 2026 trial date. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.050, subd. (b)(4).)

 

The age and procedural posture of the action nevertheless bear on the effect of reopening discovery on trial readiness. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.050, subd. (b).) This action was filed on March 16, 2021. On March 16, 2026, the Court conducted an Order to Show Cause concerning the five-year rule under Code of Civil Procedure section 583.310. The Court discharged the OSC after approving the parties’ stipulation to continue trial and continued trial to September 14, 2026. (Minute Order, Mar. 16, 2026.) That trial date was later vacated. Reopening fact discovery now would extend the period before this more-than-five-year-old action can be restored to trial readiness, particularly where the financial discovery could have been sought substantially earlier.

 

On balance, the absence of a presently scheduled trial does not outweigh the limited need for pretrial financial discovery, Plaintiffs’ unexplained delay in seeking authorization under section 3295(c), and the resulting additional delay in restoring the case to trial readiness. The request to reopen fact discovery to permit pretrial discovery of Jones’s financial condition is denied.

 

Separately, the Court notes deficiencies in the evidentiary materials submitted with Plaintiff’s motion. Plaintiffs’ memorandum states that declarations from Jourdain-Lawson and Marissa Clark were submitted in support of the motion, but neither declaration appears in the moving papers. Lawson’s declaration is signed, but the date and place of execution are blank and it does not state that it was executed under the laws of the State of California. (Lawson Decl.) Allen’s declaration is properly executed, but largely identifies the other evidence on which Plaintiffs rely. (Allen Decl. ¶ 4.) Thus, the declarations actually submitted with the motion do not match the affidavit showing described in Plaintiffs’ memorandum.

 

Because the Court denies Plaintiffs’ request to reopen fact discovery under Code of Civil Procedure section 2024.050, the Court does not reach whether Plaintiffs have established the substantial probability of prevailing on their punitive damages claim required by Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c). The Court also does not reach Defendants’ objections that the requested financial discovery is overbroad, improperly seeks tax returns and historical financial information, and should be limited to Jones’s current financial condition and subject to appropriate confidentiality protection.

 

CONCLUSION

 

Because the Court declines to reopen fact discovery for the purpose of obtaining pretrial discovery of Jones’s financial condition, Plaintiffs’ Motion for Pre-Trial Discovery of Defendant’s Financial Condition Pursuant to Civil Code Section 3295(c) is DENIED.

 

The parties’ remaining requests concerning reopening or completion of fact and expert discovery are addressed separately at the Trial Setting Conference.

OSC RE PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION

 

Application filed on August 24, 2026.

MOVING PARTY: Elly-Joy Shalomé Kaushansky (Plaintiff)

RESPONDING PARTY: Doron Shauly (Defendant)

NOTICE: OK.

RELIEF REQUESTED: Plaintiff seeks a preliminary injunction restraining Defendants from pursuing termination of Plaintiff’s tenancy or recovery of possession based on the disputed rent obligation and alleged arrearages during the pendency of this action. Plaintiff also seeks restraints against harassment, intimidation, surveillance, unwanted contact, and unauthorized entry.

RULING: Denied. The temporary restraining order issued on August 25, 2026 terminates upon denial of the preliminary injunction. Defendant’s alternative request that Plaintiff deposit unpaid and current rent into escrow is denied as moot.

 

BACKGROUND

On November 17, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint against Alon West Hills, LLC (Alon West), Alon Investments, LLC, Iris Shapiro, Walter Floridia, and Defendant Doron Shauly (Shauly) (collectively, Defendants). The First Amended Complaint asserts fifteen causes of action arising from Plaintiff’s tenancy, alleged habitability conditions, rent disputes, alleged retaliation and harassment, and an April 2023 physical altercation. (First Amended Complaint at p. 1, ¶ 135.)

 

On August 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant application with a declaration and exhibits. Plaintiff requests, among other relief, an injunction restraining Defendants from pursuing termination of her tenancy or recovery of possession based on the disputed rent obligation and alleged arrearages “during the pendency of this action, through trial or further order of this Court.” Shauly filed an opposition on August 24, 2026. Plaintiff filed a reply and additional declaration and exhibits on August 25, 2026.

 

On August 25, 2026, the Court temporarily restrained Alon West from commencing or prosecuting an unlawful detainer action based on the August 22, 2026 Three-Day Notice to Pay Rent or Quit, or a successive or replacement notice founded upon the same disputed rental obligation. The Court denied the remaining requested restraints without prejudice and set this Order to Show Cause.

 

On September 4, 2026, Shauly filed an opposition to the Order to Show Cause. Shauly argues that no unlawful detainer action has been filed, Plaintiff may raise her rent dispute as a defense if one is filed, and Plaintiff has not paid rent since September 1, 2025. On September 9, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Reply and additional declaration.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

A preliminary injunction is an interim remedy intended to preserve the status quo pending a determination on the merits. (White v. Davis (2003) 30 Cal.4th 528, 554.) In deciding whether to issue a preliminary injunction, the Court considers two interrelated factors: the likelihood that the moving party will prevail on the merits and the relative interim harm likely to result from granting or denying the injunction. (Ibid.) The factors operate on a sliding scale, but the moving party must demonstrate at least some possibility of success on the merits. (Id. at pp. 561-562.)

 

Plaintiff bears the burden of establishing the grounds for preliminary injunctive relief. (O’Connell v. Superior Court (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 1452, 1481.) A preliminary injunction requires a sufficient factual showing by a person having knowledge of the facts, made under oath or declaration under penalty of perjury. (Ancora-Citronelle Corp. v. Green (1974) 41 Cal.App.3d 146, 150.) The Court may disregard conclusory statements in a supporting declaration. (Sullivan v. Fox (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 673, 682-683.) If no evidentiary objection is made, a declaration may nevertheless be received despite hearsay or other inadmissible matter. (Waller v. Waller (1970) 3 Cal.App.3d 456, 464.)

 

DISCUSSION

 

Likelihood of Success

Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint alleges fifteen causes of action. (First Amended Complaint at p. 1.) The Application does not organize its likelihood-of-success showing by cause of action or apply particular evidence to the elements of each claim. (Application at pp. 11-19.) Instead, Plaintiff identifies numerous statutory and decisional authorities and argues generally that the record establishes probable success on “multiple grounds.” (Application at pp. 11-19.) Her September 9 Reply similarly treats habitability, disputed rent, retaliation, harassment, and possession as an intertwined controversy. (September 9 Reply at pp. 1-8.)

 

Plaintiff emphasizes that Shauly is the only Defendant who filed an opposition. (September 9 Reply at pp. 2-3.) The absence of separate opposition by the remaining Defendants does not relieve Plaintiff of her burden to establish the grounds for preliminary injunctive relief. (O’Connell, supra, 141 Cal.App.4th at p. 1481.)

 

As relevant to the restraints requested, Plaintiff’s showing principally implicates her Second Cause of Action for breach of the implied warranty of habitability, Third Cause of Action under Civil Code section 1942.4, Seventh Cause of Action for retaliation, and Fourteenth Cause of Action for landlord harassment. Plaintiff also refers to quiet enjoyment, constructive eviction, assault, and battery, but does not develop those claims as separate grounds for the preliminary restraints requested.

 

In her initial application, Plaintiff submits more than 240 pages of exhibits, but much of the submission carries limited evidentiary weight. The moving declaration generally identifies categories of materials rather than laying a foundation for many individual documents and photographs. (See, e.g., Kaushansky Decl., ¶¶ 3-6, 14.) Several photographs are not tied by testimony to a particular date or location. Plaintiff’s self-prepared letters, timelines, and medication list principally reflect her own assertions. By contrast, the inspection records and Plaintiff’s sworn testimony concerning matters within her personal knowledge provide more direct evidentiary support.

 

Plaintiff’s August 25 reply declaration provides additional foundation for certain reply materials, including the MIS Appliance video screenshots. (August 25 Kaushansky Decl., ¶¶ 2-4.) Shauly did not object to the evidence, so the Court does not exclude it on foundational or hearsay grounds. (Waller, supra, 3 Cal.App.3d at p. 464.) The source and foundation of the materials nevertheless bear on their weight.

 

Plaintiff’s strongest showing concerns habitability. The May 24, 2023 Department of Public Health inspection documented missing drywall, missing baseboard molding, bubbling paint, and rodent droppings at Unit 105. (Application, Exs. at p. 138.) The July 5, 2023 reinspection found those conditions uncorrected. (Application, Exs. at p. 144.) Plaintiff also presently declares that electrical, plumbing, HVAC, rodent, and unfinished-construction conditions remain. (August 25 Kaushansky Decl., ¶¶ 6-7.) For purposes of preliminary relief, that evidence supports some possibility of success on Plaintiff’s habitability claim.

 

That showing, however, does not by itself demonstrate a sufficient likelihood of success on a ground supporting the possession restraint requested. Plaintiff relies on Civil Code section 1942.4 to challenge the rent demand and Three-Day Notice. Section 1942.4 prohibits specified rent demands and issuance of a three-day notice to pay rent or quit only when all conditions stated in subdivision (a) are satisfied. (Civ. Code, § 1942.4, subd. (a).) Those conditions include qualifying substandard conditions, written governmental notice after inspection, continued nonabatement for 35 days beyond service of that notice without good cause, and absence of tenant causation. (Id., subd. (a)(1)-(4).)

 

Plaintiff has evidence bearing on some of those requirements, but does not make a developed preliminary showing as to each statutory predicate. The May 2023 inspection report states that it was sent to property management by email. (Application, Exs. at p. 138.) Plaintiff does not sufficiently show, for purposes of this application, when or how the written governmental notice was served so as to commence the statutory 35-day period. Her papers also do not develop whether the continued failure to abate was without good cause or make a developed showing as to the tenant-causation requirement. The present showing under section 1942.4 therefore provides limited support for the requested possession restraint.

 

Plaintiff also contends that the amount demanded was not legally due because of habitability conditions and disputed rent increases. She does not provide a calculation, supported by competent evidence, from which the Court can determine at this stage that she is likely to establish the lawful monthly rent, the reasonable rental value of the premises during the relevant period, or the resulting amount legally due. The existence of a rent dispute, without more, does not demonstrate a likelihood that the August 22 demand will ultimately be found invalid.

 

The retaliation showing is weaker. Civil Code section 1942.5, subdivision (d), prohibits specified landlord conduct undertaken for the purpose of retaliating against a tenant because the tenant lawfully and peaceably exercised rights under the law. The tenant bears the burden of producing evidence that the challenged conduct was, in fact, retaliatory. (Civ. Code, § 1942.5, subd. (d).)

 

The evidence shows that Plaintiff repeatedly complained about repairs, habitability, rent, and her tenancy. The record also reflects prior Three-Day Notices that Plaintiff contemporaneously characterized as retaliatory. Much of the evidence she offers on retaliation, however, consists of her own characterization of Defendants’ conduct or attorney correspondence reporting her allegations. Those materials document Plaintiff’s complaints and her contemporaneous contention that Defendants were retaliating against her, but provide limited evidence from which retaliatory purpose may be inferred.

 

Plaintiff relies heavily on this Court’s ordered August 21, 2026 discovery meet and confer and service of the Three-Day Notice the following day to show retaliation. (Kaushansky Decl., ¶¶ 10-12.) The timing provides some circumstantial support for Plaintiff’s theory. Its weight is limited, however, because the record does not identify who decided to issue the notice, when that decision was made, or whether the decisionmaker knew what occurred during the conference. The claimed nonpayment also substantially predates that conference. On this preliminary record, the chronology provides only limited support for a likelihood that Plaintiff will ultimately establish retaliatory purpose.

 

Plaintiff’s harassment showing likewise provides limited support for the prospective restraints requested. LAMC section 45.33 requires bad-faith landlord conduct directed at a tenant that causes detriment or harm. (LAMC, § 45.33.) Civil Code section 1940.2 separately prohibits specified conduct undertaken for the purpose of influencing a tenant to vacate. (Civ. Code, § 1940.2, subd. (a).)

 

Plaintiff describes late-night knocking, following, telephone calls, unauthorized entries, and statements concerning when she intended to move. (Kaushansky Decl., ¶ 8.) Her September 9 declaration additionally identifies Floridia as following her through the property and into the garage to deliver notices, and alleges backyard entry, interruption of internet service, and water interruptions. (September 9 Kaushansky Decl., ¶¶ 12, 14-19.) The allegation concerning Floridia is more specific, but many of the remaining incidents are undated or involve unidentified persons. As presented, the circumstances surrounding the anonymous telephone calls, unidentified following, internet interruption, and water interruptions provide little support for an inference that those events were attributable to Defendants. The identified notice-delivery incidents likewise do not sufficiently show a realistic prospect of future conduct warranting the broad restraints requested.

 

Prospective injunctive relief requires actual evidence of a realistic prospect that the party to be enjoined intends to engage in the prohibited conduct. (Korean Philadelphia Presbyterian Church v. California Presbytery (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1069, 1084.) The alleged April 2023 altercation is a completed event. The present showing does not demonstrate a likelihood of recurrence sufficient to support prospective restraint. (Huntingdon Life Sciences, Inc. v. Stop Huntingdon Animal Cruelty USA, Inc. (2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 1228, 1266.)

 

Taken as a whole, the record shows repeated complaints concerning the condition of Plaintiff’s apartment and some independent evidence supporting a habitability dispute. Beyond that, much of the submission consists of Plaintiff’s own allegations, characterizations, or materials entitled to limited evidentiary weight. The likelihood-of-success showing supporting the particular injunction requested is therefore limited.

 

Balance of Interim Harms

 

The Court considers the merits and interim-harm factors together. (Butt v. State of California (1992) 4 Cal.4th 668, 678.) The greater the plaintiff’s showing on one, the less must be shown on the other to support an injunction. (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff contends she faces serious potential harm if she ultimately loses possession of her longtime home. She declares that displacement would affect her safety and stability, impose relocation burdens, interfere with prosecution of this action, and separate her from the premises and materials she considers relevant evidence. (September 9 Reply at pp. 1-2, 9.)

 

Denial of the present injunction does not itself result in eviction. As of September 4, Shauly represented that no unlawful detainer action had been filed. (September 4 Opposition at p. 3.) If Alon West elects to pursue possession, Plaintiff may be required to defend a separate unlawful detainer proceeding, but the right to possession will be determined there and Plaintiff may assert any defenses properly bearing on that issue. The anticipated harm from actual displacement therefore depends on Alon West commencing such an action and prevailing after those defenses are adjudicated.

 

By contrast, granting the requested injunction would bar Alon West and those acting on its behalf from filing or prosecuting an unlawful detainer based on the claimed nonpayment potentially until trial in August 2027. The papers reflect that Plaintiff ceased paying rent in or around September 2025, although she disputes the amount legally due. Absent further payments, Defendants’ claimed arrearage would continue to increase during that period.

 

The Court has equitable authority to restrain a threatened unlawful detainer in a proper case. (Dennis v. Overholtzer (1957) 149 Cal.App.2d 101, 104-105.) Dennis, however, involved a pending equitable action that already placed specific performance, title, possession, competing leases, and entitlement to rents before the court, and the rents had been impounded. The Court emphasized that the pending action could determine the right to possession “as well as, if not better than,” the threatened unlawful detainer. (Id. at pp. 102-105.) No comparable circumstance is shown here.

 

Plaintiff’s reliance on Hernandez v. Stabach (1983) 145 Cal.App.3d 309 does not compel a different result. There, the evidence showed that after tenants organized and asserted statutory rights concerning allegedly dangerous and uninhabitable conditions, the landlord began filing unlawful detainer actions against participating tenants based on overcrowding the landlord had knowingly permitted when renting the units. The Court of Appeal found that chronology supported retaliatory motive. (Id. at pp. 314-315.) Here, although the record reflects prior Three-Day Notices and Plaintiff’s contention that they were retaliatory, the present showing does not comparably connect those notices or the threatened possession proceeding to a retaliatory purpose. The requested relief also differs. Hernandez required the landlord to obtain leave of court before proceeding, did not bar a nonpayment unlawful detainer outright, and required the tenants to continue paying rent into a joint trust account. (Id. at pp. 312-317.)

 

The breadth and duration of the requested relief are therefore material. A preliminary injunction should be tailored to the demonstrated likelihood of harm and employ the “least disruptive remedy adequate to the task.” (Kings County Farm Bureau v. State Water Resources Control Bd. (2025) 115 Cal.App.5th 782, 819.) Here, Plaintiff has made only a limited showing supporting the particular restraints sought, while the requested possession injunction would prevent Alon West and those acting on its behalf from invoking the statutory process for adjudicating possession potentially through the August 2027 trial date.

 

Considering the two factors together, Plaintiff’s limited showing on the grounds supporting the particular restraints, coupled with the breadth and duration of those restraints, is insufficient to justify a preliminary injunction notwithstanding the potential consequences of eventual displacement. The balance therefore does not favor issuance of a preliminary injunction.

 

The Court’s ruling on the preliminary injunction is not an adjudication of the ultimate merits of the action. (Cohen v. Board of Supervisors (1985) 40 Cal.3d 277, 286.)

 

CONCLUSION

Plaintiff’s request for a preliminary injunction is denied. The temporary restraining order issued on August 25, 2026 terminates upon denial of the preliminary injunction. Defendant’s alternative request that Plaintiff deposit unpaid and current rent into an escrow account is denied as moot.

PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT

 

Motion filed on June 15, 2026.

MOVING PARTY: Liliya Hill (Plaintiff)

RESPONDING PARTY: Westcom Property Services, Inc. (Defendant)

NOTICE: OK.

RELIEF REQUESTED: Plaintiff seeks leave to file the proposed Second Amended Complaint.

RULING: Denied without prejudice.

 

BACKGROUND

 

On March 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Complaint. On April 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint. On June 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion for Leave to File Second Amended Complaint with a supporting declaration and proposed Second Amended Complaint. On July 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed a supplemental declaration and exhibits. No opposition or reply appears in the record provided.

 

DISCUSSION

Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (a)(1) permits the Court, in furtherance of justice, to allow amendment of a pleading. This discretion should be exercised liberally in favor of amendment. (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1047.) California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324 requires a motion for leave to amend to include the proposed amended pleading and identify the allegations proposed to be deleted and added by page, paragraph, and line number. The supporting declaration must specify the effect of the amendment, why the amendment is necessary and proper, when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered, and why the request for amendment was not made earlier. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a), (b).)

 

Here, Plaintiff attaches the proposed Second Amended Complaint, but the Motion does not identify the allegations proposed to be deleted or added and their location by page, paragraph, and line number as required by rule 3.1324(a)(2) and (3). The proposed pleading restructures the operative pleading into five causes of action, including a dependent adult abuse and neglect claim.

 

Plaintiff’s declaration states that the alleged misconduct is ongoing and that she is seeking amendment at the earliest possible juncture. However, the declaration does not specifically identify when the facts giving rise to the proposed amended allegations were discovered, as required by rule 3.1324(b)(3). Plaintiff’s statements that no trial date has been set and that the amendment will cause no delay or prejudice address the procedural posture and prejudice, but do not supply the information required by subdivision (b)(3).

Plaintiff’s supplemental declaration does not cure these deficiencies. It states that Plaintiff obtained additional evidence in July 2026 and explains why that material was not included with the June 15, 2026 Motion. It does not specifically identify when the facts giving rise to the proposed amendments were discovered.

 

The Motion therefore does not comply with rule 3.1324. Because the deficiencies are curable, the Motion is denied without prejudice.

 

The Court is mindful that Plaintiff represents herself. However, Plaintiff’s self-represented status does not excuse compliance with applicable procedural rules. “[M]ere self-representation is not a ground for exceptionally lenient treatment,” and, except where a particular rule provides otherwise, “the rules of civil procedure must apply equally to parties represented by counsel and those who forgo attorney representation.” (Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 984-985.) Likewise, a party proceeding in propria persona “‘is to be treated like any other party and is entitled to the same, but no greater consideration than other litigants and attorneys,’” and “‘the in propria persona litigant is held to the same restrictive rules of procedure as an attorney.’” (First American Title Co. v. Mirzaian (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 956, 958, fn. 1.)

 

CONCLUSION

 

Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to File Second Amended Complaint is DENIED without prejudice to a renewed motion complying with California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324.
