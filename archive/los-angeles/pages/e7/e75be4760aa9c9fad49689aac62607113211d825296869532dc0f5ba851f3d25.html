DEPARTMENT C LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 26NWCV00444    Hearing Date: August 17, 2026    Dept: C

REZA, et al. vs JOS LEVIN REALTY GROUP OF L.A., et al.

CASE NO.:  26NWCV00444

HEARING:  8/17/26 @ 9:30 AM

#11

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendants Joslevin Realty Corp. of L.A., MGMT Group, Inc. and Andy Clapkin’s Motion to Strike is DENIED.

Plaintiffs to give NOTICE.

 

Defendants Joslevin Realty Corp. of L.A., MGMT Group, Inc. and Andy Clapkin (Defendants) move to strike portions of the Complaint filed by Plaintiffs Felix Herrera Reza and CW Displays, Inc. (Plaintiffs) relating to punitive damages.   

 

Background

 

Plaintiffs) have been tenants of a commercial property located at 5802 Soto Street, Huntington Park, California for 22 years, continuously operating their woodworking display business from that location. (Compl. ¶ 3.) Plaintiffs leased the property from the owner Joslevin Realty Corp. of L.A. The lease was allegedly signed by Andy Clapkin, on behalf of Joslevin Realty Corp. of L.A. The property was managed by Defendants. (Compl., ¶ 8.) Plaintiffs allege that that the property owner and property manager have breached the lease in failing to properly repair damage to the ceiling and roof after a fire that broke out in an adjacent building. They claim that the owner and property manager have failed to address common area issues such as homelessness, drug use/sales, street racing, fecal matter/urine, overgrown vegetation, broken lighting and erratic water pressure. Plaintiffs allege that the owner and property manager improperly increased the common area maintenance (“CAM”) charges as well as changing the billing from quarterly to monthly. (Compl., ¶¶ 27–29.)

 

Based thereon, Plaintiffs assert causes of action for:

1. BREACH OF WRITTEN LEASE (FAILURE TO MAINTAIN AND REPAIR);

2. BREACH OF IMPLIED COVENANT OF QUIET ENJOYMENT / CONSTRUCTIVE EVICTION;

3. BREACH OF THE IMPLIED COVENANT OF GOOD FAITH AND FAIR DEALING;

4. BREACH OF CONTRACT – IMPROPER CAM CHARGES / OVERBILLING;

5. PRIVATE NUISANCE;

6. NEGLIGENCE / PREMISES LIABILITY;

7. NEGLIGENT INTERFERENCE WITH PROSPECTIVE ECONOMIC ADVANTAGE;

8. VIOLATION OF BUS. & PROF. CODE § 17200 ET SEQ. (UNFAIR COMPETITION);

9. FINANCIAL ELDER ABUSE (WELF. & INST. CODE § 15610.30);

10. DECLARATORY AND INJUNCTIVE RELIEF;

11. ACCOUNTING AND RESTITUTION.

 

Legal Standard

The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (CCP §436.) In ruling on a motion to strike punitive-damages allegations, judges assume the truth of the pleading allegations. (Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc. v. Sup. Ct. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 696, 699 n.1.) Motions to strike punitive damages may be granted, where the alleged facts do not support conclusions of malice, fraud or oppression. (Turman v. Turning Point of Central Calif., Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.)

“Malice” is defined as conduct intended to cause injury to a person, or despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard for the rights or safety of others. (Cal. Civ. Code § 3294(c)(1); Turman v. Turning Point of Cent. Cal., Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) “Oppression” means despicable conduct subjecting a person to cruel and unjust hardship, in conscious disregard of the person's rights. (Turman v. Turning Point of Cent. Cal., Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) “Fraud” is an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known by defendant, with intent to deprive a person of property, rights or otherwise cause injury. (Ibid.)

Where the defect raised by a motion to strike or by demurrer is reasonably capable of cure, “leave to amend is routinely and liberally granted to give the plaintiff a chance to cure the defect in question.”… A pleading may be stricken only upon terms the court deems proper…, that is, terms that are just…. It is generally an abuse of discretion to deny leave to amend, because the drastic step of denial of the opportunity to correct the curable defect effectively terminates the pleader's action.’” (Velez v. Smith (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 1154, 1174-75.)

Discussion

ELDER ABUSE: financial abuse of an elder occurs when any person or entity takes, secretes, appropriates, or retains real or personal property of an elder adult to a wrongful use or with an intent to defraud, or both. A wrongful use is defined as taking, secreting, appropriating, or retaining property in bad faith. Bad faith occurs where the person or entity knew or should have known that the elder had the right to have the property transferred or made readily available to the elder or to his or her representative. (Welf. & Inst.Code, § 15610.30; Teselle v. McLoughlin (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 156, 174.)

Defendant moves to strike the recovery of punitive damages from the Complaint on the grounds that Plaintiffs fail to support the claims with specific factual allegations as required under Civil Code § 3294 and Cal. Civ. Code § 15610, et seq. and established case law. Defendant argues Plaintiffs’ factual allegations do not support punitive damages because they arise from a breach of contract – namely the lease. Punitive damages may not be granted in an action based on breach of contract, even though breach was willful or fraudulent. (Crogan v. Metz (1957) 47 Cal.2d 398.) In the absence of an independent tort, punitive damages may not be awarded for breach of contract, even where the defendant’s conduct in breaching the contract was willful, fraudulent, or malicious. (Cates Construction, Inc. v. Talbot Partners (1999) 21 Cal.4th 28.)

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that Defendants’ motion succeeds only if the Court reads paragraph 91 in isolation, disregards the ninety paragraphs preceding it, and accepts Defendants’ revision of the Complaint as an ordinary dispute over lease performance. Indeed, Plaintiffs underscore that the Complaint alleges that Defendants knowingly demanded and retained dramatically inflated common-area-maintenance charges from a 70-year-old, long-term commercial tenant; collected money for maintenance and services they allegedly did not provide; ignored repeated notices concerning leaking roofs, human waste, criminal activity, broken lighting, unsafe common areas, and deteriorating conditions; and used their control over the property and Herrera’s dependence upon his long-established business location to pressure him into continuing to pay unjustified charges or abandoning his livelihood. Therefore, those allegations are expressly incorporated into the financial elder-abuse cause of action.

The Court finds that Plaintiff has met the heightened pleading burden for punitive damages. For purposes of an elder abuse claim relating to breach of contract, wrongful conduct occurs only when the party who violates the contract actually knows that it is engaging in a harmful breach, or reasonably should be aware of the harmful breach. (Paslay v. State Farm General Ins. Co. (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 639.) To establish a “wrongful use” of property to which an elder has a contract right, the elder must demonstrate a breach of the contract, or other improper conduct. (Id. at 657.) Additionally, under subdivision (b) of 15610.30, wrongful conduct occurs only when the party who violates the contract actually knows that it is engaging in a harmful breach, or reasonably should be aware of the harmful breach. (Id. at 658.)

The following are the relevant allegations:

Plaintiffs sufficiently allege Reza is an elder within the meaning of Welfare and Institutions Code section 15610.27, in that he is “70 years old and has operated CW Displays from the premises for approximately 22 years.” (Compl., ¶¶ 1–3, 14.)

 

Plaintiffs sufficiently allege who is responsible for the wrongful conduct: “Defendant Andy Clapkin is vice president and/or principal of Jos Levin, to have signed the lease for the landlord, and to have exercised control over maintenance decisions, common area maintenance (CAM) charges, and dealings with Plaintiffs.” (Compl., ¶ 8.)

 

As to the breach of contract, Plaintiffs allege that “Pursuant to the Lease, Jos. Levin expressly and/or impliedly agreed to: a. Maintain and repair the roof, ceilings, and structural components of the building; b. Maintain the common areas, including parking areas, walkways, and landscaping, in a clean, sanitary, and safe condition; c. Provide lighting and other basic services in the common areas consistent with a commercially reasonable standard for similar properties; and d. Allow Plaintiffs quiet and peaceful enjoyment of the Premises for use as a woodworking display business.” (Compl., ¶ 38.)

 

As to what the wrongful conduct was, Plaintiffs sufficiently allege: “Beginning October 1, 2023, Defendants allegedly changed CAM billing from quarterly to monthly and increased Plaintiffs’ CAM obligation from approximately $30 per month to approximately $244.48 per month or more.” (Compl., ¶¶ 27–29.)

 

“MGMT and Jos. Levin did not provide proper written notice or justification under the Lease or applicable law for this dramatic increase. Plaintiffs were given a formula but no meaningful explanation of what common-area services justified such an increase—particularly in light of the obvious decline in services (no security, no proper cleaning, no effective maintenance). Plaintiffs are informed and believe, and on that basis allege, that Defendants overstated CAM expenses, allocated expenses improperly to Plaintiffs, and/or used CAM charges as a vehicle to extract additional revenue while failing to provide the promised services.” (Compl., ¶¶ 29–30.)

 

Plaintiffs allege they repeatedly reported the safe and unsanitary conditions “to MGMT and Jos. Levin through emails, photographs, and videos, and even called law enforcement several times to address the homeless encampments and criminal activity.” (Compl., ¶ 24.)

 

Plaintiffs contend that Defendants allegedly overstated or improperly allocated expenses, used CAM charges to extract additional revenue, collected at least $5,378.56 in improper increases, and continued demanding the inflated charges while providing fewer services. (Compl., ¶¶ 30–31, 55–59.)

 

The ninth cause of action for elder abuse alleges that Defendants took, obtained, and retained Herrera’s money and rights under the lease for a wrongful use, with intent to defraud, or by undue influence. (Compl., ¶ 87.) It identifies the wrongful conduct as:

“1. collecting charges beyond what was contractually or factually warranted;

2. retaining the payments while providing fewer services and allowing the property to become unfit for Herrera’s business;

3. ignoring Herrera’s pleas while knowing his age, long tenancy, and dependence upon the location; and

4. using superior control over the property to pressure an elderly small-business owner to pay unjustified charges or abandon his livelihood.”

 

(Compl., ¶ 88.)

 

Civil Code Sec. 3294 requires “there be specific and detailed allegations that wrongful acts were done with the knowledge, or under the direction of officials who have the power to bind the corporation” for a corporation to be exposed to punitive damages. (Toole v. Richardson-Merrell, Inc. (1967) 251Cal.App.2d 689; Scannell v. County of Riverside (1984) 152 Cal.App.3d 596, 614.) Plaintiffs have sufficiently alleged facts to demonstrate Plaintiff Herrera is an elder under the statute, Defendants contracted to safely maintain the conditions in the property but failed to do so, and Defendants had advance knowledge of the dangerous and unsanitary conditions on the Property, and should have reasonably been aware of the harmful breach. (Paslay v. State Farm General Ins. Co. (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 639, 658.) Finally, at the pleading stage, along with Plaintiff’s allegations of the harmful breach, it is sufficient that Plaintiffs states the reason the breach was harmful under the elder abuse statute was because of Defendants’ use of “superior control over the property to pressure an elderly small-business owner to pay unjustified charges or abandon his livelihood.” (Compl., ¶ 88.)

 

 

 

Accordingly, the motion to strike is DENIED.


Case Number: 26NWCV00515    Hearing Date: August 17, 2026    Dept: C

ROSS vs PIH HEALTH DOWNEY MEDICAL INCORPORATE

CASE NO.:  26NWCV00515

HEARING: 8/17/26 @ 9:30 AM

 

#12

TENTATIVE RULING

 

Defendant PIH HEALTH DOWNEY HOSPITAL’s Demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

 

Moving party to give NOTICE.

 

 

Plaintiff sought treatment from Defendant’s hospital when she was not feeling well after a meal.  The issue presented here is whether Plaintiff’s complaint was filed beyond the statute of limitations, and whether it provides enough details to allege a cause of action against Defendant.  The Court concludes that the complaint was filed too late and is uncertain.

 

Defendant PIH HEALTH DOWNEY HOSPITAL (Defendant) demurs to Pro Per Plaintiff BERNADEAN ROSS’s (Plaintiff) First Amended Complaint (FAC) on the grounds that it is uncertain, ambiguous, and unintelligible. Additionally, Defendant generally demurs to the FAC on the grounds that each cause of action is barred by the applicable statutes of limitation.

 

Background

 

Plaintiff presented to Defendant’s urgent care and reported abdominal pain, nausea, and burning sensation on her lips after eating at McDonalds. (FAC, ¶¶ 26; 28 -29.)

 

Plaintiff asserts a cause of action for medical professional negligence.  To the extent Plaintiff asserts additional causes of action, those causes of action are unintelligible.

 

Legal Standard

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (CCP §§ 430.30, 430.70.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732, internal citations omitted.) 

“Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given.” (Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227.) It is an abuse of discretion for the court to deny leave to amend where there is any reasonable possibility that plaintiff can state a good cause of action. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) The burden is on plaintiff to show¿in what manner¿plaintiff can amend the complaint, and¿how¿that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading. ¿(Id.) 

Discussion

 

Statute of Limitations

 

Code of Civil Procedure Section 340.5 states that “[i]n an action for injury ... against a health care provider based upon such person’s alleged professional negligence, the time for the commencement of action shall be three years after the date of injury or one year after the Plaintiff discovers, or through the use of reasonable diligence should have discovered, the injury, whichever occurs first.” (Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 340.5.) Once a plaintiff should have suspected wrongdoing, the statute of limitations commences to run. (Kleefeld v. Superior Court (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 1680, 1684.) “It is not tolled by efforts to learn more about the matter short of filing suit” and “regardless of extenuating circumstances, the patient must bring his suit within one year after he discovers, or should have discovered, his ‘injury.’” (Id.) Thus, possession of either presumptive knowledge or actual knowledge will commence the running of the statute. (Sanchez v. South Hoover Hospital (1976) 18 Cal.3d 93, 101.) Thus, “when the plaintiff has notice or information of circumstances to put a reasonable person on inquiry, or has the opportunity to obtain knowledge from sources open to his investigation . . . the statute commences to run.” (Sanchez v. South Hoover Hospital (1976) 18 Cal.3d 93, 101.) Waiting for confirmation is not enough to toll the statute of limitations, a Plaintiff cannot be willfully ignorant of suspicion, rather, the Plaintiff has a duty to investigate. (Miller v. Bechtel Corp (1983) 33 Cal. 3d 868; Jolly v. Eli Lilly & Co. (1988) 44 Cal. 3d 1103, 1111.) Once a Plaintiff has even a “suspicion of wrongdoing . . . [he] must decide whether to file suit or sit on his rights.” (Id.)

 

Here, Plaintiff presented to Defendant’s urgent care on July 2, 2023. (FAC, ¶¶ 1, 16.) Plaintiff filed the original Complaint on February 11, 2026, which was later amended on June 15, 2026. Plaintiff alleges to have issued a complaint to Defendant’s risk management department in “August 2025.” (FAC, ¶ 3.18.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff reiterates the same uncertain facts, as Plaintiff alleged in the FAC.

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff fails to allege facts demonstrating that the medical professional negligence cause of action is not barred by the applicable statute of limitations. Plaintiff fails to allege facts that demonstrate why Plaintiff did not reasonably discover the injury within one year of presenting to Defendant’s urgent care. Indeed, Plaintiff fails to allege at which date Plaintiff discovered the wrongdoing altogether.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

 

Uncertainty, Failure to State a Cause of Action

 

A defendant may demur to a complaint on the ground that it is uncertain. (Code Civil Proc,m § 430.10(f).) The term “uncertain” includes “ambiguous” and “unintelligible.” (Van Horn v. Department of Toxic Substances Control (2014) 231 CA4th 1287, 1295.) Demurrers for uncertainty are disfavored and judges sustain them only when the complaint is so incomprehensible that the defendant cannot reasonably respond. (Lickiss v Financial Indus. Regulatory Auth. (2012) 208 CA4th 1125, 1135.)

 

The Court finds that even under a liberal reading of Plaintiff’s allegations, the allegations are so uncertain that it is unclear what Defendant’s wrongdoing is, when Plaintiff discovered it, how Plaintiff was injured by Defendant’s wrongdoing, and what causes of action Plaintiff is pursuing. In Plaintiff’s opposition, the Court is only able to discern that Plaintiff’s grievance against Defendant is its failure to “issue that [sic] whole completed record to the patient. The discharge release record appears to be missing some information that is located in the nurse practitioner’s notes.  (Opp., p. 6: 17-19.) Plaintiff does not make clear what information is missing and how she was harmed by the missing information. 

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

 

 

 

 


Case Number: 26NWCV00737    Hearing Date: August 17, 2026    Dept: C

PALOMBI, SR., et al. vs SEQUOYAH, INC., et al.

CASE NO.: 26NWCV00737

HEARING:  8/17/26 @ 9:30 AM

 

#13

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

      I.          Defendant ELITE PROPERTY BROKERS INC.’s Demurrer is SUSTAINED with 20 days leave to amend.

    II.          The Case Management Conference scheduled for September 15, 2026 is ADVANCED to today and CONTINUED to November 17, 2026 at 9:00 a.m. in Dept. SE-C. 

 

Moving Party to give NOTICE.

 

 

This case concerns an assault that took place at a restaurant where the alleged culprit was attending a company gathering.  The issue presented is whether Plaintiffs sufficiently allege that the alleged culprit was acting in the course and scope of employment so as to trigger liability for the company.  The Court concludes that Plaintiffs have not done so. 

 

Defendant Elite Property Brokers Inc. (EPB) demurs to Plaintiffs Joseph Anthony Palombi Sr., Joseph Anthony Palombi Jr., Iliana Fragancia Palombi, and Jessica Denise Palombi’s (Plaintiff) First Amended Complaint (FAC) as to the first, second, and fourth causes of action for assault, battery, and intentional infliction of emotional distress, respectively, on the grounds that Plaintiffs fail to state facts to state a claim and the allegations are unclear, vague, and ambiguous pursuant to Code Civil Proc., §430.10(e) and (f).

 

Background

 

Plaintiffs allege that on May 23, 2024, Plaintiffs went to Dal Rae Restaurant in Pico Rivera, California, to celebrate (a) Joseph Sr.’s birthday and (b) Jessica’s upcoming wedding, which was scheduled two days later. (FAC, ¶ 13.)

 

Within approximately 10 minutes after Plaintiffs were seated, an adjacent group of eight individuals (four females and four males), including Defendant Jose Eric Perez (Perez), was seated in the larger booth next to Plaintiffs. From the moment the group arrived, it was apparent to Plaintiffs that members of the group were already intoxicated, loud, and volatile. (FAC, ¶ 16.)

 

At approximately 9:00 p.m., as Plaintiffs were leaving their booth area, Joseph Sr. had to turn toward the adjacent booth due to the proximity of the aisle/exit path. Joseph Sr. observed a female member of the adjacent group who appeared distressed and, in a calm manner, asked her words to the effect of, “Are you okay?” (FAC, ¶ 22.) In response, Defendant Perez stood up from the table and angrily told Joseph Sr. to “Mind your own fucking business,” and aggressively charged toward Joseph Sr. Jessica moved between Perez and Joseph Sr. Perez violently shoved Jessica out of the way, causing her to be thrown aside. Jessica was holding a to-go container, which flew and spilled. Perez struck Joseph Sr. with significant force. Joseph Sr. fell backward into nearby tables, suffered a sharp blow to his head, and lost consciousness. While Joseph Sr. was unconscious or semi-conscious, Perez kicked Joseph Sr. in the head twice, causing further injury and continued to kick and hit Joseph Sr.’s body while he lay stiff and unable to fight back. Jessica continued to attempt to get Perez to stop beating her unconscious father. (FAC, ¶¶ 23-26.)

 

Plaintiff alleges Perez is the employee, sole officer, and sole director of EPB and was acting within the scope of his employment at the time of the incident. (FAC, ¶¶ 10-11.)

 

Based thereon, Plaintiffs allege the following causes of action:

 

1.    Assault

2.    Battery

3.    Negligence

4.    Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

 

Meet and Confer

 

“Before filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (CCP § 430.41(a).) The Court finds that Defendant’s counsel adequately made an attempt to meet and confer. (Howard Decl., ¶ 1.)

 

Legal Standard

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (CCP §§ 430.30, 430.70.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732, internal citations omitted.) 

 

“Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given.” (Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227.) It is an abuse of discretion for the court to deny leave to amend where there is any reasonable possibility that plaintiff can state a good cause of action. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) The burden is on plaintiff to show¿in what manner¿plaintiff can amend the complaint, and¿how¿that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading. ¿(Id.) 

 

Discussion

 

1st, 2nd, and 4th CAUSES OF ACTION; Vicarious Liability

 

California Courts have long held that “respondeat superior” liability only applies if the alleged tortious conduct was a “foreseeable” consequence of the employee’s employment. (Hinman v. Westinghouse Elec Co. (1970) 2 Cal.3d 956, 960; Rogers v. Kemper Constr. Co. (1975) 50 Cal.App. 3d 608; Lisa M. v. Henry Mayo Newhall Memorial Hospital (1995) 12 Cal.4th 291.)

 

Defendant argues that the first, second, and fourth causes of action fail because Plaintiffs offers only bare legal conclusions that Perez was acting “in the course and scope” of his employment. Defendant contends that there is no statement of any basis for the claims against Elite Property Brokers – only that Perez appears to be employed by Elite Property Brokers and the Plaintiffs wish to inflict maximum harassment upon him by needlessly suing his employer.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that at his stage, the Court must accept the FAC’s agency, course-and-scope, and official-function allegations as true. (Dones v. Life Ins. Co. of N. Am. (2020) 55 Cal. App. 5th 665, 685; Kiseskey, 144 Cal. App. 3d at 230.) The FAC also alleges ratification and adoption, which the demurrer does not analyze. (FAC ¶¶ 37, 46.)

 

The Court finds that Plaintiffs have failed to sufficiently alleges facts to support the causes of action under vicarious liability. An employer will not be held vicariously liable for an employee's malicious or tortious conduct if the employee substantially deviates from the employment duties for personal purposes. (Juarez v. San Bernardino City Unified School District (2024) 106 Cal. App. 5th 1213, 327.) In cases involving assaults by employees, imputation of liability follows where the employment in some way involves the risk of force being used, that is, where the act is connected with the employment. (Golden West Broadcasters, Inc. v. Superior Court (1981)114 Cal. App. 3d 947.) Moreover, the determination whether an employee has acted within the scope of employment presents a question of fact; it becomes a question of law, however, when ‘the facts are undisputed and no conflicting inferences are possible.’ [Citation.] In some cases, the relationship between an employee's work and wrongful conduct is so attenuated that a jury could not reasonably conclude that the act was within the scope of employment. (Ayon v. Esquire Deposition Solutions, LLC (2018) 27 Cal.App.5th 487, 494–495.)

 

Here, reading the allegations liberally and in context, Plaintiffs allege (1) Perez was Defendant’s employee, sole officer, and sole director; (2) he was acting within the course and scope of that employment; (3) he was present at Dal Rae Restaurant as part of an official Defendant function; and (4) in his capacity as Defendant’s sole officer and director, he ratified and adopted the pleaded conduct. (FAC ¶¶ 10-11, 37, 46.) Although these allegations are incorporated into each challenged cause of action, Plaintiffs fail to allege facts that demonstrate the employment in some way involves the risk of force being used, that is, where the act is connected with the employment. (Golden West Broadcasters, Inc. v. Superior Court (1981)114 Cal. App. 3d 947.) There are no facts for the Court to discern what Perez’s function was as an employee of Defendant and whether it was reasonably foreseeable that in the course of his employment a physical altercation may occur.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer to the first, second, and fourth causes of action is SUSTAINED with 20 days leave to amend.
