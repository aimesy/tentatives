DFDM v. DAPPZ

TENTATIVE RULING

The OSC is CONTINUED to October 12, 2026 at 9:00 a.m. for Plaintiff to remedy the deficiencies. Plaintiff shall submit its updated default judgment package addressing the deficiencies 10 days before the hearing.

The Court also sets an Order to Show Cause Re Sanctions Against Plaintiff's Counsel in the Amount of $250 Pursuant to CRC 3.110(g)-(i) for Failure to Timely Obtain Entry of a Default Judgment for the same day and time. If a corrected default judgment package is not filed by the Court's deadline, counsel is subject to sanctions.

Declarations in response to the Orders to Show Cause are due five court days before the hearings.

ANALYSIS

FACTS OF THE CASE:

Plaintiff DFDM National, LLC supplies sports goods and memorabilia. Defendant DAPPZ Sports resells and promotes those goods. The parties dealt on an ongoing basis, with each order confirmed in writing by invoices and purchase orders. Between June 30 and August 19, 2025, Plaintiff shipped and delivered goods to Defendant at Defendant’s request, with payment due no later than September 4, 2025. Defendant accepted the goods and did not pay. Plaintiff’s counsel sent a demand letter on October 13, 2025 requiring payment by October 20; Defendant acknowledged receiving the letter but did not cure. The aggregate unpaid balance is $298,139.31. The complaint pleads breach of written contract, open book account, account stated, and the reasonable value of goods sold and delivered.

PROOF OF SERVICE/DEFAULT:

The complaint was filed October 23, 2025. Plaintiff exercised a peremptory challenge under Code of Civil Procedure section 170.6 on October 24, 2025, which was granted October 27, 2025, and the case was reassigned; it was reassigned again on February 24, 2026. Defendant DAPPZ Sports was served by substituted service on October 30, 2025 at 6860 Lexington Avenue, Los Angeles, CA 90038, the office of its agent for service of process Matt McGuckin, by leaving the papers with the person apparently in charge, followed by first-class mailing to the same address on October 31, 2025 (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 415.20(a), 416.10). Service was therefore complete on November 10, 2025 and a response was due December 10, 2025. Default was entered on December 12, 2025. Does 1 through 20 were dismissed without prejudice, entered December 31, 2025, leaving DAPPZ Sports as the sole defendant. The Case Management Conference was continued from July 27, 2026 to August 26, 2026 by order filed March 11, 2026.

Judgment requested (JUD-100 and CIV-100 filed December 31, 2025):

PRINCIPAL REQUESTED: $ 298,139.31

INTEREST: $ 8,576.40

ATTORNEY’S FEES: $ 4,871.39

COSTS: $ 522.40

TOTAL: $ 312,109.50

Discussion:

Plaintiff has submitted all the requisite forms: a CIV-100 requesting entry of default (filed December 12, 2025), a CIV-100 requesting a court judgment under Code of Civil Procedure section 585(b) (filed December 31, 2025), a summary of the case with supporting declarations under section 585(d), a memorandum of costs (MC-010), and a proposed JUD-100. Item 2 of the CIV-100 is completed. No declaration of nonmilitary status is required, because the sole remaining defendant is a corporation, and item 8 of the CIV-100 is in any event completed. Plaintiff has dismissed the Doe defendants. The request for dismissal of Does 1 through 20, inclusive, only, without prejudice, was filed December 31, 2025 and entered by the clerk that same day, leaving DAPPZ Sports as the sole defendant. The prove-up is adequate.

The declaration of Darius DiMartino, who has been the Manager of Plaintiff since its formation in 2014, is made on personal knowledge by a percipient witness competent to testify. It is supported by a summary of the unpaid invoices attached as Exhibit 1 to the complaint and incorporated in the application, together with the demand correspondence. The invoice ledger ties out to the amount sought. Eleven invoices remain open (Nos. 102, 113, 126, 127, 152, 153, 156, 165, 169, 192 and 196), carrying balances of $10,150.00, $54,359.55, $53,000.00, $2,750.00, $12,219.28, $31,450.00, $24,160.00, $24,575.00, $38,139.94, $20,295.54 and $27,040.00. Those balances total exactly $298,139.31, which is both the ledger's stated Total Open and the principal demanded. That figure is net of $15,200.00 in credited returns and includes $2,124.31 in shipping and freight billed on the invoices.

The relief requested does not exceed the complaint. The prayer seeks damages of not less than $298,139.31, interest at the maximum rate allowed by law, attorney’s fees under the contract and under Civil Code section 1717.5, and costs of suit. The judgment requests that same principal, with interest, fees, and costs, so Code of Civil Procedure sections 580 and 585 are satisfied.

Interest is properly supported and computed. The complaint prays for interest, the sum was certain as of the September 4, 2025 payment date, and the declaration supplies the daily breakdown required by rule 3.1800(a)(3): $298,139.31 at 10 percent is $29,813.93 per year, or $81.68 per day, multiplied by the days elapsed from September 4 through December 17, 2025 (Civ. Code, §§ 3287, 3289). The principal is composed of unpaid invoice amounts and includes no accrued interest.

The attorney’s fee request is calculated correctly under LASC Local Rule 3.214(a). For a judgment over $100,000 the schedule allows $2,890 plus 1 percent of the excess: $298,139.31 less $100,000 is $198,139.31, one percent of which is $1,981.39, for a total of $4,871.39. Plaintiff states that it has incurred more than $6,000 in fees but will accept the schedule amount, which is the approach Civil Code section 1717.5(b) contemplates where the local rule produces the lower figure. The invoices supply the contractual basis, providing that the “Buyer is liable to seller for all costs to collect payment due under this invoice, including attorneys’ fees.”

Costs of $522.40 are itemized and verified on both the MC-010 and the CIV-100 memorandum of costs at subpart 7 (clerk’s filing fees $435.00; process server $50.00; electronic filing fees $37.40), and are allowable under section 1033.5(a). The MC-010 total of $5,393.79 includes the $4,871.39 in attorney’s fees at line 10, so the cost component is $522.40, which is the figure carried onto the proposed judgment. PROBLEMS:

1. The pleading and the proof describe the contract differently. The complaint is captioned for breach of written contract and its first cause of action is pleaded as a written contract. The summary submitted with the application states instead that the parties “entered into an oral contract,” which was “confirmed in writing with invoices/purchase orders,” and the attorney-fee provision relied upon appears on the face of the invoices. The discrepancy does not affect the amount awarded, and fees remain recoverable in any event on the open book account count under Civil Code section 1717.5, but Plaintiff should reconcile the characterization and address legally how invoices can constitute a contract in a legal brief.

2. The interest period is stated as 105 days. The declaration recites the period as running “from September 4, 2025, through December 17, 2025,” which is 104 days measured by subtraction, and 105 only if both endpoints are counted. At the stated rate of $81.68 per day the difference is a single day, or $81.68. If the Court applies 104 days, interest is $8,494.93 and the total judgment is $312,028.03.

3. The summary misstates the costs figure. It recites that Plaintiff “will accept $4,871.39 under the Local Rule attorney’s fees and $560.50 in costs.” The verified memorandum of costs, the CIV-100, and the proposed judgment all state $522.40. The verified figure of $522.40 controls, and the $560.50 reference appears to be an error.

Case Number: 25STCV32063    Hearing Date: August 26, 2026    Dept: 224

Rosson v. Flynt Management Group, LLC

TENTATIVE RULING

The motion is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART. Plaintiff is ordered to arbitrate all claims against FMG, except that the request for public injunctive relief under the second cause of action is not arbitrable and remains in this Court.

The action is stayed pending completion of the arbitration. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.)

The Court sets a Post-Arbitration Status Conference for July 30, 2027 at 8:30 a.m. A Joint Status Report is due 5 court days in advance.

ANALYSIS

Motion to Compel Arbitration

Defendant Flynt Management Group, LLC ("FMG") moves the Court for an order compelling Plaintiff to: (i) submit his claims set forth in his Complaint against Defendant to binding arbitration in lieu of court proceedings; and (ii) stay the instant lawsuit pending completion of such arbitration.

Introduction

On November 3, 2025, Plaintiff Jesse Rosson filed the Complaint in this action against Defendant Flynt Management Group, LLC.

On January 26, 2026, Defendant Flynt Management Group, LLC filed the instant Motion to Compel Arbitration and Stay Action.

On August 13, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition to the Motion.

On August 19, 2026, Defendant filed a Reply in support of the Motion.

Legal Standard

In a motion to compel arbitration, the moving party must prove by a preponderance of evidence the existence of the arbitration agreement and that the dispute is covered by the agreement. The burden then shifts to the resisting party to prove by a preponderance of evidence a ground for denial (e.g., fraud, unconscionability, etc.). (Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin. Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413-414; Hotels Nevada v. L.A. Pacific Center, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 754, 758.)

Generally, on a petition to compel arbitration, the court must grant the petition unless it finds either (1) no written agreement to arbitrate exists; (2) the right to compel arbitration has been waived; (3) grounds exist for revocation of the agreement; or (4) litigation is pending that may render the arbitration unnecessary or create conflicting rulings on common issues. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 218-219.)

“California has a strong public policy in favor of arbitration and any doubts regarding the arbitrability of a dispute are resolved in favor of arbitration.” (Coast Plaza Doctors Hospital v. Blue Cross of California (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 677, 686.) “This strong policy has resulted in the general rule that arbitration should be upheld unless it can be said with assurance that an arbitration clause is not susceptible to an interpretation covering the asserted dispute.” (Ibid. [internal quotations omitted].) This is in accord with the liberal federal policy favoring arbitration agreements under the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), which governs all agreements to arbitrate in contracts “involving interstate commerce.” (9 U.S.C. § 2, et seq.; Higgins v. Superior Court (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1247.)

The Existence of an Agreement

A party to an arbitration agreement may seek a court order compelling the parties to arbitrate a dispute covered by the agreement. (CCP § 1281.2) The party seeking arbitration has the initial “burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence, while a party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense.” (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.4th 836, 842.) A moving party meets its initial burden by showing the agreement at issue exists, and need only establish its validity on challenge by opponent. (HM DG, Inc. v. Amini (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1100, 1114; Espejo v. Southern Calif. Permanente Med. Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1060.)

Defendant relies on the Employer-Employee Arbitration Agreement (the "Agreement") attached as Exhibit A to the Declaration of Karen Saidiner. (Saidiner Decl., Ex. A.) The Agreement identifies its parties as "El Dorado LF LLC dba HUSTLER Casino ('Employer') and its employees," who agree to:

"arbitrate any and all disputes, claims or controversies ('Claims') they may have against each other, including their current and former agents, owners, officers, directors, or employees, which arise from the employment relationship between employee and employer or the termination thereof." (Id.)

The Agreement is signed by Plaintiff (as Jesse Rivera) and by an authorized representative of El Dorado, dated September 9, 2022; the Complaint acknowledges Plaintiff "signed an arbitration agreement with El Dorado LF, LLC dba Hustler Casino, not with Flynt Management Group, LLC." (Complaint, ¶ 39; Saidiner Decl., Ex. A.)

Here, the parties do not dispute the existence of the Arbitration Agreement. Plaintiff concedes the Agreement is valid and enforceable and is presently arbitrating employment claims under it against El Dorado before JAMS.

“With respect to the moving party’s burden to provide evidence of the existence of an agreement to arbitrate, it is generally sufficient for that party to present a copy of the contract to the court.” (Baker v. Italian Maple Holdings, LLC, 13 Cal. App. 5th 1152, 1160 (2017).)

An agreement to arbitrate exists. The dispute is covered by the Agreement. Plaintiff does not dispute the existence of an agreement. The sole question is whether FMG, a non-signatory, may enforce it.

Agency/Equitable Estoppel

The parties dispute whether Defendant FMG may compel arbitration as a non-signatory under the principles of agency and equitable estoppel.

Equitable Estoppel

Equitable estoppel binds a plaintiff to arbitrate claims that are "intimately founded in and intertwined with the underlying contract obligations" and that are "based on the same facts and are inherently inseparable from the arbitrable claims against signatory defendants." (Garcia v. Pexco, LLC (2017) 11 Cal.App.5th 782, 786; JSM Tuscany, LLC v. Superior Court (2011) 193 Cal.App.4th 1222, 1241.) The inquiry turns on the nature of the plaintiff's claims, not on extrinsic proof of the parties' relationship. (Jones v. Jacobson (2011) 195 Cal.App.4th 1, 20; Gonzalez v. Nowhere Beverly Hills LLC (2024) 107 Cal.App.5th 111, 119.)

Every cause of action here arises from Plaintiff's employment at the Casino and his separation from it. Plaintiff further ties FMG to that employment relationship at every turn. Plaintiff alleges he "was a human resources manager at Flynt Management Group" (Complaint, ¶ 6); that FMG "concealed its role as Plaintiff's true employer while exercising control over his employment conditions" (Complaint, ¶ 41); and that FMG "acted as an employer of Plaintiff, and is directly liable under the FEHA" (Complaint, ¶ 48). His FEHA, retaliation, harassment, and constructive-discharge allegations rest on the conduct of FMG's executives toward him during his employment (Complaint ¶¶ 32-38, 45-46), and his wage claim alleges FMG "improperly classified Plaintiff as an exempt employee" and failed to pay him (Complaint ¶¶ 59-61.) These are claims arising from "the employment relationship between employee and employer or the termination thereof." (Saidiner Decl., Exh. A.)

Where a plaintiff alleges a non-signatory is his employer, his claims are intertwined with the employment agreement as a matter of law, because the alleged employer's obligations derive from the employment relationship, "whose obligations flow from the agreement." (Gonzalez, supra, 107 Cal.App.5th at pp. 123, 128.) A plaintiff cannot hold a non-signatory liable as his employer while disclaiming the arbitration agreement that governs that employment. (Id. at pp. 118, 126.) Plaintiff does exactly that. Here, Plaintiff may not plead FMG as his true employer, controlling his workplace and directing the decisions at issue, and simultaneously treat FMG as a stranger to the Agreement.

Plaintiff's counterarguments fail. That his claims sound in statute does not defeat estoppel. The mere fact that claims are styled as statutory violations does not insulate them from the intertwining analysis. The dispositive question is whether the plaintiff actually relies on the employment agreement's terms to make out the claim, not merely whether the employment relationship serves as the factual backdrop. Plaintiff's claims do not merely unfold against the backdrop of his employment – they depend on it. Each cause of action requires Plaintiff to establish that FMG stood in an employer relationship to him: the FEHA claim rests on FMG's asserted role as employer or as a "person" managing and directing his employment (Complaint, ¶¶ 44-48); the wage claim rests on FMG's asserted status as the employer who classified and failed to pay him (Complaint, ¶¶ 59-61); and the UCL claim is predicated in part on those same employment-law violations (Complaint, ¶ 53). FMG's obligations on each of these claims derive from the employment relationship, and that relationship is defined and governed by the Agreement. (Gonzalez, supra, 107 Cal.App.5th at pp. 123, 128.)

Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2025) 17 Cal.5th 1122 is distinguishable. Ford held estoppel inapplicable to a vehicle manufacturer that was a stranger to the dealership sales contracts and whose warranty obligations existed independently of them; the buyers neither sued on the contract nor sought any benefit from it. (Id. at pp. 1126-1128, 1137-1138.) Ford reaffirmed the "intimately founded in and intertwined with" test. (Id. at p. 1131.) FMG is not akin to the Ford manufacturer. Plaintiff alleges the relationship with FMG is the employment relationship the Agreement governs. Ford does not address the employer context and, if anything, confirms the test estoppel satisfies here.

Likewise, the Court finds that Soltero v. Precise Distribution, Inc. (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 887 does not control. Gonzalez expressly considered and disagreed with Soltero, "sid[ing] with Garcia," and explained that a plaintiff's statutory claims against an alleged employer are intertwined with the employment agreement because the employer's obligations flow from that relationship. (Gonzalez, supra, 107 Cal.App.5th at pp. 126, 128.) Soltero is also factually distinguishable, as the Soltero plaintiff did not sue her signatory employer at all and pleaded nothing tying the non-signatory to that agreement. Here, by contrast, Plaintiff signed the Agreement with his direct employer, is arbitrating under it, and affirmatively pleads FMG as his employer who controlled his employment.

The Agreement's "individual basis" and "claims between Employee and Employer alone" language does not bar enforcement. That language is a class-and-representative-action and joinder limitation governing how covered claims proceed; it does not preclude a non-signatory whom Plaintiff has alleged to be his employer from invoking the Agreement under estoppel. Gonzalez enforced comparable language. The Court notes that FMG's opening papers misquoted the Agreement as reaching "affiliated entities, successors, or assigns" (Mot. at p. 7) – language FMG concedes does not appear in Exhibit A. However, that misstatement is immaterial, because the operative employment-relationship language plus Plaintiff's own employer allegations supply the basis for estoppel.

Agency

The agency exception applies where the plaintiff alleges the non-signatory acted as, or in connection with, a party to the agreement. (Garcia, supra, 11 Cal.App.5th at p. 788; Cohen v. TNP 2008 Participating Notes Program, LLC (2019) 31 Cal.App.5th 840, 863; Thomas, supra, 204 Cal.App.4th at pp. 614-615.)

The Complaint alleges an integrated structure in which FMG's CEO and human-resources personnel managed the Casino's employment functions, made staffing decisions, and directed the retaliatory conduct against Plaintiff. (Complaint, ¶¶ 18, 22-24, 34-37, 45-46.) FMG's alleged liability is thus inseparable from the employment relationship the Agreement governs. (Metalclad Corp. v. Ventana Environmental Organizational Partnership (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1705, 1713.)

FMG may compel arbitration as a non-signatory under equitable estoppel and agency.

The UCL Claim for Public Injunctive Relief

Plaintiff separately contends that the second cause of action under the Unfair Competition Law claim cannot be compelled because it seeks public injunctive relief. The Court agrees in part. Under McGill v. Citibank, N.A. (2017) 2 Cal.5th 945, a predispute arbitration agreement is invalid and unenforceable insofar as it waives a party's statutory right to seek public injunctive relief in any forum; that rule rests on California public policy and is not preempted by the Federal Arbitration Act. (Id. at pp. 951-952, 961-964; see Broughton v. Cigna Healthplans (1999) 21 Cal.4th 1066; Cruz v. PacifiCare Health Systems, Inc. (2003) 30 Cal.4th 303.) Public injunctive relief is relief that by and large benefits the general public, benefiting the plaintiff, if at all, only incidentally or as a member of the public. (McGill, at p. 955; Mejia v. DACM Inc. (2020) 54 Cal.App.5th 691, 702-703.)

Plaintiff's requested injunction — directing FMG to obtain proper licensure or cease exerting substantial influence over gaming operations at a card room open to the public — is public injunctive relief. (Complaint, Prayer ¶ 7; ¶¶ 53-57.)

The Agreement cannot be read to permit an arbitrator to award that relief. It provides that arbitration "shall proceed solely on an individual basis in order to resolve private disputes between Employer and Employee," and that "[t]he arbitrator's authority to resolve and make written awards is limited to claims between Employee and Employer alone." (Saidiner Decl., Ex. A.) An arbitrator so confined could not enjoin FMG's conduct for the benefit of the general public.

The remedy is not denial of the motion. McGill invalidates the Agreement only "insofar as" it operates to waive public injunctive relief (McGill, at pp. 951-952.) The Agreement contains no provision making the arbitration clause non-severable, and it directs that a court "should administer, modify, or interpret it . . . as to render it enforceable" (Saidiner Decl., Ex. A).

Accordingly, the request for public injunctive relief under the second cause of action is not arbitrable and shall remain in this Court. All remaining claims are compelled to arbitration. FMG's suggestion that Plaintiff lacks UCL standing goes to the merits of that claim, not to arbitrability, and the Court does not reach it.

Conclusion

The Agreement is valid, enforceable, and its existence is undisputed. Plaintiff's claims are intimately founded in and intertwined with the employment relationship the Agreement governs, and FMG, though a non-signatory, may enforce the Agreement under the doctrines of equitable estoppel and agency.

The motion is therefore GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART. Plaintiff is ordered to arbitrate all claims against FMG, except that the request for public injunctive relief under the second cause of action is not arbitrable and remains in this Court.

The action is stayed pending completion of the arbitration. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.)
