DEPARTMENT 509 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV01233    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 509

William E. Ayllon Vilcapoma v. General Motors LLC

PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR ATTORNEY’S FEES, COSTS, AND EXPENSES
  

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff William E. Ayllon Vilcapoma

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Defendant General Motors, LLC

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

This is a Song-Beverly action. Plaintiff William E. Ayllon Vilcapoma (Plaintiff) alleged he purchased a 2024 Chevrolet Silverado 1500 manufactured by defendant General Motors, LLC (Defendant) that was unsafe and did not conform to the quality and safety guidelines reasonably expected of a motor vehicle. 

On May 7, 2026, Plaintiff filed this instant motion to recover their reasonable attorney’s fees and costs. Defendant did not file an opposition.

On June 3, 2026, this motion originally came to hearing. Because the motion was unopposed, the Court granted a total of $25,642.11 in fees and costs.

On July 31, 2026, this Court granted Defendant’s motion to vacate the June 3, 2026, order. The Court allowed Defendant to file a supplemental declaration to include the fees and costs incurred after the ruling on the original motion. Defendant opposed.
  
TENTATIVE RULING:

Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED in the amount of $20,057.78.

Plaintiff’s Motion for Costs is GRANTED in the amount of $3,874.86.

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice, unless waived.  

DISCUSSION: 

Motion for Attorney’s Fees, Costs, and Expenses

I. Analysis

A. The Motion is Timely

Defendant argues that this motion is untimely since it was filed greater than 240 days from the date of the settlement. However, timeliness of a fee motion is calculated from the date of judgment or dismissal, not from the date of settlement.

California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1702 (b)(1) states, “[a] notice of motion to claim attorney’s fees for services up to and including the rendition of judgment in the trial court -- including attorney’s fees on an appeal before the rendition of judgment in the trial court -- must be served and filed within the time for filing a notice of appeal,” i.e., within 180 days after notice of entry of judgment. Settlements entered under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 are properly treated as judgments for purposes of Rule 3.1702. (Hatlevig v. General Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644, 649, [“voluntary dismissal, though not appealable, starts the clock running on the time to move for attorney fees when the dismissal concludes the litigation and triggers a right to fees.”]); see also Sanabria v. Embrey (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 422, 426-427.) Plaintiff has “at most 180 days after the dismissal to serve and file his motion” for attorneys’ fees, and the trial court did not err in denying his motion as untimely where he failed to file his motion for attorneys’ fees until after the 180 days had already expired. (Hatlevig at p. 650.)

Here, dismissal was entered on November 17, 2025. Under the 180-day clock, the time to file a motion for attorney fees expired on May 16, 2026. Plaintiff filed the motion on May 7, 2026. Therefore, the motion is timely filed.

B. Entitlement to Fees 

On September 9, 2025, the parties reached a settlement. 

Plaintiff now moves for an order awarding attorney’s fee of $27,454.20. This amount consists of $22,878.50 in attorney fees and an additional $4,575.70 in a 1.2 times multiplier. (See Not. of Mot.) Plaintiff seeks costs of $2,763.61. (Memo of Costs.)

Civil Code, section 1794(d) provides:
 
(d) If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.

(Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d) [emphasis added].)

Here, there is no dispute that Plaintiff is the prevailing party under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act. Accordingly, Plaintiff is entitled to an award of reasonable attorney fees as the prevailing party.

Reasonableness of Fees (Lodestar)

The determination of reasonable amount of attorney fees is within the sound discretion of trial courts. (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095; Akins v. Enterprise Rent-A-Car Co. (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 1127, 1134.) “The determination of what constitutes a reasonable fee generally ‘begins with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate….’”  “[T]he lodestar is the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community; it may be adjusted by the court based on factors including, as relevant herein, (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award….” (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.) In setting the hourly rate for an attorney fees award, courts are entitled to consider the rate of “‘fees customarily charged by that attorney and others in the community for similar work.’” (Bihun v. AT&T Information Systems, Inc. (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 976, 997 [affirming rate of $450 per hour], overruled on other grounds by Lakin v. Watkins Associated Indus. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 644, 664.) The burden is on the party seeking attorney fees to prove reasonableness of the fees. (Center for Biological Diversity v. County of San Bernardino (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 603, 615.)
 
The Court has broad discretion in determining the amount of a reasonable attorney’s fee award which will not be overturned absent a “manifest abuse of discretion, a prejudicial error of law, or necessary findings not supported by substantial evidence.” (Bernardi v. County of Monterey (2008) 167 Cal. App. 4th 1379, 1393-94.) The Court need not explain its calculation of the amount of attorney’s fees awarded in detail; identifying the factors considered in arriving at the amount will suffice. (Ventura v. ABM Industries Inc. (2012) 212 Cal.App.4th 258, 274-75.)

It should be noted that state trial court opinions are unpublished and have no precedential value. (Neary v. Regents of Univ. of Calif. (1992) 3 Cal.4th 273, 282.) Likewise, “[w]hile unpublished federal district court opinions are citable, they do not constitute binding authority.” (Gong v. City of Rosemead (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 363, 375.) 

1. Reasonable Hourly Rate

Plaintiff’s counsel requests rates between $325 per hour and $575 per hour. (Pengilley Decl., Exh. A.) Plaintiff is seeking 39.4 hours for a total of $18,378.50. (Ibid.) This gives a blended hourly rate of $466.46 per hour.  [FN 1]  Here, under the totality of the circumstances and based upon general prevailing rates in the Los Angeles area for this type of litigation, this Court finds that a blended rate of $466.46 per hour is reasonable given this Court’s considerable experience in Song-Beverly cases, many of which culminate with an attorney’s fees motion. This determination considers the complexity of the case, the quality of services provided, and the attorneys’ experience. The Court considers this fact in addressing the reasonableness of the hours expended.

2. Number of Hours Reasonably Expended

Beginning to end, Plaintiff’s counsel seeks compensation for a total of 39.4 hours plus a flat fee of $4,500 for prelitigation fees. (Pengilley Decl., Ex. A.) Plaintiff incurred an additional 6.7 hours after the filing of the motion to vacate. (Myers Supp. Decl., Ex. B.)

“It is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged [within the moving party’s verified billing invoice], with a sufficient argument and citations to evidence. General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.” (Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 459, 488.)

In opposition, Defendant contends the hours purportedly spent by Plaintiff’s counsel are excessive. Defendant highlights multiple entries in the billing records it contends are excessive and duplicative. 
 
Here, this action was filed in January 2025. The docket reflects minimal law and motion practice and there was nothing particularly complex or unique about this case. The issues involved were applicable to other consumers’ vehicles, thereby triggering economies of scale in terms of Plaintiff’s counsel’s efficiency in litigating this type of lemon law case. Litigating this matter should not have required anything more than slight factual modification to existing boilerplate. Consistent with these observations, Counsel’s billing records reflect a series of repetitive administrative-type tasks.

Considering these facts, this Court ultimately agrees that in some instances, the time quoted is excessive and/or unreasonable under the circumstances. In light of the foregoing discussion, and in view of the totality of the circumstances, the Court finds that the total amount of reasonable attorney’s fees in this case, using a lodestar methodology, is $20,057.78. This was calculated by multiplying the hourly rate of $466.46 by 43 hours, which this Court deems to be the reasonable time spent on all tasks in the matter, including the fees incurred after the original motion was vacated including the time spent in support of this instant motion, and the anticipated time spent at the hearing. 

Plaintiff seeks a multiplier of 1.20. “The award of a multiplier is in the end a discretionary matter largely left to the trial court.” (Yes in My Back Yard v. City of Culver City (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 1103, 1122 [citation and internal quotations omitted].) The Court in its discretion declines to apply a multiplier given this was a relatively standard Song-Beverly case with no novel or complex issues of law.   

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED in the amount of $20,057.78.

3. Costs and Expenses

Plaintiff seeks costs in the amount of $2,763.61. (Memo. Of Costs.) Plaintiff seeks an additional $1,111.25 in costs incurred after the filing of the motion to vacate. (Myers Supp. Decl., Ex. A.)

The scope of recoverable costs under Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), is even broader than the scope of recoverable costs under Code of Civil Procedure, section 1033.5. “[I]n enacting Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) the Legislature intended the phrase ‘costs and expenses’ to cover items not included in ‘the detailed statutory definition of ‘costs’’ set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5. [Citation.]” (Warren v. Kia Motors Am., Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 42.) Plaintiff’s costs, including electronic filing fees and deposition costs, are recoverable as they were necessary for the prosecution of this action. (See e.g. Code of Civ. Pro. § 1033.5, subd. (a)(3).) 

Further, Defendant challenges Plaintiff’s supplemental memorandum of costs. Specifically, Defendant argues that because Defendant failed to appear at the June 3, 2026, hearing, Plaintiff should not be compensated for the court reporter fees incurred during that hearing. (Supp. Opp. at p. 5:27-28, [“Due to a scheduling issue already raised in GM’s Motion to Vacate, there was no hearing on that date as GM did not appear.”]) This argument is unpersuasive. The Court finds that Plaintiff’s requested court reporter expenses are recoverable.

Adding together the two memorandums of cost, the Court finds the total recoverable costs to be $3,874.86.

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s Motion for Costs is GRANTED in the amount of $3,874.86.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 26STCP02401    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 509

Uber Technologies, Inc., et al. v. Lyft, Inc.

PETITIONERS’ MOTION TO ENFORCE SUBPOENAS  

MOVING PARTY(S):  Petitioners Uber Technologies, Inc., Rasier, LLC, and Uber USA, LLC

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Respondent Lyft, Inc.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

Petitioners Uber Technologies, Inc., Rasier, LLC, and Uber USA, LLC (collectively, Petitioners) filed this action against respondent Lyft, Inc. (“Respondent” or “Lyft”) arising from a lawsuit pending in Oklahoma state court, Jane Doe v. Uber Technologies, Inc.

Petitioners move to enforce the subpoenas issued to Respondent seeking five documents and ten deposition topics. Respondent opposed.

TENTATIVE RULING:

Petitioner’s Motion to Enforce the Subpoena for Production of Business Records is GRANTED IN PART. Respondent is ordered to comply with the Subpoena for Production of Business Records issued April 6, 2026, as to Requests Nos. 2-5, only, within 30 days of this order.

Petitioners’ Motion to Enforce the Deposition Subpoena is GRANTED IN PART. Respondent is ordered to comply with the Deposition Subpoena issued April 6, 2026, as to Requests Nos. 1-4 and 6-8, only, within 30 days of this order.

Moving parties to give notice, unless waived.

DISCUSSION:

Motion to Enforce Subpoenas

A. Background

This matter arises out of an Oklahoma state court action in which a plaintiff (Plaintiff) alleges that she was sexually assaulted by an independent driver during a ride requested through the Uber platform. Plaintiff brought claims against Uber, Lyft, and the independent driver who assaulted her, Timothy Greene (Greene). Plaintiff’s claims against Uber turn on its participation with Lyft in the Industry Sharing Safety Program (ISSP). Under the terms of the ISSP, Lyft and Uber agreed to share information about independent drivers who were deactivated on their respective platforms due to serious safety incidents, including sexual assault, with a lookback period going back to December 31, 2016. Plaintiff alleges that Uber should have known about a July 2019 alleged sexual assault incident that resulted in Greene’s deactivation from Lyft through ISSP. Lyft has been dismissed in the Oklahoma Action.

In this petition, Uber seeks to obtain discovery from Lyft it deems necessary to develop its defense to the Oklahoma Action.

The Court addresses both subpoenas in turn.

B. Subpoena for Production of Business Records

Petitioners seek:

1. The July 2019 report related to Timothy Greene submitted by the guest rider or other reporter (the “Report”). 
2. Documents sufficient to show the date the Report was received. 
3. Documents sufficient to show when Lyft’s investigation of the Report began.
4. Documents sufficient to show the date Greene was deactivated from the Lyft platform.
5. Documents reflecting Plaintiff’s [In the underlying action] use of the Lyft App from January 1, 2019 to the present.

(Horn Decl., Ex. 3.) 

Respondent opposes each category of documents on similar grounds. Namely, “according to Uber, the relevant issue for its asserted defense is whether and when information concerning Greene was transmitted through the ISSP before Plaintiff’s May 2021 ride” but the requested documents go beyond such relevancy. (See e.g. Lyft Separate Statement at p. 4.) 

Here, Requests Nos. 2-4 appear relevant and narrowly tailored to support the underlying action. Information regarding the date of the reporting is relevant to if/when the report was ultimately transmitted through the ISSP. The requests appear reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2017.010; Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 540-541 [discovery statutes should be construed liberally in favor of discovery.]) The requests are not overboard, duplicative, or unduly burdensome. 

Similarly, Request No. 5 appears appropriate for this action. Whether Plaintiff continued using rideshare services after the alleged assault is relevant to Petitioners’ defense in the underlying action. While Lyft has already produced Plaintiff’s profile and ride history, Uber requests additional categories including Feedback & Ratings, further trip details, and ticket information that Lyft believes goes beyond the relevant scope of discovery. However, such information may be persuasive in evaluating Plaintiff’s emotional state following the 2021 incident. 

Further, Plaintiff has a recognized expectation of privacy in her Lyft records, although to an extent, Plaintiff has reduced that privacy interest by bringing the Oklahoma Action and alleging wrongdoing evidenced in Lyft and Uber trips. When balancing Plaintiff’s privacy interests against Petitioner’s interest in discovering documents that are relevant to Petitioner’s defense of the action, this Court finds the balance shifts in favor of disclosure. The Court agrees with Petitioners that the categories sought are relevant. Further, the Court notes the parties have an active protective order in place arising from the Oklahoma Action. Such a protective order alleviates some of the Court’s concerns with Plaintiff’s privacy rights.

Moreover, admissibility at trial is not to be mistaken with discoverability. “[A]ny party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or to the determination of any motion made in that action, if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 2017.010; Davies v. Superior Court (1984) 36 Cal.3d 291, 301 [“discovery is not limited to admissible evidence”].) Evidence is relevant if it “ha[s] any tendency in reason to prove or disprove any disputed fact that is of consequence to the determination of the action.” (Evid. Code § 210.) Requests Nos. 2-5 seek relevant evidence, even if such evidence is ultimately not admissible at trial—a question this Court expresses no opinion on.

However, Request No. 1 appears to go beyond seeking relevant dates. Petitioners seek “The July 2019 report related to Timothy Greene submitted by the guest rider or other reporter.” (Horn Decl., Ex. 3.) Here, there appears to be no dispute that this report documents a sexual assault of a third-party by Greene. How information documenting such assault, including the third-parties identity and sensitive personal information, is reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence is unexplained. Indeed, it appears the Petitioners concede this attack took place and a report exists. The third party’s sensitive information in the underlying report appears irrelevant to Petitioner’s defense.

Accordingly, Petitioner’s Motion to Enforce the Subpoena for Production of Business Records as to Request No. 1 is DENIED. 

Petitioner’s Motion to Enforce the Subpoena for Production of Business Records as to Request Nos. 2-5 is GRANTED.

Respondent is ordered to comply with the Subpoena for Production of Business Records issued April 6, 2026, as to Requests Nos. 2-5, only, within 30 days of this order.

C. Deposition Subpoena

Petitioners seek to take a deposition of the person most knowledgeable as to:

1. The date Lyft began providing information to HireRight pursuant to the ISSP.
2. The date Lyft provided information to HireRight related to Greene pursuant to the ISSP.
3. The reasons for any delay in providing historical driver deactivation information, including information related to Greene, to HireRight pursuant to the ISSP.
4. Plaintiff’s use of the Lyft App from January 1, 2019 to the present.
5. The July 2019 report related to Timothy Greene and V.W. submitted by the guest rider or other reporter (the “Report”).
6. The date Lyft received the Report. 
7. The date Lyft’s investigation of the Report began.
8. The date Greene was deactivated from the Lyft platform. 
9. The purpose of the ISSP and Lyft’s reason for agreeing to the program. 
10. The reason(s) an intermediary (HireRight) was used for the ISSP program

(Horn Decl., Ex. 4.) 

Here, the information sought is similar to the information requested in Petitioners’ Document Subpoena. As discussed above, dates regarding the Report, dates of production of reports to HireRight or the ISSP, and Plaintiff’s use of the Lyft app are all relevant to Petitioner’s defense in the Oklahoma Action. Therefore, Requests Nos. 1-4, 6-8 are all appropriate under a totality of the circumstances. For the same reasons as stated above, information and details specifically provided in the July 2019 report are not appropriate under the circumstances. Therefore Request No. 5 is improper.

Finally, Requests Nos. 9-10 are irrelevant to the Oklahoma Action. Petitioners seek “the purpose of the ISSP and Lyft’s reason for agreeing to the program” and the “reason(s) an intermediary (HireRight) was used for the ISSP program.” (Horn Decl., Ex. 4.) However, Petitioners offer no argument or evidence to support why this information is relevant to the Oklahoma Action. Instead, such discovery appears to go to subjective business decisions made within Lyft with no bearing on Petitioners’ defense. Therefore, such requests are improper.

Accordingly, Petitioners’ Motion to Enforce the Deposition Subpoena as to Request Nos. 1-4 and 6-8 is GRANTED.

Petitioners’ Motion to Enforce the Deposition Subpoena as to Request Nos. 5 and 9-10 is DENIED.

Respondent is ordered to comply with the Deposition Subpoena issued April 6, 2026, as to Requests Nos. 1-4 and 6-8, only, within 30 days of this order.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Judge of the Superior Court
