[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES

 

Petitioner Global Tel*Link Corporation (“Petitioner”) moves to compel further responses from respondents California Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation (“CDCR”) and California Department of Technology (“CDT”), (collectively, “Respondents”).

The motion is granted in part.

I.                Background

1.               Factual Allegations

The first amended petition (“FAP”) alleges in pertinent part as follows:

a.                The RFP

On July 15, 2024, California and CDCR, through CDT, published Request for Proposal No. C5611826 (“RFP”). (FAP, ¶ 19). The RFP provided that Respondents would choose up to three responsible and qualified offerors to participate in the negotiation process. (FAP, ¶ 20.) Respondents were not permitted to waive material deviations or defects in a proposal to advance an offeror to the negotiation phase. (FAP, ¶ 20.) During negotiations, Respondents would discuss aspects of an offeror’s proposal that could enhance the proposal and its potential for award, but negotiations were “not intended to allow a Bidder to completely rewrite their proposal.” (FAP, ¶ 21.) Respondents must make the award by using the original criteria in the RFP or by revising the evaluation criteria based on the results of the negotiations, informing all bidders of the revised criteria, and providing an opportunity to submit a best and final offer (“BAFO”) based on those criteria. (FAP, ¶ 21.)

On October 28, 2024, Respondents closed the bidding pool. (FAP, ¶ 22.) Only Petitioner and Securus Technologies, LLC (“Securus”) submitted timely final proposals, and Respondents invited both to negotiate. (FAP, ¶ 22.)

On or about December 13, 2024, Respondents requested BAFOs from both Petitioner and Securus. (FAP, ¶ 23.) Respondents issued a BAFO addendum and updated the RFP, then conducted BAFO clarifications. (FAP, ¶ 23.)

Respondents conducted a final evaluation to determine the bidder with the highest points that passed every mandatory requirement. (FAP, ¶ 24.)

On February 21, 2025, Respondents notified Petitioner they would award a contract to Securus. (FAP, ¶ 25.)

b.               Award Challenge

On April 1, 2025, Petitioner filed a petition for traditional mandamus challenging the award which remains pending. (FAP, ¶ 26.) Petitioner contended (a) Securus’s proposal was nonresponsive because it offered an untested technology, (b) Securus’s proposal failed to meet a mandatory requirement to provide a Letter of Bondability, and (c) Securus’s financial status rendered it a non-responsible bidder. (FAP, ¶ 27.)

Securus estimates it will complete the contract transition by March 2026. (FAP, ¶ 28.) If the transition is completed, it will become difficult to unwind the award. (FAP, ¶ 30.)

c.                CPRA Requests

On February 24, 2025, Petitioner submitted to CDT a California Public Records Act (“CPRA”) request (“February Request”) seeking (a) a complete copy of each response to the RFP and financial offer; (b) scoring documentation; (c) communication that assisted in the award; and (d) a copy of the fully-executed, current contract with the winning vendor, along with any amendments. (FAP, ¶¶ 31-32.)

On March 6, 2025, Petitioner submitted to CDT a CPRA request (“March Request”) for further documents and followed up regarding the February Request. (FAP, ¶ 34.) The same day, CDT unilaterally extended time to respond to both requests. (FAP, ¶ 34.)

By March 18, 2025, CDT had not responded. (FAP, ¶ 35.) Petitioner sent a follow-up. (FAP, ¶ 35.) CDT responded that it was making the first production pursuant to the February Request, but some documents would be withheld. (FAP, ¶ 35.) CDT did not identify which records it was withholding or explain the specific grounds justifying the withholdings. (FAP, ¶ 35.) The March 18, 2025 production contained no communications. (FAP, ¶ 36.)

On March 19, 2025, Petitioner identified absent document categories and sought identification of documents withheld, including the withholding justification. (FAP, ¶ 37.)

Petitioner sent a follow-up on March 24, 2025. (FAP, ¶ 38.)

On March 26, 2025, CDT produced additional records but stated there were no responsive records to the communications request. (FAP, ¶ 39.) CDT indicated it did not interpret the March Request for “all documents and communications with bidders” to include “any and all evaluation documents and communications related to this procurement.” (FAP, ¶ 39.) CDT stated that it withheld financial statements and documents under Government Code section 7927.705. (FAP, ¶ 39.) CDT stated that this production concluded its responses. (FAP, ¶ 39.)

On April 4, 2025, Petitioner submitted a new CPRA request (“April 4 Request”) for “[a]ny and all evaluation documents and communications related to this procurement for the time period of July 15, 2024, until February 21, 2025.” (FAP, ¶ 40.)

On April 7, 2025, Petitioner sent another CPRA request (“April 7 Request”) for documents and communications relating to the implementation plan and transition plan. (FAP, ¶ 41.)

On April 14, 2025, CDT provided a partial production to the April 4 Request. (FAP, ¶ 42.)

On April 17, 2025, CDT responded to the April 7 Request stating it had no responsive documents and that it would close the request. (FAP, ¶ 41.)

On April 30, 2025, CDT provided the remaining production for the April 4 Request. (FAP, ¶ 42.) Some documents were redacted from this production, and some withheld. (FAP, ¶ 42.) One email produced stated, “All, in preparing for the contract award, I will be deleting everything from the Sharepoint drive. Please be sure to delete anything you may have as well. This will ensure the official records are the ones available for public record requests.” (FAP, ¶ 42.) This production stated that it concluded the request. (FAP, ¶ 42.) CDCR produced some communications that CDT did not, despite CDT being party to those communications. (FAP, ¶ 43.)

On April 22, 2025, Petitioner submitted 15 CPRA requests to CDCR (“April 22 Request”). (FAP, ¶ 45.) CDCR failed to respond within ten days. (FAP, ¶ 47.)

On May 8, 2025, CDCR notified Petitioner that it required additional time to respond, promising a response by August 1, 2025. (FAP, ¶ 48.) CDCR did not respond by August 1, 2025.

On August 4, 2025, Petitioner requested the status of the production. (FAP, ¶ 50.)

On August 6, 2025, CDCR requested a narrowed scope. (FAP, ¶ 51.)

On August 11, 2025, Petitioner sent a communication objecting to deficiencies in CDCR’s response but agreed to confer regarding proposed search terms to expedite compliance. (FAP, ¶ 52.)

On August 13, 2025, CDCR stated that it had identified responsive records and proposed search terms and provided some documents that Petitioner had already received from CDT. (FAP, ¶ 53.)

On August 15, 2025, Petitioner provided revised search terms. (FAP, ¶ 54.) The same day, CDCR sent correspondence noting it would begin providing documents by August 29, 2025, and non-exempt non-communications by September 12, 2025. (FAP, ¶ 54.) Rolling productions on communications would begin August 29, 2025. (FAP, ¶ 54.)

On August 20, 2025, CDCR accepted Petitioner’s proposed search term revisions and committed to production timeframes. (FAP, ¶ 55.)

On August 22, 2025, CDCR produced documents responsive to one of the April 22 Requests, which was for scoring documentation. (FAP, ¶ 56.) CDCR did not provide individual evaluators’ comments. (FAP, ¶ 56.)

On August 27, 2025, CDCR wrote that it was providing documents but did not do so. (FAP, ¶ 57.)

On August 29, 2025, CDCR provided documents responsive to one of the requests. (FAP, ¶ 58.)

On September 4, 2025, Petitioner noted that it had not received requested communications. (FAP, ¶ 60.) CDT stated that it would respond the next day. (FAP, ¶ 60.) It did not do so.

On September 5, 2025, CDCR sent Petitioner a letter stating it would provide the rest of the April 22 Request production by September 19, 2025. (FAP, ¶ 61.) CDCR would provide modified searches by September 12, 2025, and responsive communications by October 3, 2025. (FAP, ¶ 61.)

On September 19, 2025, CDCR reported that it had no records responsive to Request 9. (FAP, ¶ 62.) Request 9 was for “[a]ll documents and communications with bidders, including but not limited to clarifications, discussions, and/or negotiations.” (FAP, ¶ 62.) Such records must exist, because there were responsive communications to Petitioner in the bidding process. (FAP, ¶ 62.)

On September 25, 2025, Petitioner requested an update on revised search terms for certain requests. (FAP, ¶ 63.) CDCR responded once without substance, then did not respond again. (FAP, ¶ 63.)

On October 3, 2025, CDCR produced eight Confidential Agreements and nothing else, including no communications. (FAP, ¶ 64.)

On October 17, 2025, CDCR produced 16 documents, 12 of which were confirmation and transmission emails for those Confidentiality Agreements, one of which was a duplicate of the RFP, and three of which were documents involved in drafting the evaluation worksheet. (FAP, ¶ 65.)

On August 27, 2025, Petitioner submitted additional CPRA requests (“August Request”). (FAP, ¶ 66.) CDCR confirmed receipt. (FAP, ¶ 66.) Petitioner included proposed search terms, but CDCR ignored the proposed terms. (FAP, ¶ 66.)

On September 9, 2025, CDCR indicated it would begin production on December 5, 2025. (FAP, ¶ 67.)

Petitioner has still not received crucial productions. (FAP, ¶ 68.)

2.               Procedural History

Petitioner filed the petition on October 23, 2025. Proofs of service filed October 24, 2025, show Petitioner served the summons and petition on CDCR by personal service on October 24, 2025 and on CDT by substituted service on the same day.

On October 29, 2025, Petitioner filed its FAP. A notice and acknowledgement of receipt file November 19, 2025 shows CDT received the summons and petition by the same date. On December 17, 2025, CDCR and CDT each filed their answer to the FAP.

On October 31, 2025, the Court denied Petitioner’s ex parte application for an order pursuant to Government Code section 7923.100 on the basis that such relief cannot be obtained ex parte but must rather be sought through the standard writ process.

On March 5, 2025, the Court stayed discovery until March 19, 2026, and ordered CDCR to provide the first 170 hits to particular search terms which would yield about 10,500 hits, to be produced by March 13, 2026.

On March 19, 2026, the Court granted Petitioner leave to file a second amended petition (“SAP”). Respondents’ answers to the FAP were deemed their answers to the SAP. The Court further ordered all discovery be completed by May 19, 2026, including review and production. The Court ordered that if CDCR had not completed its production by May 19, 2026, the CDCR representative responsible for the request and production must appear on May 28, 2026.

On July 21, 2026, the Court ordered CDCR to produce a Vaughn index for an agreed-upon subset of withheld records, prepared on a document-by-document basis. The index must identify, for each withheld record, the document’s date, author, recipient or recipients, general subject matter, the type of the record, each exemption claimed for each withheld portion, and factual justification stated with sufficient specificity to permit meaningful adversarial testing and judicial review. The Court further directed that bare descriptors such as “nonresponsive,” “deliberative process,” or “privilege,” without supporting factual explanation, would be insufficient. The Court further ordered that if CDCR were unable to provide information for any of the required categories, it must briefly explain, within the index, the reason for the omission.

The parties agreed the index would be limited to records predating March 1, 2025 and exclude documents withheld solely on the basis of PII and documents relating to gate clearances. The Court authorized production on a rolling basis with an initial production no later than August 11, 2026, and a second production no later than August 31, 2026, with an aim of completing the index by that date if reasonably possible.

The Court further ordered the parties to consistently meet and confer in good faith to address and resolve any issues concerning these productions or the adequacy of the indices. Additionally, the Court ordered that if the parties remain unable to resolve their differences, or if they seek additional relief from the Court, they must file a concise joint statement no later than September 11, 2026, identifying the specific action requested. Finally, the Court ordered that Petitioner may request a motion hearing date for discovery that occurred before the cutoff date, to the extent Petitioner contends responses were insufficiently provided.

On July 24, 2026, Petitioner filed the instant motion to compel further responses from Respondents.

II.             Discussion

A¿motion¿to compel further responses¿may be brought based¿on¿responses to requests for admission (“RFA”) that: (1) provide evasive or incomplete answers; or (2) make unmeritorious or¿overly-generalized¿objections.¿¿(Code of Civil Procedure [“CCP”][1] § 2033.290(a).)¿ However, a motion to compel further responses cannot compel the admission of matters already denied.¿¿(Holguin v. Superior Court¿(1972) 22 Cal.App.3d 812, 821.)¿

1.               Requests

a.                CDT Request Nos. 9-14

CDT RFA numbers 9 through 14 request admissions “that there are public records that include the term ‘[Search Term]’ that are responsive to VIAPATH’S [Date] CDT REQUESTS but which YOU did not produce to VIAPATH in response to VIAPATH’S [Date] CDT REQUESTS.” These six requests cover every combination of the search terms “VIAPATH” and “SECURUS” with dates February 24, March 6, and April 4.

The term “YOU” is defined to include CDT and (i) any and all of its present and former employees, evaluators, agents, representatives, accountants, or consultants; (ii) all CDT divisions, departments, units, or subordinates; and (iii) any other PERSON[2] or entity acting on its behalf.

The Term FEBRUARY 24 CDT REQUESTS is defined to mean the CPRA REQUESTS[3] that VIAPATH[4] served on CDT on February 24, 2025, and which are referenced in Paragraph 48 of the SAP. Likewise, MARCH 6 CDT REQUESTS refer to requests served on March 6, 2025, and APRIL 4 CDT REQUESTS refer to requests served on April 4, 2025.

CDT objects to these requests on the basis that the defined terms “YOU” and “VIAPATH’s [Date] CDT REQUEST” are overbroad, call for information beyond CDT’s custody, and are impermissibly compound. CDT also objects that the term “public records” is vague and ambiguous. Subject to these objections, CDT responds to each that it “admits it withheld exempt records pursuant to the California Public Records Act.”

CDT argues that it “expressly admits that responsive records were withheld in response to these RFAs.” (Opp. at 6-7.) CDT is incorrect. CDT’s answers are specifically phrased such that they avoid stating whether the withheld records were responsive to the particular CPRA requests identified in the RFAs or whether they contained the search terms at issue.

In the same way, CDT’s answers do not state whether any of the records withheld had the search terms specified by the RFAs. The referenced CPRA requests are not ambiguous, and CDT’s answers are evasive. CDT must directly admit or deny, for each search term and for each CPRA request covered by CDT RFA numbers 9 through 14, whether it withheld records that were responsive to that CPRA request and that contained that search term.

Therefore, CDT must admit or deny CDT RFA numbers 9 through 14.

b.               CDCR RFA No. 2

CDCR RFA number 2 requests admissions “that between April 22, 2025 and August 5, 2025, YOU did not search for emails responsive to Request No. 3, of the APRIL 22 CDCR REQUESTS.”

The term “YOU” is defined to include CDCR and (i) any and all of its present and former employees, evaluators, agents, representatives, accountants, or consultants; (ii) all CDCR divisions, departments, units, or subordinates; and (iii) any other PERSON[5] or entity acting on its behalf.

The Term APRIL 22 CDCR REQUESTS is defined to mean the CPRA REQUESTS[6] that VIAPATH[7] served on CDCR on April 22, 2025, and which are referenced in Paragraph 63 of the SAP.

CDCR objects to these requests on the basis that the defined terms “YOU” and “VIAPATH’s APRIL 22 CDCR REQUEST” are overbroad, call for information beyond CDCR’s custody, and are impermissibly compound. CDCR also objects that the terms “search” and “public records” are vague and ambiguous. Finally, CDCR objects on the grounds that “Request No. 3” is vague and ambiguous as it is not defined in the request. CDCR provides no substantive answer.

CDCR argues (a) the request is not full and complete in itself as there are defined terms and CDCR is required to review Request No. 3 of Petitioner’s CPRA request to determine the subject matter of the request; (b) RFA No. 2 does not indicate the subject matter of “Request No. 3” and thus the RFA is vague and ambiguous; (c) the definition of “YOU” is overbroad, including “all present employees,” all CDCR “divisions, departments units or subordinates,” and “any other PERSON or entity acting on its behalf.” (Opp. at 7.)

CDCR’s objections do not withstand scrutiny in this context. Petitioner does not reference RFA number 3 of its CPRA submission for the purpose of incorporating new substantive definitions into the RFA; rather, the RFA seeks an admission about CDCR’s actions in responding to its own CPRA obligations. CDCR RFA number 2 is facially complete, and the use of defined terms does not render it otherwise. Nor does the fact that the RFA concerns conduct relating to an external document create ambiguity. The definitions used are sufficiently clear and not overbroad for purposes of an admission request.

CDCR must therefore admit or deny CDCR RFA number 2.

c.                CDCR RFA Nos. 12-19; CDT RFA Nos. 26-31

CDCR RFA numbers 12 through 19 and CDT RFA numbers 26 through 31 seek admissions that financial information disclosed by Securus is a public record, and that any such financial information cannot be exempt or privileged from disclosure if it has already been publicly disclosed to Petitioner.

Respondents argue that, when a CPRA action seeks records which may not be disclosable, requests seeking information about those records should not be permitted. (Opp. at 5.) Respondents also argue Securus must be included as a real party to adjudicate the disclosability of its financial records.

This is a closer question than the others above, but Respondents are still incorrect. Petitioner is not seeking a determination regarding the ultimate disclosability of Securus’s financial records; rather, Petitioner seeks an admission addressing whether financial information already disclosed by Securus to Petitioner constitutes a public record and whether such information could still qualify for an exemption. If Respondents believe the records are not disclosable, Respondents may deny the RFAs. Likewise, Respondents may maintain their position that Securus’s participation is required before the Court can adjudicate any actual dispute concerning the disclosability of Securus’s financial information. But an RFA is not an adjudication of that question. Respondents may deny or admit the RFAs while maintaining their position that Securus must be joined if Petitioner wishes to adjudicate the disclosability of Securus’s financial information.

Respondents must therefore admit or deny CDCR RFA numbers 12 through 19 and CDT RFA numbers 26 through 31.

2.               Monetary Sanctions

As Respondents note, a request for sanctions must, in the notice of motion, identify every person, party, and attorney against whom the sanction is sought, and specify the type of sanction sought. (§ 2023.040.) Petitioner argues it did, but Petitioner is incorrect. Petitioner identified the parties against whom it seeks sanctions within its supporting memorandum, but not within the notice of motion. (See Mot. at 2, 16.)

However, the Court must still impose sanctions against a party who unsuccessfully opposes a motion to compel further response, unless the Court finds that party acted with substantial justification or other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.

Here, while Respondents did not act with substantial justification on every RFA, Respondents acted with substantial justification on CDCR RFA numbers 12 through 19 and CDT RFA numbers 26 through 31. Concerns about litigation regarding the status of Securus’s financial information provided a reasonable basis to object to those RFAs. Therefore, CDCR acted with substantial justification on eight of nine RFAs, and CDT acted with substantial justification on six of twelve RFAs.

Accordingly, the Court declines to impose sanctions.

III.           Conclusion

The motion is GRANTED in part. CDCR must admit or deny CDCR RFA Nos. 2 and 12 through 19. CDT must admit or deny CDT RFA numbers 9 through 14 and 26 through 31. The Court declines to impose sanctions.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION FOR RECONSIDERATION

 

Petitioner Ryan Kenneth Chapell (“Petitioner”) moves for reconsideration of the Court’s denial of his petition for leave to present a late claim for damages against respondent County of Los Angeles (“Respondent”).

The motion is denied.

I.                Background

1.               Factual Allegations for the Underlying Claim

For the underlying claim, Petitioner alleges as follows:

Adam Iza (“Iza”) hired 12 active-duty Los Angeles County Sheriff’s Department (“LASD”) deputies as his personal security and to harass his personal and business adversaries. (Pet., ¶ 10.) Iza paid these deputies about $300,000 monthly for their services and for access to LASD resources to obtain search warrants for adversaries, to seize property by those search warrants, and to arrest adversaries. (Pet., ¶¶  10-11, Ex. A.)

In 2021, Chapell worked as an event planner through his business, Anoma. (Pet., ¶ 7.) In the summer of 2021, Zort, Inc. (“Zort”) owned by Iza, requested Chapell’s assistance planning and organizing a party for Iza’s 21st birthday. (Pet., ¶ 8.)

In August of 2021, Chapell organized and held a party at Iza’s residence in Bel Air, for which Iza paid him $50,000. (Pet., ¶ 19.) After the conclusion of the party, Iza claimed he was unhappy with the party. (Pet., ¶ 21.) Multiple armed deputies, including Eric Chase Saavedra (“Saavedra”), escorted Chapell to Iza at his residence. (Pet., ¶¶  21-22.) Iza and Saavedra demanded Chapell refund a portion of the $50,000. (Pet., ¶ 23.) Saavedra demanded Chapell’s cell phone, which Chapell refused to turn over. (Pet., ¶ 24.) Saavedra and other deputies displayed their badges, drew their firearms, and pointed them at Chapell. (Pet., ¶ 25.) Iza also pointed an AR-15 “in Chapell’s direction”. (Pet., ¶ 25.)

Chapell then surrendered his phone and passwords. (Pet., ¶ 26.) Iza used the phone to transfer $25,000 from Chapell to Iza. (Pet., ¶ 27.) Iza kept Chapell’s phone and photographed documents, such as Chapell’s license, credit cards, and passports, for later use. (Pet., ¶ 28.) Iza and the deputies then allowed Chapell to leave. (Pet., ¶ 29.)

Over the next several days, Iza transferred tens of thousands of dollars from Chapell to himself. (Pet., ¶ 30.)

At Iza’s direction, Saavedra and other deputies planned to frame and arrest Chapell for drug possession. (Pet., ¶ 31.) Saavedra and other deputies contacted Chapell’s girlfriend, bribing or threatening her to secure her participation. (Pet., ¶ 32.)

On or around September 27, 2021, Chapell and his girlfriend were in a vehicle in Lakewood when Saavedra and other deputies, at Iza’s direction, performed a traffic stop of Chapell’s vehicle. (Pet., ¶¶  33-34.) The deputies informed Chapell he was being arrested on a bench warrant. (Pet., ¶ 35.) The deputies searched the vehicle and found two bags of narcotics which Chapell was carrying at Iza’s direction. (Pet., ¶ 37.) The deputies then arrested Chapell for drug possession, but did not arrest his girlfriend. (Pet., ¶ 38.) Iza arrived at the scene and contacted the deputies. (Pet., ¶ 39.)

LASD held Chapell for three days, charging him with transportation of controlled substances. (Pet., ¶ 40.) On January 9, 2022, the charges against Chapell were dropped for lack of sufficient evidence. (Pet., ¶ 40.)

Subsequently, Iza directed Saavedra and other deputies to illegally search Chapell’s house and arrest Chapell. (Pet., ¶ 41.) Saavedra and other deputies filed a sealed search warrant application accusing Chapell of “selling narcotics and storing large quantities of fentanyl and cocaine [in his] residence”. (Pet., ¶ 42.) In the accompanying affidavit, the deputies “did not disclose their existing relationship with [Iza] or payments received from [Iza],” or any illegal conduct at his direction. (Pet., ¶ 43.) Chapell had no way of knowing what was in the sealed search warrant and affidavit. (Pet., ¶ 44.)

On October 12, 2021, LASD executed the search warrant at Chapell’s apartment. (Pet., ¶¶  14, 45.) Chapell was handcuffed and placed in an unmarked LASD patrol car for the duration of the search. (Pet., ¶ 46.) The deputies never provided or displayed the search warrant. (Pet., ¶ 47.) The deputies did not locate any narcotics, but did destroy Chapell’s apartment. (Pet., ¶ 48.)

Out of fear for his life and safety, Chapell relocated out of the Los Angeles area. (Pet., ¶ 51.)

The Federal Bureau of Investigation (“FBI”) began investigating Iza and his conspirators for extortion, payments in exchange for commission of unlawful acts by law enforcement officers in their official capacities, unlawful searches and arrests to intimidate victims, and evasion of tax assessments related to receipt of money obtained through fraudulent means. (Pet., ¶ 14.)

Three deputies have since pled guilty. (Pet., ¶ 15.) Multiple deputies have been charged with violating Chapell’s constitutional rights, and other deputies are cooperating with the FBI to charge and prosecute other deputies. (Pet., ¶¶  16-17.)

On September 23, 2024, a felony criminal complaint and supporting affidavit were filed against Iza for conspiracy and tax evasion in connection with these actions. (Pet., ¶¶  12, 52.) The affidavit revealed to Chapell the extent of Iza’s activities and involvement in the issuance and execution of the search warrant against him. (Pet., ¶ 55.)

On January 30, 2025, Iza waived indictment and pled guilty to the criminal charges. (Pet., ¶ 13.)

2.               Facts Relating to Filing the Claim

On Monday, March 24, 2025, Chapell’s counsel filled and filed a Claim for Damages form for Chapell’s claim for the illegal search of his home, mistakenly using a City of Los Angeles (“City”) form rather than a Respondent form. (Steering Decl., ¶¶  56-59.)

Then and thereafter, Petitioner’s counsel suffered a severe flare-up of ulcerative colitis and was in constant severe pain. (Steering Decl., ¶¶  60-61, 71.) Petitioner’s counsel was on strong pain medication and worked a reduced schedule. (Steering Decl., ¶¶ 61-66, 73, Ex. E.) The City Claim for Damages form does not obviously display “City of Los Angeles”. (Steering Decl., ¶ 70.) The information Petitioner entered on the form made clear the claims were directed to Respondent. (Steering Decl., ¶ 71.)

On or about April 4, 2025, Petitioner’s counsel received a letter from the City acknowledging receipt. (Steering Decl., ¶ 65, Ex. F.)

On April 18, 2025, when counsel saw the claim was filed with the City, counsel filed an application to file a late claim with Respondent. (Steering Decl., ¶ 67, Exs. B, G.)

On or about May 14, 2025, Respondent denied the late claim application, and Petitioner received a letter from Respondent stating its decision on May 23, 2025. (Steering Decl., ¶ 68, Ex. H.)

II.             Evidentiary Objections

Respondent objects to portions of the Steering Declaration and portions of the Hands Declaration.

For the Steering Declaration, the objections to paragraphs 6, 9, 10, 11, 18, 19, 20, 21, 24, 26, and 30 are sustained for secondary evidence rule/testimony as to the contents of written documents. The objections to paragraphs 5, 22, 31, 32, and 33 are sustained as legal conclusions. The remaining objections are overruled.

For the Hands Declaration, all objections are overruled.

The evidence is admitted. To the extent the admitted evidence contains legal conclusions, the Court will disregard them. The Court will afford the admitted evidence its proper weight.

III.           Discussion

A motion for reconsideration constitutes the exclusive means for a party seeking modification, amendment or revocation of an order. (Morite of Calif. v. Superior Court (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 485, 490.) To be entitled to reconsideration, a party must show (1) new or different facts, circumstances, or law, and (2) a satisfactory explanation for failing to produce such evidence earlier. (Kalivas v. Barry Controls Corp. (1996) 49 Cal.App.4th 1152, 1160-61.) The requirement of satisfactory explanation for failing to provide the evidence earlier can only be described as a strict requirement of diligence. (Garcia v. Hejmadi (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 674, 690.) A motion for reconsideration cannot be granted on the ground that the court misapplied the law in its initial ruling. (Gilberd v. AC Transit (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 1494, 1500.)

A renewed motion is a motion or application for an order that was refused in whole or in part, or granted conditionally on terms. §1008(b). A renewed motion is not governed by the strict ten-day timeline but is otherwise governed by the same requirements of new or different facts, circumstances or law, and due diligence.

Petitioner’s motion is not persuasive. His only explanation for why he did not present the facts about his associate’s handling of the claim submission is that the parties did not brief that question. First, this is an insufficient showing, because Petitioner had the burden to show excusable neglect to support his petition, so his failure to sufficiently show excusable neglect at hearing cannot serve as the necessary diligence showing. (Shaddox v. Melcher (1969) 270 Cal.App.2d 598, 600-601.) Second, even if accepted, the new facts would not entitle Petitioner to relief as they go only to a legal theory Petitioner disclaimed at hearing.

Petitioner presents no explanation for why he did not present further facts about the connection between his medical condition and his error. Additionally, Petitioner does not explain in his briefing how the new facts demonstrate that connection, and instead impermissibly exports that argument to the 14-page Steering Declaration.

Petitioner likewise presents no explanation for why he did not present the “new” law on excusable neglect by lawyers in his briefing or at hearing. Further, even if accepted, this new law would not entitle Petitioner to relief, because it only shows that attorney conduct, even legal work, may be excusable neglect. As explained in the Court’s June 18, 2026 and August 27, 2026 rulings, this Court did not hold otherwise.

Essentially, Petitioner seeks an opportunity to correct the defects the Court observed in Petitioner’s original showing without any justification for why he could not originally make a sufficient showing. Petitioner must establish his diligence; failure to do so is fatal. (Garcia v. Hejmadi, supra, 58 Cal.App.4th 674, 685-90.)

IV.           Conclusion

The motion is DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: APPLICATIONS FOR WRIT OF ATTACHMENT

 

Plaintiff/cross-defendant First-Citizens Bank & Trust Company (“Plaintiff”) seeks writs of attachment against defendants/cross-complainants Admiral Hospice Health Care, Inc. (“Admiral”) and Kamran Jahangard Mahboob (“Mahboob”) (collectively, “Defendants”).

The two applications are denied.

I.                Background

1.     Factual Background

On May 2, 2023, Plaintiff and Admiral executed a promissory note (“Note”) for $1,389,000 in principal, to mature May 2, 2033. (Cain Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 1.) The same day, Mahboob executed and delivered to Plaintiff a Guaranty of Admiral’s obligations under the Note (“Guaranty”). (Cain Decl., ¶ 8, Ex. 4.)

Defendants have not made any monthly payments under the Note since February 2024. (Cain Decl., ¶ 6.)

On October 22, 2024, Plaintiff made a written demand for payment, but Defendants have not cured the default. (Cain Decl., ¶ 7, Ex. 3.) Plaintiff accelerated the Note. (Cain Decl., ¶ 7.)

2.     Procedural History

a.                Plaintiff’s Claims

On June 10, 2025, Plaintiff filed this action against Defendants for breach of note, breach of guaranty, money lent, claim and delivery, and conversion. Proofs of service filed June 24, 2025 show Plaintiff served the summons and complaint on Admiral HHC by personal service on June 16, 2025 and on Mahboob by substituted service on June 14, 2025. On August 8, 2025, Defendants filed their answer to the complaint.

b.               Defendant’s Claims

Defendants filed their cross-complaint against Plaintiff and cross-defendants Josie Jones (“Jones”), Admiral Hospice Care, Inc. (“Admiral HC”), and Admiral Home Health, Inc. (“Admiral Home”) (collectively, “Cross-Defendants”) on August 8, 2025.

On November 7, 2025, Defendants filed their first amended cross-complaint (“FAXC”). On December 17, 2025, the Court found the case related to 24STCV16210 by virtue of the FAXC. A proof of service filed April 7, 2026 shows Defendants served the cross-complaint on Jones by personal service on April 5, 2026. On June 8, 2026, the Court sustained Plaintiff’s demurrer to the second and tenth causes of action to the FAXC.

On June 29, 2026, Defendants filed their second amended cross-complaint (“SAXC”). On August 6, 2026, Cross-Defendants demurred to the SAXC.

c.                Applications for Writ of Attachment

On April 30, 2026, the trial court vacated the hearing on application for an unfiled writ of attachment scheduled for June 8, 2026.

On May 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed applications for writ of attachment against Defendants. On July 7, 2026, Defendants filed their opposition. On July 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a notice of withdrawal of its applications. On July 13, 2026, the Court vacated the hearing on the applications and admonished counsel that it must contact the courtroom to remove noticed matters from the calendar in Department 834.

On July 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant two applications for writ of attachment against Defendants, noticed for September 10, 2026. On September 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed a notice that it had not received any opposition to the applications. II.             Applicable Law

“Upon the filing of the complaint or at any time thereafter, the plaintiff may apply pursuant to this article for a right to attach order and a writ of attachment by filing an application for the order and writ with the court in which the action is brought.” (CCP § 484.010.)

The application shall be executed under oath and must include: (1) a statement showing that the attachment is sought to secure the recovery on a claim upon which an attachment may be issued; (2) a statement of the amount to be secured by the attachment; (3) a statement that the attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based; (4) a statement that the applicant has no information or belief that the claim is discharged or that the prosecution of the action is stayed in a proceeding under the Bankruptcy Act (11 U.S.C. section 101 et seq.); and (5) a description of the property to be attached under the writ of attachment and a statement that the plaintiff is informed and believes that such property is subject to attachment. (CCP § 484.020.)

The application for a writ of attachment must be supported “by an affidavit showing that the plaintiff on the facts presented would be entitled to a judgment on the claim upon which the attachment is based.” (CCP § 484.030.)

The court shall consider the showing made by the parties, as well as the pleadings and other papers in the record. (CCP § 484.090(a), (d).) The Court shall issue a right to attach order if it finds all of the following:

“(1) The claim upon which the attachment is based is one upon which an attachment may be issued.

(2) The plaintiff has established the probable validity of the claim upon which the attachment is based.

(3) The attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based.

(4) The amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.”

(CCP § 484.090(a)(1-4).)

At the times prescribed by CCP section 1005, subdivision (b), the defendant must be served with a copy of the summons and complaint, notice of application and hearing, and a copy of the application and supporting affidavits. (CCP § 484.040.)

“The Attachment Law statutes are subject to strict construction….” (Epstein v. Abrams (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1168.)

In reply, Plaintiff request judicial notice of the following exhibits: (1) the June 15, 2026 Notice of Ruling on Order in this case (RJN Ex. Request 1 is denied as the Court need not take judicial notice of documents on file before it in the current matter. Request 2 as the parties’ private terms sheet is not any category of judicially noticeable material.

IV.           Analysis

1.     Claim on Which Attachment Can Be Based

“[A]n attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney’s fees.” (CCP § 483.010(a).) “An attachment may not be issued on a claim which is secured by any interest in real property arising from agreement ….” (CCP § 483.010(b).)

Here, Plaintiff’s claims against Defendants are premised on its claims for breach of the Note and Guaranty. (Mem. at 3-4.) Plaintiff may seek attachment based on these claims.

2.     Contract Based on a Commercial Claim

If the action is against a defendant who is a natural person, an attachment may be issued only on a commercial claim which arises out of the defendant’s conduct of a trade, business, or profession. (CCP §483.010(c).) Consumer transactions cannot form a basis for attachment. (CCP §483.010(c); Kadison, Pfaelzer, Woodard, Quinn & Rossi v. Wilson (“Kadison”) (1987) 197 Cal.App.3d 1, 4 [action involving trust property was a commercial, not a consumer, transaction].)

As to Mahboob, the Note was executed to fund Admiral. (Cain Decl., ¶ 4.) Mahboob, principal of Admiral (Mahboob Decl., ¶ 1) executed the Guaranty for the Note to induce Plaintiff to execute the note. (Cain Decl., ¶ 8.) Defendants concede the Note and Guaranty are commercial. (Opp. at 11; Mahboob Decl., ¶ 24.)

Accordingly, the Court finds that, as to Mahboob, the claims are commercial in nature, not consumer.

3.     Fixed or Readily Ascertainable Amount

A claim is “readily ascertainable” where the damages may be readily ascertained by reference to the contract and the basis of the calculation appears to be reasonable and definite. (CIT Group/Equipment Financing, Inc. v. Super DVD, Inc. (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 537, 540-41 [“Super DVD”].) The fact that the damages are unliquidated is not determinative. (Ibid.) But the contract must furnish a standard by which the amount may be ascertained and there must be a basis by which the damages can be determined by proof. (Ibid.)

Here, Plaintiff’s evidence shows that Plaintiff and Admiral executed the Note in principal amount $1,389,000 to mature on May 2, 2033. (Cain Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 1.) Plaintiff and Mahboob executed the Guaranty wherein Mahboob guaranteed Admiral’s obligations under the Note. (Cain Decl., ¶ 8, Ex. 4.) Defendants have failed to make monthly payments due under the Note since February 2024. (Cain Decl., ¶ 6.) On October 22, 2024, Plaintiff made a written demand, but Defendants have not cured the default. (Cain Decl., ¶ 7, Ex. 3.) Plaintiff accelerated the Note. (Cain Decl., ¶ 7.)

Assuming, arguendo, that the claimed amount was readily ascertainable, Plaintiff’s fee and cost estimates would be mostly appropriate. Plaintiff estimates it will incur $35,863.98 in allowable attorney fees and $9,000 in allowable costs. (Rubin Decl., ¶ 4.) Plaintiff applies LASC Local Rule 3.214, which sets the amount of reasonable attorney fees recovered under a contract provision for recovery of such fees.[1] However, Plaintiff makes an apparent clerical error. The amount should be $33,863.38. The costs are attested by attorney declaration and are reasonable. Therefore, aside from a $2,000 reduction due to the clerical error, the fees and costs estimates are appropriate.

Nevertheless, Plaintiff offers only the conclusory statement that, as of April 21, 2026, the total remaining obligation on the Note is $1,529,669.23, composed of $1,318,644.21 in principal, $144,583.57 in interest, $9,238.23 in late fees, and $57,203.22 in other fees. (Cain Decl., ¶ 10.) As stated above, and as Defendants note (Opp. at 9), the damages must be readily ascertained by reference to the contract and the basis of the calculation must appear reasonable and definite. (Super DVD, supra, 115 Cal.App.4th at 540-41.) Here, moving party does not supply competent evidence to support all the amounts sought. Without more than conclusory statements, Plaintiff cannot show that the calculation is reasonable and definite.[2]

Accordingly, the amount is not readily ascertainable.[3]

V.              Conclusion

Because the amount is not readily ascertainable, the Court need not analyze the remaining statutory elements or address the undertaking. Plaintiff’s two applications for writs of attachment are DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: APPLICATION FOR WRIT OF ATTACHMENT

 

Plaintiff Darrell Gardner (“Plaintiff”) seeks a writ of attachment against defendant Gregory A. Facktor (“Defendant”).

The application is denied.

I.                Background

1.     Factual Background

Defendant founded Greg Facktor & Associates, LLC (“Company”) and was its sole owner. (Gardner Decl., ¶ 4.) Plaintiff and Jay Boyer (“Boyer”) were senior employees of the Company. (Gardner Decl., ¶ 4.)

Plaintiff and Defendant entered the Membership Interest Purchase Agreement (“Agreement”), dated April 1, 2021, by which Plaintiff purchased 50 percent of the Company, a healthcare consulting provider. (Gardner Decl., ¶¶ 2-3, Ex. A.) The Agreement references three other related contracts, and together the four contracts (collectively, “GFA Agreements”) constituted a complete reorganization of the Company’s ownership. (Gardner Decl., ¶ 4.) The GFA Agreements provide Defendant continued employment and seven years of guaranteed compensation. (Gardner Decl., ¶ 14.)

Defendant transferred about a third of the total shares, 333.2 shares, in annual transfers from 2021 through 2024. (Gardner Decl., ¶¶ 20-21.)

On August 5, 2024, Defendant and Boyer terminated Plaintiff. (Gardner Decl., ¶ 22.) On September 25, 2023, Defendant sent Plaintiff a Notice of Purchase Option by Greg Facktor & Associates (“Option Notice”), which stated that the purchase price for Company shares was calculated by Company CPAs, under an agreed formula in the GFA Agreements, to be $4,922.55 per share. (Gardner Decl., ¶ 7-8, Ex. B.) The Purchase Option stated the Company would pay a down payment of ten percent of the purchase price for the shares and execute a promissory note for the remaining balance, personally guaranteed by Defendant and Boyer. (Gardner Decl., ¶ 28.) The Company assured Plaintiff he would be paid the full value of his shares. (Gardner Decl., ¶ 29.)

On October 25, 2024, the Company sent a letter titled “Purchase of Member Shares” (Gardner Decl., Ex. G), then shortly after wired $164,019.42, ten percent of the total purchase price of Plaintiff’s 333.2 shares. (Gardner Decl., ¶ 30.)

 

2.     Procedural History

On April 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed his complaint for, inter alia, breach of contract. A Proof of service filed April 24, 2026 shows Plaintiff served the summons and complaint on Defendant by personal service on April 22, 2026. On May 29, 2026, Defendant filed his answer to the complaint.

On June 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant application for writ of attachment against Defendant, noticed for August 1, 2026. On August 5, 2026, the Court continued the hearing on the application to September 17, 2026, in accordance with the parties’ joint stipulation. On September 10, 2026, Defendant filed his opposition.

II.             Applicable Law

“Upon the filing of the complaint or at any time thereafter, the plaintiff may apply pursuant to this article for a right to attach order and a writ of attachment by filing an application for the order and writ with the court in which the action is brought.” (CCP § 484.010.)

The application shall be executed under oath and must include: (1) a statement showing that the attachment is sought to secure the recovery on a claim upon which an attachment may be issued; (2) a statement of the amount to be secured by the attachment; (3) a statement that the attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based; (4) a statement that the applicant has no information or belief that the claim is discharged or that the prosecution of the action is stayed in a proceeding under the Bankruptcy Act (11 U.S.C. section 101 et seq.); and (5) a description of the property to be attached under the writ of attachment and a statement that the plaintiff is informed and believes that such property is subject to attachment. (CCP § 484.020.)

The application for a writ of attachment must be supported “by an affidavit showing that the plaintiff on the facts presented would be entitled to a judgment on the claim upon which the attachment is based.” (CCP § 484.030.)

The court shall consider the showing made by the parties, as well as the pleadings and other papers in the record. (CCP § 484.090(a), (d).) The Court shall issue a right to attach order if it finds all of the following:

“(1) The claim upon which the attachment is based is one upon which an attachment may be issued.

(2) The plaintiff has established the probable validity of the claim upon which the attachment is based.

(3) The attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based.

(4) The amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.”

(CCP § 484.090(a)(1-4).)

At the times prescribed by CCP section 1005, subdivision (b), the defendant must be served with a copy of the summons and complaint, notice of application and hearing, and a copy of the application and supporting affidavits. (CCP § 484.040.)

“The Attachment Law statutes are subject to strict construction….” (Epstein v. Abrams (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1168.)

III.           Analysis

1.     Claim on Which Attachment Can Be Based

“[A]n attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney’s fees.” (CCP § 483.010(a).) “An attachment may not be issued on a claim which is secured by any interest in real property arising from agreement ….” (CCP § 483.010(b).)

Here, Plaintiff’s claims against Defendants are premised on his claim for breach of the Agreement. (See Mem. at 11.) Plaintiff may seek attachment based on this claim.

2.     Contract Based on a Commercial Claim

If the action is against a defendant who is a natural person, an attachment may be issued only on a commercial claim which arises out of the defendant’s conduct of a trade, business, or profession. (CCP §483.010(c).) Consumer transactions cannot form a basis for attachment. (CCP §483.010(c); Kadison, Pfaelzer, Woodard, Quinn & Rossi v. Wilson (“Kadison”) (1987) 197 Cal.App.3d 1, 4 [action involving trust property was a commercial, not a consumer, transaction].)

The Agreement was part of the GFA Agreements, which collectively sold and reorganized the Defendant’s Company and provided for Defendant’s continued employment and guaranteed income.  (Gardner Decl., ¶¶ 4, 12-14.) Accordingly, the claims are commercial in nature.

3.     Fixed or Readily Ascertainable Amount

A claim is “readily ascertainable” where the damages may be readily ascertained by reference to the contract and the basis of the calculation appears to be reasonable and definite. (CIT Group/Equipment Financing, Inc. v. Super DVD, Inc. (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 537, 540-41 [“Super DVD”].) The fact that the damages are unliquidated is not determinative. (Ibid.) But the contract must furnish a standard by which the amount may be ascertained and there must be a basis by which the damages can be determined by proof. (Ibid.)

Here, Plaintiff’s evidence shows that Plaintiff and Defendant executed the Agreement for the purchase of a 50 percent interest in the Company. (Gardner Decl., ¶¶ 2-3, Ex. A.) Defendant transferred about a third of the total shares, 333.2 shares, to Plaintiff. (Gardner Decl., ¶¶ 20-21.) On August 5, 2024, Defendant and Boyer terminated Plaintiff. (Gardner Decl., ¶ 22.) The Company indicated it would buy back the shares already transferred to Plaintiff, but not the remaining shares he would have obtained under the Agreement. (See Gardner Decl., ¶¶ 28-30.)

In particular, Plaintiff states that the value of the remaining shares “will be at least the amount on an average share basis that [the Company] sought to purchase from [Plaintiff] in 2024, i.e., $4,922.55.” (Gardner Decl., ¶¶ 10.)

As stated, the contract must furnish a standard by which the amount may be ascertained, and the basis of the calculation must appear to be reasonable and definite. (Super DVD, supra, 115 Cal.App.4th at 540-41.) Here, the Agreement provides that Plaintiff is entitled to shares equaling 50 percent ownership in the Company. Plaintiff provides an estimate of the worth of the shares he contends he is owed, based on a prior evaluation. Without opining on the accuracy of the valuation or of Plaintiff’s estimation, this calculation is not “reasonable and definite.” As Plaintiff acknowledges, the amount sought is not a calculation of the value of the shares, but a minimum estimate based on greater earnings in 2024 and 2025.[1] (Gardner Decl., ¶¶ 10-11.) Even if the Court credits Plaintiff’s minimum calculation, Plaintiff submits no authority showing that the Court may accept a minimum calculation in place of the definite calculation ordinarily required.

Accordingly, the amount is not readily ascertainable.

4.     Probable Validity of Plaintiff’s Claims

“A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.” (CCP § 481.190.) “If the defendant opposes the application, ‘the court must then consider the relative merits of the positions of the respective parties and make a determination of the probable outcome of the litigation.’ [Citations.]” (Pech v. Morgan (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 841, 855.)

Here, the application is based on claims for breach of contract. To establish a claim for breach of contract, a plaintiff must prove: (1) existence of a contract; (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendant’s breach of the contract; and (4) damages incurred by plaintiff as a result of the breach. (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1367.)

Here, as noted above, Plaintiff’s evidence shows that Plaintiff and Defendant executed the Agreement for the purchase of a 50 percent interest in the Company. (Gardner Decl., ¶¶ 2-3, Ex. A.) Defendant transferred about a third of the total shares, 333.2 shares, to Plaintiff. (Gardner Decl., ¶¶ 20-21.) On August 5, 2024, Defendant and Boyer terminated Plaintiff. (Gardner Decl., ¶ 22.) The Company indicated it would buy back the shares already transferred to Plaintiff, but not the remaining shares he would have obtained under the Agreement. (See Gardner Decl., ¶¶ 28-30.)

Plaintiff has made a prima facie showing for breach of contract.

In opposition, Defendant argues Plaintiff breached first by breaching his fiduciary duty to the Company. Specifically, Defendant alleges Plaintiff falsified time entries, breaching the Company Operating Agreement and therefore forfeiting any right to the remaining shares.

On application for prejudgment attachment, the Court must determine the relative merits of the positions and evaluate the probable outcome of the litigation. (Pech v. Morgan, supra, 61 Cal.App.5th at 855.) The Court does not here make any final adjudications of the merits. The Court must make its determination on the limited evidentiary showing before it for the application.

In direct contradiction to Plaintiff’s implications that Defendant and Boyer invented timekeeping inflation to justify a breach (Gardner Decl., ¶ 31; Mem. at 13), Defendants show Boyer expressed his belief that Plaintiff’s conduct satisfied good cause for termination in April 2024, and then initiated and completed an investigation into Plaintiff’s conduct in July 2024, before ultimately terminating Plaintiff in August 2024. (Boyer  Decl., Exs. 8-9.)

Plaintiff also supports his position with the fact that the Company terminated him without cause. (Gardner Decl., ¶ 22, Ex. F.) However, the termination procedures used are not dispositive of whether Plaintiff violated the Operating Agreement, which is the basis of Defendant’s claim that Plaintiff is not entitled to the remaining shares. Defendant also presents a plausible reasoning for terminating Plaintiff without cause: the Company, Defendant, and Boyer sought to reach compromise to avoid litigation. (Boyer Decl., Ex. 9.)

On the evidence provided, the Court is not able to find that Plaintiff is more likely than not to prevail on the merits. Accordingly, Plaintiff has not shown the probable validity of its breach of contract claim.

 

IV.           Conclusion

Because the amount is not readily ascertainable, and because Plaintiff has not shown that his claim has probable validity on this record, the Court need not analyze the remaining statutory elements or address the undertaking. Plaintiff’s application for writ of attachment is DENIED.
