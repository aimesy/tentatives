DEPARTMENT

415

HEARING DATE

July 6, 2026

CASE NUMBER

22STCV41036B

MOTION

Motion for Summary Adjudication

MOVING PARTIES

Plaintiffs Carrie Miller and Wake Leroux

OPPOSING PARTY

Defendant the HUSL Ltd.

 

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Carrie Miller (“Miller”) and Wake Leroux (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) move for summary adjudication of various issues against Defendant the HUSL Ltd. (“Defendant”).  Defendant opposes the motion.

“A party may move for summary adjudication as to one or more causes of action within an action, one or more affirmative defenses, one or more claims for damages, or one or more issues of duty, if the party contends that the cause of action has no merit, that there is no affirmative defense to the cause of action, that there is no merit to an affirmative defense as to any cause of action, that there is no merit to a claim for damages, as specified in Section 3294 of the Civil Code, or that one or more defendants either owed or did not owe a duty to the plaintiff or plaintiffs.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).) 

Plaintiffs’ Issue No. 2 does not concern an affirmative defense, issue of duty, or a claim for punitive damages.  As such, it is not a proper issue for summary adjudication.  “There is little flexibility in the procedural imperatives of [Code of Civil Procedure section 437c], and the issues raised by a motion for summary judgment (or summary adjudication) are pure questions of law. As a result, section 437c is unforgiving; a failure to comply with any one of its myriad requirements is likely to be fatal to the offending party.” (Brantley v. Pisaro (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 1591, 1607.)

Accordingly, the Court does not consider Issue No. 2. 

LEGAL STANDARD – MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT/ADJUDICATION

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law[.] There is a triable issue of material fact if, and only if, the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.)  “[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.”  (Ibid.)  

When the plaintiff seeks summary adjudication, the plaintiff bears the burden to produce admissible evidence on each element of every cause of action at issue.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).) 

EVIDENCE

            The Court declines to rule on the evidentiary objections, as the Court did not rely on the evidence objected to in its ruling.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (q).)

DISCUSSION

Eighth Cause of Action: Breach of Contract

            In the operative complaint, Plaintiffs allege Defendant agreed to pay Miller with 250,000 tokens in exchange for Miller’s acceptance of employment with Defendant and Defendants, thereafter, terminated Miller’s employment without providing him with the 250,000 tokens.  (Complaint, ¶¶ 105-107.)  The pleadings set the outer limits of materiality in a summary judgment proceeding.  (FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 382.)  While Plaintiffs seek summary adjudication of Defendant’s contractual duties with respect to other aspects of compensation agreements with Miller, the Court cannot grant this relief, as Plaintiffs do not allege breaches of those aspects of the agreements as part of the eighth cause of action in the operative complaint.  The Court, therefore, considers only subpart (i) of Issue No. 1.

In this regard, Plaintiffs seek summary adjudication of Defendant’s contractual duty.  In their separate statement in support of their motion, Plaintiffs must identify “each supporting material fact claimed to be without dispute with respect to” each issue. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1350(b), (d).)  In their separate statement, Plaintiffs cite Miller’s deposition testimony whereat Miller testified Defendant agreed to compensate Miller with 250,000 tokens for working as Defendant’s vice president of logistics.  (Declaration of Carrie Miller, Exhibit A, p. 125.)  Plaintiffs also advance Miller’s declaration, in which Miller states Defendant provided Miller with 136,386 tokens, rather than the promised 250,000 tokens.  (Miller Decl., ¶ L.)  This evidence meets Plaintiffs’ burden to show Defendant had a contractual duty to provide Miller with 250,000 tokens, which Defendant breached.  Therefore, Plaintiffs have shifted the burden to Defendant to raise triable issues of material fact as to whether Defendant owed a contractual duty, which Defendant breached.

In opposition, Defendant presents evidence to the effect it never entered into a written compensation agreement with Miller.  (Declaration of Matthew Schellhas, ¶ 6.)  That is immaterial.  Evidence that Miller and Defendant did not enter into a written agreement is not evidence no agreement was reached between them.  Defendant advances no evidence it provided Miller with the tokens it agreed to provide.  (See Separate Statement in Opposition to Motion for Summary Adjudication, p. 14.)  Accordingly, Defendant fails to raise triable issues of material fact as to whether Defendant had contractual duties which Defendant breached.  The motion for summary adjudication of subpart (i) of Issue No. 1 is granted. 

Plaintiffs fail to meet their burden to summarily adjudicate the entirety of the eighth cause of action.  Because damages are an element of a cause of action for breach of contract, Plaintiffs cannot obtain summary adjudication on a cause of action for breach of contract without proving the damages to which Plaintiffs are entitled.  (Paramount Petroleum Corp. v. Superior Court (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 226, 241.)  Plaintiffs have not presented any evidence which establish Plaintiffs are entitled to a specific sum of damages.  Instead, Plaintiffs approximate their damages and seek to leave the issue of the precise amount of damages for later.  (See, e.g., Motion for Summary Adjudication, p. 11; Miller Decl., ¶ L.)  This is improper. 

Accordingly, the motion for summary adjudication of the eighth cause of action is denied.

Eleventh Cause of Action: Fraud

Plaintiffs’ eleventh cause of action is for fraud.  “The elements of fraud, which give rise to the tort action for deceit, are (1) a misrepresentation, (2) with knowledge of its falsity, (3) with the intent to induce another's reliance on the misrepresentation, (4) justifiable reliance, and (5) resulting damage.” (Conroy v. Regents of University of California (2009) 45 Cal.4th 1244, 1255.) 

As set forth above, because damages are an element of a cause of action for fraud, Plaintiffs cannot obtain summary adjudication on a cause of action for fraud without proving the damages to which they are entitled.  As discussed above, Plaintiffs have presented no evidence which show they are entitled to a specific sum of damages.  (See, e.g., Motion for Summary Adjudication, p. 12.)  Therefore, the motion for summary adjudication as to this cause of action is deficient for the same reason. 

Accordingly, the motion for summary adjudication of the eleventh cause of action is denied. 

Sixteenth Cause of Action: Negligence

            As set forth above, because damages are an element of a cause of action for negligence, Plaintiffs cannot obtain summary adjudication of the cause of action for negligence without proving the damages to which Plaintiffs are entitled.  Plaintiffs cannot approximate their damages and leave the issue of precise damages for later.  (See, e.g., Motion for Summary Adjudication, p. 13.) 

Therefore, the motion for summary adjudication of the sixteenth cause of action is denied.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant fails to raise triable issues of material fact as to whether Plaintiff is entitled to summary adjudication of subpart (i) of Issue No. 1.  Accordingly, the motion for summary adjudication of subpart (i) of Issue No. 1 is granted.  The motion is otherwise denied.

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of the Court’s ruling and to file proof of service of same.

DISCUSSION           

Business Judgment Rule

Plaintiffs move for summary adjudication of Defendant’s affirmative defense the business judgment rule bars Plaintiff’s causes of action.  The business judgment rule generally shields a corporation and the members of its board of directors from liability for decisions they made based on “‘any rational business purpose,’” but it does not shield actions taken with improper motives or because of a conflict of interest.  (Berg & Berg Enterprises, LLC v. Boyle (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 1020, 1045.)  

            In their separate statement in support of their motion, Plaintiffs must identify “each supporting material fact claimed to be without dispute with respect to” each issue. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1350(b), (d).)  Plaintiffs do not cite evidence in support of their contention the business judgment rule cannot bar their claims.  Instead, Plaintiffs cite Defendant’s notice of motion for summary adjudication against Plaintiffs.  Defendant’s notice of motion is not evidence.  Accordingly, Plaintiffs fail to meet his burden to show the business judgment rule has no merit.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiffs fail to satisfy their burden by establishing they are entitled to summary adjudication.  As such, the motion is denied.

DISCUSSION

Ninth Cause of Action: Breach of Contract  

            Plaintiffs’ ninth cause of action is for breach of contract.  In the operative complaint, Plaintiffs allege Miller loaned Defendant $25,000, which Defendant failed to repay.  (Complaint, ¶¶ 120-122.)  The pleadings set the outer limits of materiality in a summary judgment proceeding.  (FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 382.) 

In support of their motion, Plaintiffs cite Defendant’s deposition testimony.  At deposition, Defendant testified Miller deposited $25,000 on Defendant’s behalf.  (Declaration of Carrie Miller, Exhibit A, p. 47.)  Defendant disputed Miller’s deposit of the funds into the account constituted a loan.  (Id., Exhibit A, pp. 56-57.)  Likewise, Plaintiffs advance Defendant’s responses to Plaintiffs’ written discovery, in which Defendant denied Miller loaned Defendant $25,000.  (Id., Exhibits B, C.)   As such, Plaintiffs’ evidence reveals triable issues of material fact exist as to whether Miller loaned $25,000 to Defendant. 

Accordingly, Plaintiffs fail to satisfy their burden to show Defendant breached a loan agreement with Plaintiffs.  Therefore, the motion for summary adjudication of the ninth cause of action for breach of contract is denied.

Thirteenth Cause of Action: Breach of Fiduciary Duty

            In the complaint, Plaintiffs allege Defendant breached his fiduciary duty to Plaintiffs as a financial advisor and money manager with respect to investing $50,000 in tokens on Plaintiffs’ behalf.  (Complaint, ¶¶ 205-207.)  In support of their argument Defendant breached fiduciary duties owed to them, Plaintiffs cite their own complaint and mediation brief.  The complaint and mediation brief are not evidence.  Plaintiffs also cite “communications” from Defendant.  (Declaration of Carrie Miller, ¶ 7, Exhibit E.)  Plaintiffs fail to explain in what manner Defendant made these alleged representations, when Defendant made them, and how they were received by Plaintiffs.  As such, Plaintiffs fail to authenticate the communications.  (Evid. Code, §§ 1400, 1401.) 

As Plaintiffs fail to advance admissible evidence to support their claim Defendant owed fiduciary duties to Plaintiffs, the Court denies the motion for summary adjudication of Issue No. 5.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiffs fail to meet their burden to show Plaintiffs are entitled to summary adjudication.  As such, the motion is denied.

MOTIONS

Demurrer to Complaint; Motion to Strike

MOVING PARTY

Defendant Leslie Koral

OPPOSING PARTY

Plaintiff Pedro Onofre

 

MOTION

Plaintiff Pedro Onofre (“Plaintiff”) brought this action against Defendant Leslie Koral (“Defendant”), alleging Defendant was responsible for injuries Plaintiff sustained resulting from a motor vehicle collision.  Defendant demurs to the fourth cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress in Plaintiff’s complaint.  Defendant also moves to strike the punitive damages allegations in the complaint.  Plaintiff opposes the motions.

ANALYSIS

“It is black letter law that a demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.”  (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.)  In ruling on a demurrer, the court must “liberally construe[]” the allegations of the complaint.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)  “This rule of liberal construction means that the reviewing court draws inferences favorable to the plaintiff, not the defendant.”  (Perez v. Golden Empire Transit Dist. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1228, 1238.)

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a motion to strike the whole pleading or any part thereof. (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1322(b).)  On a motion to strike, the court may: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782.)

Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action is for intentional infliction of emotional distress.  “The elements of the tort of intentional infliction of emotional distress are: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff's suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant's outrageous conduct. Conduct to be outrageous must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.  The defendant must have engaged in conduct intended to inflict injury or engaged in with the realization that injury will result.” (Potter v. Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 965, 1001, internal quotations and citations omitted.) 

In this regard, Plaintiff alleges Defendant collided with Plaintiff’s vehicle and, thereafter, left the scene of the accident.  (Complaint, Fourth Cause of Action.)  While leaving the scene of an accident without rendering aid is intentional conduct, it is independently actionable only if the defendant doing so was a proximate cause of further injury or death beyond the accident itself.  (See Brooks v. E.J. Willig Truck Transp. Co. (1953) 40 Cal.2d 669, 678-679 [“One who negligently injures another and renders him helpless is bound to use reasonable care to prevent any further harm which the actor realizes or should realize threatens the injured person”].)  Plaintiff does not allege he sustained additional injuries because Defendant left the scene of the accident.  Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer as to this claim.  However, it will provide Plaintiff with an opportunity to amend the cause of action.

As the Court sustains the demurrer to the fourth cause of action, the motion to strike the associated prayer for punitive damages is moot.  However, to the extent it is not moot, the Court grants the motion to strike. 

In ruling on a motion to strike punitive damages, “judges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth.” (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.) To state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a plaintiff must allege the elements set forth in the punitive damages statute, Civil Code section 3294. (College Hospital, Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 721 (College Hospital).)  Pursuant to Civil Code section 3294, subdivision (a), a plaintiff must allege  the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. “Malice is defined in the statute as conduct intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (College Hospital, supra, 8 Cal.4th at p. 725.)  “The mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages.  Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim.”  (Grieves v. Superior Ct. (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166, internal citations and footnotes omitted.) 

In the instant case, Plaintiff fails to allege specific facts to establish Defendant acted with oppression, fraud, or malice.  Accordingly, Plaintiff must amend such allegations concerning punitive damages.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant’s demurrer to the fourth cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.  To the extent it is not moot, Defendant’s motion to strike is granted with leave to amend.

Plaintiff is to file an amended complaint within 20 days of  notice of the Court’s order.

Defendant is ordered to provide notice of the Court’s ruling and to file proof of service of same.
