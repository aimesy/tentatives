Plaintiff David Gutierrez’s Motion for Attorney Fees

Facts: This is a lemon law action. The First Amended Complaint (FAC) alleges as follows. Plaintiff David Gutierrez (Plaintiff) entered into a warranty for his vehicle with Defendant on May 23, 2018. (FAC ¶ 6.) During the warranty period, the vehicle contained or developed defects that Defendant has been unable to remedy. (Complaint ¶¶ 7–14.)   

 

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on July 27, 2021, and filed the FAC on January 4, 2022, alleging five causes of action:

1.      VIOLATION OF SUBDIVISION (D) OF CIVIL CODE SECTION 1793.2

2.      VIOLATION OF SUBDIVISION (B) OF CIVIL CODE SECTION 1793.2

3.      VIOLATION OF SUBDIVISION (A)(3) OF CIVIL CODE SECTION 1793.2

4.      BREACH OF THE IMPLIED WARRANTY OF MERCHANTABILITY

5.      FRAUD BY OMISSION

On March 30, 2022, this court denied and overruled Defendant’s motion to strike and demurrer to the FAC.

On June 7, 2022, this court granted Plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses to requests for production.

A notice of settlement was filed on December 17, 2025.

Plaintiff filed the present motion for attorney fees on June 26, 2026.

Defendant filed an opposition on September 8, 2026.

Analysis:

 

I.                   MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES

Parties to litigation must generally bear their own attorney’s fees, unless they otherwise agree. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1021.) However, the Song-Beverly Act provides for the award of attorneys’ fees to prevailing plaintiffs as follows:

If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney's fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.

(Civ. Code § 1794, subd. (d).)

“It is well established that the determination of what constitutes reasonable attorney fees is committed to the discretion of the trial court, whose decision cannot be reversed in the absence of an abuse of discretion.”  (Melnyk v. Robledo (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 618, 623.)  In exercising its discretion, the court should consider a number of factors, including the nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, the skill required in handling the matter, the attention given, the success or failure, and the resulting judgment.  (See id.) 

In determining the proper amount of fees to award, courts use the lodestar method.  The lodestar figure is calculated by multiplying the total number of reasonable hours expended by the reasonable hourly rate.  “Fundamental to its determination . . . [is] a careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney . . . in the presentation of the case.”  (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 48 (Serrano III).)  A reasonable hourly rate must reflect the skill and experience of the attorney.  (Id. at p. 49.)  “Prevailing parties are compensated for hours reasonably spent on fee-related issues.  A fee request that appears unreasonably inflated is a special circumstance permitting the trial court to reduce the award or deny one altogether.”  (Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 635 (Serrano IV).)  The Court in Serrano IV also stated that fees associated with preparing the motion to recover attorneys’ fees are recoverable.  (See id. at p. 624.)

Plaintiff David Gutierrez (“Plaintiff”) seeks an award of fees and costs in the total amount of $42,410.76, representing a fee lodestar of $28,692.60, with a multiplier of 1.35, plus costs in the amount of $2,175.88, and an additional $1,500.00 for fees incurred in responding to Defendant General Motors, LLC’s (“Defendant”) opposition to the present motion. (Motion at p. ii.)

The $30,192.60 lodestar ($28,692.60 plus $1,500.00 anticipated work defending the motion) represents 60.2 hours of work by 17 timekeepers, charging rates of $205 to $650 per hour, with a mean hourly rate of $501.54. (Shahian Decl. Exh. 1.)

Defendant in opposition argues that the motion is untimely under California Rules of Court (“CRC”) Rule 3.1702, subd. (b)(1), which provides the time to file an attorney fee motion as follows:

A notice of motion to claim attorney's fees for services up to and including the rendition of judgment in the trial court--including attorney's fees on an appeal before the rendition of judgment in the trial court--must be served and filed within the time for filing a notice of appeal under rules 8.104 and 8.108 in an unlimited civil case or under rules 8.822 and 8.823 in a limited civil case.

(CRC Rule 3.1702, subd. (b)(1).)

CRC Rule 8.104 requires a notice of appeal to be filed 60 days after the court or a party serves notice of entry of judgment, or “180 days after entry of judgment.” (CRC Rule 8.104, subd. (a)(1)(A)–(C).) Dismissal was entered here by court order, pursuant to stipulation of the parties, on December 29, 2025, and Defendant argues the motion should have been filed within 60 days of this date. (Opposition at p. 14.) But although Defendant states in its memorandum that “[t]he Court served the parties with the order of dismissal on this same date” (Opposition at p. 14), nothing in the evidence cited or the court’s file indicates that the dismissal entered pursuant to the stipulation was served as Defendant describes. Therefore, because the motion was filed on June 26, 2026 — 179 days after entry of dismissal — and there is no evidence of service of a notice of dismissal, the motion is timely under CRC Rule 3.1702 and Rule 8.104.

Proceeding to its objections to the fees in particular, Defendant in opposition argues that this court should disallow fees incurred after its service of a section 998 offer on November 14, 2023. (Opposition at pp. 10–11.) Defendant at that time offered Plaintiff $15,000.00, inclusive of fees and costs. (Rader Decl. Exh. 3.) Plaintiff rejected the offer, and on December 16, 2025, accepted an offer for $9,000.00, plus prejudgment interest up to the date of acceptance (which amounted to $3,952.68), with fees and costs to be determined by motion. (Rader Decl. ¶ 18, Exh. 5.) Defendant argues that since Plaintiff accepted a settlement offer that awarded less than the original November 2023 offer, Plaintiff should not be awarded fees accrued after that offer was served. (Opposition at pp. 10–11.)

No such reduction is appropriate. The accepted offer granted Plaintiff greater recovery than the first because it offered Plaintiff fees and costs to be determined by motion, rather than included in the $15,000.00 amount. Binding authority holds “that it is an error of law for the trial court to reduce or deny an award of attorney fees in a civil rights or public interest case on the basis of a plaintiff's rejection of a Code of Civil Procedure section 998 offer when the ultimate recovery has exceeded the rejected offer.” (Reck v. FCA US LLC (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 682, 697.) The court that made the above holding relied upon Joyce v. Town of Dennis (1st Cir. 2013) 720 F.3d 12. (Reck, supra, 64 Cal.app.5th at p. 695.) There, a trial court reduced a prevailing plaintiff’s  $170,000 fee request because she had rejected a $35,001 fee-inclusive settlement offer, and thereafter obtained an ultimate damages award of only $15,000. ( Joyce, supra, 720 F.3d at pp. 28–29.) The appellate court reversed, noting that the ultimate total award, including  “damages, costs, and attorney's fees,” exceeded the offer, meaning the settlement offer could not form a basis for a fee reduction. (Id. at p. 32.)

The same principle applies here. Defendant offered $15,000.00, inclusive of fees and costs, and Plaintiff ultimately settled for an award of $12,952.68 (i.e. $9,000 plus prejudgment interest), with fees and costs to be determined on motion. Defendant argues that, at the time of the first offer, Plaintiff’s “reasonable fees” amounted only to $3,392.50, and her costs to $1,551.95. (Opposition at p. 10.)  Even accepting Defendant’s calculation of fees and costs, Plaintiff’s recovery on the second settlement is greater. But Defendant’s calculation is not even based on Plaintiff’s record of fees. Defendant instead offers its own judgment as to what fees incurred prior to the first settlement offer were “reasonable.” Plaintiff’s own billing records indicate that the period before the November 2023 settlement offer — which period included a demurrer, a motion to compel further, and preparation for a trial date then scheduled for  December 2023 — amounted to more than $20,000, the majority of the fee lodestar sought here.  Plaintiff’s rejection of the first settlement offer was therefore reasonable, and furnishes no basis to reduce the requested fee award.

Defendant is persuasive, however, when arguing for the excessiveness of some of the requested hourly rates of Plaintiff’s counsel. (Opposition at pp. 2–4.) The “reasonable hourly rate [used to calculate the lodestar] is the product of a multiplicity of factors ... the level of skill necessary, time limitations, the amount to be obtained in the litigation, the attorney's reputation, and the undesirability of the case.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1139.) Based on the evidence presented by the parties — including the testimony of Plaintiff’s counsel, their record of prior fee awards in other cases, and Defendant’s presentation of the 2023 Real Rate Report prepared by Wolters Kluwer (Quezada Decl. Exh. S) — as well as the relative complexity of the case and the amount at issue in this litigation, the court concludes that the hourly rates presented by Plaintiff’s counsel ought properly to be reduced to a rate of $550 per hour for partners, and $425 per hour for other attorneys. 

Applying these reduced rates yields the following lodestar reductions associated with each affected attorney:

·         Tionna Carvalho: $290

·         Mani Arabi: $70

·         Olivia Avelino: $340

·         James Carroll: $884

·         Joy de Leon: $360

·         Scott Friedman: $690

·         Regina Liou: $105

·         Ian McAllister: $493

·         Hunter McMillan: $20

·         Rebecca Neubauer: $10

·         Maro Orte: $22.50

·         Emily Rader: $203

The reduction in rates identified above thus yields an overall lodestar reduction of $3,197.50.

Defendant further argues that several reductions are appropriate for unreasonable or excessive time billed for various tasks. (Opposition at pp. 6–9.) Certain of the charges identified are properly deducted. These include a $1,025.00 charge incurred on January 4, 2022, for revision of a largely templated motion to compel further for which another drafting attorney elsewhere billed. (Shahian Decl. Exh. 1.) This also includes $2,009.00 incurred in opposing Defendant’s demurrer and motion to strike, which exclusively concerned Plaintiff’s fraud cause of action and accompanying prayer for punitive damages — neither of which are among the Song Beverly claims for which fees are awardable.  (See Santana v. FCA US, LLC (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 334, 349 [apportionment of time between compensable and non-compensable claims is required when it is reasonably feasible “to separate the attorney's time into compensable and noncompensable units”].) These two charges amount to a lodestar reduction of $3,034.00.

It is also proper to deduct various charges for revision of discovery responses and meet-and-confer letters which are largely based on templates, and which were drafted by other attorneys who also bill for the same work. These charges are for $316.00, $197.50, and $205.00, on October 14, 2021, December 23, 2021, and January 25, 202, respectively, yielding a further reduction of $718.5.

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s counsel overstaffed this matter with excessive timekeepers. (Opposition at p. 12–13.) It is true that unreasonable multiplicity of attorneys performing duplicative work may furnish a basis to substantially reduce a cost award. In Morris v. Hyundai Motors America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, the trial court substantially reduced a requested attorney fees award involving 11 attorneys and two firms in a lemon law action. (Id. at p. 32.) “Out of a total of 283.3 hours of billed work, the court did not award any fees for 83.5 hours of work billed by six associates.” (Ibid.) The appellate court affirmed: “Plainly, it is appropriate for a trial court to reduce a fee award based on its reasonable determination that a routine, non-complex case was overstaffed to a degree that significant inefficiencies and inflated fees resulted.” (Id. at p. 39.) The appellate court determined that the trial court’s remedy of cutting out several attorneys’ billings was permissible because it “was designed to yield a revised lodestar figure that reflected a total amount of fees that were reasonably incurred.” (Id. at p. 40.)

The present motion evidences a large number of timekeepers on Plaintiff’s case. But a large number of attorneys is not on its own reason to reduce a fee award, and the cases cited above upheld lodestar reductions based on the “duplicative” and “inefficient” billings that resulted from the number of attorneys employed. (Mikhaeilpoor, supra, 48 Cal.App.5th at p. 255; Morris, supra, 41 Cal.App.5th at p. 40.) Here, it does not appear that overstaffing resulted in such great inefficiencies that only an overall reduction in the lodestar can account for them. Review of the billing records presented by Plaintiff permits reduction where duplicative or inefficient work is present, as described above.

 

Thus, applying the reductions identified above, the fee lodestar is properly reduced, from the requested amount of $30,192.60 to the lesser amount of $23,242.60.

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s costs should not be awarded, as the only verification of the costs requested is the table thereof presented in connection with Plaintiff’s billings. (Opposition at p. 14.) Yet this table of costs and the authentication provided therewith by Plaintiff’s counsel is adequate basis upon which to seek the costs requested in the present motion. (Shahian Decl. ¶¶ 26–28, Exh. 1), The only reduction apparently proper here is a $12.95 charge in connection with filing the opposition to Defendant’s demurrer, which once again did not address Plaintiff’s compensable Song Beverly claims. (Shahian Decl. Exh. 1.) Thus the overall costs award is reduced by $12.95 to $2,162.93.

 

Finally, no lodestar multiplier is appropriate in this case. “Once the court has fixed the lodestar, it may increase or decrease that amount by applying a positive or negative ‘multiplier’ to take into account a variety of other factors, including the quality of the representation, the novelty and complexity of the issues, the results obtained, and the contingent risk presented.” (Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank., N.A. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 819, 833.) The court’s file and the billings submitted in support of the motion demonstrate that this lemon law case, which did not proceed to trial, was not particularly novel or complex.

Accordingly, the motion for attorney fees is GRANTED for fees in the amount of $23,242.60. and costs in the amount of  $2,162.93.

Facts: This is an action for unfair competition, interference with economic relations, and trademark infringement. The First Amended Complaint (FAC) alleges as follows. Plaintiff Huge USA, Inc. (Plaintiff) manufactured and supplied Defendant Fashion Nova, Inc. (Defendant) with popular, brand-name jeans, for the latter to sell to customers. (FAC ¶¶ 16–22.) Defendant has since engaged manufacturers to create jeans effectively identical to those manufactured by Plaintiff, including the use of Defendant’s distinctive “style numbers,” and has used unfair tactics to promote and sell these products to customers and create brand confusion as to which product they are buying. (FAC ¶¶ 23–33.)

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the original complaint on November 27, 2023, and filed the FAC on April 11, 2024, alleging eight causes of action:

On June 14, 2024, this court sustained Defendant Fashion Nova, Inc.’s demurrer to the fifth and eighth causes of action for unjust enrichment and receipt of stolen property, and otherwise overruled the demurrer.

This court on April 3, 2025, denied Defendant’s motion for protective order.

Analysis

II.    MOTIONS FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT OR ADJUDICATION

 

A party may move for summary judgment “if it is contended that the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (a).) “[I]f all the evidence submitted, and all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law,” the moving party will be entitled to summary judgment.  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) A motion for summary adjudication may be made by itself or as an alternative to a motion for summary judgment and shall proceed in all procedural respects as a motion for summary judgment.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (f)(2).)

A.    Plaintiff’s Motion

The moving party bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact, and if he does so, the burden shifts to the opposing party to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850; accord Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) Plaintiffs moving for summary judgment may meet their initial burden by “prov[ing] each element of the cause of action entitling the party to judgment on the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(p)(1).)

Once the plaintiff has met that burden, the burden shifts to the defendant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(p)(1).)  The defendant may not rely upon the mere allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists but, instead, shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(p)(1).)  To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence.  (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

 

Plaintiff Huge USA, Inc., dba Vibrant M.I.U. (“Plaintiff”) seeks summary adjudication of its first, second, third, fourth, sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action against Defendant Fashion Nova, Inc. (“Defendant”), respectively for unfair competition, intentional interference with economic advantage, intentional interference with contract, negligent interference with economic advantage, trademark infringement, trade dress infringement, . leaving the question of damages “for the jury.” (Motion at pp. 2, 4.) Plaintiff also seeks summary adjudication of the issues of functionality, lack of secondary meaning, and likelihood of confusion. (Motion at pp. 2, 14–15.)

 

1.      Initial Matters

 

As an initial matter, Plaintiff’s motion fails as to the second, third, and fourth causes of action for intentional and negligent interference with contract and economic advantage, because summary adjudication on these claims is not available absent adjudication of the element of damages.

 

May a plaintiff seek summary adjudication of liability only, leaving the resolution of damages to a later trial? The statutory language mandates the question be answered in the negative. A plaintiff can obtain summary adjudication of a cause of action only by proving “each element of the cause of action entitling the party to judgment on that cause of action.” As damages are an element of a breach of contract cause of action [citation], a plaintiff cannot obtain judgment on a breach of contract cause of action in an amount of damages to be determined later.

 

(Paramount Petroleum Corp. v. Superior Court (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 226, 241.)

 

Similarly, damages are an element of Plaintiff’s second, third, and fourth causes of action for interference with contract and economic advantage.  (Reeves v. Hanlon (2004) 33 Cal.4th 1140, 1148 [element of “resulting damage” for contractual interference];  Nelson v. Tucker Ellis, LLP (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 827, 844 [“resulting economic harm” element of negligent interference];  Roy Allan Slurry Seal, Inc. v. American Asphalt South, Inc. (2017) 2 Cal.5th 505, 512 [requiring “economic harm proximately caused by the defendant's action” for economic advantage claim].) Plaintiff thus cannot seek summary adjudication of liability of these claims, while leaving damages to be determined by the trier of fact.

 

Nor may Plaintiff seek separate summary adjudication as to the issues of functionality, lack of secondary meaning, and likelihood of confusion. Plaintiff seeks summary adjudication on these points by framing them as affirmative defenses upon which summary adjudication may be had. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (f)(1).) But they are not affirmative defenses. Plaintiff acknowledges in its motion that the non-functionality of a given trade dress is not an affirmative defense, but an element of a plaintiff’s claim for trade dress infringement. (Motion at p. 19; Clicks Billiards, Inc. v. Sixshooters, Inc. (9th Cir. 2001) 251 F.3d 1252, 1258 [“nonfunctional[ity]” is an element of trade dress infringement].) Likewise, a plaintiff claiming trademark infringement must prove the existence of a secondary meaning for descriptive marks and a likelihood of confusion resulting from the infringement. (See North Carolina Dairy Foundation, Inc. v. Foremost-McKesson, Inc. (1979) 92 Cal.App.3d 98, 106.) Thus summary adjudication cannot be had on these matters.

 

It is appropriate then to proceed to Plaintiff’s claims for unfair competition and trademark and trade dress infringement.

 

2.      Unfair Competition

 

The common law tort of unfair competition was described in the case Los Defensores, Inc. v. Gomez (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 377:

 

Although the term “unfair competition” applies to several types of misconduct [citation], the tort of unfair competition pertinent here is “the act of ‘passing off’ one's goods as those of another.” [Citation.] That tort “developed as an equitable remedy against the wrongful exploitation of trade names and common law trademarks that were not otherwise entitled to legal protection.” [Citation.] Injunctive relief and damages are available for common law unfair competition involving fraud or an intent to mislead consumers. [Citations.]

 

“The purpose of the equitable doctrine is to prevent unfair competition through misleading or deceptive use of a term exclusively identified with the claimant's product and business [citation], affording judicial protection whenever ‘the name and the business [through continued association] become synonymous in the public mind; and submerges the primary meaning of the name ... in favor of its meaning as a word identifying that business.’ [Citation.] The crucial element is the mental association in the buyer's mind between the mark used in connection with the product and a single source of origin.” [Citations.]

 

Under the doctrine, even a term or mark with a common meaning may trigger the crucial mental association when it acquires a “secondary meaning,” that is, becomes identified in “the relevant marketplace” with a product from “a unique or particular source.” [Citation.] The development of that secondary meaning does not require “that the buyer must actually know the name of the source[,] but only that the buyer directly associate the mark with but one, though, anonymous, source.” [Citation.] Thus, “ ‘secondary meaning is a shorthand phrase which describes the existence of conditions from which public confusion will flow if the defendant is permitted to pursue his deceptive scheme [citation]. If words have been used or employed... in such a manner that the public has learned to associate them with the thing described, they acquire a secondary meaning [citation].... If an association is thereby formed in the minds of the public which fixes plaintiffs as the source of something of a particular nature to be available in a particular place, this is sufficient....’ ” ([Citations.]

 

(Los Defensores, Inc. v. Gomez (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 377, 393–394.)

 

Plaintiff’s claim for unfair competition arises from Defendant’s alleged passing off of its own jean styles as Plaintiff’s (FAC ¶¶ 36–37.) Plaintiff’s motion focuses on Defendant’s conduct in using Plaintiff’s “style numbers” on its jeans. (Motion at pp. 8–10.) These style numbers are alphanumeric codes — e.g. P88, P453, P1691, etc. (Jin Ser Park (“JSP”) Decl. ¶ 3)  — which per the declaration of Plaintiff’s CEO “appeared on product hang tags, sewn-in garment labels, packaging, invoices, B2B order forms, e-commerce listings, trade show displays, and throughout Vibrant's consumer-facing and retailer-facing marketing materials.” (JSP Decl. ¶ 4.) Plaintiff began using these marks with the launch of its P88 jeans in 2012. (JSP Decl. ¶ 3.)

 

Plaintiff sold these jeans to multiple retailers, but from 2016 through 2019, sales to Defendant, a popular online fashion vendor, accounted for the majority of Plaintiff’s output. (JSP Decl. ¶ 14.) Plaintiff contends that in 2022 and 2023, it became aware that Defendant was using the same style numbers on its jeans. (JSP ¶ 16.) Plaintiff argues that summary adjudication is warranted because its style numbers had acquired secondary meaning by the time Defendant began to use them, and that consumer confusion has resulted. (Motion at p. 9.)

 

Triable issues exist as to whether Plaintiff’s style numbers acquired a secondary meaning such that a claim for unfair competition may lie. Whether secondary meaning has been established in an unfair competition case is a question of fact. (Family Record Plan, Inc. v. Mitchell (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 235, 243.)

 

We assess many factors to determine whether secondary meaning exists, including: “direct consumer testimony; survey evidence; exclusivity, manner, and length of use of a mark; amount and manner of advertising; amount of sales and number of customers; established place in the market; and proof of intentional copying by the defendant.” [Citation.] “Because of the intensely factual nature” of the secondary meaning inquiry, “summary judgment is generally disfavored

 

(P and P Imports LLC v. Johnson Enterprises, LLC (9th Cir. 2022) 46 F.4th 953, 961.)[1]

 

Plaintiff argues that Defendant has admitted to intentional copying of the style numbers. (Motion at p. 14.) Defendant indeed admitted in response to Request for Admission No. 28 that it “did not design or develop the Contested Style Numbers independently but adopted them from [Plaintiff] VIBRANT.” (Daniel Park Decl. Exh. B at p. 32.) “[P]roof of copying strongly supports an inference of secondary meaning.” [Citation.] That is because competitors generally copy “to realize upon a secondary meaning that is in existence.” (P and P Imports LLC v. Johnson Enterprises, LLC (9th Cir. 2022) 46 F.4th 953, 961.)

 

But the admission of intentional copying does not require a finding of secondary, meaning, create a presumption, or otherwise shift the burden of proof: “Competitors may intentionally copy product features for a variety of reasons. They may, for example, choose to copy wholly functional features that they perceive as lacking any secondary meaning because of those features' intrinsic economic benefits.” (Fuddruckers, Inc. v. Doc's B.R. Others, Inc. (9th Cir. 1987) 826 F.2d 837, 844–845.)

 

Here, Defendant presents evidence that may ultimately vitiate any inference of secondary meaning derived from intentional copying. Defendant’s General Manager of Buying for Women Fashion states in her declaration that the style numbers are not displayed on its website, but are used for tracking product orders internally. (Duran Decl. ¶ 11) The style numbers appear in purchase orders to vendors, in the Shopify point of sale system to track customer orders, and in the file names for product photos — not in consumer-facing elements of Defendant’s website. (Duran Decl. ¶ 11.) Defendant cites its influencer advertising campaigns, in which celebrities were photographed in the jeans in question, and which did not include the style numbers at issue. (Shalamitski Decl. Exh. 39.) This evidence could reasonably support an inference that Defendant’s copying of the style numbers was for its own internal convenience,  based on its prior use of the same style numbers in its transactions with Plaintiff, and not intended to trade on Plaintiff’s goodwill.

 

Plaintiff’s other evidence is not uniformly supportive of the existence of secondary meaning for its style numbers. For instance, Plaintiff’s advertising materials do not evidence consistent use of the style numbers. Plaintiff presents wholesale promotional flyers which offer jeans according to style numbers that are different from those claimed in the present action — for instance, instead of P88, they offer “P88DS,” “P88MS,” or “EP88.” (JSP Decl. Exh. C.)  The style numbers sometimes appear as they are pleaded (See JSP Decl. Exh. C at P_013985, P_013993; Exh. J at 000474), but the usage is inconsistent. Nor can it be determined from the evidence whether those instances in which the style numbers are used as pleaded occurred in materials to consumers, such as would by products through Defendant’s site, or other vendors looking to purchase the goods wholesale.

 

Plaintiff argues for the existence of consumer confusion. (Motion at pp. 17–18.) But the court’s ability to assess the existence of this confusion is hampered by the confused manner in which the evidence is presented.  Plaintiff presents one exhibit consisting of customer reviews for an unspecified product in which customers state that the jeans are similar to Fashion Nova jeans. (JSP Decl. Exh. D.) Plaintiff places some reliance on a consumer review, again for an unspecified product, entitled, “BUYER BEWARE: you can get fakes.” (Motion at pp. 8, 9, 18.) But the review, in addition to referring to a product with Vibrant’s (i.e. Plaintiff’s) label, rather than Defendant’s, refers to an experience in which they received a product different than that advertised, not confusion between Defndant’s and Plaintiff’s products based on style number. (JSP Decl. Exh. E.) Plaintiff finally submits hundreds of pages of reviews to indicate misattribution of Defendant’s products to Plaintiff. (JSP Decl. ¶ , Exh. L.) But Plaintiff does not identify or highlight which of the hundreds of reviews it submits are intended to support this contention, and review of the unsorted, low-resolution screenshots presented does not reveal the confusion or misattribution that Plaintiff claims.

 

Accordingly, no summary adjudication is proper on Plaintiff’s first cause of action for unfair competition. As Plaintiff’s remaining claims for trademark and trade dress infringement likewise rely upon the existence of secondary meaning in the style numbers at issue (Motion at pp. 13–15), triable issues likewise prevent summary adjudication.

 

Plaintiff’s motion for summary adjudication is therefore DENIED in its entirety.

 

B.     Defendant’s Motion

 

The moving party bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact, and if he does so, the burden shifts to the opposing party to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850; accord Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)

 

Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto.  (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at 850.)  The plaintiff may not rely upon the mere allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists but, instead, shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto.  (Ibid.)  To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence.  (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

 

1.      Statute of Limitations / Laches

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s claims fail because they are barred by applicable statutes of limitations and the doctrine of laches. (Motion at pp. 15–19.) Plaintiff’s interference claims have a two-year limitations period (Knoell v. Petrovich (1999) 76 Cal.App.4th 164; Code Civ. Proc. § 339, subd. 1), and an action for common law unfair competition and infringement on trademark or trade dress is “no more than four years.” (Magic Kitchen LLC v. Good Things Internat., Ltd. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1144, 1157.)

 

Plaintiff’s FAC alleges that it uncovered Defendant’s wrongful conduct in 2022 when it uncovered Defendant’s wrongful conduct, i.e. that it “deliberately instructed manufacturers to exactly replicate Vibrant’s jeans,” and that it “sold these counterfeit jeans on its website, continuing to use images of models and celebrities donning Vibrant’s jeans for marketing.” (FAC ¶ 23.)

 

But Defendant presents to emails which it contends establishes Plaintiff’s constructive knowledge of the alleged wrongdoing as early as March 2016. The first email, dated March 19, 2026, is from a representative of Defendant to Sonny Yoon, an officer of Plaintiff. (Shalamitski Decl. Exh. 18.) In this email, Defendant’s representative complains of several orders arriving late, and states, “Due to the ongoing issues and lack of real response from Christian, I have brought on new denim suppliers to reduce the amount of orders being placed with Vibrant.” (Ibid.)

 

A second email, dated April 7, 2017, was sent by Sonny Yoon to Defendant, in response to an email complaining of a recent change in fabrics employed by Plaintiff. (Shalamitski Decl. Exh. 19.) The letter sent by Sonny Yoon in response — from “my boss,,” and confirmed by Plaintiff’s CEO to have been drafted by him (JSP Decl. ¶¶ 4–5) — described hardships faced by Plaintiff as a company manufacturing domestically, the impossibility of further price renegotiation, and stating as follows: “Also, I would like to ask you again as a favor to not send our product to other places to copy because if you do not like our products or it does not sell well for you anymore, then it’s much simpler to just not order from us anymore rather than tarnishing the good long-term relationship and image that you once had.” (Shalamitski Decl. Exh. 19.)

 

Defendant argues that these two emails establish that Plaintiff suspected wrongdoing by April 2017, more than four years before filing suit on November 27, 2023. Thus Defendant argues that the limitations period had run out by April 2021, and that the ensuing period accrued only delay for the purposes of the application of the laches defense. (Motion at pp. 16–17.)  

 

The discovery rule “postpones accrual of a cause of action until the plaintiff discovers, or has reason to discover, the cause of action.” (Fox v. Ethicon Endo-Surgery, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 797, 807.) “A plaintiff has reason to discover a cause of action when he or she “has reason at least to suspect a factual basis for its elements.” (Ibid.) “In so using the term “elements,” we do not take a hyper technical approach to the application of the discovery rule. Rather than examining whether the plaintiffs suspect facts supporting each specific legal element of a particular cause of action, we look to whether the plaintiffs have reason to at least suspect that a “ has injured them.” (Ibid.)

 

Triable issues of fact exist as to whether Plaintiff was on inquiry notice of the alleged wrongdoing of Defendant such that the statute of limitations or laches bars its claims. The only evidence indicating Plaintiff’s potential awareness of tis claims is that dated April 7, 2017. It does not purport to describe conduct that Defendant had thereto engaged in, indicate knowledge that such conduct had already occurred, and offers only a warning that Defendant — with whom Plaintiff had an ongoing, non-competitive relationship — ought not to turn that relationship into a competitive one.

 

Courts exercise caution when determining whether a trademark plaintiff ought to have known of their claims:

 

While either actual or constructive knowledge can satisfy the standard for knowledge of a potential cause of action, triggering accrual of the cause of action for statute of limitations purposes, trademark holders are not required to “constantly monitor every nook and cranny of the entire nation and to fire both barrels of [its] shotgun instantly upon spotting a possible infringer.” [Citations.] Moreover, discovery of a cause of action does not necessarily equate to discovery of a defendant's first unpermitted use of the contested mark, as Defendant seems to suggest in its Motion, because Lanham Act § 43(a) also involves other elements, such as likelihood of confusion, that must be satisfied before a claim can accrue. [Citations.] Thus, some authority suggests that, for trademark infringement claims, for example, “laches creates an obligation to object and sue only at the identifiable point in time a likelihood of confusion existed.” [Citations.]

 

(Luna Distributing LLC v. Stoli Group (USA), LLC (C.D. Cal., July 10, 2018, No. SACV171552DOCJDEX) 2018 WL 5099277, at *6.)

 

The scant nature of the evidence here, and the concerns discussed in the above authority, militate against any conclusion that Plaintiff’s claims are barred by the statute of limitations or the laches doctrine as a matter of law.

 

1.      Trademark Infringement

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff cannot succeed on its sixth cause of action for trademark infringement because the style numbers at issue had not acquired secondary meaning. (Motion at pp. 19–23.)

 

A trademark infringement plaintiff “must show that: (1) it has a valid, protectable trademark, and (2) that eBay's use of the mark is likely to cause confusion.” (Applied Information Sciences Corp. v. eBay Inc. (9th Cir. 2007)  511 F.3d 966, 969.) “ There are three ways to establish a protectable trademark: “(1) [the plaintiff] has a federally registered mark in goods or services; (2) its mark is descriptive but has acquired a secondary meaning in the market; or (3) it has a suggestive mark, which is inherently distinctive and protectable.” (Ibid.) Defendant argues that because Plaintiff lacks a federally registered mark, and because its alphanumeric style numbers are not inherently distinctive, it must show that its mark has acquired a secondary meaning, which it cannot do. (Motion at pp. 19–23.)

 

Factors considered in determining whether a secondary meaning has been achieved include: (1) whether actual purchase[r]s of the product bearing the claimed trademark associate the trademark with the producer, (2) the degree and manner of advertising under the claimed trademark, (3) the length and manner of use of the claimed trademark and, (4) whether use of the claimed trademark has been exclusive.

 

(Levi Strauss & Co. v. Blue Bell, Inc. (9th Cir. 1985) 778 F.2d 1352, 1358.)

 

Defendant argues that no secondary meaning has attached to the style numbers. It argues that the bulk of the advertising for Plaintiff’s products has been conducted by other entities, including Defendant, under other entities’ brands, without reference to the style numbers at issue. (Motion at pp. 20–21.) Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s own marketing materials, to the extent they referenced style numbers, referred to alphanumeric indicators different from those at issue in this case, and were in any event directed to vendors for the purpose of soliciting wholesale purchase of Plaintiff’s goods, not consumers. (Motion at p. 21.) Defendant argues that Plaintiff cannot claim exclusivity in its use of the style numbers because those style numbers most frequently appeared under other companies’ branding (such as Defendant’s), and other clothing and pants products now on the market also use the “P88” term in their branding. (Motion at p. 22.) Defendant finally argues that Plaintiff has not conducted a consumer survey to determine whether purchasers associate the style numbers at issue with a singular source. (Motion at pp. 22–23.)

 

The evidence presented by Defendant broadly supports its contentions in relation to the factors described above. But Defendant discounts the significance of the existence of intentional copying. ““[P]roof of copying strongly supports an inference of secondary meaning.” (P and P Imports LLC v. Johnson Enterprises, LLC (9th Cir. 2022) 46 F.4th 953, 961.) Defendant contends that it uses the style numbers only for internal purposes, such as tracking inventory and shipments. (Duran Decl. ¶ 11.)  But the style numbers are visible to consumers externally via the garment tags (Jun Decl. Exh. A), and Defendant acknowledges that the style numbers are used to “differentiate and organize products so that they are readily identifiable.” (Duran Decl. ¶ 11.)

 

Defendant argues that it copied the style numbers only for functional reasons. (Reply at p. 17.) The trier of fact may indeed conclude that the copying arose from motives other than to trade upon the numbers’ secondary meaning. But the argument raised by Defendant as to functionality is derived from cases addressing trade dress and product design, not the use of a trademark. (See Tempo Communications, Inc. v. King Technology of Missouri, LLC (S.D. Cal., July 19, 2021, No. 21-CV-80-AJB-WVG) 2021 WL 4935524, at *7.) And although Defendant raises a functional argument as to its use of style numbers in general, it raises no evidence or argument as to the functional benefits related to the use of Plaintiff’s style numbers, as opposed to another alphanumeric signifier.

 

The existence of secondary meaning is a question of fact. (Family Record Plan, Inc. v. Mitchell (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 235, 243.) “[S]ummary judgment is generally disfavored in the trademark arena” due to “the intensely factual nature of trademark disputes.” (Marketquest Group, Inc. v. BIC Corp. (9th Cir. 2017) 862 F.3d 927, 932.)

 

Accordingly, the motion is DENIED as to the sixth cause of action for trademark infringement.

 

2.      Trade Dress Infringement

 

“To sustain a claim for trade dress infringement, LNB must prove: “(1) that its claimed dress is nonfunctional; (2) that its claimed dress serves a source-identifying role either because it is inherently distinctive or has acquired secondary meaning; and (3) that the defendant's product or service creates a likelihood of consumer confusion.” (Nitro NRG Corporation v. Liquid Nitro Beverages, Inc. (C.D. Cal., Jan. 17, 2017, No. EDCV162315JGBDTBX) 2017 WL 2964802, at *3.)

 

Defendant here argues that Plaintiff’s claimed trade dress infringement is too generic and overbroad to form any basis for a trade dress claim. (Motion at pp. 24–25.) “Courts exercise “particular caution” when extending protection to product designs because such claims present an acute risk of stifling competition. [Citation.] “While most trademarks only create a monopoly in a word, a phrase or a symbol, granting trade dress protection to an ordinary product design  create[s] a monopoly in the goods themselves.” (Walker & Zanger, Inc. v. Paragon Industries, Inc. (N.D. Cal. 2007) 549 F.Supp.2d 1168, 1174.) “[G]eneric product designs are unprotectible even upon a showing of secondary meaning.”” (Ibid.) “Cases addressing product design suggest that the term “genericness” covers three situations: (1) if the definition of a product design is overbroad or too generalized; (2) if a product design is the basic form of a type of product; or (3) if the product design is so common in the industry that it cannot be said to identify a particular.” (Ibid.)

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s identified protected product designs for its jeans are too overbroad to be protected. (Motion at pp. 24–25.) The following are examples of Plaintiff’s claimed protected trade dress elements, proffered in interrogatory responses:

 

·         P88 and P453: “ultra-high-rise waistband, flat faux front pockets, sculpted curved back pockets, narrow double- stitching, rayon-rich fabric blend, rear yoke configuration, and sleek ankle taper”;

·         P522: “distinctive flare silhouette with specific measurements, high-rise waistband, unique side seam stitching pattern, and specific fabric wash creating the overall aesthetic”;

·         P633: “mid-rise waist-band positioned at specific measurements, skinny taper configuration, unique stitching pattern, and specific fabric wash”;

·         P711: “asymmetric distressing configuration, strategically placed rips, singular frayed patch on upper thigh, varied slash sizes and shapes, clean surface allowing distressing to serve as visual centerpiece.”

 

(See Shalamitski Decl. Exh. 32.)

 

Defendant analogizes the present case to that of Walker & Zanger, Inc. v. Paragon Industries, Inc. (N.D. Cal. 2007) 549 F.Supp.2d 1168, 1175, in which the court granted summary judgment on a trade dress claim after finding that certain trade dress descriptions, both pleaded and set forth in discovery, to be too overbroad and generic to support a claim for trade dress infringement:

 

The court first finds that some of the elements of plaintiff's proposed trade dress are overbroad. The court acknowledges that trade dress of “decorative or artistic” works may be harder to capture in words, and “may need descriptions more broadly framed.” Yurman, 262 F.3d at 115. But for some of the trade dress elements, plaintiff resorts to empty generalities in the face of more precise alternatives. For instance, to describe the colors of the trade dress, plaintiff should list the actual colors rather than claim a “palette of colors reminiscent of Provence.” Or instead of defining the three-dimensional relief as “complex,” plaintiff should provide the magnitude and angle of relief that render plaintiff's tiles distinctive. Finally, some terms leave the boundary of plaintiff's trade dress rights unclear, such as the terms “rustic look,” “weathered look,” “architectural character” or even “Old World handiwork.” These terms fail to provide adequate notice to competitors in the decorative tile business regarding the breadth of plaintiff's exclusivity rights.

(Walker & Zanger, Inc. v. Paragon Industries, Inc. (N.D. Cal. 2007) 549 F.Supp.2d 1168, 1176.)

 

Defendant is correct that the descriptions of Plaintiff’s proposed trade dress are like those set forth in Walker. Plaintiff’s characterizations do not describe the proposed trade dress in such a manner that gives notice to its competitors as to the bounds of its claimed protections. Instead of identifying the specific design elements that make Plaintiff’s trade dress unique, Plaintiff continually resorts to mere to a list of nouns, each preceded by the adjective like “distinctive,” “specific,” and “unique.” These descriptions, which ought to demonstrate or at least describe the features that render the products at issue unique, instead merely insist upon uniqueness without explanation. They thus do not provide notice to competitors about the bounds of Plaintiff’s proposed trade dress.

 

Defendant further argues that Plaintiff’s proposed trade dresses are generic, and merely articulate styles of jean already on the market, and thus no secondary meaning can be said to attach to Plaintiff’s designs. (Motion at pp. 24–27.) Plaintiff in opposition objects that Defendant’s argument “conflates the existence of a broad product category with the distinctiveness of Vibrant's specific combination of elements across 17 distinct styles over more than a decade.” (Opposition at pp. 29–30.) But Defendant approaches the issue on the terrain shaped by Plaintiff and its definitions of their proposed trade dresses and their overbroad formulations. Plaintiff cannot fairly contend that the motion does not adequately account for the unique combination of features that constitute its trade dress, while simultaneously refusing to describe what those precise features are.

 

As in Walker & Zanger, the appropriate remedy is to grant summary adjudication on the trade dress claim, on the grounds that Plaintiff’s proposed trade dress definitions are too broad to support relief. (Walker & Zanger, Inc., supra, 549 F.Supp.2d at p. 1177.)

 

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED as to the seventh cause of action for trade dress infringement.

 

3.      Interference with Contract

 

“To prevail on a cause of action for intentional interference with contractual relations, a plaintiff must plead and prove (1) the existence of a valid contract between the plaintiff and a third party; (2) the defendant's knowledge of that contract; (3) the defendant's intentional acts designed to induce a breach or disruption of the contractual relationship; (4) actual breach or disruption of the contractual relationship; and (5) resulting damage.” (Reeves v. Hanlon (2004) 33 Cal.4th 1140, 1148.) “Plaintiff need not allege an actual or inevitable breach of contract in order to state a claim for disruption of contractual relations. We have recognized that interference with the plaintiff's performance may give rise to a claim for interference with contractual relations if plaintiff's performance is made more costly or more burdensome.” (Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. Bear Stearns & Co. (1990) 50 Cal.3d 1118, 1129.)

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff cannot establish the elements of its contractual interference claim because it has presented no contractual relationship that was breached or disrupted, and because there is no evidence that Defendant knew of or intentionally acted to disrupt any such contract. (Motion at p. 28.)

 

Defendant presents evidence that Plaintiff, in response to requests for documents evidencing contracts that it claimed had been disrupted, did not present evidence of any contracts that had not been fully performed. (Shalamitski Decl. ¶ 12.) Plaintiff in opposition argues that triable issues exist as to whether Defendant disrupted useful economic relationships with other manufacturers. (Opposition at p. 31.)

 

No triable issues exist as to Plaintiff’s contractual interference claims because Plaintiff has no evidence that any contractual relationship or obligation was disrupted, breached, or made more burdensome to perform. Asked in interrogatories to describe the contractual relations at issue in this claim, Plaintiff described “purchase arrangements and business relationships” in general terms, with the only specific relationships identified being “recurring purchase orders” with entities called E.8 Inc. and Twin Dragon. (Daniel Park Decl. Exh. 6, SROG No. 82.)  These are the same entities and relationships that Plaintiff identifies in its own motion for summary adjudication. (JSP (MSA) Decl. ¶ 19.) Plaintiff thus identifies no contractual interference suffered save the failure to continue a relationship built on recurring purchase orders. No contract disruption is identified.

 

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED as to the third cause of action for interference with contract.

 

4.      Interference with Economic Advantage

 

“Intentional interference with prospective economic advantage has five elements: (1) the existence, between the plaintiff and some third party, of an economic relationship that contains the probability of future economic benefit to the plaintiff; (2) the defendant's knowledge of the relationship; (3) intentionally wrongful acts designed to disrupt the relationship; (4) actual disruption of the relationship; and (5) economic harm proximately caused by the defendant's action.” (Roy Allan Slurry Seal, Inc. v. American Asphalt South, Inc. (2017) 2 Cal.5th 505, 512.)

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s claim for intentional interference with economic advantage cannot succeed because it had no continuing economic relationships to which the tort could be subject, and Defendant has committed no “wrongful acts” upon which an intentional interference claim may lie. (Motion at pp. 29–30.)

 

These arguments are unpersuasive. Defendant cites Plaintiff’s discovery responses as not identifying any economic relationship, but such relationships were in fact identified in the discovery responses. (Daniel Park Decl. Exh. 6, SROG No. 82.) Sheri Duran testifies that Defendant did not know of these relationships (Duran Decl. ¶ 31), but Plaintiff’s CEO testifies that he was personally involved in interactions through which Defendant would have learned of Plaintiff’s relationships. (JSP (MSA) Decl. ¶ 20.)  Plaintiff also presents evidence that one of these manufacturers — E.8 — ceased doing business with Plaintiff and commenced doing business with Defendant under an altered corporate form with the same officers from the same addresses, to supply materials for the very jean styles that Plaintiff alleges were copied from Plaintiff. (Jun Decl. ¶¶ 2–5.) Triable issues exist as to the elements of Plaintiff’s economic interference claim.

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff cannot establish a duty of care for the purposes of Plaintiff’s negligent interference claim, because competitors do not owe one another a duty of care. (See Stolz v. Wong Communications Limited Partnership (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 1811, 1825 [“The complaint did not allege such a duty, nor could it, since it was plain that plaintiff and defendants were competitors.”].)  But it is not so plain that Plaintiff and Defendant were competitors at the time the alleged tort occurred. Defendant’s evidence makes clear that it is a popular retail clothing vendor, while Plaintiff manufactures clothes principally for wholesale, and indeed that Defendant’s purchases constituted the majority of Plaintiff’s business. (PUMF No. 45.)

 

Accordingly, the motion is DENIED as to the second and fourth causes of action for intentional and negligent interference with economic advantage.  Defendant’s argument as to the first cause of action for unfair competition is that it is purely derivative of the other claims, and as such the motion is DENIED as to that claim as well.

 

In summary, Defendant’s motion is GRANTED as to the third cause of action for intentional interference with contract and seventh cause of action for trade dress infringement, and is otherwise DENIED.

Analysis

 

I.                    MOTION TO VACATE DISMISSAL

“The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).) Such an application must include “a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein,” and must be made within a “reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).) “The test of whether neglect was excusable is whether “ ‘a reasonably prudent person under the same or similar circumstances' might have made the same error.” (Luri v. Greenwald (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 1119, 1128.)

 

Plaintiff Majid Ghamami (“Plaintiff”) seeks to vacate the court’s order of August 25, 2025, finding in favor of Defendants F. Bari Nejadpour and Law Offices of LA Law, Inc. (“Defendants”) and dismissing Plaintiff’s claims. Plaintiff brings the motion on the grounds that he was in pro per during trial, and was deprived of the opportunity to obtain counsel because the court denied Plaintiff’s request to continue trial. (Motion at pp. 9–10.)

 

A denial of a request for a continuance constitutes an abuse of discretion where the ruling is arbitrary, capricious, and contrary to the interests of justice under all the circumstances. [Citation] It cannot be held that litigants in all cases may demand a continuance by engaging counsel just prior to a trial date, where there is no showing of any necessity for any change of counsel, but a necessary substitution of counsel just prior to trial may justify the granting of a continuance, in some cases.

 

(Vann v. Shilleh (1975) 54 Cal.App.3d 192, 196.)

 

Plaintiff makes no showing of entitlement to a continuance as would justify vacating the August 25, 2025 order, and a narrative of the proceedings confirms that no such continuance was warranted.  

 

The parties appeared on August 11, 2025, for the final status conference, with Plaintiff represented by Ron Tavakoli, and trial was confirmed for August 25, 2025. (See 8/11/2025 Order.) Then, on August 14, 2025, Plaintiff (through counsel) filed an ex parte application for continuance of trial. The reason for the continuance was explained in the application: “On August 13, 2025 Plaintiff terminated the representation of Ron Tavakoli and is searching for new counsel.” (8/14/2025 Ex Parte Application at p. 2, Tavakoli Decl. ¶ 3.) No justification for the change was provided. The minute order of August 18, 2025, indicates that the court denied the application for a continuance and that Plaintiff understood that he was now representing himself. (See 8/18/2025 Order.) The parties proceeded to trial, and toward a conclusion that was adverse to Plaintiff, as reflected in the August 25, 2025 order.

 

Plaintiff’s present motion offers scant basis for relief. His prior application for continuance at least informed the court of the basis for the request:  Plaintiff wanted a new attorney, although he did not offer a reason why. At the hearing on the application, Plaintiff was informed that the trial would not be continued, and that if he proceeded to terminate his counsel, he would have to proceed in pro per. Plaintiff elected this latter option.

 

In the present motion, Plaintiff offers no argument as to the necessity for a change in counsel or why the dismissal of his counsel was necessary, but instead devotes the motion largely to recapitulation of his substantive claims. This is insufficient. “[A] necessary substitution of counsel just prior to trial may justify the granting of a continuance, in some cases,” but no relief is warranted when “there is no showing of any necessity for any change of counsel.” (Vann,  supra, 54 Cal.App.3d at p. 196.) No showing is made here, and no relief is therefore appropriate.

 

The motion is therefore DENIED.

Young Yi Lim v. Tailor Gibbons, et al.

 

Wednesday, September 23, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 26STCV12349

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Defendant Okoa Capital, LLC’s Demurrer to the Complaint ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is a derivative action brought on behalf of a California religious corporation, nominal Defendant Oriental Mission Church (“OMC”), by Plaintiff Young Yi Lim (“Plaintiff”), a member thereof. (Complaint ¶ 11.) The Complaint alleges as follows. CMC entered into an agreement with Defendants Tailor Gibbons, Freestar, Inc., and Made Solar, LLC (“Solar Defendants”) to install solar panels on certain OMC property. (Complaint ¶¶ 27–43.) Solar Defendants procured this agreement by fraudulent representations as to their prior clients and as to their intention to proceed with the project, which they did not intend to proceed with following payment. (Complaint ¶¶ 29–34.) Solar Defendants directed OMC to Defendant Okoa Capital, LLC (“Okoa”), to be the financier of the project, and OMC and Okoa entered into an agreement for a loan of $1,150,000.00, to be secured by substantially all of OMC’s real property, which in valuation vastly exceeded the loan amount. (Complaint ¶¶ 44–53.) The loan agreement is invalid because the encumbrance of all of OMC’s assets was done without member approval as required by its bylaws, nor were the documents signed by the requisite number of church officers. (Complaint ¶¶ 54–68.)

Okoa paid the loan amount to OMC, OMC paid Solar Defendants, but Solar Defendants performed no work on the project. (Complaint ¶¶ 84–89.) OMC failed to make payments on the loan, Okoa declared a default, initiated foreclosure on OMC’s properties, and OMC paid Okoa $1.7 million to have its property reconveyed. (Complaint ¶¶ 90–97.) 

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on April 14, 2026, alleging 12 causes of action:

1.      Fraud

2.      Negligent Misrepresentation

3.      Breach of Contract (solar Agreement)

4.      Breach of Contract (Loan Payment Waiver)

5.      Breach of Contract (Memorandum of Reconveyance)

6.      Conversion

7.      Rescission

8.      Money Had and Received

9.      Money Had and Received (Okoa)

10.  Restitution and Unjust Enrichment

11.  Restitution and Unjust Enrichment (Okoa)

12.  Failure to Reconvey

Defendant Okoa filed the present demurrer on May 22, 2026.

Plaintiff filed an opposition on September 10, 2026.

Defendant filed a reply on September 16, 2026.

Analysis:

I.                   DEMURRER

A demurrer should be sustained only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (Code Civ. Pro., §§ 430.30, et seq.) In particular, as is relevant here, a court should sustain a demurrer if a complaint does not allege facts that are legally sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (See id. § 430.10, subd. (e).) As the Supreme Court held in Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311: “We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law. . . . Further, we give the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Id. at p. 318; see also Hahn. v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747 [“A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. [Citation.]”)

 

“In determining whether the complaint is sufficient as against the demurrer … if on consideration of all the facts stated it appears the plaintiff is entitled to any relief at the hands of the court against the defendants the complaint will be held good although the facts may not be clearly stated.”  (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 639.)

 

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of Cal., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Such demurrers “are disfavored, and are granted only if the pleading is so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (Mahan v. Charles W. Chan Insurance Agency, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 841, 848.)

 

A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment. (Schifando v. City of Los Angeles, supra, 31 Cal.4th at p. 1081.) The demurrer also may be sustained without leave to amend where the nature of the defects and previous unsuccessful attempts to plead render it probable plaintiff cannot state a cause of action. (Krawitz v. Rusch (1989) 209 Cal.App.3d 957, 967.)

 

Defendant Okoa Capital, LLC (“Okoa”) demurrers to each claim asserted against it by Plaintiff Young Yi Lim, derivatively on behalf of nominal defendant Oriental Mission Church, a religious corporation (“OMC”), on the following grounds. First, Okoa argues that Plaintiff lacks standing to bring a derivative claim against a third party for wrongs done to OMC, because individual members of a religious corporation can only bring claims against the corporation’s officers or directors under Corporations Code § 9141. (Demurrer at pp. 5–6.) Okoa next argues that the claims for rescission and unjust enrichment are not actual causes of action. (Demurrer at pp. 6–9.) Okoa finally argues that the claims for rescission, money had and received, and unjust enrichment fail because Okoa only received repayment of a loan obligation with OMC. (Ibid.)

 

Defendant argues persuasively that Plaintiff lacks standing to bring the present action against it. The action against Okoa is for entering into a loan agreement with OMC which Plaintiff contends was not authorized per OMC’s bylaws and governing statutes. (Complaint ¶¶ 54–74.)  Plaintiff’s claim is thus one based on the “limitation[s] upon the activities, purposes, or powers of the corporation or upon the powers of the members, officers, or directors, or the manner of exercise of such powers,” under Corporations Code § 9141, subd. (a), applicable to religious corporations.

 

Section 9141 states that no action based upon such limitations “shall be asserted as between the corporation or member, officer or director and any third person,” except in specified circumstances. (Corp. Code § 9141, subd. (a).) The two exceptions are a proceeding:

 

(1) by the authorized number of members (Section 5036), by any person authorized by the articles or bylaws to bring an action, or by the state to enjoin the doing or continuation of unauthorized activities by the corporation or its officers, or both, in cases where third parties have not acquired rights thereby, or

 

(2) by the authorized number of members (Section 5036), by any person authorized by the articles or bylaws to bring an action, by any member suing in a representative suit, or by the corporation, against the officers or directors of the corporation for violation of their authority.

 

(Corp. Code § 9141, subd. (a)(1), (2).) The only exception in which “any member” is authorized to bring a representative action on behalf of the religious corporation is a suit “against the officers or directors of the corporation for violation of their authority.” (Corp. Code § 9141, subd. 9a).) Plaintiff thus cannot sue Okoa, a third party, on behalf of OMC, under this statute.

 

Plaintiff in opposition argues that he brings the action under Corporations Code § 9142, not section 9141. (Opposition at pp. 3–4.) That statute states, “Notwithstanding Section 9141, any of the following may bring an action to enjoin, correct, obtain damages for or to otherwise remedy a breach of a trust under which any or all of the assets of a corporation are held,” and goes on to list among the authorized persons “[t]he corporation, a member, or a former member asserting the right in the name of the corporation, provided that for the purpose of this paragraph the provisions of Section 5710 shall apply to such action.” (Corp. Code § 9142, subd. (a)(1).) Plaintiff thus argues that he brings claims  “relating to a breach of trust over the majority of the church’s assets,” and thus has standing under section 9142. (Opposition at p. 4.) The Complaint briefly alleges that Plaintiff brings these claims as a member of OMC under this statute. (Complaint ¶ 15.)

 

Plaintiff’s argument is not persuasive. Section 9142 authorizes an action to remedy “a breach of a trust under which any or all of the assets of a corporation are held.” (Corp. Code § 9142, subd. (a).) The statute goes on to define precisely what is meant by “trust”:

 

(c) No assets of a religious corporation are or shall be deemed to be impressed with any trust, express or implied, statutory or at common law unless one of the following applies:

 

(1) Unless, and only to the extent that, the assets were received by the corporation with an express commitment by resolution of its board of directors to so hold those assets in trust.

 

(2) Unless, and only to the extent that, the articles or bylaws of the corporation, or the governing instruments of a superior religious body or general church of which the corporation is a member, so expressly provide.

 

(3) Unless, and only to the extent that, the donor expressly imposed a trust, in writing, at the time of the gift or donation.

 

(Corp. Code § 9152, subd. (c)(1)–(3).)

 

In bringing an action under Section 9142, it is not enough merely to allege that the action is to remedy a breach of trust; the plaintiff must allege facts to establish the existence of a trust under the above provisions. This much was held by the court in Wimber v. Scott (2025) 113 Cal.App.5th 349, in which the court affirmed the sustaining of a demurrer to representative claims brough on behalf of a religious corporation under the above statute:

 

11Here, Appellants alleged they “donated substantial monetary funds to [Dwelling Place], in addition to their tithes, ... with the belief that the funds were being used to support, and further the mission of the Vineyard Movement and Vineyard USA.” Those allegations do not establish the existence of a trust within the contours of section 9142. In their reply brief, Appellants admit as much, but claim they are “prepared to plead they had written communication and allege on information and belief that others had written communication with [Dwelling Place's] leadership outlining the purpose of their donations.” These proposed amendments are still insufficient to establish the existence of a trust within the meaning of section 9142. Rather, Appellants must be prepared to allege: (1) at the time of their gifts or donations; (2) they “expressly imposed a trust”; and (3) this expression of trust was “in writing.” (§ 9142, subd. (c)(1)–(3).) Their proposed amendments do not satisfy these requirements.

 

(Wimber v. Scott (2025) 113 Cal.App.5th 349, 361–362.) Under this authority, Plaintiff must make allegations sufficient “to establish the existence of a trust.” Plaintiff has not done so here.

 

The demurrer is therefore SUSSTAINED with leave to amend.

Defendant HRB Digital LLC’s Motion for Reconsideration of July 28, 2026 Order Denying Peremptory Challenge.

___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for invasion of privacy. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff Dallas James (“Plaintiff”) used Defendant HRB Digital, LLC’s website, and despite opting out of tracking technologies, was subjected to spyware and tracking software on his browsing activity. (Complaint ¶¶ 7–12.)   

 

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on June 18, 2026, alleging one cause of action for invasion of privacy.

On July 28, 2026, this court denied Defendant’s 170.6 peremptory challenge as untimely.

Defendant filed the present reconsideration motion on August 14, 2026.

Analysis:

I.       MOTION FOR RECONSIDERATION

Code Civ. Proc. § 1008 is the exclusive means for seeking reconsideration of an order or renewing a motion.  (Kerns v. CSE Ins. Group (2003) 106 Cal.App.4th 368, 384.)  The application to reconsider the matter and modify, amend, or revoke the prior order must be made within 10 days after service upon the party of written notice of entry of the order to the same judge or court that made the order.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 1008(a).) A motion for reconsideration may only be brought if the party moving for reconsideration can offer “new or different facts, circumstances, or law” which it could not, with reasonable diligence, have discovered and produced at the time of the prior motion.  (Id.) There is a strict requirement of diligence - i.e., the moving party must present a satisfactory explanation for failing to provide the evidence or different facts earlier.  (Garcia v. Hejmadi (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 674, 690.) “The burden under section 1008 is comparable to that of a party seeking a new trial on the ground of newly discovered evidence: the information must be such that the moving party could not, with reasonable diligence, have discovered or produced it at the trial.” (New York Times Co. v. Superior Court (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 206, 212–13.)

Defendant HRB Digital, LLC (“Defendant”) seeks reconsideration of this court’s order of July 28, 2026, denying Defendant’s peremptory challenge under Code of Civil procedure § 170.6. The court in its order denied the motion as untimely.

Defendant here argues that the motion was only untimely under the first prong of Code of Civil Procedure § 170.6, timeliness provisions for civil cases. (Motion at p. 9.)

 

If directed to the trial of a civil cause that has been assigned to a judge for all purposes, the motion shall be made to the assigned judge or to the presiding judge by a party within 15 days after notice of the all purpose assignment, or if the party has not yet appeared in the action, then within 15 days after the appearance.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 170.6, subd. (a)(2).)

 

The court in its July 28, 2026 order denied Defendant’s motion as untimely: “Defendant had 15 days from the notice of assignment and had received that notice on 6/30/26 when the complaint was served along with the notice of assignment.” (7/28/2026 Order.) But Defendant had not appeared in this case at the time of the notice of case assignment. While the notice of case assignment was filed on June 18, 2026, Defndant did not appear in this action until it sought an extension of time to file a demurrer on July 24, 2026. Thus Defendant argues that its 170.6 motion was timely because, “[I]f the party has not yet appeared in the action, then within 15 days after the appearance.” (Motion at p. 10.)

 

Defendant’s argument is persuasive. It had not appeared in this matter at the time of the notice of case assignment, and its peremptory challenge was filed within 15 days of the appearance on July 24, 2026.

 

The motion is therefore GRANTED.
