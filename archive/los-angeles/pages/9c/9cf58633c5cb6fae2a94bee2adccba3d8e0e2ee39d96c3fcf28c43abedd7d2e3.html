[TENTATIVE] RULING ON PETITION FOR WRIT OF MANDATE

 

Petitioner Luigi Girotto (“Petitioner”) petitions for a writ of administrative mandate against Respondent Department of Motor Vehicles (“Respondent”) directing it to set aside its decision to suspend his driver license.

The petition for writ of mandate is denied.

I.                Factual Background

On April 8, 2025, Petitioner suffered an episodic lapse of consciousness (“Episode”), and the treating physician submitted a Confidential Morbidity Report (“CMR”) to Respondent on April 9, 2025 in accordance with Health & Safety Code section 103900. (AR 8.)[1] Another physician at the same facility also submitted a CMR. (AR 9.) Both listed the date of onset and date of occurrence as April 8, 2025. (AR 8, 9.)

Respondent directed Petitioner to have his doctor fill out certain medical forms. (AR 10-14.) Respondent’s family physician (Dr. Eshom) prepared and submitted the Driver Medical Evaluation form (“DME”) on May 7, 2025. (AR 10-14.) Per the DME that Dr. Eshom filled out and submitted, Petitioner had the Episode on April 22, 2025 (AR 13) and was prescribed medications that the physician indicated have side effects which would interfere with Petitioner’s ability to drive safely. (AR 11.) Where directed to explain, the physician wrote “unknown.” (AR 11.) The form directs the physician to state whether Petitioner’s condition affects safe driving. (AR 11.) The physician indicated “Yes,” but beside this indication, where the DME directs the physician to provide further explanation for a “yes” answer, Dr. Eshom wrote only, “unknown.” Dr. Eshom indicated “uncertain” as to whether Petitioner experienced each of sporadic loss of awareness, loss of consciousness, and impaired motor function. (AR 13.)

On May 12, 2025, Respondent reviewed the information and determined Petitioner presented a risk to traffic safety. (AR 6-7.) The next day, Respondent provided Petitioner a notice of suspension. (AR 61-62.) Petitioner requested a hearing, and on May 28, 2025, Driving Safety Hearing Officer Lauren Medina (“Hearing Officer”) heard the matter. (AR 25-58.)

On May 28, 2025, the Hearing Officer issued a Notification of Findings And Decision (“Decision”) (AR 3-5) concluding that due to Petitioner’s medical condition and ongoing medical investigation into the cause of the Episode, Petitioner’s driver license must be suspended for additional time. (AR 4.)

II.             Procedural History

On October 10, 2025, Petitioner filed his petition. On January 15, 2026, Respondent filed its answer to the petition.

On April 28, 2026, Petitioner filed his opening brief. On June 17, 2026, Respondent filed its opposition. On July 23, 2026, Petitioner filed his reply.

III.           Standard of Review

Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 1094.5 is the administrative mandamus provision that structures the procedure for judicial review of adjudicatory decisions rendered by administrative agencies. (Topanga Ass’n for a Scenic Community v. County of Los Angeles (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506, 514-15) [“Topanga”].) Section 1094.5 does not specify which cases are subject to independent review, leaving that issue to the courts. (Fukuda v. City of Angels (1999) 20 Cal.4th 805, 811 [“Fukuda”].) In cases reviewing decisions that affect a vested, fundamental right, the trial court exercises independent judgment on the evidence. (Bixby v. Pierno (1971) 4 Cal.3d 130, 143 [“Bixby”]; see CCP § 1094.5(c).)

While driving is a privilege, not a right, suspension or revocation of a driver license is a fundamental right for purposes of judicial review and requires application of the independent judgment test. (Berlinghieri v. Department of Motor Vehicles (1983) 33 Cal.3d 392, 398.) Under the independent judgment test, “the trial court not only examines the administrative record for errors of law but also exercises its independent judgment upon the evidence disclosed in a limited trial de novo.” (Id. at 395.) The court must draw its own reasonable inferences from the evidence and make its own credibility determinations. (Morrison v. Housing Authority of the City of Los Angeles Board of Commissioners (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 860, 868 [“Morrison”].) In short, the court substitutes its judgment for the agency’s regarding the basic facts of what happened, when and why, and the credibility of witnesses. (Guymon v. Board of Accountancy (1976) 55 Cal.App.3d 1010, 1013-16 [“Guymon”].)

“In exercising its independent judgment, a trial court must afford a strong presumption of correctness concerning the administrative findings, and the party challenging the administrative decision bears the burden of convincing the court that the administrative findings are contrary to the weight of the evidence.” (Fukuda, supra, 20 Cal.4th at 817.) Unless a petitioner can demonstrate that the agency’s actions are not grounded upon any reasonable basis in law or any substantial basis in fact, the courts should not interfere with the agency’s discretion or substitute their wisdom for that of the agency. (Bixby, supra, 4 Cal.3d at 150-151; Bank of America v. State Water Resources Control Board (1974) 42 Cal.App.3d 198, 208.)

Where the issue is whether a fair administrative hearing was conducted, a petitioner is entitled to an independent judicial determination of the issue. (Sinaiko v. Superior Court (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 1133, 1141; Pomona Valley Hospital Medical Center v. Superior Court (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 93, 101.) Therefore, the court must independently review the fairness of the administrative proceedings as a legal issue. (Rosenblit v. Superior Court, (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1434, 1438.)

The agency’s decision must be based on a preponderance of the evidence presented at the hearing. (Board of Medical Quality Assurance v. Superior Court, (1977) 73 Cal.App.3d 860, 862.) The hearing officer is only required to issue findings that give enough explanation so that parties may determine whether, and upon what basis, to review the decision. (Topanga, supra, 11 Cal.3d at 514-15.) Implicit in CCP section 1094.5 is a requirement that the agency set forth findings to bridge the analytic gap between the raw evidence and ultimate decision or order. (Id. at 515.)

An agency is presumed to have regularly performed its official duties (Evid. Code § 664), and the petitioner therefore has the burden of proof on mandamus. (Steele v. Los Angeles County Civil Service Commission (1958) 166 Cal.App.2d 129, 137.) “[T]he burden of proof falls upon the party attacking the administrative decision to demonstrate wherein the proceedings were unfair, in excess of jurisdiction or showed prejudicial abuse of discretion.” (Alford v. Pierno, (1972) 27 Cal.App.3d 682, 691 [“Alford”].)

Taken together, these standards require Petitioner to do more than show uncertainty in the evidence. He must show that Respondent’s decision lacked any reasonable factual or legal foundation. As explained below, he has not done so.

IV.           Analysis

Petitioner seeks administrative mandamus to set aside Respondent’s decision to suspend his license on the grounds that (1) Respondent did not proceed in the manner required by law; (2) Respondent’s findings are not supported by the evidence; and (3) Respondent abused its discretion by disregarding material evidence.

1.               Extra-Record Evidence

“The general rule is that a hearing on a writ of administrative mandamus is conducted solely on the record of the proceeding before the administrative agency.” (Toyota of Visalia v. New Motor Vehicle Bd. (1987) 188 Cal.App.3d 872, 881.) The court can only admit additional evidence where the party seeking its inclusion shows (1) the evidence could not have been presented to the agency in the first instance in the exercise of reasonable diligence or (2) was improperly excluded. (CCP § 1094.5(e); Western States Petroleum Assn. v. Superior Court (1995) 9 Cal.4th 559, 578 [“Western States”]; Eureka Citizens for Responsible Govt. v. City of Eureka (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 357, 366.) Extra-record evidence is admissible only if it is relevant. (Western States at 570.)

While Petitioner does not bring a motion to augment,[2] Petitioner presents extra-record evidence he argues may be considered as it could not in the exercise of reasonable diligence have been produced at the hearing. (CCP § 1094.5(e).)  Petitioner presents a cardiologist report dated August 13, 2025 showing no signs of recurrence after the Episode. (Girotto Decl., Ex. 1 at pp. 2, 6.)

Petitioner makes a minimal showing that the evidence could not have been produced at the hearing, but Respondent does not argue the evidence is inadmissible under the CCP section 1094.5 standard. Respondent notes, however, that the Court should if necessary remand for further consideration by Respondent rather than set aside its Decision on the basis of the new evidence. (Opp. at 12-13; CCP § 1094.5(e), (f).)

On a follow-up physician appointment, the physician again stated the syncope condition was stable, observed various examination results were “assuring,” and noted “[n]o signs of  recurrence.” (Girotto Decl., Ex. 1 at p. 6.) However, this physician also recommended further monitoring and expressed continued uncertainty as to the origin of the syncope. (Girotto Decl., Ex. 1 at p. 6.)

Even assuming admissibility under CCP section 1094.5(e), the extra-record evidence does not meaningfully advance Petitioner’s position. The August 13, 2025 record does not identify a cause for the Episode, does not state that recurrence is impossible or sufficiently unlikely, and does not opine that Petitioner can safely operate a vehicle. The cardiologist’s continued recommendation for monitoring reinforces the Hearing Officer’s conclusion that additional control time is warranted. The Court therefore declines to rely on the extra-record evidence because it does not resolve or clarify the matters at issue. Even if admitted, the extra-record evidence does not help Petitioner carry his burden of showing an abuse of discretion.

2.               Respondent Proceeded in the Manner Required by Law

Petitioner argues that Respondent did not proceed in the manner required by law by applying an incorrect legal standard. According to Petitioner, Respondent may only act summarily when it determines on investigation or reexamination that the safety of the subject or other persons on the highways requires such action. (Pet. Op. Br., citing Veh. Code § 13953.) [3]  Petitioner contends that Respondent may not suspend a license unless it can affirmatively determine that public safety requires suspension and no lesser restriction or condition will serve.[4] (Op. at 9.)

Petitioner overstates the substance of section 13953. That statute does not specify that Respondent must make findings that lesser restrictions or conditions would not suffice. There is no requirement in the statute that Respondent make an affirmative finding that Petitioner presently suffers some condition which poses a risk to himself or another person. (See § 13953.) The section provides that Respondent may suspend or revoke driving privileges if “the safety of the person subject to investigation or reexamination or the other person upon the highways require such action.” (§ 13953.) In other words, Petitioner misreads section 13953 by trying to convert a permissive safety-based authorization into a mandatory evidentiary threshold that the statute’s text simply does not support.

Further, once Petitioner’s license was suspended pursuant to section 13953 (which permits Respondent to act “forthwith and without hearing,” see § 13953), because Petitioner’s license was suspended “based upon a physical or mental condition” (AR 6-7), the suspension must continue “until evidence satisfactory to [Respondent] establishes that the cause for which the action was taken has been removed or no longer renders the person incapable of operating a motor vehicle safely.” (§ 13556(c).)

Additionally, Petitioner has the burden of showing Respondent did not proceed in the manner required by law. (Alford, supra, 27 Cal.App.3d at 691.) Critically, Petitioner identifies no portion of the Decision applying or relying on any incorrect legal standard, nor does he demonstrate that Respondent applied any narrower or more demanding framework than the Vehicle Code provides. Petitioner points to the Decision, where the Hearing Officer relies on the recency and uncertainty related to Petitioner’s Episode to determine continued suspension is required. (Pet. Op. Br. at 9-10; see AR 3-4.) That Petitioner recently experienced a brief loss of consciousness, and that such loss of consciousness is currently unexplained and so could recur, is clearly relevant to the question of whether the safety of Petitioner or others on the highway requires action by Respondent. Therefore, the Hearing Officer’s reliance on these findings does not support the proposition that the Hearing Officer applied an incorrect standard.

Moreover, neither Respondent nor the Hearing Officer at any point articulated an incorrect standard. (See AR 3-5, 25-58.) On the initial suspension, Respondent notes that the suspension should remain until Petitioner “no longer presents a risk to traffic safety.” (AR 6.) The Hearing Officer stated at the outset of the hearing, “the hearing issues for today’s hearing is to determine if a medical condition, lapse of consciousness or control affects your ability to operate a motor vehicle safely.” (AR 26.) These comport with the requirement under section 13556(c) that the suspension should continue until Respondent is satisfied the evidence shows the risk to traffic safety has abated. (§ 13556(c).)

Accordingly, Petitioner has not met his burden to show Respondent did not proceed in the manner required by law.

3.               The Weight of the Evidence Supports the Hearing Officer’s Findings

Petitioner argues that the Hearing Officer’s findings were not supported by the weight of the evidence. Specifically, Petitioner argues that the DME was internally contradictory, and therefore did not constitute evidence on which the Hearing Officer could rely. Petitioner further argues that Petitioner had no second occurrence of syncope on April 22, 2025, and thus the second occurrence could not support the Decision. Finally, Petitioner contends that the Hearing Officer’s stated rationale was “predictive and precautionary, not evidentiary.”

Applying the independent judgment test, under which the court must draw its own reasonable inferences from the evidence and make its own credibility determinations while substituting its judgment for the agency’s only as to the basic facts (Morrison, supra, 107 Cal.App.4th at 868; Guymon, supra, 55 Cal.App.3d at 1013-1016), but then affording the agency’s determination significant deference (Fukuda, supra, 20 Cal.4th at 817), and recognizing that judicial intervention is unwarranted unless the petitioner demonstrates that the agency’s action lacks any reasonable basis in law or substantial basis in fact (Bixby, supra, 4 Cal.3d at 150-151; Bank of America, supra, 42 Cal.App.3d at 208), the Court concludes as follows:

The record consistently shows medical professionals who were unwilling to affirm that Petitioner was safe to drive. Petitioner experienced an episode of syncope. (E.g. AR 8-9.) Both CMRs indicated the two physicians were uncertain as to whether further lapses of consciousness would occur. (AR 8-9.) One treating physician indicated on the CMR that he was uncertain whether the patient should be advised not to drive or would represent a permanent driving disability. (AR 8.) Respondent’s family physician indicated on the DME both “Yes” and “unknown” for whether Petitioner’s condition interferes with his ability to drive safely, and indicated the same for his medication. (AR 11.) While Petitioner claims this renders the evaluation entirely meaningless, the Court infers that Petitioner’s physician did not know for certain and so wrote “unknown,” but was sufficiently concerned as to indicate “Yes” rather than “No.” (AR 11.) This inference is supported by the physician’s further indications, specifically (a) it was “uncertain” whether Petitioner displayed sporadic loss of conscious awareness, loss of consciousness, or impaired motor function; and (b) Petitioner experienced post-Episode confusion. (AR 13.) Yet another physician indicated that Petitioner’s syncope was “stable,” but that the cause was unclear and further examination and treatment would be considered. (AR 17.)

Based on the evidence in the record, after Petitioner’s Episode, physicians were unable to determine its cause. Consequently, the physicians were unwilling to represent that Petitioner was not likely to experience recurrence.

Petitioner’s argument relies heavily on attacking the quality of the DME, but the argument overlooks that uncertainty in the medical evidence does not favor Petitioner. It favors caution. Respondent is not required to resolve uncertainty in Petitioner’s favor, particularly where treating physicians themselves declined to conclude that Petitioner can safely drive. The Court also disagrees that the DME’s ambiguity makes it meaningless. That ambiguity cuts the other way and provides Respondent with no affirmative assurance that Petitioner could safely drive, and therefore reasonably supports a decision to maintain a suspension pending further clarity.

The Hearing Officer found that Petitioner suffers a medical condition causing lapse of consciousness or control affecting his ability to drive. (AR 3.) This is established by the undisputed fact that Petitioner suffered a syncope episode. The Hearing Officer found “a longer period of control is required” to show Petitioner can drive safely. (AR 3.) This is well supported by the strong showing in the record that physicians were unwilling to affirmatively report otherwise; unresolved medical certainty; and evidence of ongoing changes in Petitioner’s medication regimen. These objective circumstances amount to substantial evidence supporting the suspension.

Accordingly, Petitioner has not met his burden to show the findings were not supported by the weight of evidence.

4.               Respondent Did Not Abuse Its Discretion under Topanga

Petitioner argues that Respondent abused its discretion by disregarding material evidence. Specifically, Petitioner contends the Hearing Officer observed evidentiary uncertainty regarding whether Petitioner had a second occurrence, and used that uncertain justification for the findings and decision.

The Hearing Officer’s findings of fact state that the DME had conflicting information, and this is clearly correct. (AR 3; 10-14.) Respondent’s findings also state that Petitioner testified he only had one episode. (AR 3; see AR 41-42.) Even if the Hearing Officer did rely on this uncertainty (which the record does not show), this is a question of weighing evidence.

Petitioner references Topanga, supra, 11 Cal.3d at 515, to argue “an agency abuses its discretion when it sustains an order without meaningfully considering evidence that cuts against that order.” (Pet. Op. Br. at 14.) This is not what Topanga requires. Topanga requires the administrative agency make findings that demonstrate its “analytic route” from the evidence to the action. (Topanga at 515.) In other words, the findings must “bridge the analytic gap between the raw evidence and the ultimate decision or order.” (Ibid.)

The Hearing Officer has done so in the Decision. The findings, while brief, identify the coexistent factors motivating Respondent’s decision: the recent occurrence of the Episode, unresolved causation, medication changes, ongoing medical issues, and pending diagnostic follow-up. (AR 3-4.) These findings sufficiently “bridge the analytic gap” between the raw medical evidence and the conclusion that additional control time was required. Nothing in Topanga requires the Hearing Officer to articulate a more exhaustive explanation.

The analytic route is clear. The Hearing Officer believed that the events and medical examinations after the Episode were insufficient to show Petitioner could drive safely by the time of the hearing, and therefore declined to remove the suspension. Petitioner’s contrary argument attempts to recast evidentiary uncertainty as evidence of abuse of discretion. However, under CCP section 1094.5, uncertainty is not abuse; it is a permissible and relevant basis for caution where the safety of a driver and the public is at stake.

Accordingly, Petitioner has not met his burden to show the conclusion was not supported by the findings.

V.              Conclusion

Petitioner has not met his burden to show that Respondent failed to proceed in the manner required by law, that the findings were not supported by the weight of the evidence, or that the conclusion was not supported by the findings. The petition for administrative mandamus is DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION TO APPOINT PROVISIONAL DIRECTOR 

 

Plaintiff Leon Ahdoot (“Plaintiff”), individually and as trustee of The L&L Ahdoot Family Trust (“Trust”) seeks and order appoint Stephen J. Donell (“Donell”) provisional director for nominal defendant Oxford Financial, Inc. (“Oxford Inc.”).

The unopposed motion is granted.

I.                Background

1.               Factual Background

In February 2009, Plaintiff and defendant Shahram Mokhtarzadeh (“Mokhtarzadeh”) formed Oxford Inc., a California corporation. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 3.) Plaintiff and Mokhtarzadeh were and have since been equal 50% owners of Oxford Inc. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 4.) Plaintiff and Mokhtarzadeh elected themselves to the two seats on Oxford Inc.’s Board of Directors (“Board”). (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 5; RJN Ex. 14.)

On February 23, 2009, The Board held its first meeting and adopted Oxford Inc.’s bylaws. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 7., Exs. 1-2.)

On February 26, 2009, the Plaintiff and Mokhtarzadeh signed a Shareholders’ Agreement relating to Oxford Inc.. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 10, Ex. 3.)

On March 3, 2009, Plaintiff and Mokhtarzadeh formed nominal defendants Santa Monica Corner, LLC (“Santa Monica LLC”) and Santa Fe Corner, LLC (“Santa Fe LLC”). (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 1; RJN Exs. 4-5.) Plaintiff held and still holds a 49% interest in Santa Monica LLC and Sante Fe LLC as trustee for the Trust. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶¶ 12, 17.) Mokhtarzadeh directed his 49% interest in Santa Monica LLC and Santa Fe LLC to defendant 3A Family Limited Partnership (“3AF Limited Partnership”), which still holds that interest. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶¶ 13, 17.) Oxford Inc. held and still holds the remaining 2% interest in Santa Monica LLC and Santa Fe LLC. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶¶ 16-17.) Plaintiff and Mokhtarzadeh agreed that Oxford Inc. would be the sole manager of both Santa Monica LLC and Santa Fe LLC. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 18.)

On or about March 11, 2009, Oxford Inc., Mokhtarzadeh for 3AF Limited Partnership, and Plaintiff for the Trust all agreed to Operating Agreements for Santa Monica LLC and Santa Fe LLC. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶¶ 19-20, Exs. 4-5.) Santa Monica LLC and Santa Fe LLC each acquired one piece of income-producing real property. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 21; RJN Exs. 6-8.)

On December 27, 2023, Mokhtarzadeh recorded one quitclaim deed each for Santa Monica LLC and Santa Fe LLC purporting, as a member of the LLCs, to transfer a 49% interest in each company’s real property to 3AF Limited Partnership. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 22; RJN Exs. 9-10.)

Plaintiff never agreed to change the structure or manager of either Santa Monica LLC or Santa Fe LLC, nor did he consent to transfer any real property interest away from Santa Monica LLC or Santa Fe LLC. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶¶ 23-30.)

Because Plaintiff and Mokhtarzadeh can no longer agree on how to operate Oxford Inc., neither Oxford Inc. nor Santa Fe LLC have filed a corporate tax return since 2018, and Santa Monica LLC has not filed annual tax returns since 2021. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶¶ 34-42, Exs. 6-9.) On July 1, 2025, Plaintiff received a Notice of Suspension from the Franchise Tax Board. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 39, Ex. 7.)

III.           Applicable Law

Corporations Code[1] section 308 provides two avenues for appointment of a provisional director.

First, the court may appoint a provisional director if a corporation has an even number of directors who cannot agree on management of the corporation, to the extent that it can no longer conduct its business or to the extent that there is a danger its property or business will be impaired or lost. (§ 308(a).) In this circumstance, the court may appoint a provisional director on petition of any director, or by holders of at least a third of the corporation’s voting power. (Id.)

Second, the court may appoint a provisional director if the shareholders of the corporation are deadlocked such that they cannot elect directors at an annual shareholder meeting. (§ 308(b).) In this circumstance, the court may appoint a provisional director on petition by at least half of the corporation’s voting power. (Id.)

The provisional director must not be a creditor to the corporation, a shareholder of the corporation, or related to any corporation director or any judge of the appointing court by consanguinity or affinity within the third degree. (§ 308(c).) The provisional director holds all rights and powers of a director until the deadlock in the board or among shareholders is broken or until removal by the court or by approval of the outstanding shares. (Id.)The provisional director is entitled to compensation as determined by the court unless otherwise agreed with the corporation. (Id.)

IV.           Analysis

1.               Grounds for Appointment

Plaintiff seeks appointment of a provisional director under section 308, subsection (a).

A petition for appointment of a provisional director under subsection (a) must be brought by a director of the corporation or by holders of at least a third of its voting power. (§ 308(a).)

Plaintiff is a director of Oxford (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 5.) and also holds half of its voting power (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 4.) Therefore, Plaintiff may seek appointment of a provisional director under subsection (a).

A petition for appointment of a provisional director under subsection (a) must show that the corporation has an even number of directors who are deadlocked to an extent that the corporation cannot conduct business or is endangering its the business or property. (§ 308(a).)

Oxford has an even number of directors, specifically two. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 5.) Those directors are Plaintiff and Mokhtarzadeh. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶ 5.) Plaintiff and Mokhtarzadeh have been unable to agree on management of Oxford since at least 2018, and as a result Oxford has not filed tax returns since 2018, and the companies it manages, Santa Monica LLC and Santa Fe LLC, have similarly failed to file tax returns. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶¶ 34-42.) Additionally, Santa Monica LLC has been suspended by the Franchise Tax Board, and Oxford Inc. and Santa Fe are both at risk of suspension. (Ahdoot Decl., ¶¶ 35-36, 39, Ex. 7.) Based on this evidence, the court finds the deadlock amongst Oxford’s directors is both preventing it from conducting its business and endangering its property and business.

Plaintiff has shown that he may seek appointment of a provisional director, and that Oxford Inc.’s even-numbered board of directors is deadlocked. Accordingly, the court may appoint a provisional director for Oxford.

2.               Proposed Provisional Director

The provisional director must not be a creditor to the corporation, a shareholder of the corporation, or related to any corporation director or any judge of the appointing court by consanguinity or affinity within the third degree. (§ 308(c).)

Donell is not a creditor or shareholder of Oxford, nor is he related to any Oxford director or any judge of Los Angeles County Superior Court. (Donell Decl., ¶ 15.)

Defendants have not opposed in general or specifically to the appointment of Donell.

Therefore, Donell is qualified to be appointed as provisional director for Oxford.

V.              Conclusion

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION FOR LEAVE TO PROCEED UNDER PSEUDONYM 

 

Fictitiously named petitioner John Doe (“Petitioner”) seeks leave to proceed in this action as John Doe.

The unopposed motion is denied.

I.                Background

1.               Factual Background

In or around January 2018, the University of California, Los Angeles (“UCLA”) Director of Administrative Policies & Compliance received a whistleblower report regarding unauthorized fees charged to international postgraduate Orthodontics students. (Osher Decl., ¶ 3.) An investigation by Hueston Hennigan, LLP (“Hueston”) determined two students paid $30,000 annually above the amount paid by other residents, and that money went to a fund established by Dr. Eric Kang Ting (“Ting”). (Osher Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 1 at p. 1.) Hueston concluded Ting and two other professors arranged for these increased fees, representing them to UCLA as donations. (Osher Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. 1 at pp. 1-3.) Ting used the funds to purchase equipment for his lab and his spouse’s lab. (Osher Decl., ¶ 7, Ex. 1 at p. 28.)

Hueston’s report indicated Ting made these anonymous reports intending to damage the Associate Dean’s professional prospects. (Osher Decl., ¶ 7, Ex. 1 at p. 50-56.)

Ting published news articles disparaging Petitioner and his colleagues. (Petitioner Decl., ¶ 14, Ex. A; Osher Decl., ¶ 8-9.) In those articles, Ting references the research subject to the ORI investigation. (Osher Decl., ¶ 9.)

On August 27, 2025, RPI submitted a California Public Records Act (“CPRA”) request to respondent Regents of the University of California (“Respondent”) for, inter alia, “All records concerning investigation by UCLA into allegations made in or around 2018 that UCLA Dentistry faculty members, professors, and/or associate deans or deans published manipulated data, including all records reflecting any conclusions reached therein.” (“Request No. 4”) (Osher Decl., ¶ 11, Ex. 2.)

On or around January 8, 2026, UCLA’s Director of Information Practices notified Petitioner he was identified as a respondent in a report subject to disclosure under CPRA. (Osher Decl., ¶ 12, Ex. 3) While witnesses and complainants’ identities would be redacted, UCLA informed Petitioner his name, as the respondent, would be disclosed. (Osher Decl., ¶ 12, Ex. 3.)

On or around January 14, 2026, Petitioner objected to the release of the investigative report on him, arguing that the records were exempt from CPRA disclosure as part of an ongoing investigation, subject to confidentiality under Title 42 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Part 93, exempt from CPRA disclosure as confidential personnel files or similar files, and exempt from CPRA disclosure because the public interest in nondisclosure outweighs the public interest in disclosure. (Osher Decl., ¶ 13, Ex. 4.)

2.               Procedural History

On February 3, 2026, Petitioner filed this writ action against the Respondent, naming RPI as real party. A proof of service filed February 17, 2026 shows Petitioner served RPI with the summons and petition on February 10, 2026. Additionally, a notice of acknowledgment and receipt filed March 19, 2026 shows RPI received the summons and petition by March 16, 2026. On April 30, 2026, RPI filed its answer to the petition. On May 27, 2026, RPI filed its first amended answer. On June 4, 2026, Respondent filed its answer to the petition.

On May 8, 2026, the parties stipulated to consolidate the hearing on a motion for preliminary injunction in this case with the hearing on a motion for preliminary injunction in related case no. 26STCP00428. On June 24, 2026, the Court granted preliminary injunctions in both cases.

On June 12, 2026, Petitioner filed the instant motion. On July 24, 2026, real party Cohen Williams LLP (“RPI”) filed a statement of non-opposition to the instant motion. To date, no party has filed an opposition. Any opposition was due no later than July 22, 2026. (Code of Civil Procedure [“CCP”] § 1005(b).)

II.             Discussion

Petitioner seeks leave to proceed as “John Doe.” Petitioner argues he has a significant constitutional privacy interest in his employment record with UCLA.

“¿‘[A] party may preserve his or her anonymity in judicial proceedings in special circumstances when the party’s need for anonymity outweighs prejudice to the opposing party and the public’s interest in knowing the party’s identity.’¿” (Doe v. Lincoln Unified School Dist. (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 758, 767 [“Lincoln USD”], quoting Does I thru XXIII v. Advanced Textile Corp. (9th Cir. 2000) 214 F.3d 1058, 1068 [“Advanced Textile”].) A court may permit plaintiffs to use pseudonyms in three situations: “(1) when identification creates a risk of retaliatory physical or mental harm [Citation]; (2) when anonymity is necessary ‘to preserve privacy in a matter of sensitive and highly personal nature’ [Citation]; and (3) when the anonymous party is ‘compelled to admit [his or her] intention to engage in illegal conduct, thereby risking criminal prosecution.’¿” (Advanced Textile. at 1068.)

Where the request for anonymity is based on the purported need “to preserve privacy in a matter of sensitive and highly personal nature,” the proper test is whether “the party’s need for anonymity outweighs prejudice to the opposing party and the public’s interest in knowing the party’s identity.” (Ibid.; Lincoln USD at 767.) “Outside of cases where anonymity is expressly permitted by statute, litigating by pseudonym should occur ‘only in the rarest of circumstances.’¿” (Department of Fair Employment and Housing v. Superior Court of Santa Clara County, (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 105, 111-12 [“Department Fair Employment and Housing”].) “In deciding the issue the court must bear in mind the critical importance of the public’s right to access judicial proceedings.” (Ibid.)

Petitioner has a privacy interest in the investigative report he seeks to protect from CPRA disclosure. (Associated Chino Teachers v. Chino Valley Unified School Dist. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 530, 541 [“Chino Teachers”] [disclosure of results of investigation into misconduct would compromise employee’s significant privacy interest].) Petitioner also relies on Bakersfield City School District v. Superior Court (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 1041. While the Court of Appeal in that case ultimately ordered disclosure of the investigation complaint, it reaffirmed that personnel records may fall within CPRA’s privacy exemption when disclosure would constitute an unwarranted invasion of personal privacy. (Id. at 1045.)

These authorities, however, do not establish a public employee’s privacy interest in concealing his identity from the public. It is not clear from Petitioner’s showing why protection of his identity is necessary. His status as a public employee is itself public, and he identifies no privacy interest tied to shielding that fact. Nor has he demonstrated harm from public disclosure of Ting’s accusations; according to Petitioner’s own evidence, Ting has already publicized those allegations. (Osher Decl., ¶¶ 8–10.)

A motion to proceed under a pseudonym is analogous to a motion to close the courtroom or seal a court record. (Department Fair Employment and Housing, supra, 82 Cal.App.5th at 111.) Courts will not seal records that are already public. (H.B. Fuller Co. v. Doe, (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 879, 898 [“It should go without saying that there is no justification for sealing records that contain only facts already known or available to the public.”].) Similarly, courts have rejected invasion of privacy actions on the basis that “[a] matter that is already public or that has previously become part of the public domain is not private. (Sipple v. Chronicle Publishing Co. (1984) 154 Cal.App.3d 1040, 1047, 201 Cal.Rptr. 665.)” (Moreno v. Hanford Sentinel, Inc. (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 1125.) The same principle applies here. Petitioner cannot assert an overriding privacy interest in anonymity while acknowledging his involvement has already entered the public domain.

Petitioner also argues his identity as a witness in the related ongoing federal academic investigation must be protected. This argument fails for the same reasons: his identity as a witness to the investigation has been publicized. Moreover, Petitioner specifically alleges his identity will be used by Ting to interfere with the investigation, but every indication in the evidence and arguments before this Court show Ting has always been aware of Petitioner’s status as a witness (and subject) of all relevant investigations. (E.g. Osher Decl., ¶¶ 4-6, 8-10; Mot. at 2-5.) Even assuming Ting were unaware, Petitioner offers no explanation as to how Ting could use knowledge of his identity to compromise any investigation. (See Mot. at 8-10.)

Accordingly, Petitioner has not demonstrated a qualifying privacy interest and is not entitled to proceed under a pseudonym.

III.           Conclusion

Petitioner has not met his burden to show a privacy interest in his identity as petitioner in this action. The motion to proceed under a pseudonym is DENIED.

III.           Legal Standard

The appointment of a receiver is a drastic remedy to be utilized only in “exceptional cases.” As such, a receiver should not be appointed unless absolutely essential and because no other remedy will serve its purpose. (City & County of San Francisco v. Daley, (1993) 16 Cal.App.4th 734, 744 [“Daley”].) Generally, a plaintiff who seeks appointment of a receiver of certain property has the burden to establish by a preponderance of the evidence that plaintiff has a joint interest with defendant in the property, that the property is in danger of being lost, removed or materially injured, and that plaintiff's right to possession is probable. (AlhambraShumway Mines, Inc. v. Alhambra Gold Mine Corp., (1953) 116 Cal.App.2d 869, 873.)

IV.           Analysis

Plaintiff seeks appointment of a receiver to manage the Properties under CCP section 564, subdivisions (b)(11) and (b)(12), and Civil Code section 2938, subdivision (c), to enforce an assignment of rents provision on Defendants’ default. Plaintiff also seeks a preliminary injunction in aid of the receiver.

1.               Appointment of Receiver

CCP section 564(b)(11) provides for the appointment of a receiver in actions brought by a secured lender for specific performance of an assignment of rents provision in a deed of trust. (CCP § 564(b)(11).) Civil Code section 2938(c)(1) likewise provides that, upon default of an obligation secured by assignment of leases, rents, issues, and profits, the court may appoint a receiver to collect and receive all rents, issues, and profits accrued but unpaid and uncollected. (Civ. Code § 2938(c)(1).) CCP section 564(b)(12) provides for appointment of a receiver in actions brought under Civil Code section 2938(g). (CCP § 564(b)(12).) Civil Code section 2938(g) provides that an assignee of rents who enforces the assignment under subdivision (c) in a manner other than subdivision (c)(1) appointment of receiver must pay to preserve the property at issue until it obtains appointment of a receiver. (Civ. Code § 2938(g).)

Plaintiff provides evidence to show Defendants entered into the Loan, including the Deed of Trust which provides for assignment of all rents to Plaintiff, with a license to Defendants to collect rents revoked upon default. (RJN Exs. 16-18 at pp. 4-5.) Defendants failed to pay the Loan by February 28, 2025, the maturity date under the final Loan Modification Agreement. (Rothstein Decl., ¶¶ 28, 30, Ex. 29.) Plaintiff has also shown that Defendants owe $283,408.05 in overdue taxes. (Rothstein Decl., ¶ 36, Ex. 19.)

In opposition, Opponents argue primarily that a receivership is a drastic remedy which is not mandatory for the Court. Here, Opponents are correct. (Daley, supra, 16 Cal.App.4th at 744.)

Opponents then argue that a lesser remedy would suffice. Opponents support this position by noting it has now insured the Properties, there is no pending tax sale, Opponents are prepared to pay off the Loan through a refinance arrangement, and the Properties are not subject to waste or abandonment. (Opp. at 7.) Opponents therefore conclude that a lesser remedy such as requiring “confirmation of insurance, periodic confidential updates concerning refinancing, notice before transfers outside the ordinary course, or other measures tailored to a demonstrated risk.” (Opp. at 8-9.) This argument is not persuasive.

Opponents do not contest the existence of the Loan, the assignment of rents in the Deed of Trust, or that they are in default on the Note and Loan Agreement. (See generally Opp.) Opponents do not contest that Plaintiff has extended the maturity date for the Loan four separate times through Loan Modification Agreements, and then subsequently rescinded Notices of Default and Election to Sell Under Deed of Trust and offered a proposed forbearance agreement. (See generally Opp.) Opponents do not contest that they were overdue on property taxes by $283,408.05, nor do they contend they have since paid the overdue taxes, but only note they are “not aware of any imminent tax sale.” (White Decl., ¶ 4.) Opponents do not declare or argue that they never allowed insurance to lapse on the Properties, but only conspicuously assert, “[t]o my knowledge, the Subject Properties are presently insured.” (White Decl., ¶ 3.) Opponents argue that they have received a letter of intent for a refinance, but have not provided the letter of intent, asserting public disclosure could impair the transaction. [1] (White Decl., ¶ 5.) Opponents offer the conclusory statement that “[t]here has been no abandonment, material waste, destruction of improvements, or deterioration beyond ordinary wear and tear.” (White Decl., ¶ 2.) Finally, Opponents argue they continue to make timely monthly interest payments. For this contention, Opponents cite to paragraphs 4 and 7-10 and Exhibit A to the White Declaration. (Opp. at 7.) Paragraphs 4 and 7-8 do not show this. (White Decl., ¶¶ 4, 7-8.) The White Declaration ends with paragraph 8 and has no exhibits. (See generally White Decl.)

Even assuming arguendo that Opponents contentions are true, despite their conclusory declarations, evasive phrasings, and lack of documentary support, the overall evidence still reflects that Opponents are unable or unwilling to keep current with the Properties’ upkeep and tax obligations. While, as the Court ruled previously, Plaintiff did not make the necessary emergency showing to obtain ex parte relief, the evidence here shows the Properties are indeed in danger of being lost or materially injured.

The lesser remedies are insufficient for two reasons. First, Opponents essentially ask the Court to require Opponents to make showings that the Properties are not in danger. Opponents had the opportunity to make that showing in opposition to the instant motion, and offered scant, conclusory evidence. Second, the evidence before the Court on this motion shows the Property is in danger of being lost or harmed. Reporting requirements would only keep Plaintiff and the Court apprised of when such danger becomes so imminent as to potentially require Plaintiff to seek extraordinary remedies ex parte. Third, foreclosure is a remedy for Plaintiff to seek satisfaction on the Loan, not to enforce its assignment of rents.[2]

Accordingly, Plaintiff has shown entitlement to appointment of a receiver.

2.               Preliminary Injunction

In determining whether to issue a preliminary injunction, the trial court considers two factors: (1) the reasonable probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits at trial (CCP §526(a)(1)), and (2) a balancing of the “irreparable harm” that the plaintiff is likely to sustain if the injunction is denied as compared to the harm that the defendant is likely to suffer if the court grants a preliminary injunction. (CCP §526(a)(2); 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396, 1402; Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 1279, 1283; Davenport v. Blue Cross of California (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 435, 446; Abrams v. St. John’s Hospital (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 628, 636.) Thus, a preliminary injunction may not issue without some showing of potential entitlement to such relief. (Doe v. Wilson (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 296, 304.)

Opponents’ argument against a preliminary injunction is substantially identical to their argument opposing a receiver, claiming that a less intrusive remedy would suffice. (See Opp. at 8-9.) Opponent adds, however, that the injunction is essentially mandatory. A mandatory injunction is permissible when necessary to prevent irreparable harm pending adjudication; a court also has the authority to issue a preliminary injunction that mandates an affirmative act that changes the status quo in extreme cases where the right is clearly established. (See Integrated Dynamic Solutions, Inc. v. VitaVet Labs, Inc. (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 1178, 1183-1184.) Here, this is one such extreme case, because Opponents do not even contest the rights at issue. They are in default on the Loan, Note, and Loan Agreement, and the recorded Deed of Trust has an absolute assignment of rents to Plaintiff. (Rothstein Decl., ¶¶ 28, 30, Ex. 16-18 at pp. 4-5, Ex. 29.) As Opponents do not dispute the default or the assignment of rents, and thereby tacitly acknowledge Plaintiff has the right to the rents, they cannot argue they will be harmed on the basis that they will no longer receive the rents. (See Opp. at 8.) Plaintiff therefore has shown a likelihood of success on the merits, and the balance of harms favors Plaintiff.

Accordingly, Plaintiff is entitled to a preliminary injunction in aid of receiver.

3.               Bonds

Pursuant to CCP section 567(b), a receiver (unless a public entity or officer) must file an undertaking in an amount to be set by the Court to ensure faithful performance of the receiver’s duties.

In setting the bond for preliminary injunction, the court must assume that the preliminary injunction was wrongly issued. (Abba Rubber Co. v. Seaquist (1991) 235 Cal.App.3d 1, 15.) The attorney fees necessary to successfully procure a final decision dissolving the injunction also are damages that should be included in setting the bond. (Id. at 15-16.) While Abba reasoned that the plaintiff’s likelihood of prevailing is irrelevant to setting the bond, a more recent case disagreed, stating that the greater the likelihood of the plaintiff prevailing, the less likely the preliminary injunction will have been wrongly issued, and that is a relevant factor for setting the bond. (Oiye v. Fox (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 1036, 1062.)

The parties did not address the issue of either the receiver’s bond or the bond for preliminary injunction. Accordingly, the Court will hear from the parties at the hearing regarding the appropriate amount of the respective bonds.

V.              Conclusion

The motion for appointment of receiver is GRANTED. Kevin A. Singer is appointed as Receiver for the Properties. A preliminary injunction will issue in aid of receiver.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: APPLICATIONS FOR WRIT OF ATTACHMENT

 

Oncar Financial Services, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) seeks writs of attachment against defendants Continuum Analytics (“Continuum”) and Mahender Makhijani (“Makhijani”)(collectively, “Defendants”).

The two unopposed applications are granted.

I.                Background

1.     Factual Background

On August 1, 2022, Plaintiff executed two agreements: (a) with Continuum, a Promissory Note (“Note”) stating the repayment terms for a $5,000,000 loan (“Loan”) extended the same day; and (b) with Makhijani, principal of Continuum, a guarantee of Continuum’s performance and repayment under the Note (“Guarantee”). (Jalli Decl., ¶ 2, Exs. 1-2.) Under the Note, Continuum agreed to repay the Loan principal and interest within three months. (Jalli Decl., Ex. 1 at p. 1.)

Thereafter, the parties agreed to convert $1,000,000 of the Loan principal into an investment in real property not otherwise related to this litigation. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 3.)

Continuum did not pay the loan off in full within the three-month period. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 5.) Continuum did, however, make periodic payments over the next three years, including $200,000 total in 2022, $450,000 total in 2023, $400,000 total in 2024, and $333,333.33 total in 2025. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 5.) Continuum made no payments from November 2025 through January 2026. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 6.) Continuum made a total of $66,666.66 in payments in February and March 2026. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 9.) Makhijani likewise did not fulfill his Guarantee by providing any payments beyond what Continuum provided. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 7.)

2.     Procedural History

On June 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed this action against Defendants for breach of the Note, breach of the Guaranty, and common count. To date, neither Continuum nor Makhijani has filed an answer or made an appearance.

On July 2, 2026, the Court denied Plaintiff’s ex parte application for right to attach orders against Defendants, but granted a Temporary Protective Order (“TPO”) against Continuum only. The Court noted notice was deficient as to Makhijani. The Court ordered Plaintiff to post a $10,000 undertaking before the TPO would issue. The Court further set a hearing on an application for writ of attachment against Continuum on regular notice, and likewise against Makhijani if Plaintiff properly noticed and served the appropriate documents, including the summons and complaint, on Makhijani.

On July 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed its $10,000 undertaking for the TPO.

On July 9, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant applications for writ of attachment against Defendants. Proofs of service filed July 9, 2026, show Plaintiff served the summons and complaint on Continuum by personal service on June 30, 2026 and on Makhijani by personal service on July 9, 2026. Further proofs of service filed July 9, 2026 show Plaintiff served the moving papers[1] for the instant applications on both Defendants on July 9, 2026 by personal service. To date, no opposition by either defendant has been filed. Any opposition was due no later than July 28, 2026. (CCP § 484.060(a).)

II.             Applicable Law

“Upon the filing of the complaint or at any time thereafter, the plaintiff may apply pursuant to this article for a right to attach order and a writ of attachment by filing an application for the order and writ with the court in which the action is brought.” (CCP § 484.010.)

The application shall be executed under oath and must include: (1) a statement showing that the attachment is sought to secure the recovery on a claim upon which an attachment may be issued; (2) a statement of the amount to be secured by the attachment; (3) a statement that the attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based; (4) a statement that the applicant has no information or belief that the claim is discharged or that the prosecution of the action is stayed in a proceeding under the Bankruptcy Act (11 U.S.C. section 101 et seq.); and (5) a description of the property to be attached under the writ of attachment and a statement that the plaintiff is informed and believes that such property is subject to attachment. (CCP § 484.020.)

The application for a writ of attachment must be supported “by an affidavit showing that the plaintiff on the facts presented would be entitled to a judgment on the claim upon which the attachment is based.” (CCP § 484.030.)

The court shall consider the showing made by the parties, as well as the pleadings and other papers in the record. (CCP § 484.090(a), (d).) The Court shall issue a right to attach order if it finds all of the following:

“(1) The claim upon which the attachment is based is one upon which an attachment may be issued.

(2) The plaintiff has established the probable validity of the claim upon which the attachment is based.

(3) The attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based.

(4) The amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.”

(CCP § 484.090(a)(1-4).)

At the times prescribed by CCP section 1005, subdivision (b), the defendant must be served with a copy of the summons and complaint, notice of application and hearing, and a copy of the application and supporting affidavits. (CCP § 484.040.)

“The Attachment Law statutes are subject to strict construction….” (Epstein v. Abrams (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1168.)

III.           Analysis

1.     Claim on Which Attachment Can Be Based

“[A]n attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney’s fees.” (CCP § 483.010(a).) “An attachment may not be issued on a claim which is secured by any interest in real property arising from agreement ….” (CCP § 483.010(b).)

Here, Plaintiff’s claims against Defendants are premised on its causes of action for breach of the Note and Guaranty. (Mem. at 7-8.) Plaintiff may seek attachment based on its breach claims.

2.     Contract Based on a Commercial Claim

If the action is against a defendant who is a natural person, an attachment may be issued only on a commercial claim which arises out of the defendant’s conduct of a trade, business, or profession. (CCP §483.010(c).) Consumer transactions cannot form a basis for attachment. (CCP §483.010(c); Kadison, Pfaelzer, Woodard, Quinn & Rossi v. Wilson (“Kadison”) (1987) 197 Cal.App.3d 1, 4 [action involving trust property was a commercial, not a consumer, transaction].)

As to Makhijani, he is the owner and principal of Continuum. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 2.) While Plaintiff makes only a conclusory showing that the Loan and Guaranty are commercial in nature (Jalli Decl., ¶ 3), the Court accepts this evidence as Makhijani has not opposed and as Continuum is a real estate investment firm (Jalli Decl., ¶ 3) and the parties’ relationship is generally commercial in nature (see Jalli Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 3).

Accordingly, the Court finds the claims are commercial in nature, not consumer.

3.     Fixed or Readily Ascertainable Amount

A claim is “readily ascertainable” where the damages may be readily ascertained by reference to the contract and the basis of the calculation appears to be reasonable and definite. (CIT Group/Equipment Financing, Inc. v. Super DVD, Inc. (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 537, 540-41.) The fact that the damages are unliquidated is not determinative. (Ibid.) But the contract must furnish a standard by which the amount may be ascertained and there must be a basis by which the damages can be determined by proof. (Ibid.)

Here, Plaintiff’s evidence shows that on August 1, 2022, the parties executed the Note and Guarantee for the $5,000,000 Loan. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 2, Exs. 1-2.) Under the Note, Continuum agreed to repay the Loan principal and interest within three months. (Jalli Decl., Ex. 1 at p. 1.) Thereafter, the parties agreed to convert $1,000,000 of the Loan principal into an investment in real property not otherwise related to this litigation. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 3.) Continuum did not pay the loan off in full within the three-month period. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 5.) Continuum did make payments over the next three years totaling $1,383,333.33. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 5.) Continuum made no payments from November 2025 through January 2026. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 6.) Continuum made a total of $66,666.66 in payments in February and March 2026. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 9.) Makhijani likewise did not fulfill his Guarantee by providing any payments beyond what Continuum provided. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 7.)

Plaintiff provides a separate calculation of remaining principal, fees, and interest showing Defendants now owe $4,351,900. (Jalli Decl., ¶¶ 10-14.) This amount is readily ascertainable.

Plaintiff also estimates Oncar will incur $500,000. Plaintiff provides only a bare assertion by its CEO supporting this amount.[2] (Jalli Decl., ¶ 10.) This amount is not readily ascertainable.

Accordingly, the total readily ascertainable amount is $4,351,900.

4.     Probable Validity of Plaintiff’s Claims

“A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.” (CCP § 481.190.) “If the defendant opposes the application, ‘the court must then consider the relative merits of the positions of the respective parties and make a determination of the probable outcome of the litigation.’ [Citations.]” (Pech v. Morgan (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 841, 855.)

Here, the application is based on claims for breach of contract. To establish a claim for breach of contract, a plaintiff must prove: (1) existence of a contract; (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendant’s breach of the contract; and (4) damages incurred by plaintiff as a result of the breach. (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1367.)

Here, as noted above, Plaintiff’s evidence shows that on August 1, 2022, the parties executed the Note and Guarantee for the $5,000,000 Loan. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 2, Exs. 1-2.) Under the Note, Continuum agreed to repay the Loan principal and interest within three months. (Jalli Decl., Ex. 1 at p. 1.) Thereafter, the parties agreed to convert $1,000,000 of the Loan principal into an investment in real property not otherwise related to this litigation. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 3.) Continuum did not pay the loan off in full within the three-month period. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 5.) Continuum did make payments over the next three years totaling $1,383,333.33. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 5.) Continuum made no payments from November 2025 through January 2026. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 6.) Continuum made a total of $66,666.66 in payments in February and March 2026. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 9.) Makhijani likewise did not fulfill his Guarantee by providing any payments beyond what Continuum provided. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 7.)

On January 8, 2026, Plaintiff sent Defendants a notice of default and demand to cure by January 20, 2026. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 8.) Defendants did not cure or attempt to cure by January 20, 2026. (Jalli Decl., ¶ 9.)

Plaintiff has made a prima facie showing for breach of contract. Defendants do not oppose the applications. Accordingly, Plaintiff has shown the probable validity of its breach of contract claim.

5.     Purpose of Attachment

The other required finding under CCP section 484.090 is that the “attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based.” (CCP § 484.090(a)(3).)

Plaintiff declares that “[a]ttachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on a claim upon which the attachment is based.” (App. ¶ 4.) Attachment is sought for proper purpose.

6.     Bankruptcy

CCP section 484.020(d) requires a “statement that the applicant has no information or belief that the claim is discharged in a proceeding under Title 11 of the United States Code (Bankruptcy) or that the prosecution of the action is stayed in a proceeding under Title 11 of the United States Code (Bankruptcy).”

Plaintiff provides this statement. (App. ¶ 5.) This requirement has been satisfied.

7.     Property Subject to Attachment

CCP section 487.010(a) states that, where the defendant is a corporation, all corporate property for which a method of levy is provided in CCP section 488.300 et seq. is subject to attachment.

Where the defendant is a natural person, the property to which attachment can apply are limited per CCP section 487.010(c).

Plaintiff seeks property described as follows:

“All of the following types of property owned by Makhijani: real property, personal property, equipment, motor vehicles, chattel paper, negotiable and other instruments, securities, deposit accounts, safe deposit boxes, accounts receivable, general intangibles, property subject to pending actions, and final money judgments.” (Apps. ¶ 9.)

This description is sufficient.

8.     Claims of Exemption

No claims of exemption are sought.

9.     Undertaking

CCP section 489.210 requires the plaintiff to file an undertaking before issuance of a writ of attachment. CCP section 489.220 provides, with exceptions, for an undertaking in the amount of $10,000. Defendants do not object to the amount of the undertaking. Accordingly, the Court will order an undertaking in the amount of $10,000.

IV.           Conclusion

Plaintiff’s two applications for attachment against Defendants are GRANTED in the reduced amount of $4,351,900. The undertaking will be $10,000. See CCP §489.220. No writ shall issue against any defendant until Plaintiff posts the $10,000 undertaking for that defendant.
