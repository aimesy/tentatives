DEPARTMENT 514 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 23STCV05533    Hearing Date: August 17, 2026    Dept: 514

Doe v. Young
23STCV05533 
Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Defendant Young to Provide Further Responses to Form Interrogatories (Set Two)

Tentative Ruling

The Court’s tentative ruling is that the motion to compel is denied as untimely.

The Court will call this matter to discuss the status of the other pending motions to compel.

Background

This action arises out of an employment relationship.

On March 13, 2023, Jane Doe (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Kevin Young a/k/a Kevin Young Commander of the British Empire (“Young”) and Properties GB7 LLC (“GB7”) (collectively “Defendants”).

On April 5, 2023, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (the “FAC”).  In the FAC, Plaintiff asserts causes of action for: (1) sexual battery; (2) sexual assault; (3) gender violence; (4) harassment in violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act (the “FEHA”); (5) constructive discharge in violation of public policy; and (6) intentional infliction of emotional distress.

On May 8, 2023, Defendants filed an Answer to Plaintiff’s FAC.  On the same day, Defendants also filed a Cross-Complaint against Plaintiff, Arch Hoffman (“Hoffman”), and Roes 1 through 20, asserting causes of action for: (1) conspiracy to defraud; (2) breach of fiduciary duty; (3) breach of contract; (4) conversion; (5) money had and received; and (6) money paid.

On June 1, 2023, Plaintiff filed an Answer to the Cross-Complaint.

On September 18, 2023, the Court granted Hoffman’s motion to compel arbitration of the claims in the Cross-Complaint.  The Court stayed litigation of the claims against Hoffman in the Cross-Complaint and also stayed arbitration pending resolution of the claims in the Complaint.

On April 13, 2026, the Court granted Defendants leave to file a First Amended Answer.  The Court also continued the trial date, then set for May 4, 2026, to July 13, 2026.

On April 15, 2026, Defendant filed the First Amended Answer.

At the Final Status Conference on July 2, 2026, the Court continued the trial date to October 12, 2026.

On July 21, 2026, Plaintiff filed this motion to compel Defendant Young to provide further responses to Form Interrogatories (Set Two), along with six other motions.  The motions were set for hearing on August 17, 18, and 19, 2026.

Defendants applied ex parte to vacate all of the hearings, arguing that they were untimely.  On July 24, 2026, the Court denied the application but also stayed the briefing on all motions under than this motion to compel further responses to form interrogatories.

Defendants filed an opposition on August 4, and Plaintiff filed a reply on August 10.

Trial is set for October 12, 2026.

Legal Standard

“On receipt of a response to interrogatories, the propounding party may move for an order compelling a further response if the propounding party deems that any of the following apply: (1) An answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete. (2) An exercise of the option to produce documents under Section 2030.230 is unwarranted or the required specification of those documents is inadequate. (3) An objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (a).)

Notice of a motion to compel further responses must be given “within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the propounding party and the responding party have agreed in writing.” (Id., subd. (c).)

A motion to compel further responses must be accompanied by a meet-and-confer declaration and a separate statement or, in the discretion of the Court, a “concise outline of the discovery request and each response in dispute.” (Id., subd. (b)(1) & (b)(2); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345.)

“The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a further response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (d).)

In Chapter 7 of the Civil Discovery Act, Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030, subdivision (a) provides, in pertinent part, that the court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that any person “engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.” A “misuse of the discovery process” includes (among other things) failing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery; making, without substantial justification, an unmeritorious objection to discovery; making an evasive response to a discovery request; disobeying a court order to provide discovery; and making or opposing, unsuccessfully, a motion to compel without substantial justification. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subds. (d)-(h).)

Code of Civil Procedure section 2024.020 establishes deadlines for discovery and provides:

“(a) Except as otherwise provided in this chapter, any party shall be entitled as a matter of right to complete discovery proceedings on or before the 30th day, and to have motions concerning discovery heard on or before the 15th day, before the date initially set for the trial of the action.

(b) Except as provided in Section 2024.050 [relating to a motion to reopen discovery], a continuance or postponement of the trial date does not operate to reopen discovery proceedings.”

Discussion

Plaintiff moves to compel a further responses to Form Interrogatory 17.1 as it relates to Request for Admission No. 13.

A motion to compel must be heard at least 15 days prior to the date initially set for trial.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.020, subd. (a).)  Unless the Court orders otherwise, a continuance of the trial date does not reopen or extend discovery deadlines.  (Id., subd. (b).)

Here, the trial date was initially set and then continued on multiple occasions, ultimately to May 4, 2026.  The trial continuance orders expressly reset the discovery deadlines based on the new trial date.  Accordingly, at least as of early April 2026, the last day for discovery motions to be heard was Friday, April 17, 2026 (or perhaps Monday, April 20, fourteen days before trial).

On April 13, 2026, the Court continued the trial date to July 13, 2026.  The Court stated:

“No discovery deadlines are extended by this order, except that discovery is reopened only as to the limited issue of the new affirmative defense [based on the statute of limitations] set forth in the Amended Answer. As to that discovery only, the deadlines are reset based on the new trial date.”

(Minute Order dated April 13, 2026.)  Accordingly, at least as of the end of June 2026, the last day for discovery motions to be heard had passed.  For hearings on discovery unrelated to the new statute of limitations defense, the last day had been in April 2026.  For hearings on discovery related to the new statute of limitations defense, the last day had been Friday, June 26, 2026 (or perhaps Monday, June 29, fourteen days before trial).

At the Final Status Conference on July 2, 2026, the Court, on its own motion, continued the Final Status Conference to October 2, 2026, and continued the trial date to October 12, 2026.  The Court did not extend or reset any discovery deadlines. 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2024.020, subdivision (b), the Court’s Order of July 2 did not extend or reset any discovery deadlines.  The motion to compel deadline passed in June 2026.  No court order has extended or reset the motion to compel deadline.  Accordingly, this motion to compel is denied on the ground that it is untimely.

Plaintiff argues that the Court’s Order of April 13, which reset discovery deadlines “based on the new trial date” should be read to mean that any continuance that occurred in the future (such as came to pass on July 2) would automatically reset the discovery deadlines.  There is no support for this interpretation of the Court’s Order.  This is contrary to both the letter and spirit of Code of Civil Procedure section 2024.020, subdivision (b), as well as to the common practice and understanding of judges and litigants alike.  A trial continuance that is silent on extending discovery deadlines is, under section 2024.020, the equivalent of an order that expressly states that no discovery deadlines are extended.  The presumption is that a continuance does not extend discovery deadlines; any exception must be expressly made in the order.

The Court also notes that the continuance ordered on July 2 was not requested by any party.  It was ordered on the Court’s own motion, and the primary reason for the continuance was the Court’s schedule, not any concern relating to the inability of any party to complete necessary discovery.

The Court also makes two additional observations.

First, although not briefed by the parties, it appears to the Court that Plaintiff did not comply with the statutory requirement to meet and confer before filing a motion to compel.  A party moving to compel further responses to interrogatories must file, with the motion, a declaration establishing that the moving party has satisfied the statutory requirement to meet and confer as set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (b)(1).) 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040, as amended by the Legislature effective January 1, 2026, provides:

“A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.”

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040, subd. (a).)

Here, however, it appears that the only meet and confer communication was in writing.

Second, the Court notes that the parties discuss in their briefing the issue of whether the requirements for a motion to reopen discovery under Code of Civil Procedure section 2024.050 are satisfied.  Plaintiff does not request such relief in her moving papers, however, and the Court will not grant relief that is not requested in the notice of motion and motion.

The Court need not reach, and does not reach, the other arguments in Defendants’ opposition.

The motion is denied on the ground that it is untimely.

As the motion is denied, so too is Plaintiff’s request for sanctions.

Conclusion

The Court DENIES the motion of Plaintiff Jane Doe to compel Defendant Kevin Young to provide further responses to Form Interrogatories (Set Two).

Moving Party is ORDERED to give notice.


Case Number: 23STCV25942    Hearing Date: August 17, 2026    Dept: 514

Watkins Real Estate Group v. Dunning
23STCV25942
Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment

Tentative Ruling

The motion is denied.

Background

This case involves a dispute regarding the entry, possession, and occupation of real property located at 2652 South Palm Grove Avenue in Los Angeles (the “Property”).

On October 23, 2023, Watkins Real Estate Group (“Plaintiff”) filed the complaint in this action against Wesley Dunning (“Defendant”) and Does 1 through 10 for trespass and damages.

On November 27, 2024, Defendant filed an answer.

On April 27, 2026, Defendant filed this motion for summary judgment.  Plaintiff filed an opposition on July 9, and Defendant filed a reply on August 6.

The motion was initially set for hearing on July 29, 2026, and the Court continued the hearing to August 17.

Trial is set for September 22, 2026.

Legal Standard

“The purpose of the law of summary judgment is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67, citing FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 381-382.)

As to each cause of action as framed by the complaint, a defendant moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to show “that one or more elements of the cause of action ... cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2); see also Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at pp. 850-851; Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.) Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a “triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2); see also Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at pp. 850-851.)

The party opposing a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication may not simply “rely upon the allegations or denials of its pleadings” but must instead “set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subds. (p)(1) & (p)(2). To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)  

Discussion

Certain facts in this matter are undisputed.

Prior to April 26, 2022, the Property was owned by the Eugene F. Dunning and Alice M. Dunning Revocable Trust Dated June 9, 1999 (the “Trust”).  (Defendant’s Statement of Undisputed Material Facts [“DSUMF”], No. 1.)  In or about 2013, Defendant moved into the Property.  (DSUMF, No. 2.)

Alice Dunning, trustee of the Trust, died in November 2020.  (DSUMF, No. 5.) 

On April 26, 2022, Plaintiff purchased the Property from the Trust.  (DSUMF, No. 7.) 

Defendant continued to occupy the property.  After serving Defendant with a three-day notice to vacate, Plaintiff initiated an Unlawful Detainer proceeding against Defendant (the “UD Proceeding”) in June 2022.  (DSUMF, No. 10.)  In October 2023, Plaintiff filed this trespass action.  (DSUMF, No. 12.)

On July 22, 2024, Defendant purchased the Property from Plaintiff.  (DSUMF, No. 13.)  The UD Proceeding was dismissed four days later.  (DSUMF, No. 14.)

In support of his motion, Defendant presents additional evidence.  Defendant states that he moved into the Property in 2013 pursuant to a written lease agreement with his mother, Alice Dunning, as trustee of the Trust, in which he agreed to pay $250 per month to rent a room in the Property.  (Dunning Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.)  On about January 1, 2019, Defendant states, he entered into a further written agreement (the “Lease”) to extend his rental agreement ease (again at a rent of $250 per month) through December 30, 2027.  (Dunning Decl., ¶ 5 & Exh. A.)

The complaint in this action asserts a single cause of action for trespass. 

“Trespass is an unlawful interference with possession of property.”  (Ralphs Grocery Co. v. Victory Consultants, Inc. (2017) 17 Cal.App.5th 245, 261.)  In general, owners of land “have a right to exclude persons from trespassing on private property; the right to exclude persons is a fundamental aspect of private property ownership.”  (Id., at p. 258.)   

The essential elements of a cause of action for trespass, in this context, are: (1) that the plaintiff owned, leased, or controlled the property; (2) that the defendant intentionally, recklessly, or negligently entered the property; (3) that the plaintiff did not give the defendant permission to enter the property; (4) that the plaintiff was harmed; and (5) that the defendant’s entry was a substantial factor in causing the plaintiff’s harm.  (McBride v. Smith (2018) 18 Cal.App.5th 1160, 1174; Ralphs Grocery Co., supra, 17 Cal.App.5th at pp. 261-262; Capogeannis v. Super. Ct. (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 668, 674; Civic Western Corp. v. Zila Industries, Inc. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 1, 16-17; see also CACI No. 2000.)

It is no defense to a cause of action for trespass that the defendant honestly, but mistakenly, believed that he had a right to be on the property.  (Cassinos v. Union Oil Co. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 1770, 1780; Miller v. NBC (1986) 187 Cal.App.3d 1463, 1480-1481.)  Where the trespass involves a physical intrusion into the property, nominal damages are available.  (Staples v. Hoefke (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 1397, 1406; see also San Diego Gas & Electric Co. v. Super. Ct. (1996) 13 Cal.4th 893, 936.)

Defendant now moves for summary judgment, making essentially four arguments: (1) that it is undisputed that Plaintiff entered the Property with permission of the then-owner (Mem. at pp. 5-6); (2) that Plaintiff has no non-speculative damages (Mem., at pp. 6-8); (3) that Plaintiff has no legal basis to recover attorney’s fees (Mem., at p. 8); and (4) that the undisputed facts show that there is no basis for Plaintiff to recover punitive damages, as Defendant did not act with oppression, fraud, or malice (Mem., at p. 8.)  The Court considers each of these arguments in turn.

First, Defendant argues that he has presented evidence (cited above) that he entered the Property pursuant to a written agreement with the owner.  Defendant has no direct evidence that the lease is fraudulent, but Defendant argues that there is circumstantial evidence to that effect, including: (a) that Defendant never mentioned the lease when Plaintiff contacted him after Plaintiff acquired the Property (Watkins Decl., ¶¶ 5-8); (b) that Defendant aggressively defended the UD Proceeding, including arguing that Plaintiff had not properly acquired the Property and that others lived on the Property, but he never produced the lease (Watkins Decl., ¶¶ 9-10); and (c) that Defendant’s sister, Janet Taylor, the successor trustee of the Trust after the death of Alice Dunning, stated that to her knowledge Defendant did not ever have a lease (Taylor Decl., ¶ 6).

The evidence that the lease is fraudulent is circumstantial, but circumstantial evidence is evidence. The evidence, and the inferences that may be drawn from the evidence, are sufficient here to defeat Defendant’s motion for summary judgment. (See Kaney v. Custance (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 201, 219-220; 3 Weil & Brown, California Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶ 10:272.1.  To be clear, the Court is not suggesting that Plaintiff is entitled to summary judgment on this point; rather, the evidence is sufficient to support a reasonable inference that the lease is fraudulent.  On this record, the trier of fact could reasonably believe Defendant and determine that the lease is valid, or the trier of fact could reasonably disbelieve Defendant, credit the circumstantial evidence cited by Plaintiff, and determine that the lease is a fraud.

Moreover, and independently, Code of Civil Procedure section 473c, subdivision (e), provides in part that “summary judgment may be denied in the discretion of the court if the only proof of a material fact offered in support of the summary judgment is an affidavit or declaration made by an individual who was the sole witness to that fact.”  In the unusual circumstances present here, the Court exercises its discretion under subdivision (e). 

Defendant’s first argument is not a sufficient basis to grant his motion for summary judgment.

Second, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s damages are speculative.  The damages that Defendant cites as speculative, however, relate primarily to lost profits.  It may well be that some of the damages that Plaintiff will not be able to prove a basis in fact to recover all of its claimed damages, but a summary judgment motion cannot be directed at only part of a damages claim.  Among other items of damages, Plaintiff cites carrying costs and attorney’s fees associated with the UD Proceeding (DSUMF, No. 16); these items are not speculative.

On this record, the Court cannot conclude that all of Plaintiff’s damages are speculative as a matter of law.  In addition, and independently, the Court cannot conclude as a matter of law that Plaintiff is not entitled to nominal damages.  Accordingly, Defendant’s second argument is not a sufficient basis to grant his motion for summary judgment.   

Third, Defendant argues that there is no legal basis for Plaintiff to recover attorney’s fees.  But Defendant did not include in his notice of motion and motion a request for summary adjudication on this issue, as is required.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1350(b); see also, e.g., Gonzales v. Super. Ct. (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 1542, 1545–1546.)  Moreover, and independently, there is no basis for a motion for summary adjudication directed to only one type of damages (other than punitive damages).  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

Defendant’s third argument is not a sufficient basis to grant his motion for summary judgment.

Fourth, Defendant argues that, as a matter of law, there is no basis to award Plaintiff punitive damages.  As noted above, however, Defendant did not properly move for summary adjudication of this issue (or any issue).  In addition, and independently, as also noted above, Plaintiff has presented sufficient evidence to support a reasonable determination by the finder of fact that Defendant prepared a fraudulent lease in an attempt to stay on the Property; that finding, if supported by clear and convincing evidence, would be a sufficient basis to award punitive damages.  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subds. (a) & (c)(1).)

Defendant’s fourth argument is not a sufficient basis to grant his motion for summary judgment.

Defendant’s motion is denied.

Conclusion

The Court DENIES the motion of Defendant Wesley Dunning for summary judgment.

Plaintiff, as the prevailing party, is ordered to give notice.


Case Number: 25STCV03297    Hearing Date: August 17, 2026    Dept: 514

White v. Dorsey
25STCV03297
Defendants’ Motion to Compel Plaintiff Frederick M. White to Respond to Form Interrogatories (Set One)
Defendants’ Motion to Compel Plaintiff Frederick M. White to Respond to Special Interrogatories (Set One)
Defendants’ Motion to Compel Plaintiff Frederick M. White to Respond to Requests for Production (Set One)

Tentative Ruling

The motions are granted.  The requests for sanctions are granted in part.

Discussion

On January 28, 2026, Defendants, Cross-Complainants, and Cross-Defendants Nico Designs & Construction and Matthew Bahrami (“Defendants”) served Plaintiff with Form Interrogatories (Set One), Special Interrogatories (Set One), and Requests for Production (Set One).  (Saracyan Decls., ¶ 2; Exhs. 1.)  No responses have been served. (Id., ¶ 4.)

Defendants need show nothing more.

The motions to compel Plaintiff to provide initial responses to the form interrogatories, special interrogatories, and requests for production are granted.

Defendants’ requests for monetary sanctions are granted in part.  In light of the straightforward nature of these motions, the economies of scale associated with preparing multiple, parallel discovery motions, and the absence of any opposition, the Court sets sanctions on each motion in the amount of $525, based on 1.5 hours of attorney time multiplied by counsel’s billing rate of $310 per hour, plus a $60 filing fee. (See Saracyan Decls., ¶ 6.) 

Conclusion

The Court GRANTS the motions of Defendants Nico Designs & Construction and Matthew Bahrami to compel Plaintiff Frederick M. White to respond to form interrogatories, special interrogatories, and requests for production.

The Court ORDERS Plaintiff Frederick M. White to serve code compliant, verified, written responses, without objections, to the Form Interrogatories (Set One), Special Interrogatories (Set One), and Requests for Production (Set One) propounded by Defendants Nico Designs & Construction and Matthew Bahrami by no later than September 8, 2026.

The Court GRANTS IN PART the requests for sanctions.

The Court ORDERS Plaintiff Frederick M. White, and his counsel of record, Michael D. McLachlan, Esq., jointly and severally, to pay monetary sanctions under the Civil Discovery Act in the amount of $525 per motion (for a total of $1,575) to Defendants Nico Designs & Construction and Matthew Bahrami (through counsel) by no later than September 8, 2026.

Moving party is ORDERED to give notice.

Case Number: 26STCV12186    Hearing Date: August 17, 2026    Dept: 514

Windheim v. Tse
26STCV12186
Defendants’ Demurrer to Complaint

Tentative Ruling

The demurrer is sustained in part and overruled in part.

Background

This matter arises out of an alleged investment, joint venture, and business loans.

On April 15, 2026, Kenneth A. Windheim, individually and as trustee of the Global Uniform Investment Trust dated August 4, 1995 (“Windheim”), and Allotrope Prime, LLC (“Allotrope”) (collectively “Plaintiffs”) filed the complaint in this action against Christian Tse (“Tse”), Safe Lock Storage, LLC, Christian Tse Designs & Manufacturing, Inc. (“CTDM”), and Formula 3D Corporation, asserting causes of action for: (1) breach of contract, (2) breach of fiduciary duty, (3) accounting, (4) breach of promissory note – loan for $1,000,000, and (5) breach of promissory note – loan for $1,400,000.

On June 25, 2026, Tse, CTDM, and Formula 3D Corporation (collectively “Defendants”) filed this demurrer.  Plaintiffs filed an opposition on August 4, and Defendants filed a reply on August 10.

No trial date has been set.

Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10 provides:

“The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds:

…

(e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.  …”

A general demurrer under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e), tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388; Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; see also 1 Weil & Brown, California Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial [2025], ¶ 7:40.)

“We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.  We also consider matters which may be judicially noticed.”  (Centinela Freeman Emergency Medical Associates v. Health Net of California (2016) 1 Cal.5th 994, 1010; Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318; accord Yvanova v. New Century Mortgage Corp. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 919, 924; Marina Pacific Hotel and Suites v. Fireman’s Fund Insurance Company (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 96, 104.)  All reasonable inferences must be drawn in favor of the pleading.  (Schifando v. City of Los Angeles (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1074, 1081; Marina Pacific Hotel and Suites, supra, 81 Cal.App.5th at p. 104.)  Even “improbable” facts alleged in the pleading must be accepted as true.  (Marina Pacific Hotel and Suites, supra, 81 Cal.App.5th at pp. 104-105.)

Courts must “liberally construe the pleading” and “give the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in context.”  (Id. at p. 105.)

A complaint must contain a “statement of facts constituting the cause of action, in ordinary and concise language.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.10, subd. (a)(1); see also C.A. v. William S. Hart High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872 [“the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action”].)  Ordinarily, a complaint “is sufficient if it alleges ultimate rather than evidentiary facts.”  (Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 550.)  Ultimate facts are those upon which “the right to recover depends” and are “essential” to the cause of action.  (Estes v. Eaton Corp. (2020) 51 Cal.App.5th 636, 643 fn. 2; see also 1 Weil & Brown, supra, ¶ 6:124.)

A plaintiff is required to plead only “the essential facts of [its] case” that are sufficient “to acquaint a defendant with the nature, source and extent of [the] cause of action.”  (Doe, supra, 42 Cal.4th at p. 550.)  Mere boilerplate or pleading of legal conclusions is not sufficient.  (Id. at p. 551 fn. 5.)  But a plaintiff need not allege “each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of plaintiff’s proof” at trial.  (C.A., supra, 53 Cal.4th at p. 872.)

A demurrer can be sustained only when it disposes of an entire cause of action. (Poizner v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redev. Agency (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1046.)

Leave to amend should be granted when “there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment.”  (Centinela Freeman, supra, 1 Cal.5th at p. 1010; Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318; Shaeffer v. Califia Farms (2020) 44 Cal.App.5th 1125, 1145.)

Meet and Confer

Before filing a demurrer, “the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41, subd. (a).) This requirement is satisfied. (Vanderpool Decl., ¶ 2.)

Discussion             

Defendants demur to Plaintiffs’ First, Second and Third Causes of Action.  The Court considers each in turn.

First Cause of Action (for Breach of Contract by Plaintiff Allotrope against Defendant CTDM)

The elements of a cause of action for breach are contract are: “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.”  (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821; see also, e.g., Richman v. Hartley (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1182, 1186.)

In the Complaint, Plaintiff Allotrope alleges that it entered into the Colored Diamond Joint Venture Agreement (the “CDJVA”) with CTDM in approximately July 2016.  (Compl., ¶¶ 9-12, 21.)  Under the terms of the CDJVA, CTDM is responsible for arranging secure storage of the Colored Diamonds.  (Compl., ¶¶ 9-10, 22-23.)  Plaintiff alleges that CTDM has breached the CDJVA by failing to arrange for the safeguarding and security of the Colored Diamonds.  (Compl., ¶ 24.)  Plaintiff seeks specific performance and other relief.  (Compl., ¶¶ 26-29 & Item 1 of Prayer for Relief.)

Defendants argue that in this cause of action, Plaintiff has not sufficiently alleged a breach of the CDJVA or non-speculative damages.  (Mem., at pp. 6-7.)  Having reviewed the Complaint and its attachments, the Court concludes that Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged a breach of the CDJVA, at least as a matter of pleading.  Plaintiff has also sufficiently alleged resulting injury and damages; although quantifying those damages may be difficult, as Plaintiff acknowledges in the Complaint, Plaintiff seeks specific performance as part of its prayer for relief.

The demurrer to the First Cause of Action is overruled.

Second Cause of Action (for Breach of Fiduciary Duty against Tse and CTDM)

The elements of a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty are: (1) that the defendant and the plaintiff were in a relationship giving rise to fiduciary duties: (2) that the defendant breached a fiduciary duty owed to plaintiff; and (3) that the breach of fiduciary duty was a substantial factor in causing harm to the plaintiff.  (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 820-821; Williamson v. Brooks (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 1294, 1300; Shopoff & Cavallo LLP v. Hyon (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1489, 1509; Stanley v. Richmond (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1070, 1086; see also CACI No. 4106.) 

Defendants argue (1) that they owed no duty to Plaintiff Windheim as an individual; (2) that Plaintiffs do not sufficiently allege breach; and (3) that Plaintiffs do not sufficiently allege injury or damages.  (Mem., at pp. 7-9.)

Having reviewed the Complaint and its attachments, the Court concludes that Plaintiffs have sufficiently alleged breach and a resulting harm.  (Compl., ¶¶ 24-27, 37-39.) 

The Complaint does not sufficiently allege, however, that any fiduciary duty was owed by Defendants CTDM and TSE to Plaintiff Windheim himself (individually or as trustee).  The joint venture was between Allotrope and CTDM.  (Compl., ¶ 9.)  Although Plaintiff alleges that Windheim signed the CDJVA on behalf of Allotrope (Compl., ¶ 21), that alone does not give rise to fiduciary duties owed to him – even if Allotrope is a single member LLC and Windheim is the only member (which is not alleged in the Complaint).  Based on the allegations of the Complaint, Windheim, at most, entered into the CDJVA through an LLC her controlled (Allotrope).  The corporate entity is the joint venturer.  An individual cannot use an LLC (or any other corporate entity) to obtain certain benefits under the law (including limited liability for the owner) but then disclaim the corporate entity – and ask the Court to ignore the corporate form – when seeking to obtain benefits under a transaction or business arrangement.

The demurrer to the Second Cause of Action is overruled as to Plaintiff Allotrope and sustained as to Plaintiff Windheim.  Leave to amend is granted.

Third Cause of Action (for Accounting by Plaintiff Windheim against Formula 3D and Tse)

Plaintiff Windheim alleges that he invested $5 million in Defendant Formula 3D (a California corporation), that he owns 25 percent of the voting shares of the company, that he has requested an accounting and other information regarding the company’s business, and that the company and Tse (its Chief Executive Officer and Chief Financial Officer) have refused.  (Compl., ¶¶ 7, 13-14, 43-45.)  Plaintiff seeks an order to enforce his inspection rights as a shareholder and an accounting.  (Compl., ¶¶ 46-47 & Item 3 in Prayer for Relief.)

The basic elements for an accounting are: (1) “that a relationship exists between the plaintiff and defendant that requires an accounting”; and (2) “that some balance is due the plaintiff that can only be ascertained by an accounting.”  (Sass v. Cohen (2020) 10 Cal.5th 861, 869; see also Conservatorship of Farrant (2021) 67 Cal.App.5th 370, 376; Fleet v. Bank of America N.A. (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1413; Teselle v. McLoughlin (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 156, 179; 5 Witkin, California Procedure (6th ed. 2021) Pleadings, § 816.)  An accounting action is not available, however, “where the plaintiff alleges the right to recover a sum certain or a sum that can be made certain by calculation.”  (Sass, supra, 10 Cal.5th at p. 869 [quoting Teselle, supra, 173 Cal.App.4th at p. 179]; see also Fleet, supra, 229 Cal.App.4th at p. 1413.)

“The right to an accounting can arise from the possession by the defendant of money or property which, because of the defendant’s relationship with the plaintiff, the defendant is obliged to surrender.” (Teselle v. McLoughlin (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 156, 179-80.)  

“An accounting is an equitable proceeding which is proper where there is an unliquidated and unascertained amount owing that cannot be determined without an examination of the debits and credits on the books to determine what is due and owing.  …  Equitable principles govern, and the plaintiff must show the legal remedy is inadequate.” (Prakashpalan v. Engstrom, Lipscomb & Lack (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 1105, 1136-37.)

Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged a cause of action against Defendant Formula 3D for an order enforcing Plaintiff’s rights, as a shareholder, to inspect corporate records.  (Corps. Code, §§ 1600-1605.) 

Plaintiff labels his cause of action as for an “accounting,” but he has not alleged any facts sufficient to give rise to an accounting.  Nonetheless, the substance of the allegations in the pleading, and not the label attached by the pleader, controls.

Plaintiff has not, however, alleged a cause of action for a corporate inspection (or an accounting) against Defendant Tse personally.

The demurrer to the Third Cause of Action is overruled as to Defendant Formula 3D and sustained as to Defendant Tse.  Leave to amend is granted.

Conclusion 

The Court SUSTAINS IN PART and OVERRULES IN PART the demurrer of Defendants Christian Tse, Christian Tse Designs & Manufacturing, Inc., and Formula 3D Corporation.

The Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the allegations of Plaintiff Windheim (only) in the Second Cause of Action and as to the allegations against Defendant Tse (only) in the Third Cause of Action.

The Court OVERRULES the demurrer in all other respects.

The Court GRANTS Plaintiffs LEAVE to file an amended complaint addressing the pleading defects identified in this order by no later than September 8, 2026.

Moving party is ORDERED to give notice.
