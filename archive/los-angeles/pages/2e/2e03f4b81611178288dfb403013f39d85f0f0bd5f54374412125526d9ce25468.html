DEPARTMENT 732 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV14969    Hearing Date: September 28, 2026    Dept: 732


Newgen Capital, LLC v. Paul Hemming, et al.

 

Monday, September 28, 2025

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 24STCV14969

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Defendants Paul Hemming, Wendy Hemming, Terrence Hemming, Wendell Jamon Jones, Realty One Group, Infinity, and Garfield Building LA, LLC’s Motion to Expunge Lis Pendens

___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for breach of contract and fiduciary duty. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff Newgen Capital, LLC (“Plaintiff”) entered into a partnership with Defendants Paul Hemming and Wendy Hemming to purchase certain real property, and Plaintiff in the course of this partnership disclosed details of a proposed purchase deal to the Hemmings. (Complaint ¶¶ 11–15.) But the Hemmings then backed out of the purchase deal, and shortly thereafter bought the property for themselves. (Complaint ¶¶ 16–17.)

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on June 27, 2023, alleging six causes of action:

1.      Breach of Contract

2.      Unjust Enrichment

3.      Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

4.      Unfair Competition

5.      Breach of Fiduciary Duty

6.      Constructive Fraud / Constructive Trust

Defendants filed the present motion to expunge lis pendens on August 31, 2026.

Plaintiff filed an opposition on September 14, 2026.

Defendants filed a reply on September 18, 2026.

Analysis

I.                   MOTION TO EXPUNGE LIS PENDENS

“’A lis pendens is a recorded document giving constructive notice that an action has been filed affecting title to or right to possession of the real property described in the notice.’ [Citation.] A lis pendens may be filed by any party in an action who asserts a ‘real property claim.’ [Citation.] Section 405.4 defines a ‘“Real property claim”’ as ‘the cause or causes of action in a pleading which would, if meritorious, affect (a) title to, or the right to possession of, specific real property . . . .’ ‘If the pleading filed by the claimant does not properly plead a real property claim, the lis pendens must be expunged upon motion under CCP 405.31.’ [Citation.]” (Kirkeby v. Superior Court of Orange County (2004) 33 Cal.4th 642, 647.)

“The potential for a notice of lis pendens to pour sand into the smooth gears of a real estate transaction has been well remarked in the cases.” (Gale v. Superior Court (“Gale”) (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 1388, 1394.) “[T]he question of whether pleadings state a real property claim is tested by a ‘“demurrer-like analysis’” that centers on the adequacy of the pleading. [Citation.] It is strictly a binary process: If you properly plead a real property claim, you can file a notice of lis pendens; if you don't, you can't.” (Id. at p. 1395.)

Code Civ. Proc. section 405.32 provides that “[i]n proceedings under this chapter, the court shall order that the notice be expunged if the court finds that the claimant has not established by a preponderance of the evidence the probable validity of the real property claim.”

“Unlike other motions, the burden is on the party opposing the motion to expunge — i.e., the claimant-plaintiff — to establish the probable validity of the underlying claim. [Citation.] The claimant-plaintiff must establish the probable validity of the claim by a preponderance of the evidence.” (Howard S. Wright Construction Co. v. Superior Court (2003) 106 Cal.App.4th 314, 319.) “[T]he plaintiff must ‘at least establish a prima facie case. If the defendant makes an appearance, the court must then consider the relative merits of the positions of the respective parties and make a determination of the probable outcome of the litigation.’ [Citation.]” (Ibid.)

 

Defendants Paul Hemming, Wendy Hemming, Terrence Hemming, Wendell Jamon Jones, Realty One Group, Infinity, and Garfield Building LA, LLC (“Defendants”) move to expunge the lis pedens notice recorded on July 7, 2023, on certain real property known to the parties as the Garfield Building. Defendants argue that Plaintiff Newgen Capital LLC (“Plaintiff”) has not alleged a real property claim on this property, and cannot establish the probable validity of his claims. (Motion at pp. 6–14.)

 

Defendants argue that Plaintiff has pleaded only a request for a constructive trust on real property that it contends belongs to a partnership alleged in the Complaint, which is not a real property claim as held in BGJ Associates, LLC v. Superior Court (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 952. (Motion at pp. 7–8.)

 

Defendants’ characterization of the holding in BGJ Associates is accurate. BGJ applied a line of cases holding that claims for the remedy of a constructive trust as a means of securing a prayer for damages were not “real property claims” under the lis pendens statute. (Id. at p. 970.) The court there addressed an alleged joint venture, and noted, “[T]his case is unlike any of the cases in the line from Burger to Lewis, because here plaintiffs do not seek a constructive trust remedy solely as “collateral” for money damages. In part they seek to be awarded title to the same specific real property, the property they bargained for, which is the subject of the same wrongful conduct giving rise to the constructive trust remedy in the first place.” (Id. at pp. 971–972.) The court ultimately held that even in such a case, no lis pendens was available: “In a case such as this where the pleading combines theories of liability for monetary damages and for a constructive trust, we hold that plaintiffs should not be able to maintain a lis pendens. The danger is too great that a lis pendens, which effectively renders the property unmarketable, will have the coercive effects condemned by the cases.” (Id. at p. 972.)

 

The difficulty with Defendants’ argument as to the existence of a real property claim is “that the BGJ Associates court's approach has since been discredited.” (Shoker v. Superior Court (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 271, 280.) The court in Shoker disapproved BGJ and held that the assertion of a constructive trust through a real property claim could proceed, notwithstanding the concurrent allegation of other claims for damages. (Shoker, supra, 81 Cal.App.5th at p. 283.)

 

Here, Plaintiff asserts a constructive trust claim, and claims for damages, but does not assert the former merely as a means of preserving the latter. Rather, the constructive trust is sought in order to return property that Plaintiff alleges was wrongfully taken from the alleged partnership. Defendants in reply do not address cases subsequently disapproving BGJ, and do not persist in arguing that the Complaint alleges no real property claim.

 

Thus Plaintiff may maintain the lis pendens if he establishes the probable validity of his claims. These claims rest upon the existence of a partnership agreement between Plaintiff and Defendants Paul and Wendy Hemming. (Complaint ¶¶ 16, 22, 26, 39, 48.) Defendants argue that no such agreement was formed. (Motion at pp. 9–11.)

 

“[T]he association of two or more persons to carry on as coowners a business for profit forms a partnership, whether or not the persons intend to form a partnership.” (Corp. Code, § 16202, subd. (a).)

 

The question of the existence of a partnership depends primarily upon the intention of the parties ascertained from the terms of the agreement and from the surrounding circumstances. [Citations.] Ordinarily the existence of a partnership is evidenced by the right of the respective parties to participate in the profits and losses and in the management of the business. [Citations.] In ascertaining the intention of the parties, where they have entered into a written agreement, such intention should be determined chiefly from the terms of the writing. [citation]. While the question of whether a partnership exists is to be determined from the nature of the relation agreed upon rather than the name which the parties have given to it, some weight must be given to the language of the parties themselves. [Citations.] It is the intention as evidenced by the terms of the agreement, and not the subjective or undisclosed intention of the parties, that controls.”

 

(Eng v. Brown (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 675, 694.)

 

Defendants argue that the evidence does not support the existence of a partnership agreement to purchase real property at a specific price, as alleged in the FAC. Defendants note that one of Plaintiff’s  managing members testified at deposition that no final purchase price had been agreed when the alleged partnership was formed. (Viner Decl. Exh. F at pp. 238–239.) Defendant Paul Hemming describes the meeting where the partnership agreement allegedly took place as operating only on general terms, with no commitment to form a partnership. (P. Hemming Decl. ¶ 5.) Plaintiff notes that shortly after this meeting Plaintiff prepared a purchase proposal on the subject property the proposal listed only Plaintiff as the buyer, and did not appear to account for any proposed partnership contribution. (Viner Decl. Exh. J.) When Plaintiff subsequently emailed Paul Hemming a different proposal with different terms (P. Hemming Decl. Exh. B), he rejected it telephonically the same day. (Id. at  ¶ 7.) And although Plaintiff characterizes the agreement as one with both Paul and Wendy Hemming, Wendy never attended any meeting in which the proposed partnership on the building at issue in this litigation was discussed. (Viner decl. Exh. I at pp. 117–118.)

 

Plaintiff in opposition argues that it had long hoped to purchase the relevant property, and had devoted substantial work into pursuing it, including an offer in 2021. (Shahin Simon Neman (“Simon”) Decl. ¶ 4.) It presents a declaration of one of its managing members stating that Defendant Paul Hemming had approached Plaintiff to purchase property downtown in partnership with Plaintiff, and that Plaintiff in response revealed confidential details of the subject property and proposed transaction to him. (Simon Decl. ¶¶ 7–8.)

 

Plaintiff has not satisfied its burden to show a probability of success on its real property claims. Although Plaintiff argues that Paul Hemming agreed to enter a partnership to purchase the subject property, Paul Hemming denies such an agreement. The contemporaneous purchase proposal which did not include any mention of Defendants corroborates Defendants’ account. Additionally, Plaintiff’s account does not account for the position of Wendy Hemming, another alleged partner who did not participate in the discussions. Although Plaintiff makes an adequate showing that the parties were negotiating a partnership deal on the subject property, its evidence that such an agreement was ultimately reached is not persuasive. To the extent confidential information was disclosed during these negotiations, they were evidently disclosed in the hopes that Defendants would agree to the partnership terms proposed. They do not demonstrate that any such agreement was reached.

 

Accordingly, the motion to expunge lis pendens is GRANTED. 

 

“The court shall direct that the party prevailing on any motion under this chapter be awarded the reasonable attorney's fees and costs of making or opposing the motion unless the court finds that the other party acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of attorney's fees and costs unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 405.38.) Defendants here seek $5,097.00 in fees, representing 3.3 hours and 4.5 hours of attorney work respectively conducted at $665 and $645 per hour. (Viner Decl. ¶ 10.)

 

No fees are awarded, as Plaintiff opposed the motion with substantial justification.

Amber Villagante v. Tesla, Inc., et al.

 

Monday, September 28, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 25STCV15739

                                         

UNOPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Defendant Tesla, Inc.’s Motion to Compel Arbitration,.

___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is a lemon law action. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff Amber Villagante (“Plaintiff”) purchased a 2023 Tesla Model Y on September 4, 2023, with an express written warranty. (Complaint ¶ 6.) During the warranty period, the vehicle contained or developed various defects that the manufacturer has been unable to remedy. (Complaint ¶¶ 7–15.)

 

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on June 27, 2025, alleging five causes of action:

1.      Civ. Code § 1793.2(d)

2.      Civ. Code § 1793.2(b)

3.      Civ. Code § 1793.2(a)(3)

4.      Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability

5.      Fraudulent Concealment

 

Defendant Tesla Inc. filed the present motion to compel arbitration on January 20, 2026. No opposition has been filed.

 

Analysis:

I.                   MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement to arbitrate a controversy, a court must order the petitioner and respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines the arbitration agreement exists, unless (1) the petitioner has waived its right to arbitrate; (2) grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement; or (3) “[a] party to the arbitration agreement is also a party to a pending court action or special proceeding with a third party, arising out of the same transaction or series of related transactions and there is a possibility of conflicting rulings on a common issue of law or fact.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.)

 

“[T]he party moving to compel arbitration bears the burden of establishing the existence of a valid agreement to arbitrate, and the party opposing arbitration bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense. The role of the trial court is to sit as a trier of fact, weighing any affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, together with oral testimony received at the court's discretion, to reach a determination on the issue of arbitrability.” (Hotels Nevada v. L.A. Pacific Center, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 754, 758.)

 

Defendant Tesla, Inc. (“Defendant”) moves to compel arbitration of the claims of Plaintiff Amber Villagante (“Plaintiff”) based on an arbitration agreement contained in the Motor Vehicle Offer Agreement (“MBOA”) electronically executed by Plaintiff when she purchased the vehicle over the internet. (Kim Decl. ¶¶ 3–4, Exh. 1.) In that agreement, the signatory agrees that  “any dispute arising out of or relating to any aspect of the relationship between you and Tesla will not be decided by a judge or jury but instead by a single arbitrator in an arbitration administered by the American Arbitration Association (AAA) under its Consumer Arbitration Rules.” (Kim Decl. Exh. 1 at p. 3.) Plaintiff also signed another arbitration agreement upon taking delivery of the vehicle, requiring arbitration of “[a]ny claim or dispute. . . . between you and us . . . which arises out of or relates to your credit application, purchase or condition of this vehicle, this contract or any resulting transaction or relationship (including any such relationship with third parties who do not sign this contract) shall, at your or our election, be resolved by neutral, binding arbitration and not by a court action..” (Kim Decl. Exh. 2.)

 

Defendant has shown the existence of an arbitration agreement applicable to the present controversy. Plaintiff has filed no opposition to the motion.

 

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED.

Case Number: 26STCV00634    Hearing Date: September 28, 2026    Dept: 732

 

Luckett Palmer Jr. v. Carrie Y. Palmer, et al.

 

Monday, September 28, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 26STCV00634

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Plaintiff Luckett Palmer Jr.’s Motion for Interlocutory Judgment

 

Defendant Carrie Y. Palmer’s Motion for Reconsideration

 _________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for partition. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff Luckett Palmer Jr. (“Plaintiff”) and Defendant Carrie Palmer (“Defendant”) are joint tenants with 50% interests in property on Hobart Boulevard in Los Angeles. (Complaint ¶¶ 1–3.) Plaintiff seeks partition by sale of the property. (Complaint ¶ 122.) 

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on January 8, 2026, alleging one cause of action for partition.

This court on April 14, 2026, granted in part Defendant’s motion for contribution of mortgage payments.

On April 29, 2026, this court denied Defendant’s motion for an accounting.

On June 24, 2026, this court denied Plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses from Defendant.

Plaintiff filed the present motion for interlocutory judgment on March 18, 2026. Defendant filed an opposition on March 24, 2026. Plaintiff filed a reply on April 14, 2026.

On April 21, 2026, this court continued hearing on Plaintiff’s motion for interlocutory judgment.

Plaintiff filed the present motion for reconsideration on August 24, 2026. Defendant filed an opposition on September 16, 2026. Plaintiff filed a reply on September 21, 2026.

Analysis

 

I.                   MOTION FOR INTERLOCUTORY JUDGMENT

“If the court finds that the plaintiff is entitled to partition, it shall make an interlocutory judgment that determines the interests of the parties in the property and orders the partition of the property and, unless it is to be later determined, the manner of partition.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 872.720, subd. (a).)

 

“ ‘[P]artition’ is ‘the procedure for segregating and terminating common interests in the same parcel of property.’ ” (14859 Moorpark Homeowner's Assn. v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396, 1404-1405, 74 Cal.Rptr.2d 712.) It is a “ ‘ “remedy much favored by the law. The original purpose of partition was to permit cotenants to avoid the inconvenience and dissension arising from sharing joint possession of land. An additional reason to favor partition is the policy of facilitating transmission of title, thereby avoiding unreasonable restraints on the use and enjoyment of property.” ’ ” (Cummings, supra, 13 Cal.App.5th at p. 596, 220 Cal.Rptr.3d 463.)

 

The governing statute is section 872.720. Subdivision (a) declares that “[i]f the court finds that the plaintiff is entitled to partition, it shall make an interlocutory judgment that determines the interests of the parties in the property and orders the partition of the property.” (§ 872.720, subd. (a).) The order of partition “shall order that the property be divided among the parties in accordance with their interests as determined in the interlocutory judgment.” (§ 872.810.) Section 872.720, subdivision (b), allows the court to issue sequential interlocutory judgments for original concurrent and successive owners if the court determines that it “is impracticable or highly inconvenient to make a single interlocutory judgment that determines, in the first instance, the interest of all the parties in the property.” (§ 872.720, subd. (b).)

 

When the trial court “determines the interests of the parties in the property and orders the partition of the property,” it shall decide the manner of partition “unless [this] is to be later determined.” (§ 872.720, subd. (a).) “The manner of partition may be ‘in kind’—i.e., physical division of the property [citation] according to the parties’ interests as determined in the interlocutory judgment. [Citations.] Alternatively, if the parties agree or the court concludes it ‘would be more equitable,’ the court may order the property sold and the proceeds divided among the parties.” (Cummings, supra, 13 Cal.App.5th at p. 597, 220 Cal.Rptr.3d 463; § 872.820.)

 

Two points are made clear by these provisions.  First an interlocutory judgment in a partition action is to include two elements: a determination of the parties’ interests in the property and an order granting the partition. (§ 872.720, subd. (a).) Second, the manner of partition—i.e., a physical division or sale of the property—is to be decided when or after the parties’ ownership interests are determined, but not before. (Ibid.)

 

(Summers v. Superior Court (2018) 24 Cal.App.5th 138, 142–143.)

 

Plaintiff Luckett Palmer Jr. (“Plaintiff”) seeks an interlocutory judgment determining the interests of himself and Defendant Carrie Y. Palmer (“Defendant”) with respect to the real property at issue, and ordering its partition by sale under the supervision of a referee. (Motion at pp. 7–8.) Plaintiff presents the deed in which the parties were each made equal joint tenants of the property in question. (Anand Decl. Exh. 3.) Per an attached title report, the property is a “single family residence.” (Anand Decl. Exh. 2.) Defendant testifies in opposition that the property is a duplex with separate living spaces. (Carrie Palmer Decl. ¶ 35.)

 

Defendant opposes the motion on the grounds that no accounting has yet taken place, and on the grounds that she seeks to exercise a statutory option to buy out Plaintiff’s interest in the property. (Opposition at pp. 2–4.)

 

Both arguments are unpersuasive. First, the statutory buyout right that Defendant identifies (See Code Civ. Proc., § 874.317) applies to tenancies in common, not joint tenancies like that at issue here. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 874.311, subd. (b) [“This act applies to real property held in tenancy in common where there is no agreement in a record binding all the cotenants which governs the partition of the property.”].) Second, the accounting between the parties may be conducted after the sale of the property, and take into account the proceeds thereof. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 873.850.)

 

Plaintiff seeks the appointment of a referee to determine the manner of sale and to conduct the sale. (Motion at pp. 7–8.) “The referee appointed by the court to make a sale of the property shall sell the property in the manner and following the procedures provided in this chapter.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 873.510.) “The property shall be sold at public auction or private sale as the court determines will be more beneficial to the parties. For the purpose of making this determination, the court may refer the matter to the referee and take into account the referee's report.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 873.520.)

 

Partition by sale in this manner does not prevent Defendant from attempting to buy out Plaintiff’s interest in the property for such price as Plaintiff is willing to accept. Prior to the sale, Plaintiff and Defendant may employ appraisers to evaluate the property, and either party may make such offers for the interest of the other, acceptance of which may obviate the necessity of a sale to a third party. But to mandate that either party accept such a buyout to the preference of sale to a third party risks preventing both parties from obtaining the highest available price for the property. Nor is it necessary or equitable that an accounting take place prior to the sale of the property, as the taking of such an accounting would delay proceedings and prolong the period of  Plaintiff and Defendant’s contentious co-ownership of the premises. An accounting is properly had following the sale of the property or buyout of one of the other party’s interests therein.

 

Defendant in her presently pending motion for reconsideration argues that this court ought to order an accounting prior to interlocutory judgment, and thereafter permit Defendant to buy out Plaintiff’s interest in the property, adjusted for the credits and debits accounted for. (Motion for Reconsideration at p. 6.) Plaintiff argues that since prior motions on her request for accounting and buyout were denied, she has obtained the means of financing any proposed buyout, including lender pre-approval for a loan of $504,000.00 (Defendant Decl. Exh. A), and a written investor agreement to pay $400,000.00 toward Defendant’s purchase of Plaintiff’s interest in the property (Defendant Decl. Exh. B.) Defendant argues that these funds are sufficient to cover any adjudicated interest that Plaintiff may have in the property, given its appraised value of $941,000, the current mortgage balance of $506,112.49, and thus $434,887.51 in equity remaining in the property, which, prior to accounting of credits and debits, would be split equally between Plaintiff and Defendant upon sale. (Defendant Decl. ¶ 18.)

 

Plaintiff’s motion for reconsideration does not alter the equitable analysis undertaken above. The new evidence presented by Plaintiff consists of evidence that, if an accounting and buyout were ordered rather than a third-party sale, she would be able to finance the buyout. But while this evidence shows that a buyout is financially possible on Plaintiff’s end, it does not compel a conclusion that such a remedy is equitable or efficient in this case.

 

Plaintiff has demonstrated that he and Defendant have equal joint tenancies in the property and that partition by sale is proper due to the impracticability of partition in kind. Accordingly, an interlocutory judgment shall be entered and a referee appointed to conduct the sale.

 

The motion for interlocutory judgment is therefore GRANTED. Defendant’s motion for reconsideration is DENIED.
