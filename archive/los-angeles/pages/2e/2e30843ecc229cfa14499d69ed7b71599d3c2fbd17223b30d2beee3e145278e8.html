BACKGROUND

 

This case arises from a dispute between adjacent property owners Plaintiff Northwestern Engineering Company (“Plaintiff” or “Northwestern”) and Defendant Joseph Shemaria (“Defendant” or “Shemaria”). 

 

Case number SC128312 was filed on November 1, 2017, and involves a dispute over a portion of the pathway between the properties, located toward the front of the properties, where Plaintiff has historically kept items in storage.  The operative verified Second Amended Complaint alleges four causes of action for (1) quiet title to a prescriptive easement; (2) trespass; (3) nuisance; and (4) temporary restraining order and preliminary injunction.

 

The crux of the complaint asserts that Plaintiff has used the “Breezeway,” a path that runs from Ocean Front Walk at the front or western part of the properties to the “Speedway,” an alley at the rear or eastern part of the properties for ingress and egress since July 1999 and has made improvements, including erecting shelving for storage and pouring concrete on a portion of the pathway.  (SAC ¶¶ 7-8.)

 

On May 6, 2019, Shemaria filed a Verified Cross-Complaint in case number SC128312 for (1) trespass, (2) unjust enrichment, and (3) nuisance.  The crux of Shemaria’s cross-complaint is that from 1983 through 2017, Plaintiff and its tenants used the Breezeway with Shemaria’s permission to store beer kegs and other materials, but Shemaria revoked this permission in June 2017 and demanded that Plaintiff remove the kegs, an enclosure, and shelving, which Plaintiff has refused to do.  (Cross-Complaint ¶¶ 2-5, 10-11.)

 

Case number 22SMCV00758, which was filed on May 24, 2022, concerns a fence Defendant allegedly constructed in December 2021, creating a barrier between the properties and preventing Plaintiff and its tenants from receiving deliveries and disposing of trash via a southeastern corner of Defendant’s property, as they had done since 1999.  (Complaint ¶¶ 8-12.) 

 

Following a bench trial in the related cases (consolidated for trial only), the Court issued an Amended Statement of Decision on June 11, 2026. 

 

Regarding SC128312, the Court entered judgment in Defendant’s favor on the SAC, and entered judgment in Plaintiff’s favor on the Cross-Complaint. 

 

Regarding 22SMCV00758, the Court entered judgment in Plaintiff’s favor concerning the first two causes of action for a prescriptive easement and Defendant’s unreasonable interference therewith vis-à-vis the construction of a fence blocking the Breezeway, as well as the fourth cause of action for injunctive relief, but entered judgment in Defendant’s favor concerning the third cause of action for private nuisance.

 

Defendant now moves for a new trial, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 657.  The Notice of Intent lists the following five grounds for the motion: (1) irregularity in the proceedings; (2) accident or surprise, which ordinary prudence could not have guarded against; (3) newly discovered evidence, material to Defendant that could not have been discovered and presented with reasonable diligence at trial; (4) insufficiency of the evidence to justify the verdict and/or decision, and is contrary to law; and (5) legal errors occurring at trial that were properly excepted to.

 

However, the memorandum of points and authorities addresses only two grounds: (1) insufficiency of the evidence pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 657, subdivision (6) and (2) errors in law pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 657, subdivision (7).  As such, the Court deems forfeited any arguments as to irregularity in the proceedings, accident or surprise, and newly discovered evidence.

 

Plaintiff opposes the motion.

 

LEGAL STANDARDS – MOTION FOR A NEW TRIAL

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 657, in pertinent part, provides:

 

The verdict may be vacated and any other decision may be modified or vacated, in whole or in part, and a new or further trial granted on all or part of the issues, on the application of the party aggrieved, for any of the following causes, materially affecting the substantial rights of such party:

 

6.     Insufficiency of the evidence to justify the verdict or other decision, or the verdict or other decision is against law.

 

7.     Error in law, occurring at the trial and excepted to by the party making the application.

 

TIMELINESS

 

            Motions for New Trials are governed by strict, jurisdictional statutory time limits.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 659.)

 

            The notice of motion must be filed and served within 15 days of the Clerk of the Court’s notice of entry of judgment.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 659, subd. (a)(2).)  Here, the judgment and the notice of entry of judgment were entered on July 22, 2026.  Fifteen days later is August, 6, 2026.  Defendant timely filed a Notice of Intent to Move for New Trial on August 6, 2026.

 

            Further, 10 days after filing the notice, the moving party must file and serve the motion(s) and supporting papers.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 659a.)  Ten days after August 6 is Sunday, August 16.    By operation of Code of Civil Procedure section 12a, subdivision (b), which expressly applies to Section 659’s deadlines, the deadline was extended to Monday, August 17.  Defendant timely filed and electronically served a Memorandum of Points and Authorities and Declaration in support of the Motion for New Trial on August 17.

 

            The opposing party has 10 days after service of the moving papers to serve the opposing papers.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 659a.)  That deadline may be extended by up to 10 days, for good cause shown by affidavit.  (Ibid.)  On August 31, Plaintiff filed and served the opposition.

 

            The Court notes that while Code of Civil Procedure sections 1013 and 1010.6 do not apply to extend the time within which the moving party may file a Notice of Intent to Move for New Trial (see Code Civ. Proc., § 659, subd. (b); § 1013, subd. (a), (c), (e); § 1010.6, subd. (a)(3)(B)), it is not immediately clear to the Court that they do not apply to extend the time within which the opposing party may file an opposition to the motion. 

 

Notably, the notice of intent must be filed within fifteen days after service by the clerk of the notice of entry of judgment, and that time is not extended by operation of Sections 1013 or 1010.6, but the opposition to the motion must be filed within only ten days after service by the moving party of the motion and supporting papers.  Thus, it appears as though the five additional days for service by mail are incorporated into the moving party’s fifteen-day deadline, but not the opposing party’s deadline. 

 

Even further, the Court is generally within its discretion to extend the opposition deadline by up to 10 days, and the Court does not generally lose jurisdiction to consider late-filed affidavits, especially where, as here, the opposing party has not objected to the timeliness.  As such, the Court exercises its discretion and considers the opposition.

 

            The reply is due five days after service of the opposition.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 659a.)  Five days after August 31, is Saturday, September 5, 2026.  By operation of Code of Civil Procedure section 12a, subdivision (b), which expressly applies to Section 659’s deadlines, the deadline was extended to Monday, September 7, 2026.  As of September 21, 2026, no Reply had been received by the Court. 

 

ANALYSIS

 

I.                INSUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE

 

Defendant argues insufficiency of the evidence in four ways:

 

1.     Insufficiency of the evidence to establish a “definite and certain” path of use.

 

2.     Insufficiency of the evidence to find the necessary element to establish Plaintiff’s use was “open and notorious” as opposed to mere passive acquiescence.

 

3.     Insufficiency of the evidence to establish Plaintiff’s usage was “continuous and uninterrupted” for five (5) years.

 

4.     Insufficiency of the evidence to establish Plaintiff’s five (5) years of usage of the Speedway.

 

a.     Definite and Certain Path of Use

 

Defendant first argues that because the Court determined that Plaintiff’s access was limited so as not to interfere with parked vehicles, Plaintiff was effectively prevented from using the “easement” to the dumpsters and to receive deliveries except when the parking space was vacant, transforming it to a conditional privilege, not a true easement.

 

The Court actually held (1) Plaintiff met its burden of proof by clear and convincing evidence that Plaintiff and/or its tenants have since 1999 traversed over the 42” by 98” corner of Defendant’s parking lot for the purposes of disposing trash and receiving deliveries, and therefore had established a definite and certain path of use; (2) Plaintiff merely requested to continue traversing the Speedway without permanent impediments, such as fencing or a barrier; (3) Defendant failed to present evidence substantiating his argument that Plaintiff’s requested prescriptive easement would require Defendant to reconfigure and re-stripe the parking lot; and (4) Defendant’s contention that Plaintiff intended to use the Speedway for illegal purposes, such as a “dump site” creating a public nuisance was unsupported by the evidence.

 

In essence, Defendant is arguing that because the Court held both parties were entitled to use and traverse the Speedway, so long as neither party erected a permanent or immovable barrier preventing the other’s use, Plaintiff’s ability to use the Speedway is effectively blocked whenever a car is temporarily parked there, and because Plaintiff’s ability to traverse the Speedway is sometimes blocked, it is not a true easement.

 

The only case Defendant cites in support of his argument is Connolly v. McDermott (1984) 162 Cal.App.3d 973.  That case focused on the scope of use of the prescriptive easement, not temporary obstacles in the easement due to the nature of the joint use.   The court first explained:   “The scope of a prescriptive easement is determined by the use through which it is acquired. A person using the land of another for the prescriptive period may acquire the right to continue such use, but does not acquire the right to make other uses of it.”  (Id. at p. 977.)

 

The appellate court concluded the trial court had not erred in restricting the scope of the easement at issue to “livestock herding operations” by “men and horses,” refusing to grant an easement for motor vehicle use, notwithstanding evidence that motorcycles also sometimes traversed the easement, because the evidence showed plaintiffs concealed their vehicular use from defendants, and thus, the vehicular use was not “open and notorious” and expanding the easement to allow motor vehicle traffic would constitute a substantial new burden on the servient tenement, as motor vehicles like motorcycles create more noise than livestock.  (Connolly, supra, 162 Cal.App.3d at pp. 977-979.)

 

But nothing in Connolly supports Defendant’s argument that the path cannot be properly characterized as an easement if sometimes the other party’s joint use of the area temporarily blocks the path.

 

Further, Defendant has not established that there was insufficient evidence supporting the Court’s determination that the easement in question constitutes the 42” by 98” corner of Defendant’s parking lot.

 

b.     Open and Notorious

 

Defendant’s “open and notorious” argument is not the model of clarity.  It appears as though Defendant is arguing that Plaintiff’s use was not “open and notorious” because it was not adverse to Defendant and Defendant acquiesced in Plaintiff’s use.[1]

 

The elements for a prescriptive easement are for a period of five years prior to the commencement of the action:

 

1.     Plaintiff has been using Defendant’s property;

 

2.     Plaintiff’s use was continuous and uninterrupted;

 

3.     The use was open and easily observable, or was under circumstances that would give reasonable notice to Defendant; and

 

4.     Plaintiff did not have Defendant’s permission to use the property.

 

(See CACI 4901 (2026 ed.))

 

Thus, to be “open and notorious” the use must have been open and easily observable, or done under circumstances that would give reasonable notice to the Defendant.  Because Defendant argues he acquiesced in Plaintiff’s use, Defendant concedes he had actual notice of the use, making such use open and notorious.

 

Further, the Court found Exhibits 201, 221, 223, 224, 226, 227, and 227 (photos taken by the Defendant” illustrate the “open and easily observable use of the Speedway by Plaintiff and its tenants” or that Defendant had reasonable notice of Plaintiff’s and its tenants’ use.

 

Thus, Defendant has not demonstrated that the Court’s “open and notorious” finding was inadequately supported by the evidence.

 

c.      Continuous and Uninterrupted for Five Years

 

Defendant argues the Court’s finding that Plaintiff’s use was continuous and uninterrupted for a period of five years was unsupported by the evidence, because Plaintiff did not advance any evidence about how often the rear parking space was occupied, effectively blocking the easement.  Further, Defendant argues that the Court’s finding that Plaintiff’s use of the Speedway did not interfere with Defendant’s tenants’ parked vehicles was a tacit recognition that the tenants’ parking rights conflict with Plaintiff’s purported rights in the easement. 

 

But Defendant’s own authorities demonstrate such evidence was not required. 

 

To acquire a prescriptive easement, the easement must be used in the required manner continuously and without interruption for the full prescriptive period [of five years]. The actual use required depends on the nature of the easement. It need not be used every day during the prescriptive period. The use is sufficient if it occurs on those occasions when it is necessary for the convenience of the user.  If a right of way over another's land has been used for more than five years, it is not necessary, to make good such use, that the claimant has used it every day.  He uses it every day, or once in every week, or twice a month, as his needs require. He is not required to go over it when he does not need it, to make his use of the way continuous.  The claimant is required to make such reasonable use of the way as his needs require.

(Fogerty v. State of California (1986) 187 Cal.App.3d 224, 239 [cleaned up].)

 

            Further, Defendant cites to Zimmer v. Dykstra (1974) 39 Cal.App.3d 422.  In Zimmer, a landowner obstructed its neighbor’s use of the landowner’s half of the alleyway by erecting a “grape stake” fence, permitting her tenants to park automobiles there, and erecting a chain link fence.  The trial court held that the plaintiffs had a prescriptive easement over the defendant’s half of the alleyway, ordered her to remove the chain link fence, and awarded damages.  The Court of appeal affirmed.  The Defendant had argued the Plaintiffs had never acquired a prescriptive easement, and even if they had, it had been extinguished.  The Court of Appeal disagreed on the ground that neither the “grape stake” fence, nor the parking of automobiles, nor the chain link fence existed for the statutory period for Defendant to reclaim the land and they did not meet the criteria for “tacking.” 

 

            Here, the Court cited the following evidence as supporting its finding that Plaintiff and its tenants used the Speedway continuously since 1999 to access trash dumpsters: RT 1 - 157:11-160:15; 166:6-26; 199:1-15; 200:1-23; Exh. 75, p. 14.)  Further, Zachary Best testified that he and other employees of the Venice Beach Bar traverse the Speedway several times per day to dispose of trash or accept deliveries.  (RT 2 – 38:4-39:18; 40:3-19.)  Further, Jim Adelstein testified he observed employees of Plaintiff’s tenants Venice Bistro and Piccolo use the Speedway to access the dumpster and to receive deliveries.  (RT 1 at 38:11-18; 56:1-7; 58:24-28.)  Further, Antonio Mure testified his employees would use the Speedway to access trash bins when he leased space from Plaintiff.  (RT 2 – 4:12-28; 24:12-17.)

 

            That a tenant’s car may have occasionally parked in the parking space, temporarily obstructing Plaintiff’s use of the Speedway does not, pursuant to Fogarty, undermine Plaintiff’s continuous and uninterrupted use.  Further, pursuant to Zimmer, the burden is on Defendant to demonstrate he reclaimed the Speedway by permanently blocking Plaintiff’s path with a parked vehicle, not on Plaintiff to demonstrate how often and for how long a car was sometimes parked there.

 

            Therefore, Defendant has not met his burden of demonstrating that the evidence inadequately supports the Court’s finding of continuous use for a period of five years.

 

II.             ERRORS IN LAW

 

Defendant argues there were three errors in law:

 

1.     Granting judgment in favor of Plaintiff on the first and second causes of action because the Complaint in the 22SMCV00758 matter was filed prior to the required five (5) years of usage and should have been dismissed.

 

2.     Shifting the burden of proof to Defendant prior to Plaintiff establishing a prima facie case for all necessary elements for a prescriptive easement.

 

3.     Requiring Defendant to prove express “consent” as opposed to implied consent or mere acquiescence and/or a “neighborly accommodation.”

 

a.     22SMCV00758 Complaint Timeliness

 

Plaintiff alleged, and as discussed above, the evidence presented at trial supported the Court’s finding that Plaintiff had traversed the Speedway to receive deliveries and access the dumpster since July 1999.  The Complaint in Case No. 22SMCV00758 was filed more than five years later, on May 24, 2022.

 

Defendant argues that Mr. Mure testified that he moved into the Piccolo restaurant location in March 2004 and left in the middle of 2009.  Because Mr. Adelstein (Plaintiff’s president) did not take over Mr. Abelar’s restaurant until July 2016, more than five years later, Mr. Mure’s time cannot be tacked onto Adelstein’s time.  Not so.

 

Plaintiff purchased the multi-tenant retail property in 1999.  Plaintiff’s tenants include the Venice Bistro, formerly owned by Abelar until it was directly taken over by Adelstein in 2016, Venice Beach Bar, currently managed by Best, and the former Piccolo restaurant, which was co-owned by Mure from March 2004 to 2009.  Thus, the use by any of these tenants constitute Plaintiff’s continuous use of the Speedway.  Taken together, the evidence demonstrates that Plaintiff’s tenants, including Venice Bistro, Venice Beach Bar, and Piccolo Restaurant used the Speedway continuously for the statutory period long before the Complaint was filed in 2022. 

 

  Thus, there was no legal error in considering the 22SMCV00758 complaint, as it was filed more than five years after the continuous and uninterrupted uses began. 

 

b.     Shifting the Burden of Proof to Defendant and Express Consent versus Implied Consent or Acquiescence

 

As discussed above, because there was no error in determining that the five-year prescriptive period had run before the Complaint was filed, there was similarly no error in shifting the burden of proof to Defendant to demonstrate otherwise.

 

            As for consent, the Court found Defendant not credible in his testimony about consent, because although he first testified that he gave Abelar permission to access the Speedway in June 1984 and revoked that permission in June 2017, on cross-examination he denied ever having granted permission and seemed ambivalent about use of the Speedway.  Further, this testimony contrasted with Adelstein’s testimony that neither Abelar nor other tenants told him they had permission to use the Speedway, Defendant’s insistence that he did not give Adelstein permission to use the Speedway, Mure’s testimony that neither Defendant nor anyone else gave him permission to use the Speedway, and Best’s testimony that Best never asked Defendant for permission to use the Speedway.  Even further, in Exhibit 201, Defendant tacitly concedes he did not give permission to use the Speedway.

 

            Taken together, the Court found a reasonable inference that the tenants used the Speedway without Defendant’s consent.  Thus, there was no error in law in finding Plaintiffs had proved no consent to use the Speedway by clear and convincing evidence and Defendant failed to meet his evidentiary burden to demonstrate otherwise in defense of Plaintiff’s showing.

 

III.           INTERNALLY INCONSISTENT AND IRRECONCILABLE FINDINGS

 

Although not clearly stated in the introduction roadmap to his memorandum of points and authorities, Defendant also argues the Court made several internally inconsistent and irreconcilable findings.

 

a.     Inconsistent Credibility Determinations Regarding Defendant’s Testimony on the Speedway versus the Breezeway

 

First, Defendant argues the Court applied opposite credibility determinations to the same witness on the same facts within a single ruling.  Specifically, Defendant contends the statement of decision reaches opposite conclusions on the Breezeway and Speedway, despite an evidentiary record that is essentially the same. 

 

As to the Breezeway, the Court gave more weight to Defendant’s testimony that he granted Abelar permission to use the Breezeway in June 1984, as corroborated by the January 7, 2000 letter, purporting to “re-establish” or take “back” the three feet pathway from the Boardwalk to the parking lot (effectively revoking consent thereto), and found that Adelstein’s failure to confer with Defendant about the 2000 letter and its June 2016 email seeking permission to install steel gates and to concrete in the entire space demonstrated Plaintiff knew it lacked rights to the Breezeway.   

 

Yet, as to the Speedway, the Court found Defendant not credible in light of the fact that Defendant filed to proffer any supporting documentary evidence that he gave permission for Plaintiff or its tenants to use the Speedway.

 

Defendant argues while the Court is within its discretion to believe a witness on one point but disbelieve that same witness on another point, here the Court’s disparate treatment was error, because both positions arose from the same 1984 conversation and the same landlord-tenant relationship.

 

The Court disagrees that there was any erroneous contradiction here.  In light of Defendant’s contradictory testimony and lack of supporting documentary evidence vis-à-vis the Speedway, the Court found that the 1984 conversation encompassed use of the Breezeway, but not the Speedway.  And as Defendant concedes, it is well within the Court’s discretion to determine whether a witness is lying, exaggerating, or mistaken about one point of testimony, but not another, especially where, as here, the corroborating evidence supports that split finding.

 

b.     Inconsistent Treatment of Defendant’s Revocation

 

Second, Defendant argues that the Court inconsistently treated Defendant’s 2000 revocation of permission as evidence that permission existed for the Breezeway but treated the Exhibit 201 email’s purported revocation of permission to use the Speedway tacit admission that no such permission existed in the first instance.

 

The differential treatment is explained by the contents of those respective correspondences.

 

The 2000 letter says, “I need back the three feet that runs from the Boardwalk to my parking lot” and “We have decided to re-establish our three feet pathway to the Boardwalk.”  (Ex. 2, emphasis added.)  Thus, the implication from the 2000 letter is that permission was previously granted and Defendant was revoking such permission by way of the 2000 letter.

 

By contrast, Exhibit 201, which is a June 16, 2022 email from Defendant to Plaintiff’s counsel indicates:

 

For years, I resisted putting up a fence and had to deal with any number of regular complaints from my tenants regarding the intolerable garbage/trash/foodstuffs/grease due to your client’s failure and refusal to respect my property vis-à-vis the trash situation.  If Mr. Adelstein wants to know the reason for my putting up the fence, let them know that it was a direct result of his own negligence and recklessness in the handling of what is depicted in these photographs.  Numerous former tenants have written to the Health Department regarding this long-running situation that could only be stopped by defense that I finally put in.

 

            Defendant clearly communicated dissatisfaction with Plaintiff’s tenants’ use of the dumpster by way of the Speedway, which suggests Defendant did not consent to such use.  Further, the fact that the situation “could only be stopped” by Defendant’s putting in a fence suggests the mere revocation of consent was not an available option, as it would have been had Defendant originally provided consent. 

 

            Thus, the Court did not err in reaching differing inferences from the 2000 letter vis-à-vis the Breezeway and the 2022 email regarding the Speedway on the issue of whether consent had originally been provided for each purported easement.

 

c.      Inconsistent Treatment of Adelstein’s Conduct and Testimony

 

Defendant next takes issue with the Court’s asymmetrical treatment of Adelstein’s conduct and testimony vis-à-vis the Speedway and Breezeway.  Specifically, the Court cites Adelstein’s failure to confer with Defendant about the January 2000 letter, Adelstein’s June 2016 email inquiring about gates and concrete, his failure in Exhibit 30 to disclose that he had become the owner of the Venice Bistro, and his general “state of mind” regarding the Breezeway as evidence undermining his credibility vis-à-vis the Breezeway. 

 

Yet, the Court did not make similar adverse credibility findings regarding Adelstein’s testimony vis-à-vis the Speedway.

 

But crucially, several other tenant witnesses corroborated Adelstein’s testimony vis-à-vis the Speedway, whereas the documentary evidence undermined Adelstein’s testimony vis-à-vis the Breezeway.  Thus, it was not error to make differing credibility determinations on Adelstein’s testimony about the Breezeway versus the Speedway.

 

d.     Inconsistent Weight Given to Exhibit 75

 

Defendant next challenges the Court’s giving “little weight” to Exhibit 75, yet relying on it to determine the scope of the easement.

 

Exhibit 75 is a parking analysis chart Defendant proffered as evidence that he would need to re-stripe the parking lot, if the Court found in favor of Plaintiff with respect to the Speedway easement.  The Court expressly held that granting Plaintiff a prescriptive easement over the Speedway would not prevent Defendant’s tenants from also using the Speedway, except in a manner that would permanently block Plaintiff’s use of it (for example by erecting a permanent fence or other barrier).  Thus, the parking analysis diagram did not materially support Defendant’s argument that the easement would necessitate re-striping the parking lot.

 

Notwithstanding, the diagram was useful for the Court to visualize and quantify the disputed area and proposed pathway.

 

Thus, there was no error in weighing the same exhibit differently with respect to different legal and factual arguments.

 

e.      Selective Credibility Findings Avoid the Five-Year Prescriptive Period Problem

 

Defendant points out that the Statement of Decision notes in a footnote that the 22SMCV00758 case was commenced on May 24, 2022, which is less than 5 years from the date Defendant purportedly revoked his permission to use the Speedway in June 2017.  Thus, the inconsistent credibility determination vis-à-vis the 1984 permission testimony conveniently avoids the 5-year prescriptive period problem as to the Speedway.

 

But as discussed above, the Court’s disparate treatment of the two separate easement disputes at issue is supported by the lopsided corroborating evidence in the record, as well as the differing nature and locations of the two easements.  The Breezeway involved the erection of storage shelves and the permanent storage of materials.  Evidence of this permission was corroborated by the 2000 revocation letter and Plaintiff’s own email inquiring about a grate and concrete (which would not have been necessary, had Plaintiff been under the impression that it had a legal right to the area in question). 

 

By contrast, the Speedway involved merely traversing a path at the back of the properties to access the dumpster and to receive deliveries.  Defendant proffered no corroborating documentary evidence (like the 2000 letter or 2016 email for the Breezeway) evidencing his purported permission, and Plaintiff’s narrative that no permission to use the Speedway was granted was corroborated by the testimonies of several of Plaintiff’s tenants. 

 

Thus, the nature, location, and corroborating evidence demonstrates that while Defendant formally granted Plaintiff permission to use the larger, more prominent Breezeway in a permanent way in 1984, and then attempted to revoke that permission by way of the 2000 letter, the same permission was not granted for Plaintiff’s use of the Speedway as a shortcut path to and from the dumpster and delivery trucks.

 

That the Court found permission was not granted vis-à-vis the Speedway in the same way it had been granted for the Breezeway also avoided Defendant’s ripeness arguments does not mean that there was insufficiency of the evidence or that the Court made a legal error in making the differing findings as to the different easements.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the foregoing reasons, the Court does not find any insufficiency of the evidence or errors in law to support Defendant’s request for a new trial.  As such, the Court denies Defendant’s motion for a new trial in its entirety.

LEGAL STANDARD

 

Amendments to Pleadings: General Provisions

 

            Under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (a)(1), “The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect; and may, upon like terms, enlarge the time for answer or demurrer. The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.”

 

            To wit, without notice to the other party the Court has wide discretion to allow either party (i) to add or strike the name of a party or (ii) to correct a mistake in the name of a party or a mistake in any other respect “in furtherance of justice” and “on any terms as may be proper.” (See Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a)(1); see also Marriage of Liss (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1429.) Alternatively, after notice to the other party, the Court has wide discretion to allow either party to amend pleadings “upon any terms as may be just.” (See Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a)(1). Similarly, Code of Civil Procedure section 576 states “Any judge, at any time before or after commencement of trial, in the furtherance of justice, and upon such terms as may be proper, may allow the amendment of any pleading or pretrial conference order.”

 

            Judicial policy favors resolution of all disputed matters between the parties and, therefore, leave to amend is liberally granted. (Berman vs. Bromberg (1986) 56 Cal.App.4th 936, 945; Hirsa v. Superior Court (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 486, 488-489 [this has been an established policy in California since 1901] (citing Frost v. Whitter (1901) 132 Cal. 421, 424; Thomas v. Bruza (1957) 151 Cal.App.2d 150, 155).) The Court of Appeal in Morgan v. Superior Court held “If the motion to amend is timely made and the granting of the motion will not prejudice the opposing party, it is error to refuse permission to amend and where the refusal also results in a party being deprived of the right to assert a meritorious cause of action or a meritorious defense, it is not only error but an abuse of discretion.” (Morgan v. Superior Court (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 527, 530, citations omitted.) Moreover, “it is an abuse of discretion for the court to deny leave to amend where the opposing party was not misled or prejudiced by the amendment.” (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1048 [opposing party did not establish harm by the delay in moving to amend the complaint].)

 

            “The court may grant leave to amend the pleadings at any stage of the action.” (Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶ 6:636 (hereafter Weil & Brown).) Denial of a motion to amend is rarely justified if the motion is timely made and granting the motion will not prejudice the opposing party. (Id. at ¶ 6:639, citations omitted.) However, if the party seeking the amendment has been dilatory, and the delay has prejudiced the opposing party, the judge has discretion to deny leave to amend. (Id. at ¶ 6:655, citations omitted. Absent prejudice, any claimed delay alone is not grounds for denial. “If the delay in seeking the amendment has not misled or prejudiced the other side, the liberal policy of allowing amendments prevails. Indeed, it is an abuse of discretion to deny leave in such a case even if sought as late as the time of trial. (Id. at ¶ 6:653 (citing Higgins v. Del Faro (1981) 123 Cal.App.3d 558, 564-565).) “Prejudice exists where the amendment would result in a delay of trial, along with loss of critical evidence, added costs of preparation, increased burden of discovery, etc. . . . But the fact that the amendment involves a change in legal theory which would make admissible evidence damaging to the opposing party is not the kind of prejudice the court will consider.” (Weil & Brown, supra, at ¶ 6:656, citations omitted.)

 

            “Even if some prejudice is shown, the judge may still permit the amendment but impose conditions, as the Court is authorized to grant leave ‘on such terms as may be proper.’” (Weil & Brown, supra, at ¶ 6:663, citation omitted.) For example, the court may cause the party seeking the amendment to pay the costs and fees incurred in preparing for trial. (Id. at ¶ 6:664 (citing Fuller v. Vista Del Arroyo Hotel, 42 Cal.App.2d 400, 404).)

 

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324: Procedural Requirements

 

            Pursuant to California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a), a motion to amend a pleading before trial must:

 

“(1) Include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleadings, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments;

(2) state what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and

(3) State what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.”

 

            In addition, under Rule 3.1324(b), a motion to amend a pleading before trial must be accompanied by a separate declaration that specifies the following:

 

“(1) the effect of the amendment;

(2) why the amendment is necessary and proper;

(3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and

(4) the reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier.”

LEGAL STANDARDS – MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT/ADJUDICATION

 

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law[.] There is a triable issue of material fact if, and only if, the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.” ¿(Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850 (hereafter Aguilar).) ¿“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” ¿(Ibid.; Smith v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 1463, 1474 [summary judgment standards held by Aguilar apply to summary adjudication motions].) 

 

Further, “the trial court may not weigh the evidence in the manner of a factfinder to determine whose version is more likely true.  Nor may the trial court grant summary judgment based on the court's evaluation of credibility.”  (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th. at p. 840 [cleaned up]; see also Weiss v. People ex rel. Department of Transportation (2020) 9 Cal.5th 840, 864 [“Courts deciding motions for summary judgment or summary adjudication may not weigh the evidence but must instead view it in the light most favorable to the opposing party and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of that party”].) 

 

A party may move for summary adjudication as to one or more causes of action, affirmative defenses, claims for damages, or issues of duty if that party contends that there is no merit to the cause of action, defense, or claim for damages, or if the party contends that there is no duty owed.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)  “A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.”  (Ibid.)  A cause of action has no merit if: (1) one or more elements of the cause of action cannot be separately established, even if that element is separately pleaded, or (2) a defendant establishes an affirmative defense to that cause of action.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (n); Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 573, 583.)  Once the defendant has shown that a cause of action has no merit, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of material fact exists as to that cause of action.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (o)(2); Union Bank v. Superior Court, supra, 31 Cal.App.4th at p. 583.)  Additionally, in line with Aguilar, “[o]n a motion for summary adjudication, the trial court has no discretion to exercise.  If a triable issue of material fact exists as to the challenged causes of action, the motion must be denied. If there is no triable issue of fact, the motion must be granted.”  (Fisherman's Wharf Bay Cruise Corp. v. Superior Court (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 309, 320.)

“The pleadings play a key role in a summary judgment motion.  The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues and to frame the outer measure of materiality in a summary judgment proceeding.  As our Supreme Court has explained it: The materiality of a disputed fact is measured by the pleadings, which set the boundaries of the issues to be resolved at summary judgment.  Accordingly, the burden of a defendant moving for summary judgment only requires that he or she negate plaintiff's theories of liability as alleged in the complaint; that is, a moving party need not refute liability on some theoretical possibility not included in the pleadings.”  (Hutton v. Fidelity National Title Co. (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 486, 493 [cleaned up]; see also Laabs v. City of Victorville (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1258 [“The complaint limits the issues to be addressed at the motion for summary judgment. The rationale is clear: It is the allegations in the complaint to which the summary judgment motion must respond”].)

a.     HOA’s Evidence

 

The HOA argues that the Burkes, and not the HOA, caused the water runoff damage by altering their landscaping and drainage without HOA approval,  Yet the HOA acted expeditiously in addressing the issue, and the HOA’s actions in this regard are generally presumed reasonable, under the Business Judgment Rule.

 

The HOA also argues that it worked expeditiously to resolve the tree root issue by offering to pay to repair the issue, but Plaintiffs unreasonably rejected the offer.

 

However, as indicated by the Court’s evidentiary rulings above, while Landis obviously has personal knowledge of emails upon which he was copied, as well as HOA matters generally, including the CC&Rs, his characterizations of which are corroborated by a copy of the CC&Rs attached to the Landis declaration, much of the Landis declaration constitutes matters of which lack personal knowledge, or which are inadmissible hearsay and improper opinions.

 

As to the tree root issue, none of Landis’ statements about the experts’ analyses or the HOA’s resulting actions are properly before the Court.

 

Therefore, the HOA has not met its initial prima facie burdens of production and persuasion to demonstrate that Plaintiffs will be unable to prevail on their first cause of action for breach of the CC&Rs.

 

2.     ISSUE 2 – SECOND CAUSE OF ACTION – NEGLIGENCE

 

The HOA similarly argues that its response to the water runoff and tree root issues are entitled to a presumption of reasonableness pursuant to the Business Judgment Rule.

 

But as discussed above, most of the HOA’s evidence regarding the expert analysis and the efforts it undertook are not properly before the Court.  As such, the HOA has not met its initial burdens of production and persuasion to demonstrate it did not breach its duty of care to Plaintiffs.

 

3.     ISSUE 3 – THIRD CAUSE OF ACTION – BREACH OF FIDUCIARY DUTY

 

The HOA concedes there is a special relationship giving rise to a fiduciary duty as between the HOA and its members to enforce its governing documents in good faith and not in an arbitrary, capricious, or inconsistent or unfair manner.

 

Plaintiffs argue the HOA breached its fiduciary duty by failing to properly address the drainage and tree root issues.  (Complaint ¶¶ 61-63.) 

 

Thus, as discussed above, because of the evidentiary problems, particularly as to the tree root issue, the HOA has not met its initial prima facie burdens of production and persuasion with respect to Issue 3.

 

4.     ISSUE 4 – FOURTH CAUSE OF ACTION – TRESPASS

 

            “Trespass is an unlawful interference with possession of property.”  (Ralphs Grocery Co. v. Victory Consultants, Inc. (2017) 17 Cal.App.5th 245, 261 (hereafter Ralphs).) Moreover, “Trespass may be by personal intrusion of the wrongdoer or by his failure to leave; by throwing or placing something on the land; or by causing the entry of some other person.  A trespass may be on the surface of the land, above it, or below it.”  (Martin Marietta Corp. v. Insurance Co. of North Am. (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 1113, 1132 [cleaned up].)  

           

            Against that backdrop, “the elements of trespass are: (1) the plaintiff's ownership or control of the property; (2) the defendant's intentional, reckless, or negligent entry onto the property; (3) lack of permission for the entry or acts in excess of permission; (4) harm; and (5) the defendant's conduct was a substantial factor in causing the harm.”  (Ralphs, supra, 17 Cal.App.5th at p. 262, citing CACI No. 2000.)[1]  

 

The Complaint alleges Defendants “have caused, authorized, permitted and/or allowed and/or failed to repair and correct, conditions which have caused water and roots to intrude upon Plaintiffs’ Property, causing damage to Plaintiffs’ Property, Plaintiffs’ personal property and interference with Plaintiffs’ use and enjoyment of Plaintiffs’ Property.”  (Complaint ¶ 69.)

 

HOA argues that its actions vis-à-vis the water runoff and tree root issues are entitled to deference by way of the Business Judgment Rule.

 

But as discussed above, because of the evidentiary problems, particularly with respect to the tree root issue, HOA has not met its initial prima facie burdens of production and persuasion that it did not cause or contribute to a trespass upon Plaintiffs’ property.

 

5.     ISSUE 5 – FIFTH CAUSE OF ACTION – NUISANCE

 

A “nuisance” is “[a]nything which is injurious to health, including, but not limited to, the illegal sale of controlled substances, or is indecent or offensive to the senses, or an obstruction to the free use of property, so as to interfere with the comfortable enjoyment of life or property, or unlawfully obstructs the free passage or use, in the customary manner, of any navigable lake, or river, bay, stream, canal, or basin, or any public park, square, street, or highway, is a nuisance.”  (Civ. Code, § 3479.)

 

The Complaint alleges Defendants’ conduct caused, created, and/or permitted the conditions on Plaintiffs’ property that are harmful to Plaintiffs’ health, indecent or offensive to the senses, and/or obstructs the free use of Plaintiffs’ property.  (Complaint ¶¶ 74-75.)

 

The HOA argues that its conduct is entitled to a presumption of reasonableness under the Business Judgment Rule.  But as discussed above, the HOA has failed its initial prima facie burdens of production and persuasion to demonstrate that it took reasonable actions, especially with respect to the tree root issue.

 

Further, there is no reasonableness element to nuisance – either the HOA caused or contributed to a condition which is injurious to health, indecent or offensive to the senses, or an obstruction to the free use of property, or it did not. 

 

6.     ISSUE 6 – SIXTH CAUSE OF ACTION – SELECTIVE ENFORCEMENT OF THE CC&Rs

 

HOA argues it did not selectively enforce the HOAs, as supported by the Declaration of Gregg Landis.  But as discussed above, the HOA has not met its initial prima facie burdens of production and persuasion to demonstrate that it did not selectively enforce the HOAs.

 

7.     ISSUE 7 – SEVENTH CAUSE OF ACTION – ELDER ABUSE

 

The HOA argues it did not commit elder abuse because its actions are entitled to deference pursuant to the Business Judgment Rule.  But as discussed above, the HOA has not met its initial prima facie burdens of production and persuasion with respect to its actions and whether those actions were or were not reasonable under the circumstances. 

 

8.     ISSUE 8 – EIGHTH CAUSE OF ACTION – INJUNCTIVE RELIEF

 

            “A permanent injunction is an equitable remedy for certain torts or wrongful acts of a defendant where a damage remedy is inadequate. A permanent injunction is a determination on the merits that a plaintiff has prevailed on a cause of action for tort or other wrongful act against a defendant and that equitable relief is appropriate. A permanent injunction is not issued to maintain the status quo but is a final judgment on the merits.”  (Art Movers, Inc. v. Ni West, Inc. (1992) 3 Cal.App.4th 640, 646, citation omitted; see also Civ. Code, § 3422 [“[a] final injunction may be granted to prevent the breach of an obligation existing in favor of the applicant: 1.  Where pecuniary compensation would not afford adequate relief . . . 3. Where the restraint is necessary to prevent a multiplicity of judicial proceedings”].)[2] 

 

The HOA moves for summary adjudication of the eighth cause of action on the grounds that an injunction is a remedy and not an independent cause of action.

 

            In opposition, Plaintiffs concede that injunctive relief is a remedy as opposed to a standalone cause of action, but they argue that, to the extent the other underlying causes of action have merit, injunctive relief is appropriate.

 

Therefore, the Court grants summary adjudication of the eighth cause of action on the grounds that injunctive relief is not an independent cause of action.

 

9.     ISSUE 9 – NINTH CAUSE OF ACTION – DECLARATORY RELIEF

 

“Any person interested under a written instrument, excluding a will or trust, or under a contract, or who desires a declaration of his or her rights or duties with respect to another, or in respect to, in, over or upon property […] may, in cases of actual controversy relating to the legal rights and duties of the respective parties, bring an original action or cross-complaint in the superior court for a declaration of his or her rights and duties in the premises, including a determination of any question of construction or validity arising under the instrument or contract.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1060.)  “The declaration may be either affirmative or negative in form and effect, and the declaration shall have the force of a final judgment. The declaration may be had before there has been any breach of the obligation in respect to which said declaration is sought.”  (Ibid.)

 

Causes of action for declaratory relief require “(1) a proper subject of declaratory relief, and (2) an actual controversy involving justiciable questions relating to the party’s rights or obligations.”  (Jolley v. Chase Home Finance, LLC (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 872, 909.)  Because declaratory relief operates prospectively, and not merely for the redress of past wrongs, (Snyder v. California Ins. Guarantee Assn. (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 1196, 1207), a plaintiff or cross-complainant may prevail on a claim for declaratory relief, regardless of whether they have yet suffered any damages.

 

Plaintiffs seek a judicial declaration that:

 

(a) the HOA must comply with its duties and obligations and comply with the powers and authorities provided by the CC&Rs as well as Articles of Incorporation to protect Plaintiffs Property, as well as health, safety, and welfare, from the conditions on the Burke Property and Appurtenant Areas affecting Plaintiffs and Plaintiffs Property;

 

(b) the HOA must remove all of the HOA Tree roots from the Plaintiffs Property;

 

(c) the HOA must follow industry standards for removal of the HOA Tree roots;

 

(d) the HOA must take steps to abate the condition and prevent the reoccurrence of the condition concerning the HOA Tree and roots;

 

(e) the HOA is liable for damage caused by the HOA Tree roots to Plaintiffs Property;

 

(f) that the HOA make all necessary repairs and take corrective action regarding the Burke Tree roots and 2905 Trees' roots, after notice and non-compliance by the Burkes and/or 2905 Tree owner.

 

(Complaint ¶ 116.)

 

            HOA argues that the declaratory relief sought is improper because it is duplicative of the other causes of action.

 

Generally, an action in declaratory relief will not lie to determine an issue which can be determined in the underlying tort action.  The relief statute should not be used for the purpose of anticipating and determining an issue which can be determined in the main action. The object of the statute is to afford a new form of relief where needed and not to furnish a litigant with a second cause of action for the determination of identical issues.  Under section 1061 of the Code of Civil Procedure the court may refuse to exercise the power to grant declaratory relief where such relief is not necessary or proper at the time under all of the circumstances. The availability of another form of relief that is adequate will usually justify refusal to grant declaratory relief. The refusal to exercise the power is within the court's legal discretion and will not be disturbed on appeal except for abuse of discretion.

 

(California Ins. Guarantee Assn. v. Superior Court (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1617, 1623–1624 [cleaned up]; see also General of America Ins. Co. v. Lilly, 258 Cal.App.2d 465, 470 [affirming the trial court’s sustaining of the demurrer and subsequent dismissal where the declaratory relief sought was duplicative of an issue to be determined in the main action].)

 

            In opposition, Plaintiffs argue it is not duplicative, because it seeks prospective relief.  The Court disagrees.  Plaintiff requests a declaration that the HOA “take corrective action” and be “liable for damage caused” and “take steps to abate the condition” and prevent its reoccurrence, etc.  All of the relief sought pertains to the past alleged wrong of failing to properly address the issues related to the appurtenant/common area, and the tree roots, causing harm to Plaintiffs’ property.

 

            Therefore, the Court grants summary adjudication of Issue 9.

 

10.  ISSUE 10 – PUNITIVE DAMAGES

 

HOA argues the nature of the case, which involves maintenance issues on the property, does not support the imposition of punitive damages and the evidence does not support a claim for punitive damages.  The Court disagrees on both counts.

 

As for the first argument, Plaintiffs seek punitive damages in connection with Defendants’ alleged oppression, fraud, or malice vis-à-vis the fourth, fifth, and seventh causes of action for trespass, nuisance, and elder abuse, respectively.  (Complaint ¶ 71, 81, 96.)  Thus, there is nothing inherently problematic with Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages based on the structure of the Complaint alone.

 

Further, as discussed above, HOA has not met its initial prima facie burden of production and persuasion to demonstrate that it acted reasonably under the circumstances.

 

Therefore, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issue 10.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

Therefore, the Court grants in part and denies in part HOA’s motion for summary adjudication.  Because injunctive relief is a remedy as opposed to a standalone cause of action, and because Plaintiffs’ declaratory relief cause of action does not seek true prospective relief and is duplicative of the other causes of action, the Court grants summary adjudication of Issues 8 and 9.

 

However, in light of the evidentiary problems, finding the HOA has failed to meet its initial prima facie burdens of production and persuasion to demonstrate it acted reasonably and in accordance with the terms of the CC&Rs, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issues 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 10. 

 

Further, because the Court denies in part HOA’s request for summary adjudication, it similarly denies HOA’s request for summary judgment.

 

HOA shall lodge a proposed Order in conformance with the Court’s ruling on or before October 2, 2026.

 

HOA shall also provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

MOTION

Motion for Reconsideration

MOVING PARTY

Defendant Bryan Freedman

OPPOSING PARTY

Plaintiff Alexa Nikolas

 

BACKGROUND

 

The operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) filed by Plaintiff Alexa Nikolas (“Plaintiff”) against Defendants Bryan Freedman (“Freedman”) and Melissa Nathan (“Nathan”) alleges two causes of action for (1) defamation and (2) intentional infliction of emotional distress.

 

On August 12, 2026, the Court denied Freedman’s Anti-SLAPP motion.  Freedman now moves for reconsideration of that order.  Plaintiff opposes the motion.

 

LEGAL STANDARD - RECONSIDERATION

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a), “[w]hen an application for an order has been made to a judge, or to a court, and refused in whole or in part, or granted, or granted conditionally, or on terms, any party affected by the order may, within 10 days after service upon the party of written notice of entry of the order and based upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, make an application to the same judge or court that made the order, to reconsider the matter and modify, amend, or revoke the prior order.  The party making the application shall state by affidavit what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a).)  Where the statutory requirements are met, reconsideration should be granted; upon reconsideration, however, the court may simply reaffirm its original order.  (Corns v. Miller (1986) 181 Cal.App.3d 195, 202.) 

 

ANALYSIS

 

            Defendant proffers as “new or different facts” the August 11, 2026 deposition testimony of Brian W. Horton, the expert who filed an expert report on December 8, 2025 in the Stephanie Jones case. 

 

            The Court previously relied on the existence and filing of the December 8, 2025 report in connection with Defendant’s argument that Plaintiff’s claims are barred by the 1-year statute of limitations, to demonstrate that this report was the first time Plaintiff had any actual or constructive notice of information connecting the anonymous online postings back to Defendants, and thus, it was the first opportunity Plaintiff had to sue Defendants in connection with the defamatory websites.

 

            Specifically, the December 2025 Horton report “connect[ed] the dots” showing a common authorship between websites smearing his client Stephanie Jones in 2024 with prior websites that had smeared Amanda Ghost in 2024 and Plaintiff in 2022-2023.

 

            Defendant now points to testimony from the August 11 deposition indicating:

 

(1)   Horton did not attribute the Nikolas posts to any person and had no opinion that any defendant created, commissioned, funded, directed, or operated any website he examined.

 

(2)   Horton could not identify any defendant who created any content; his report was not meant to assign human attribution; it connects no defendant to any website, and he has no evidence that any defendant ever visited, edited, commissioned, or paid for any listed page.

 

(3)   Horton’s report offered no opinions concerning the Nikolas sites which are not included in Section 8, the core basis of his commonality opinion.

 

(4)   The Nikolas material was not significant or material to his overlapping-indicators opinion and does not appear in the section of his Report devoted to comparisons between defamatory websites. 

 

The Court does not find this testimony material to the Court’s prior determination that Plaintiff’s claims are not time barred because she could not have, through reasonable diligence, discovered the potential connection between the websites defaming her and Defendants until the Horton report was publicly filed in December 2025.

 

In the Anti-SLAPP ruling, the Court correctly characterized the Horton report as connecting the dots between websites defaming Jones, Ghost, and others like Plaintiff as having the hallmarks of common authorship.  That Horton did not additionally identify specifically who that common author was because that determination was beyond the scope of his technical analysis is immaterial.  Rather, once Horton’s report alerted Plaintiff that the defamatory websites had at least some hallmarks of common authorship, Plaintiff was able to connect the dots to Defendants via additional information publicly disclosed in connection with those other cases, and by comparing the victims and identifying Defendants as antagonists they all had in common.

 

Thus, it was not error for the Court to determine that Plaintiff could not, through reasonable diligence, have discovered any potential connection between the websites defaming her and Defendants until the Horton report was publicly filed in December 2025, describing commonalities between the websites.

 

Nor is it material to the Court’s actual malice finding that although the Horton Report identified some commonalities with the websites defaming Plaintiff, it ultimately excluded Plaintiff’s websites from the core of its commonality analysis in connection with the Samantha Jones case. 

 

As a threshold matter, Freedman argues on the one hand that the Horton Report is hearsay and not admissible for the truth of its substance, which the Court generally agrees with, and ultimately took judicial notice of the fact that it was publicly filed for purposes of determining when Plaintiff was on notice of its contents.  Yet, now, Freedman argues on the other hand that the substance of the Horton Report, as clarified by the Horton Deposition, exonerates Freedman, which is generally improper.

 

Moreover, the selected pages Freedman provided of the Horton deposition are insufficient to give the Court full context as to what commonalities and differences Horton identified between Plaintiff’s websites and the websites involving the other victims, or why Horton omitted Plaintiff’s websites from the core of his commonality analysis.

 

The Court notes, however, that the websites defaming Plaintiff came first, years before the other websites.  But that does not mean those websites and the websites about Plaintiff definitely or even probably do not have common authorship.  The report still identified the websites defaming Plaintiff has having commonalities with the other websites, which suggests Plaintiff will similarly be able to retain her own expert, whether Horton or some other digital forensics expert, to identify and explain the commonalities between the websites in connection with this case. 

 

 Therefore, the selected excerpts of the Horton Deposition provided do not warrant reconsideration of the Court’s Anti-SLAPP ruling.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court denies Freedman’s Motion for Reconsideration.

BACKGROUND

 

This case arises from a dispute between adjacent property owners Plaintiff Northwestern Engineering Company (“Plaintiff” or “Northwestern”) and Defendant Joseph Shemaria (“Defendant” or “Shemaria”). 

 

Case number SC128312 was filed on November 1, 2017, and involves a dispute over a portion of the pathway between the properties, located toward the front of the properties, where Plaintiff has historically kept items in storage.  The operative verified Second Amended Complaint alleges four causes of action for (1) quiet title to a prescriptive easement; (2) trespass; (3) nuisance; and (4) temporary restraining order and preliminary injunction.

 

The crux of the complaint asserts that Plaintiff has used the “Breezeway,” a path that runs from Ocean Front Walk at the front or western part of the properties to the “Speedway,” an alley at the rear or eastern part of the properties for ingress and egress since July 1999 and has made improvements, including erecting shelving for storage and pouring concrete on a portion of the pathway.  (SAC ¶¶ 7-8.)

 

On May 6, 2019, Shemaria filed a Verified Cross-Complaint in case number SC128312 for (1) trespass, (2) unjust enrichment, and (3) nuisance.  The crux of Shemaria’s cross-complaint is that from 1983 through 2017, Plaintiff and its tenants used the Breezeway with Shemaria’s permission to store beer kegs and other materials, but Shemaria revoked this permission in June 2017 and demanded that Plaintiff remove the kegs, an enclosure, and shelving, which Plaintiff has refused to do.  (Cross-Complaint ¶¶ 2-5, 10-11.)

 

Case number 22SMCV00758, which was filed on May 24, 2022, concerns a fence Defendant allegedly constructed in December 2021, creating a barrier between the properties and preventing Plaintiff and its tenants from receiving deliveries and disposing of trash via a southeastern corner of Defendant’s property, as they had done since 1999.  (Complaint ¶¶ 8-12.) 

 

Following a bench trial in the related cases (consolidated for trial only), the Court issued an Amended Statement of Decision on June 11, 2026. 

 

Regarding SC128312, the Court entered judgment in Defendant’s favor on the SAC, and entered judgment in Plaintiff’s favor on the Cross-Complaint. 

 

Regarding 22SMCV00758, the Court entered judgment in Plaintiff’s favor concerning the first two causes of action for a prescriptive easement and Defendant’s unreasonable interference therewith vis-à-vis the construction of a fence blocking the Breezeway, as well as the fourth cause of action for injunctive relief, but entered judgment in Defendant’s favor concerning the third cause of action for private nuisance.

 

Defendant now moves for a new trial, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 657.  The Notice of Intent lists the following five grounds for the motion: (1) irregularity in the proceedings; (2) accident or surprise, which ordinary prudence could not have guarded against; (3) newly discovered evidence, material to Defendant that could not have been discovered and presented with reasonable diligence at trial; (4) insufficiency of the evidence to justify the verdict and/or decision, and is contrary to law; and (5) legal errors occurring at trial that were properly excepted to.

 

However, the memorandum of points and authorities addresses only two grounds: (1) insufficiency of the evidence pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 657, subdivision (6) and (2) errors in law pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 657, subdivision (7).  As such, the Court deems forfeited any arguments as to irregularity in the proceedings, accident or surprise, and newly discovered evidence.

 

Plaintiff opposes the motion.

 

LEGAL STANDARDS – MOTION FOR A NEW TRIAL

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 657, in pertinent part, provides:

 

The verdict may be vacated and any other decision may be modified or vacated, in whole or in part, and a new or further trial granted on all or part of the issues, on the application of the party aggrieved, for any of the following causes, materially affecting the substantial rights of such party:

 

6.     Insufficiency of the evidence to justify the verdict or other decision, or the verdict or other decision is against law.

 

7.     Error in law, occurring at the trial and excepted to by the party making the application.

 

TIMELINESS

 

            Motions for New Trials are governed by strict, jurisdictional statutory time limits.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 659.)

 

            The notice of motion must be filed and served within 15 days of the Clerk of the Court’s notice of entry of judgment.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 659, subd. (a)(2).)  Here, the judgment and the notice of entry of judgment were entered on July 22, 2026.  Fifteen days later is August, 6, 2026.  Defendant timely filed a Notice of Intent to Move for New Trial on August 6, 2026.

 

            Further, 10 days after filing the notice, the moving party must file and serve the motion(s) and supporting papers.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 659a.)  Ten days after August 6 is Sunday, August 16.    By operation of Code of Civil Procedure section 12a, subdivision (b), which expressly applies to Section 659’s deadlines, the deadline was extended to Monday, August 17.  Defendant timely filed and electronically served a Memorandum of Points and Authorities and Declaration in support of the Motion for New Trial on August 17.

 

            The opposing party has 10 days after service of the moving papers to serve the opposing papers.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 659a.)  That deadline may be extended by up to 10 days, for good cause shown by affidavit.  (Ibid.)  On August 31, Plaintiff filed and served the opposition.

 

            The Court notes that while Code of Civil Procedure sections 1013 and 1010.6 do not apply to extend the time within which the moving party may file a Notice of Intent to Move for New Trial (see Code Civ. Proc., § 659, subd. (b); § 1013, subd. (a), (c), (e); § 1010.6, subd. (a)(3)(B)), it is not immediately clear to the Court that they do not apply to extend the time within which the opposing party may file an opposition to the motion. 

 

Notably, the notice of intent must be filed within fifteen days after service by the clerk of the notice of entry of judgment, and that time is not extended by operation of Sections 1013 or 1010.6, but the opposition to the motion must be filed within only ten days after service by the moving party of the motion and supporting papers.  Thus, it appears as though the five additional days for service by mail are incorporated into the moving party’s fifteen-day deadline, but not the opposing party’s deadline. 

 

Even further, the Court is generally within its discretion to extend the opposition deadline by up to 10 days, and the Court does not generally lose jurisdiction to consider late-filed affidavits, especially where, as here, the opposing party has not objected to the timeliness.  As such, the Court exercises its discretion and considers the opposition.

 

            The reply is due five days after service of the opposition.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 659a.)  Five days after August 31, is Saturday, September 5, 2026.  By operation of Code of Civil Procedure section 12a, subdivision (b), which expressly applies to Section 659’s deadlines, the deadline was extended to Monday, September 7, 2026.  As of September 21, 2026, no Reply had been received by the Court. 

 

ANALYSIS

 

I.                INSUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE

 

Defendant argues insufficiency of the evidence in four ways:

 

1.     Insufficiency of the evidence to establish a “definite and certain” path of use.

 

2.     Insufficiency of the evidence to find the necessary element to establish Plaintiff’s use was “open and notorious” as opposed to mere passive acquiescence.

 

3.     Insufficiency of the evidence to establish Plaintiff’s usage was “continuous and uninterrupted” for five (5) years.

 

4.     Insufficiency of the evidence to establish Plaintiff’s five (5) years of usage of the Speedway.

 

a.     Definite and Certain Path of Use

 

Defendant first argues that because the Court determined that Plaintiff’s access was limited so as not to interfere with parked vehicles, Plaintiff was effectively prevented from using the “easement” to the dumpsters and to receive deliveries except when the parking space was vacant, transforming it to a conditional privilege, not a true easement.

 

The Court actually held (1) Plaintiff met its burden of proof by clear and convincing evidence that Plaintiff and/or its tenants have since 1999 traversed over the 42” by 98” corner of Defendant’s parking lot for the purposes of disposing trash and receiving deliveries, and therefore had established a definite and certain path of use; (2) Plaintiff merely requested to continue traversing the Speedway without permanent impediments, such as fencing or a barrier; (3) Defendant failed to present evidence substantiating his argument that Plaintiff’s requested prescriptive easement would require Defendant to reconfigure and re-stripe the parking lot; and (4) Defendant’s contention that Plaintiff intended to use the Speedway for illegal purposes, such as a “dump site” creating a public nuisance was unsupported by the evidence.

 

In essence, Defendant is arguing that because the Court held both parties were entitled to use and traverse the Speedway, so long as neither party erected a permanent or immovable barrier preventing the other’s use, Plaintiff’s ability to use the Speedway is effectively blocked whenever a car is temporarily parked there, and because Plaintiff’s ability to traverse the Speedway is sometimes blocked, it is not a true easement.

 

The only case Defendant cites in support of his argument is Connolly v. McDermott (1984) 162 Cal.App.3d 973.  That case focused on the scope of use of the prescriptive easement, not temporary obstacles in the easement due to the nature of the joint use.   The court first explained:   “The scope of a prescriptive easement is determined by the use through which it is acquired. A person using the land of another for the prescriptive period may acquire the right to continue such use, but does not acquire the right to make other uses of it.”  (Id. at p. 977.)

 

The appellate court concluded the trial court had not erred in restricting the scope of the easement at issue to “livestock herding operations” by “men and horses,” refusing to grant an easement for motor vehicle use, notwithstanding evidence that motorcycles also sometimes traversed the easement, because the evidence showed plaintiffs concealed their vehicular use from defendants, and thus, the vehicular use was not “open and notorious” and expanding the easement to allow motor vehicle traffic would constitute a substantial new burden on the servient tenement, as motor vehicles like motorcycles create more noise than livestock.  (Connolly, supra, 162 Cal.App.3d at pp. 977-979.)

 

But nothing in Connolly supports Defendant’s argument that the path cannot be properly characterized as an easement if sometimes the other party’s joint use of the area temporarily blocks the path.

 

Further, Defendant has not established that there was insufficient evidence supporting the Court’s determination that the easement in question constitutes the 42” by 98” corner of Defendant’s parking lot.

 

b.     Open and Notorious

 

Defendant’s “open and notorious” argument is not the model of clarity.  It appears as though Defendant is arguing that Plaintiff’s use was not “open and notorious” because it was not adverse to Defendant and Defendant acquiesced in Plaintiff’s use.[1]

 

The elements for a prescriptive easement are for a period of five years prior to the commencement of the action:

 

1.     Plaintiff has been using Defendant’s property;

 

2.     Plaintiff’s use was continuous and uninterrupted;

 

3.     The use was open and easily observable, or was under circumstances that would give reasonable notice to Defendant; and

 

4.     Plaintiff did not have Defendant’s permission to use the property.

 

(See CACI 4901 (2026 ed.))

 

Thus, to be “open and notorious” the use must have been open and easily observable, or done under circumstances that would give reasonable notice to the Defendant.  Because Defendant argues he acquiesced in Plaintiff’s use, Defendant concedes he had actual notice of the use, making such use open and notorious.

 

Further, the Court found Exhibits 201, 221, 223, 224, 226, 227, and 227 (photos taken by the Defendant” illustrate the “open and easily observable use of the Speedway by Plaintiff and its tenants” or that Defendant had reasonable notice of Plaintiff’s and its tenants’ use.

 

Thus, Defendant has not demonstrated that the Court’s “open and notorious” finding was inadequately supported by the evidence.

 

c.      Continuous and Uninterrupted for Five Years

 

Defendant argues the Court’s finding that Plaintiff’s use was continuous and uninterrupted for a period of five years was unsupported by the evidence, because Plaintiff did not advance any evidence about how often the rear parking space was occupied, effectively blocking the easement.  Further, Defendant argues that the Court’s finding that Plaintiff’s use of the Speedway did not interfere with Defendant’s tenants’ parked vehicles was a tacit recognition that the tenants’ parking rights conflict with Plaintiff’s purported rights in the easement. 

 

But Defendant’s own authorities demonstrate such evidence was not required. 

 

To acquire a prescriptive easement, the easement must be used in the required manner continuously and without interruption for the full prescriptive period [of five years]. The actual use required depends on the nature of the easement. It need not be used every day during the prescriptive period. The use is sufficient if it occurs on those occasions when it is necessary for the convenience of the user.  If a right of way over another's land has been used for more than five years, it is not necessary, to make good such use, that the claimant has used it every day.  He uses it every day, or once in every week, or twice a month, as his needs require. He is not required to go over it when he does not need it, to make his use of the way continuous.  The claimant is required to make such reasonable use of the way as his needs require.

(Fogerty v. State of California (1986) 187 Cal.App.3d 224, 239 [cleaned up].)

 

            Further, Defendant cites to Zimmer v. Dykstra (1974) 39 Cal.App.3d 422.  In Zimmer, a landowner obstructed its neighbor’s use of the landowner’s half of the alleyway by erecting a “grape stake” fence, permitting her tenants to park automobiles there, and erecting a chain link fence.  The trial court held that the plaintiffs had a prescriptive easement over the defendant’s half of the alleyway, ordered her to remove the chain link fence, and awarded damages.  The Court of appeal affirmed.  The Defendant had argued the Plaintiffs had never acquired a prescriptive easement, and even if they had, it had been extinguished.  The Court of Appeal disagreed on the ground that neither the “grape stake” fence, nor the parking of automobiles, nor the chain link fence existed for the statutory period for Defendant to reclaim the land and they did not meet the criteria for “tacking.” 

 

            Here, the Court cited the following evidence as supporting its finding that Plaintiff and its tenants used the Speedway continuously since 1999 to access trash dumpsters: RT 1 - 157:11-160:15; 166:6-26; 199:1-15; 200:1-23; Exh. 75, p. 14.)  Further, Zachary Best testified that he and other employees of the Venice Beach Bar traverse the Speedway several times per day to dispose of trash or accept deliveries.  (RT 2 – 38:4-39:18; 40:3-19.)  Further, Jim Adelstein testified he observed employees of Plaintiff’s tenants Venice Bistro and Piccolo use the Speedway to access the dumpster and to receive deliveries.  (RT 1 at 38:11-18; 56:1-7; 58:24-28.)  Further, Antonio Mure testified his employees would use the Speedway to access trash bins when he leased space from Plaintiff.  (RT 2 – 4:12-28; 24:12-17.)

 

            That a tenant’s car may have occasionally parked in the parking space, temporarily obstructing Plaintiff’s use of the Speedway does not, pursuant to Fogarty, undermine Plaintiff’s continuous and uninterrupted use.  Further, pursuant to Zimmer, the burden is on Defendant to demonstrate he reclaimed the Speedway by permanently blocking Plaintiff’s path with a parked vehicle, not on Plaintiff to demonstrate how often and for how long a car was sometimes parked there.

 

            Therefore, Defendant has not met his burden of demonstrating that the evidence inadequately supports the Court’s finding of continuous use for a period of five years.

 

II.             ERRORS IN LAW

 

Defendant argues there were three errors in law:

 

1.     Granting judgment in favor of Plaintiff on the first and second causes of action because the Complaint in the 22SMCV00758 matter was filed prior to the required five (5) years of usage and should have been dismissed.

 

2.     Shifting the burden of proof to Defendant prior to Plaintiff establishing a prima facie case for all necessary elements for a prescriptive easement.

 

3.     Requiring Defendant to prove express “consent” as opposed to implied consent or mere acquiescence and/or a “neighborly accommodation.”

 

a.     22SMCV00758 Complaint Timeliness

 

Plaintiff alleged, and as discussed above, the evidence presented at trial supported the Court’s finding that Plaintiff had traversed the Speedway to receive deliveries and access the dumpster since July 1999.  The Complaint in Case No. 22SMCV00758 was filed more than five years later, on May 24, 2022.

 

Defendant argues that Mr. Mure testified that he moved into the Piccolo restaurant location in March 2004 and left in the middle of 2009.  Because Mr. Adelstein (Plaintiff’s president) did not take over Mr. Abelar’s restaurant until July 2016, more than five years later, Mr. Mure’s time cannot be tacked onto Adelstein’s time.  Not so.

 

Plaintiff purchased the multi-tenant retail property in 1999.  Plaintiff’s tenants include the Venice Bistro, formerly owned by Abelar until it was directly taken over by Adelstein in 2016, Venice Beach Bar, currently managed by Best, and the former Piccolo restaurant, which was co-owned by Mure from March 2004 to 2009.  Thus, the use by any of these tenants constitute Plaintiff’s continuous use of the Speedway.  Taken together, the evidence demonstrates that Plaintiff’s tenants, including Venice Bistro, Venice Beach Bar, and Piccolo Restaurant used the Speedway continuously for the statutory period long before the Complaint was filed in 2022. 

 

  Thus, there was no legal error in considering the 22SMCV00758 complaint, as it was filed more than five years after the continuous and uninterrupted uses began. 

 

b.     Shifting the Burden of Proof to Defendant and Express Consent versus Implied Consent or Acquiescence

 

As discussed above, because there was no error in determining that the five-year prescriptive period had run before the Complaint was filed, there was similarly no error in shifting the burden of proof to Defendant to demonstrate otherwise.

 

            As for consent, the Court found Defendant not credible in his testimony about consent, because although he first testified that he gave Abelar permission to access the Speedway in June 1984 and revoked that permission in June 2017, on cross-examination he denied ever having granted permission and seemed ambivalent about use of the Speedway.  Further, this testimony contrasted with Adelstein’s testimony that neither Abelar nor other tenants told him they had permission to use the Speedway, Defendant’s insistence that he did not give Adelstein permission to use the Speedway, Mure’s testimony that neither Defendant nor anyone else gave him permission to use the Speedway, and Best’s testimony that Best never asked Defendant for permission to use the Speedway.  Even further, in Exhibit 201, Defendant tacitly concedes he did not give permission to use the Speedway.

 

            Taken together, the Court found a reasonable inference that the tenants used the Speedway without Defendant’s consent.  Thus, there was no error in law in finding Plaintiffs had proved no consent to use the Speedway by clear and convincing evidence and Defendant failed to meet his evidentiary burden to demonstrate otherwise in defense of Plaintiff’s showing.

 

III.           INTERNALLY INCONSISTENT AND IRRECONCILABLE FINDINGS

 

Although not clearly stated in the introduction roadmap to his memorandum of points and authorities, Defendant also argues the Court made several internally inconsistent and irreconcilable findings.

 

a.     Inconsistent Credibility Determinations Regarding Defendant’s Testimony on the Speedway versus the Breezeway

 

First, Defendant argues the Court applied opposite credibility determinations to the same witness on the same facts within a single ruling.  Specifically, Defendant contends the statement of decision reaches opposite conclusions on the Breezeway and Speedway, despite an evidentiary record that is essentially the same. 

 

As to the Breezeway, the Court gave more weight to Defendant’s testimony that he granted Abelar permission to use the Breezeway in June 1984, as corroborated by the January 7, 2000 letter, purporting to “re-establish” or take “back” the three feet pathway from the Boardwalk to the parking lot (effectively revoking consent thereto), and found that Adelstein’s failure to confer with Defendant about the 2000 letter and its June 2016 email seeking permission to install steel gates and to concrete in the entire space demonstrated Plaintiff knew it lacked rights to the Breezeway.   

 

Yet, as to the Speedway, the Court found Defendant not credible in light of the fact that Defendant filed to proffer any supporting documentary evidence that he gave permission for Plaintiff or its tenants to use the Speedway.

 

Defendant argues while the Court is within its discretion to believe a witness on one point but disbelieve that same witness on another point, here the Court’s disparate treatment was error, because both positions arose from the same 1984 conversation and the same landlord-tenant relationship.

 

The Court disagrees that there was any erroneous contradiction here.  In light of Defendant’s contradictory testimony and lack of supporting documentary evidence vis-à-vis the Speedway, the Court found that the 1984 conversation encompassed use of the Breezeway, but not the Speedway.  And as Defendant concedes, it is well within the Court’s discretion to determine whether a witness is lying, exaggerating, or mistaken about one point of testimony, but not another, especially where, as here, the corroborating evidence supports that split finding.

 

b.     Inconsistent Treatment of Defendant’s Revocation

 

Second, Defendant argues that the Court inconsistently treated Defendant’s 2000 revocation of permission as evidence that permission existed for the Breezeway but treated the Exhibit 201 email’s purported revocation of permission to use the Speedway tacit admission that no such permission existed in the first instance.

 

The differential treatment is explained by the contents of those respective correspondences.

 

The 2000 letter says, “I need back the three feet that runs from the Boardwalk to my parking lot” and “We have decided to re-establish our three feet pathway to the Boardwalk.”  (Ex. 2, emphasis added.)  Thus, the implication from the 2000 letter is that permission was previously granted and Defendant was revoking such permission by way of the 2000 letter.

 

By contrast, Exhibit 201, which is a June 16, 2022 email from Defendant to Plaintiff’s counsel indicates:

 

For years, I resisted putting up a fence and had to deal with any number of regular complaints from my tenants regarding the intolerable garbage/trash/foodstuffs/grease due to your client’s failure and refusal to respect my property vis-à-vis the trash situation.  If Mr. Adelstein wants to know the reason for my putting up the fence, let them know that it was a direct result of his own negligence and recklessness in the handling of what is depicted in these photographs.  Numerous former tenants have written to the Health Department regarding this long-running situation that could only be stopped by defense that I finally put in.

 

            Defendant clearly communicated dissatisfaction with Plaintiff’s tenants’ use of the dumpster by way of the Speedway, which suggests Defendant did not consent to such use.  Further, the fact that the situation “could only be stopped” by Defendant’s putting in a fence suggests the mere revocation of consent was not an available option, as it would have been had Defendant originally provided consent. 

 

            Thus, the Court did not err in reaching differing inferences from the 2000 letter vis-à-vis the Breezeway and the 2022 email regarding the Speedway on the issue of whether consent had originally been provided for each purported easement.

 

c.      Inconsistent Treatment of Adelstein’s Conduct and Testimony

 

Defendant next takes issue with the Court’s asymmetrical treatment of Adelstein’s conduct and testimony vis-à-vis the Speedway and Breezeway.  Specifically, the Court cites Adelstein’s failure to confer with Defendant about the January 2000 letter, Adelstein’s June 2016 email inquiring about gates and concrete, his failure in Exhibit 30 to disclose that he had become the owner of the Venice Bistro, and his general “state of mind” regarding the Breezeway as evidence undermining his credibility vis-à-vis the Breezeway. 

 

Yet, the Court did not make similar adverse credibility findings regarding Adelstein’s testimony vis-à-vis the Speedway.

 

But crucially, several other tenant witnesses corroborated Adelstein’s testimony vis-à-vis the Speedway, whereas the documentary evidence undermined Adelstein’s testimony vis-à-vis the Breezeway.  Thus, it was not error to make differing credibility determinations on Adelstein’s testimony about the Breezeway versus the Speedway.

 

d.     Inconsistent Weight Given to Exhibit 75

 

Defendant next challenges the Court’s giving “little weight” to Exhibit 75, yet relying on it to determine the scope of the easement.

 

Exhibit 75 is a parking analysis chart Defendant proffered as evidence that he would need to re-stripe the parking lot, if the Court found in favor of Plaintiff with respect to the Speedway easement.  The Court expressly held that granting Plaintiff a prescriptive easement over the Speedway would not prevent Defendant’s tenants from also using the Speedway, except in a manner that would permanently block Plaintiff’s use of it (for example by erecting a permanent fence or other barrier).  Thus, the parking analysis diagram did not materially support Defendant’s argument that the easement would necessitate re-striping the parking lot.

 

Notwithstanding, the diagram was useful for the Court to visualize and quantify the disputed area and proposed pathway.

 

Thus, there was no error in weighing the same exhibit differently with respect to different legal and factual arguments.

 

e.      Selective Credibility Findings Avoid the Five-Year Prescriptive Period Problem

 

Defendant points out that the Statement of Decision notes in a footnote that the 22SMCV00758 case was commenced on May 24, 2022, which is less than 5 years from the date Defendant purportedly revoked his permission to use the Speedway in June 2017.  Thus, the inconsistent credibility determination vis-à-vis the 1984 permission testimony conveniently avoids the 5-year prescriptive period problem as to the Speedway.

 

But as discussed above, the Court’s disparate treatment of the two separate easement disputes at issue is supported by the lopsided corroborating evidence in the record, as well as the differing nature and locations of the two easements.  The Breezeway involved the erection of storage shelves and the permanent storage of materials.  Evidence of this permission was corroborated by the 2000 revocation letter and Plaintiff’s own email inquiring about a grate and concrete (which would not have been necessary, had Plaintiff been under the impression that it had a legal right to the area in question). 

 

By contrast, the Speedway involved merely traversing a path at the back of the properties to access the dumpster and to receive deliveries.  Defendant proffered no corroborating documentary evidence (like the 2000 letter or 2016 email for the Breezeway) evidencing his purported permission, and Plaintiff’s narrative that no permission to use the Speedway was granted was corroborated by the testimonies of several of Plaintiff’s tenants. 

 

Thus, the nature, location, and corroborating evidence demonstrates that while Defendant formally granted Plaintiff permission to use the larger, more prominent Breezeway in a permanent way in 1984, and then attempted to revoke that permission by way of the 2000 letter, the same permission was not granted for Plaintiff’s use of the Speedway as a shortcut path to and from the dumpster and delivery trucks.

 

That the Court found permission was not granted vis-à-vis the Speedway in the same way it had been granted for the Breezeway also avoided Defendant’s ripeness arguments does not mean that there was insufficiency of the evidence or that the Court made a legal error in making the differing findings as to the different easements.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the foregoing reasons, the Court does not find any insufficiency of the evidence or errors in law to support Defendant’s request for a new trial.  As such, the Court denies Defendant’s motion for a new trial in its entirety.
