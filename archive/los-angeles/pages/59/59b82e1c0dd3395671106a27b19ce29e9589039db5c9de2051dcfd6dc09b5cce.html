COURT’S [TENTATIVE] ORDER RE:

Motion for Attorneys’ Fees

[THE FOLLOWING IS A TENTATIVE RULING IN THE ABOVE CASE]:

Grant in the reduced sum of $13,848.50.

 

I.                BACKGROUND

On December 23, 2022, Plaintiffs Bryan Rojo and Leo Rojo (“Plaintiffs”) leased a 2022 Acura MDX (the “Vehicle”). (Complaint ¶ 8.) The Vehicle was defective. (Complaint ¶ 10.)

On April 30, 2024, Plaintiffs filed a complaint against Defendants American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (“Defendant”) and Does 1 through 10, alleging causes of action for: (1) violation of Song-Beverly Act - breach of express warranty; (2) violation of Song-Beverly Act - breach of implied warranty; and (3) violation of the Song-Beverly Act section 1793.2.

On March 30, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a notice of settlement of entire case.

On June 10, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a motion for attorneys’ fees (the “Motion”). On July 10, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition. On July 20, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a reply.

II.             DEFENDANT’S EXHIBIT A

In reply, Plaintiff requests the court strike the arguments exclusively found in Exhibit A attached to Defendant’s opposition. (Reply p. 2.)

Exhibit A summarizes Defendant’s specific objections to Plaintiff’s billing chart. (Cabibi Declaration ¶ 5.) The place for legal arguments is in Defendant’s memorandum of points and authorities, not an exhibit attached to the opposition.  Further, the page limit for responding memorandums in this situation is 15 pages. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1113(d).) Defendant filed no application to submit a longer memorandum. (Cal Rules of Court, rule 3.1113(e).) The court will not consider Exhibit A.

III.           LEGAL STANDARD

California Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) states:

 

If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney's fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.

 

“The plain wording of the statute requires the trial court to base the fee award upon actual time expended on the case, as long as such fees are reasonably incurred – both from the standpoint of time spent and the amount charged.” (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (“Robertson”) (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 817, original italics.) The “prevailing party has the burden of showing that the fees incurred were reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation, and were reasonable in amount.” (Id. at pp. 817-818.) “The amount of an attorney fee to be awarded is a matter within the sound discretion of the trial court.” (Id. at p. 822.)

 

“[T]he lodestar adjustment method is the prevailing rule for statutory attorney fee awards to be applied in the absence of clear legislative intent to the contrary[.]” (Robertson, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at p. 818.) The lodestar adjustment method “requires the trial court to first determine a touchstone or lodestar figure based on a careful compilation of the actual time spent and reasonable hourly compensation for each attorney. [Citation] The touchstone figure may then be augmented or diminished by taking various relevant factors into account, including (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved and the skill displayed in presenting them; (2) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys; and (3) the contingent nature of the fee award, based on the uncertainty of prevailing on the merits and of establishing eligibility for the award.” (Id. at p. 819.) However, a trial court is “not required to apply a multiplier.” (Id. at p. 822, original italics.)

 

IV.           DISCUSSION

Plaintiffs move for an order awarding Plaintiffs attorneys’ fees in the sum of $21,021.88. (Motion p. 21.) This reflects $13,617.50 in attorneys’ fees, a 1.25 multiplier enhancement increasing the award by $3,404.38, and an additional $4,000.00 in fees for reviewing the opposition, drafting the reply, and attending the Motion’s hearing. (Motion p. 21.)

Hourly Rates:

Plaintiff’s counsels and supporting staff billed at rates ranging from $175.00 per hour to $600.00 per hour. (Motion p. 15.)

In opposition, Defendant argues Plaintiffs’ counsels’ hourly rates are excessive. (Opposition pp. 13-14.)

Plaintiffs’ counsel, Kevin Jacobson, submits a declaration discussing counsels’ and counsels’ supporting staff’s relevant experience, except for Nima Elie and Collin Woolheater. (Jacobson Declaration ¶¶ 3-5, 47-64; Motion p. 15.)

The court finds the requested hourly rates are reasonable. (See Goglin v. BMW of North America, LLC (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 462, 464, 473-474 [concluding a trial court did not abuse its discretion in finding a $575 hourly rate was appropriate in a Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act action.])

Time Billed:

Plaintiffs’ counsels and supporting staff billed 31.10 hours on this matter, excluding time billed relating to the opposition, the reply, and the Motion’s hearing. (Jacobson Declaration Ex. 41 pdf p. 265.)

In opposition, Defendant the Motion “attributes to many tasks reflects what counsel believes the ‘value’ of that task should be, not the actual time spent”, counsels should not recover time anticipated to be billed, and counsels improperly block billed. (Opposition pp. 9-12.)

The court agrees with Defendant that some of the tasks billed are excessive. It is appropriate to reduce a fee award because of “a lack of efficiency in litigating the case and a lack of clarity in tasks performed.” (See Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240 256 [“Further, counsel’s billing entries demonstrate a lack of efficiency in litigating the case and a lack of clarity in tasks performed. This evidence supports the trial court’s finding that plaintiff’s counsel failed to act efficiently.”]) Plaintiff’s counsels are experienced in this area of the law. Thus, the court will adjust the reflected time to reflect the court’s understanding that the time billed should reflect Plaintiffs’ counsels’ expertise.

The adjustments above reflect a reduction of $3,184.50 reduction in the requested fees.

The court finds the reasonable number of hours expended on this matter was 30.9 hours, at the requested hourly rates.

Lodestar Multiplier:

Plaintiffs request a 1.25 lodestar multiplier. (Motion p. 21.)

The court understands that litigating Song-Beverly actions requires technical expertise.  But “a trial court should award a multiplier for exceptional representation only when the quality of representation far exceeds the quality of representation that would have been provided by an attorney of comparable skill and experience billing at the hourly rate used in the lodestar calculation. Otherwise, the fee award will result in unfair double counting and be unreasonable.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1139.)  Here, Plaintiffs’ counsels displayed the skill, proficiency, and technical expertise commensurate with counsels’ experience and specialty. Nothing before the court indicates Plaintiffs’ counsels’ representation exceeded that which would have been provided by other attorneys of comparable skill and experience billing at counsels’ rates.

 

Accordingly, the court denies Plaintiffs’ request for an enhancement.

 

V.              CONCLUSION

 

For the foregoing reasons, the request for attorneys’ fees is granted in the reduced sum of $13,848.50, to be paid by Defendant to Plaintiff’s counsel within thirty (30) days of this order.

   v.

COURT’S [TENTATIVE] ORDER RE:

Motion to Compel Non-Party’s Deposition

 

[THE FOLLOWING IS A TENTATIVE RULING IN THE ABOVE CASE]:

 

 (1) Grant Motion and compel non-party Catalina Paz’s deposition; (2) Deny Defendant’s and Plaintiff’s requests for sanctions.

 

I.                BACKGROUND

 

On September 30, 2023, Plaintiff Guillermina Paz (“Plaintiff”) purchased a 2024 Acura Integra (the “Vehicle”). (Complaint ¶ 8.) The Vehicle was defective. (Complaint ¶ 10.)

 

On October 31, 2024, Plaintiff filed a complaint against Defendants American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (“Defendant”) and Does 1 through 10, alleging causes of action for: (1) violation of Song-Beverly Act - breach of express warranty; (2) violation of Song-Beverly Act - breach of implied warranty; and (3) violation of the Song-Beverly Act section 1793.2.

 

On April 1, 2026, Defendant filed a motion to compel non-party Catalina Paz’s deposition (the “Motion”). On May 26, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On July 20, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

 

II.             MEET AND CONFER AND TIMELINESS

Defendant’s notice of motion cites Code of Civil Procedure sections 1987.1, subdivision (a) and 2025.480, subdivision (b). (Notice of Motion p. 1.)

Motions under section 2025.480 must “be made no later than 60 days after the completion of the record of the deposition” and must “be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under section 2016.040.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (b).) “A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040, subd. (a).)

Defendant’s counsel’s declaration states Defendant served non-party Catalina Paz (“Paz”) with a deposition subpoena on March 10, 2026. (Sadanaga Declaration ¶ 4.) On March 27, 2026, Defendant took the non-appearance on behalf of Paz. (Sadanaga Declaration ¶ 7.) Defendant filed the Motion less than 60-days later, on April 1, 2026. Thus, the Motion is timely.

In opposition, Plaintiff argues Defendant failed to properly meet and confer. (Opposition p. 3.)

Defendant repeatedly emailed Plaintiff’s counsel but Plaintiff’s counsel did not respond. (Sadanaga Declaration ¶¶ 3, 6.) Meet and confer efforts by email are insufficient under Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040. Further, a single email stating Defendant received untimely objections and stating Defendant would be taking the non-appearance is insufficient to show good faith meet and confer efforts. (Sadanaga Declaration Ex. E pdf p. 110.) Thus, Defendant failed to sufficiently meet and confer before filing the Motion.

“[N]ot every finding that additional informal resolution efforts are required can be categorized as a failure so egregious as to justify summary denial of discovery. Such categorical rulings should be reserved for cases of clear intent to burden or harass, cases of clear flaunting of statutory responsibilities, cases of established track records of lack of good faith, and the like.” (In re Marriage of Moore (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 1275, 1296.)

Defendant submits evidence with the Motion showing Plaintiff’s counsel repeatedly ignored Defendant’s counsel’s emails. (Sadanaga Declaration ¶¶ 3, 6.) The record here shows no clear intent to burden or harass, flaunting of statutory responsibilities, or lack of good faith. The court will consider the Motion’s merits.

III.           LEGAL STANDARD

“The attendance and testimony of any other deponent, as well as the production by the deponent of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing for inspection and copying, requires the service on the deponent of a deposition subpoena under Chapter 6 (commencing with Section 2020.010).” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.280, subd. (b).)

“If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b), or upon the court's own motion after giving counsel notice and an opportunity to be heard, may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders. In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)

“If a deponent fails to answer any question or to produce any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing under the deponent's control that is specified in the deposition notice or a deposition subpoena, the party seeking discovery may move the court for an order compelling that answer or production.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (a).)

IV.           DISCUSSION

Defendant requests the court order Paz’s deposition and production of documents. (Notice of Motion pp. 1-2.)

In opposition, Plaintiff states Paz is willing and available to appear for deposition, and the delay is attributable to Paz being out of the country. (Opposition pp. 2-3.) Plaintiff requests the court deny the Motion because it is unnecessary. (Opposition p. 5.)

In reply, Defendant argues Plaintiff did not propose a deposition date until after Defendant filed the Motion. (Reply p. 2.) Defendant states the parties have confirmed a date for the deposition, but requests the court issue an order compelling Paz’s appearance to ensure the deposition is completed. (Reply p. 2.)

Nothing before the court indicates the parties completed Paz’s deposition. Thus, the Motion is not moot.

The parties do not appear to dispute that Paz’s deposition should be taken. In contrast, the dispute here appears to concern scheduling the date to complete the deposition. Thus, the court will order the parties to complete Paz’s deposition.

Defendant’s notice of motion also indicates Defendant is also moving to compel Paz to produce documents. (Notice of Motion pp. 1-2.) Motions to compel further responses to demands for inspection of documents or tangible things must be accompanied by a separate statement. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(a)(3).) Defendant filed no separate statement. As such, the request to compel Paz to produce documents is denied.

V.              SANCTIONS

Defendant moves for sanctions against Plaintiff and/or Plaintiff’s counsel under Code of Civil Procedure sections 2023.030 and 2025.450, subdivision (g)(1).  (Memorandum pp. 6-7.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.450 applies when “a party to the action or an officer, director, managing agent, or employee of a party, or a person designated by an organization that is a party under Section 2025.230” fails to appear for deposition. As a non-party, section 2025.450 does not apply to Paz. As such, the court denies Defendant’s request for sanctions under section 2025.450.

“A court may invoke its independent authority to impose sanctions under sections 2023.010 and 2023.030 only when confronted with an unusual form of discovery abuse, or a pattern of abuse, not already addressed by a relevant sanctions provision.” (City of Los Angeles v. PricewaterhouseCoopers, LLP (2024) 17 Cal.5th 46, 74.)

Plaintiff’s counsel did not respond to Defendant’s counsel’s inquiries about scheduling Paz’s deposition. (Sadanaga Declaration ¶¶ 3, 6.) The court finds this is not an unusual form of discovery abuse or pattern of abuse that warrants sanctioning Plaintiff or Plaintiff’s counsel.

In opposition, Plaintiff requests the court sanction Defendant and Defendant’s counsel because Defendants’ counsel’s meet and confer efforts were insufficient and Defendant’s counsel filed the Motion rather than resolve the matter informally. (Opposition p. 4.)

Defendant’s counsel repeatedly requested dates from Plaintiff’s counsel to schedule Paz’s deposition. (Sadanaga Declaration ¶¶ 3, 6, 9.) Plaintiff’s counsel appears to have first informed Defendant’s counsel Paz was out of the country on May 22, 2026. (Gopstein Declaration ¶ 6.) May 22, 2026, was just a few days before Plaintiff filed the opposition and more than a month after Defendant filed the Motion. Defendant’s counsel’s conduct here does not warrant imposing sanctions against Defendant and Defendant’s counsel. Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions against Defendant and Defendant’s counsel is denied.

VI.           CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the request to compel non-party Paz’s deposition is granted. Catalina Paz is ordered to appear for deposition within fifteen (15) days of this order. Defendant’s and Plaintiff’s requests for sanctions are denied.

COURT’S [TENTATIVE] ORDER RE:

Motion to Enforce Settlement

 

[THE FOLLOWING IS A TENTATIVE RULING IN THE ABOVE CASE]:

 

Deny

 

I.                BACKGROUND

This is an employment dispute. On February 5, 2026, Plaintiff Robert Ruiz (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants Viva Medcare Trans, Inc. and Does 1 to 10, alleging causes of action for: (1) misclassification as independent contractor (Labor Code section 226.8); (2) failure to pay overtime wages (Labor Code sections 510, 1194); (3) failure to provide meal periods (Labor Code sections 226.7 and 512); (4) failure to provide rest periods (Labor Code section 226.7); (5) failure to reimburse business expenses (Labor Code section 2802); (6) failure to provide accurate itemized wage statements (Labor Code section 226); (7) failure to pay all wages due upon termination (Labor Code sections 201-203); and (8) unfair business practices (Bus. & Prof. Code section 17200, et seq.)

On June 15, 2026, Defendant Viva Medcare Trans, Inc. (“Defendant”) filed a motion to enforce settlement agreement pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 (the “Motion”). On July 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On July 20, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

II.             LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6, subdivision (a) states:

If parties to pending litigation stipulate, in a writing signed by the parties outside of the presence of the court or orally before the court, for settlement of the case, or part thereof, the court, upon motion, may enter judgment pursuant to the terms of the settlement. If the parties to the settlement agreement or their counsel stipulate in writing or orally before the court, the court may dismiss the case as to the settling parties without prejudice and retain jurisdiction over the parties to enforce the settlement until performance in full of the terms of the settlement.

III.           DISCUSSION

Defendant moves to enforce a confidential settlement and release agreement entered into by Plaintiff and Defendant on January 15, 2025, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6.  (Motion pp. 1-2.)

In opposition, Plaintiff cites Kirby v. Southern California Edison Co. (“Kirby”) (2000) 78 Cal.App.4th 840 and argues a section 664.6 motion is not the proper procedural mechanism to enforce a settlement agreement entered into before the complaint was filed. (Opposition p. 2.)

In reply, Defendant cites no authority suggesting a motion brought under Code of Civil Procedure 664.6 is proper here, but states:

Whatever the proper procedural label, the underlying release is enforceable on the terms set out above, and Defendant respectfully requests that the Court enforce it on this record. To the extent the Court concludes that a differently captioned motion is required to reach that result, Defendant requests leave to promptly renew this showing in that form. 

(Reply p. 4.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 is authorized only when the agreement is made while the litigation is pending. (Kirby, supra, 78 Cal.App.4th at p. 845.) “There can be no litigants until there is a case on file to litigate. And no one can stipulate to a settlement of ‘the case’ unless and until that case has been filed.” (Id. at pp. 844-845.)

Plaintiff filed the complaint on February 5, 2025. Defendant seeks to enforce a settlement entered into on January 15, 2025. (Motion pp. 1-2; Petrosian Declaration ¶¶ 4-5.) Thus, Defendant seeks to enforce a settlement agreement entered into before this litigation was pending. Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 is inapplicable here.

Defendant requests the court grant it leave to renew its motion brought under a different procedural label. (Reply p. 4.) The court here concludes only that a motion brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 is improper here. The court takes no position on what motion is required or permitted to reach Defendant’s desired result.

IV.           CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Motion is denied.

COURT’S [TENTATIVE] ORDER RE:

Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

[THE FOLLOWING IS A TENTATIVE RULING IN THE ABOVE CASE]:

 

Deny

 

I.                BACKGROUND 

 

On April 8, 2023, Plaintiff Michael Scruton (“Plaintiff”) acquired a certified pre-owned 2021 Ford Ecosport (the “Vehicle”). (Complaint ¶ 6.) The Vehicle was defective. (Complaint ¶ 11.)

 

On February 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed a complaint against Defendants Ford Motor Company (“Defendant”) and Does 1 through 20, alleging causes of action for: (1) breach of express warranty under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; (2) breach of implied warranty under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; and (3) violation of the Song-Beverly Act section 1793.2, subdivision (b).

 

On June 26, 2026, Defendant filed a motion for judgment on the pleadings (the “Motion”). On July 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On July 20, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

II.             MEET AND CONFER

Before filing a motion for judgment on the pleadings, the moving party is required to meet and confer “in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading . . .”(Code Civ. Proc., § 439, subd. (a).)

Defendant’s counsel submits a declaration stating they met and conferred with Plaintiff’s counsel by telephone, but the parties did not reach an agreement. (Villanueva Declaration ¶ 5.) Defendant satisfied the meet and confer requirement.

III.           TIMELINESS

“No motion may be made pursuant to this section if a pretrial conference order has been entered pursuant to Section 575, or within 30 days of the date the action is initially set for trial, whichever is later, unless the court otherwise permits.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (e).)

This matter is set for trial July 13, 2027. (06/29/26 Minute Order.) The Motion is brought more than 30 days before the date initially set for trial. Thus, it is timely.

IV.           LEGAL STANDARD

A defendant may move for judgment on the pleadings on the ground that “[t]he complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (c)(1)(B)(ii).) “In considering whether a defendant is entitled to judgment on the pleadings [the court] look[s] only to the face of the pleading under attack and do[es] not consider matters raised in the answer. All facts alleged in the complaint are admitted for purposes of the motion, and the court determines whether those facts constitute a cause of action. The court also may consider matters subject to judicial notice.” (Hunt v. County of Shasta (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 432, 440.)

 

V.              DISCUSSION

Defendant moves for judgment on the pleadings as to all of Plaintiff’s causes of action. (Motion p. 2.) Defendant argues the Motion should be granted because Plaintiff did not purchase a new vehicle subject to remedies under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act. (Motion p. 2.) Defendant also argues Plaintiff’s implied warranty claim fails because Defendant is a manufacturer and Plaintiff cannot assert implied warranties against Defendant. (Motion pp. 7-8.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues they were provided with new warranties at the time of purchase, so the Vehicle is a new motor vehicle as defined by Civil Code section 1793.22. (Opposition p. 4.) Plaintiff also argues the “new motor vehicle” provision in Civil Code section 1793.22 applies only to claims brought under Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (d), and does not apply to Plaintiff’s first and third causes of action. (Opposition p. 5.) Finally, Plaintiff argues their implied warranty claims are viable because Plaintiff alleges Defendant issued the Vehicle’s express warranty and authorized repairs. (Opposition p. 6.)

In reply, Defendant argues Plaintiff improperly relies on matters outside the pleadings. (Reply pp. 1-2.) Defendant further argues the implied warranty claim fails because Plaintiff purchased a used vehicle, Defendant is the manufacturer, and Plaintiff cannot state an implied warranty claim against Defendant under Civil Code section 1795.5. (Reply pp. 4-5.)

As an initial matter, Plaintiff relies on Plaintiff’s counsel’s declaration submitted with the Motion and exhibits attached to that declaration. (Opposition pp. 3, 5-6.) The court here considers only the pleading. (See Hunt v. County of Shasta, supra, 225 Cal.App.3d at p. 440.)

“If the manufacturer or its representative in this state is unable to service or repair a new motor vehicle, as that term is defined in paragraph (2) of subdivision (e) of Section 1793.22, to conform to the applicable express warranties after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer shall either promptly replace the new motor vehicle in accordance with subparagraph (A) or promptly make restitution to the buyer in accordance with subparagraph (B).”¿¿(Civ. Code, § 1793.2, subd. ¿(d)(2).)¿ 

In¿Rodriguez v. FCA US LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189, 196, the Supreme Court of California held that “a motor vehicle purchased with an unexpired manufacturer's new car warranty does not qualify as a ‘motor vehicle sold with a manufacturer's new car warranty’ under section 1793.22, subdivision (e)(2) ’s definition of ‘new motor vehicle’ unless the new car warranty was issued with the sale.”¿The¿Court found that “it would be anomalous to construe¿‘other motor vehicle sold with a manufacturer's new car warranty’¿(§ 1793.22, subd. (e)(2)) to include any used car sold by a distributor or retailer with a preexisting, unexpired manufacturer's warranty.”¿¿(Id. at p. 202.) 

Plaintiff alleges they “acquired a certified pre-owned 2021 Ford Ecosport[.]” (Complaint ¶ 6.) Plaintiff further alleges:

Along with the acquisition of the Vehicle, Plaintiff received written warranties and other express and implied warranties including, but not limited to, warranties from Defendants that the Vehicle and its components would be free from all defects in material and workmanship; that the Vehicle would pass without objection in the trade under the Contract description; that the Vehicle would be fit for the ordinary purposes for which it was intended; that the Vehicle would conform to the promises and affirmations of fact made; and that Defendants would perform any repairs, alignments, adjustments, and/or replacements of any parts necessary to ensure that the Vehicle was free from any defects in material and workmanship.

(Complaint ¶ 9.) Finally, Plaintiff alleges:

The warranties included a three (3) year or 36,000 mile basic warranty and a five (5) year or 60,000 mile powertrain warranty, which was extended by the Certified Pre-Owned warranty to include an additional twelve (12) month or 12,000 mile comprehensive limited warranty, as well as a seven (7) year or 100,000 mile powertrain limited warranty.

(Complaint ¶ 10.)

Plaintiff relies on Kiluk v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (“Kiluk”) (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 334, to argue new motor vehicles under Civil Code section 1793.22, subdivision (e) includes CPO vehicles. (Opposition p. 5.) In Kiluk, supra, 43 Cal.App.5th at p. 340, the Court of Appeal held the jury was permitted to analyze a manufacturer’s liability under Civil Code section 1793.2 because the manufacturer stepped into the role of a retailer and was subject to a retailer’s obligations under Civil Code section 1795.5. In reply, Defendant argues Kiluk, supra, 43 Cal.App.5th is distinguishable and is no longer good law following Rodriguez. (Reply p. 4.)

Plaintiff also relies on Stiles v. Kia America, Inc. (“Stiles”) (2024) 101 Cal.App.5th 913, 920. (Opposition p. 5.) Following Rodriguez, supra, 17 Cal.5th 189, Stiles was transferred and rendered not citable. The court does not consider it.

In Rodriguez, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 195, the Supreme Court of California held that “a motor vehicle purchased with an unexpired manufacturer’s new car warranty does not qualify as a ‘motor vehicle sold with a manufacturer’s new car warranty’ under section 1793.22, subdivision (e)(2)’s definition of ‘new motor vehicle’ unless the new car warranty was issued with the sale.”

Here, Plaintiff alleges the Vehicle was “certified” and “pre-owned[.]” (Complaint ¶ 6.) Plaintiff further alleges that “[a]long with the acquisition of the Vehicle, Plaintiff received written warranties and other express and implied warranties . . .” (Complaint ¶ 9.) It is unclear whether Plaintiff is alleging they received a new car warranty issued with the sale, such that this case falls outside of Rodriguez, or whether Plaintiff is alleging they purchased the Vehicle with an unexpired manufacturer’s new car warranty, such that this case falls within the rule as stated in Rodriguez.  However, nothing in the complaint suggests the warranty Plaintiff alleges they acquired with the Vehicle is not the “same type of manufacturer’s warranty that accompan[ies] new cars.” (Rodriguez, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 198.) The court concludes that whether the alleged warranty is the same type that accompanies new cars is a factual issue. 

“[I]n the sale of used consumer goods, liability for breach of implied warranty lies with distributors and retailers, not the manufacturer, where there is no evidence the manufacturer played any role in the sale of the used car to plaintiff.” (Ruiz Nunez v. FCA US LLC (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 385, 398, italics added (Ruiz Nunez).) “Under the lemon law, only distributors and retail sellers, not manufacturers, are liable for breach of implied warranties in the sale of a used car where … the manufacturer did not offer the used car for sale to the public.” (Id. at p. 289, italics added.) 

Here, Plaintiff alleges the Vehicle was “engaged in the manufacture, sale, distribution, and/or importing of Ford motor vehicles and related equipment.” (Complaint ¶ 1.) Plaintiff alleges they “relied on Defendants’ advertised trustworthiness of the CPO certification program in selecting the Vehicle” and received warranties from Defendant “[a]long with the acquisition of the Vehicle[.]” (Complaint ¶¶ 8-9.) Plaintiff further alleges:

Certified Pre-Owned ("CPO") vehicles undergo rigorous scrutiny by the manufacturer or an authorized dealership to meet specific quality standards before receiving the CPO designation. This process includes a comprehensive multi-point inspection of the vehicle’s mechanical systems, safety features, and overall condition, as well as necessary repairs or refurbishments to ensure compliance with stringent certification requirements.

(Complaint ¶ 7.) Thus, Plaintiff alleges Defendant played a role Plaintiff’s acquisition of the Vehicle, which takes this matter outside of the rule as stated in Ruiz Nunez.

VI.           CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Motion is denied.
