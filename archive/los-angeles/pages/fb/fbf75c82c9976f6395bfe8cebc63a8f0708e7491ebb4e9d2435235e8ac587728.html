DEPARTMENT W LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24VECV05930    Hearing Date: July 28, 2026    Dept: W

[TENTATIVE] ORDER: The Court CONTINUES the hearing on these four motions to August 31, 2026. The parties are ordered to meet and confer over the phone or in person for at least one hour to discuss the underlying discovery issues addressed in this motion and to potentially resolve these discovery disputes informally. The parties are also ordered to file a joint declaration describing in detail the results of the meet and confer by August 24, 2026.

 

Background

This is a motor vehicle negligence case. Plaintiff Manijeh Motamen alleges that, on January 19, 2024, Defendant Ivory Calabria crashed her vehicle into the driver-side door of Plaintiff’s car while driving southbound on Topanga Canyon Boulevard. Plaintiff alleges that this crash caused her door to jam, and she was trapped in her vehicle until emergency responders tore the door off with a hydraulic rescue tool.

On December 2, 2024, Plaintiff filed suit, alleging two causes of action for motor vehicle and general negligence.

Now, Plaintiff brings four motions to compel further discovery responses from Defendant Ivory Calabria.

            Discussion 

            Plaintiff moves to compel further responses for four separate discovery requests from Defendant. Specifically, Plaintiff moves to compel further responses to:

 RFAs, Set Two, Nos. 22, 23, and 25-65; 

SROGs, Set Two, Nos. 27-28, 30, 34, 36-37, 40-41, 49-56, and 59;

FROGs, Set Two, Nos. 15.1, 16.1-16.8, and 17.1;

RFPs, Set Two, Nos. 31, 41-45, and 47-49.

i)               Inadequate Meet and Confer

As a threshold matter, the Court finds that Plaintiff has failed to meet and confer properly before filing this motion. A motion¿to compel further responses, “shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.”¿(CCP § 2033.290(b)(1); CCP §§ 2030.300(b)(1); 2031.310(b)(2).) The declaration must state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented in the motion. (CCP § 2016.040.) Here, Plaintiff provides the declaration of their lawyer Daniel Hakhamzadeh in support of all four motions. (See e.g., MTCF Re: Form Interrogatories, Hakhamzadeh Decl.) Mr. Hakhamzadeh states that on March 22, 2026, he sent a meet and confer email to Defendant’s counsel. (Hakhamzadeh Decl. ¶ 4.) Plaintiff attaches a copy of this email to the separate statements. (See e.g., Sep. Statement Re: FROGs, Ex. C.) Defendant emailed back one week later, agreeing to supplement some responses, but not others. (Id. ¶ 5; see also Sep. Statement Re: FROGs, Ex. D.)

This Court does not find that sending a single meet and confer email shows a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution to a discovery dispute of this magnitude. Thus, Plaintiff has failed meet their meet and confer obligations. For this reason, the Court will continue this motion to August 31, 2026. Both parties are ordered to meet and confer over the phone or in person for at least one hour to discuss the underlying discovery issues addressed in this motion and to potentially resolve these discovery disputes informally. The parties are also ordered to file a joint declaration describing in detail the results of the meet and confer by August 24, 2026.

ii)             Informal Notes from the Court

However, in an attempt to facilitate a productive discussion, the Court will briefly address some of the common themes of these requests.

First, the Court agrees with Plaintiff that many of Defendant’s responses constitute improper boilerplate objections. For example, RFAs Nos. 22-32 all request admissions to simple factual questions. (See generally, Sep. Statement Re: RFAs, pp. 1-9.) Plaintiff responds to all of these requests with the same boilerplate objection:

“The request calls for speculation. The request is not relevant and is improper as it does not seek the adjudication of facts. Without waiving said objections, a reasonable inquiry concerning the matter has been made, and that the information known or readily obtainable is insufficient to enable Responding party to admit or deny the matter.” (See e.g., Id. p. 8:13-17.)

            Similarly, Defendant provides boilerplate objections to simple factual special interrogatories, such as “Do YOU contend that Plaintiff suffered no new injury to her cervical spine as a result of the INCIDENT?” (Sep. Statement Re: SROGs, p. 5:4-5.) Plaintiff improperly objects to this SROG, stating, “Objection. The interrogatory is overbroad. The interrogatory seeks a legal conclusion and calls for a premature disclosure of expert opinion. Without waiving said objections, Responding party lacks information and belief to respond at this time.” (Id. p. 5:8-10.) This is not a question that requires either a legal conclusion or an expert opinion. Thus, this response, as well as other similar ones, are improper.

Additionally, the Court disagrees with Defendant that Plaintiff’s separate statement as to FROGs is procedurally defective. Plaintiff’s responses to FROGs, which are attached as Exhibit B to the FROGs separate statement, match the responses in the separate statement. The one exception to this is FROG 15.1, which involved a multi-page explanation of every affirmative defense Defendant plans to employ. In this instance, Plaintiff’s brevity is appreciated, not problematic.

While this is not a comprehensive list, the Court hopes that these clarifications will help the parties resolve this discovery dispute informally.

Conclusion

The Court CONTINUES the hearing on these four motions to August 31, 2026. The parties are ordered to meet and confer over the phone or in person for at least one hour to discuss the underlying discovery issues addressed in this motion and to potentially resolve these discovery disputes informally. The parties are also ordered to file a joint declaration describing in detail the results of the meet and confer by August 24, 2026.

 

            IT IS SO ORDERED,

            [TENTATIVE] ORDER: The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Further Responses to Form Interrogatories 215.1 and 12.2. The Court also GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for discovery sanctions in the modified amount of $1,500.00.

Background

This is an employment law case. Plaintiff Jose Maciel alleges that his former employer, Defendants Pruno Veterinary Enterprises, Inc. and Michael Pitt violated multiple provisions of the California Labor Code, and wrongfully terminating Plaintiff’s employment. Among other things, Plaintiff alleges he was regularly not paid for overtime work, or his standard wages.

On August 29, 2025, Plaintiff brought suit against Defendants, alleging eleven separate causes of action: 1) Retaliation in Violation of Cal. Labor Code § 98.6; 2) Retaliation in Violation of Cal. Labor Code § 1102.5; 3) Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy; 4) Failure to Pay Overtime Compensation; 5) Failure to Pay Minimum Wage; 6) Failure to Provide Meal Periods; 7) Failure to Provide Rest Breaks; 8) Failure to Pay Wages Due Upon Termination; Waiting Time Penalties; 9) Failure to Issue Accurate and Itemized Wage Statements; 10) Failure to Indemnify; and 11) Unlawful Business Practices in Violation of CA B&P Code 17200.

Now, Plaintiff moves to compel further responses to two form interrogatories from Defendant Pruno Veterinary Enterprises, Inc. 

Meet and Confer

A motion¿to compel further responses, “shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.”¿(CCP § 2033.290(b)(1); CCP §§ 2030.300(b)(1); 2031.310(b)(2).) The declaration must state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented in the motion. (CCP § 2016.040.)

Here, Plaintiff submits the declaration of Kelly Ha, who states that Plaintiff sent two separate meet and confer letters to Defendant to discuss the issues raised in this discovery motion. (Ha Decl. ¶ 5, 7.) While the parties were able to agree on some supplemental responses, two outstanding interrogatories remain in dispute. (Id. ¶¶ 6, 8, 9.)

Typically, this Court does not find that simple meet and confer emails are sufficient to establish a good faith effort to meet and confer. However, given that Plaintiff met and conferred repeatedly, and was able to narrow down the scope of this discovery dispute to a single topic, the Court will opt to hear the motion on the merits.

            Discussion 

i)               Motion to Compel Further

Plaintiff moves to compel further responses to two form interrogatories from Defendant Pruno Veterinary Enterprises, Inc. 

CCP § 2030.300(a) provides, “On receipt of a response to interrogatories, the propounding party may move for an order compelling a further response if the propounding party deems that any of the following apply:¿1) An answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete; 2) An exercise of the option to produce documents under Section 2030.230 is unwarranted or the required specification of those documents is inadequate; 3) An objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general.” (CCP § 2030.300(a).)

Here, Plaintiff moves to compel further responses to General Form Interrogatory No. 12.2, and Employment Interrogatory No. 215.1. (Sep. Statement, p. 2:3-12.) These two FROGs request functionally identical information. 

FROG No. 215.1 asks:

“Have YOU OR ANYONE ACTING ON YOUR BEHALF interviewed any individual concerning the ADVERSE EMPLOYMENT ACTION? If so, for each individual state:

(a) the name, ADDRESS, and telephone number of the individual interviewed;

(b) the date of the interview; and

(c) the name, ADDRESS, and telephone number of the PERSON who conducted the interview.” (Sep. Statement, p. 2:7-12.)

Similarly, Employment FROG No. 12.2 states:

“Have YOU OR ANYONE ACTING ON YOUR BEHALF interviewed any individual concerning the INCIDENT? If so, for each individual state:

(a) the name, ADDRESS, and telephone number of the individual interviewed;

(b) the date of the interview; and

(c) the name, ADDRESS, and telephone number of the PERSON who conducted the interview.” (Id. p. 3:21-26.)

In response to both of these discovery requests, Plaintiff objects, stating that the terms “incident”, “adverse employment action”, and “interviewed” are vague and ambiguous terms. (Sep. Statement, p. 3:3-6.) Plaintiff also states that this information is protected by attorney client privilege. (Sep. Statement, p. 3:7-10.) Finally, Plaintiff states that, “[s]ubject to and without waiving the foregoing objections . . . Defendant has not conducted any non-privileged interviews.” (Id. p. 3:11-13.)

The Court finds these objections improper and answers incomplete. First, this is a lawsuit about Plaintiff’s wrongful firing. (Compl. ¶ 19.) While there are other employment incidents at issue, the context of this case makes it clear that the terms “incident” and “adverse employment action” reference Plaintiff’s firing.

Second, the Court is unconvinced that answers to either of these interrogatories involve privileged information. Notably, these requests only ask for contact details for individuals who were interviewed, not the contents of those interviews. Thus, the only way for Defendant to establish that this information is privileged is to show that simply knowing the identities of the individuals interviewed would disclose privileged work product.

In theory, witness-related interview information may be protected if the resisting party establishes that “answering the interrogatory would reveal the attorney's tactics, impressions, or evaluation of the case, or would result in opposing counsel taking undue advantage of the attorney's industry or effort.” (Coito v. Superior Court (2012) 54 Cal.4th 480, 502.) 

Defendant fails to establish that this information would reveal any crucial impressions or information.  Rather than address specific facts about the interviews, Mr. Poplawska relies on general statements about the strategic nature of the interviews, such as, “[d]isclosure of the identities of any such interviewees would reveal counsel’s tactical and evaluative judgments, including which witnesses counsel considered significant, which factual areas counsel prioritized, and how counsel chose to allocate investigative effort” and that fact that prior counsel did not interview, “every conceivable witness.” (Id. ¶¶ 12, 15.) These are not facts that establish the need for discovery protections. To give a contrasting example, the hypothetical bus accident the Coito court used to illustrate when disclosure of identities might reveal tactical judgments was highly factually specific as to the context of the interviews, the unique qualities of the interviewed witnesses, as well as the attorney’s efforts in procuring that information. (Coito, supra, at 501.) Here, Defendant does not provide similar factual specificity.

Additionally, the Court is not convinced that Defendant’s attorney Mr. Poplawska has the appropriate foundation to provide a declaration in support of this argument. Mr. Poplawska states that he substituted into this case in June of 2026, and did not personally conduct any of these supposedly privileged interviews. (Poplawska Decl. ¶ 4.) If Mr. Poplawska was not responsible for these interviews, it strikes the Court as implausible that can he attest to their strategic nature.

Moreover, Mr. Poplawska repeatedly uses the language “if any” in reference to these interviews. For instance, he states, “any interviews that were conducted, if any, were the product of counsel’s selective judgment concerning which individuals, if any, warranted follow-up based on counsel’s evaluation of the issues.” (Id. ¶ 13.) This language suggests one of two things – either Defendant’s counsel is not aware of any interviews that took place, or that none took place at all. Either scenario undermines Mr. Poplawska’s declaration.

For these reasons, the Court finds Defendant’s objections and responses to these two interrogatories are insufficient. Thus, the Court GRANTS the Motion to Compel Further as to both interrogatories.

ii)             Discovery Sanctions

Plaintiff also requests $3,250.00 in discovery sanctions against Defendant and their counsel of record. (MTCF, p. 7:1-3.)

Under CCP § 2030.290(c), “[t]he court shall impose a monetary sanction . . . against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (CCP § 2030.290(c).)

Here, the Court has already found Defendant’s objections to these discovery requests unfounded. Thus, the Court finds sanctions are appropriate.

However, the Court finds Plaintiff’s requested amount unreasonable. Plaintiff bases this request on 6.5 hours of work, billed at a rate of $500 an hour. (Poplawska Decl. ¶ 10.) The Court takes issue with Plaintiff billing 1 hour for anticipate attendance at the hearing on this motion. The Court does not anticipate this hearing lasting more than a few minutes. Thus, the Court reduces this request to 0.5. Additionally, the Court does not find the combined 3 hours in anticipated billing for review of the Opposition and drafting a Reply to be reasonable. The Court reduces these hours by 1.

Additionally, the Court finds Plaintiff’s request for $500 an hour unreasonable. This is a simple discovery dispute involving two nearly identical requests. Incurring such high billable rates in pursuit of a minor discovery dispute is not a reasonably incurred cost. Thus, the Court reduces this hourly rate to $300 an hour.

Based on Plaintiff’s reduced hours and rates, the Court calculates Plaintiff’s reasonably incurred fees at $1,500.00.

Thus, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for discovery sanctions in the modified amount of $1,500.00.

Conclusion

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s Motion for Compel Further Responses to Form Interrogatories 215.1 and 12.2. The Court also GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for discovery sanctions in the modified amount of $1,500.00.

 

            IT IS SO ORDERED, Plaintiff TO GIVE NOTICE.

            [TENTATIVE] ORDER: The Court SUSTAINS Moving Defendant’s Demurrer as to all four causes of action. The Court TENTATIVELY DENIES leave to amend.

 

Background

This is an unfair insurance claims settlement suit, arising out of a motor vehicle accident. Plaintiff in pro-per La’asia Stith is a driver for the app-based platform Uber Eats. Non-moving Defendants in this case are Uber Technologies, Inc., Raiser, LLC, an Uber subsidiary, and AIG Specialty Insurance Company, Uber’s insurer. Moving Defendant in this case is Toggle Services, LLC, AIG’s authorized third-party claims administrator.

Plaintiff alleges that on December 31, 2025, while making an Uber Eats delivery, another vehicle crashed into her car. Plaintiff’s vehicle was severely damaged. Plaintiff alleges that her vehicle was fully insured via a policy distributed by Defendant AIG, and administered by Defendant Toggle Services. Plaintiff further alleges that she filed an insurance claim in early January, 2026, but that Defendants did not issue a formal assessment of the claim until January 30, 2026. Defendant explains that they needed a swift resolution to this claim because their vehicle is also their primary residence. Ultimately, Defendants denied Plaintiff’s claim.

On February 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed suit against Defendants alleging four causes of action: 1) breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealings, 2) negligence, 3) unfair business practices, and 4) declaratory relief.

Now, Moving Defendant demurs to all four causes of action.

            Meet and Confer

Before filing a demurrer, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (CCP § 430.41(a).) A declaration must be filed with a demurrer regarding the results of the meet and confer process. (CCP § 430.41(a)(3).) 

Here, Moving Defendant provides the declaration of James Lemieux, who states that on March 26, 2026, he sent Plaintiff a meet and confer letter addressing the issues raised in this filing. (Lemieux Decl. ¶ 2.) Mr. Lemieux states that the parties were unable to settle this dispute out of court.

Generally, the Court does not find meet and confer efforts that do not involve a phone call or an in-person meeting satisfy these requirements. However, given the facts of this motion, the Court opts to hear this motion on the merits.

Discussion 

Moving Defendant demurs to all four causes of action in this case, arguing that Plaintiff fails to allege facts sufficient to support any of these claims, as to Moving Defendant Toggle Services, LLC. (Demurrer, p. 2:1-10.)

A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of other pleadings. It raises issues of law, not fact, regarding the form or content of the opposing party’s pleading (complaint, answer or cross-complaint). (CCP §§ 422.10, 589; see, Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) It is not the function of the demurrer to challenge the truthfulness of the complaint; and for purposes of the ruling on the demurrer, all facts pled in the complaint are assumed to be true. (Id. at 994.)

i)               Cause of Action 1: Breach of Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealings

First, Moving Defendant demurs to this first cause of action, arguing that Plaintiff cannot support this claim because Moving Defendant was not a party to any contract. (Demurrer, p. 5:3-28.)

The elements of a cause of action for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing are: 1) a contractual relationship; 2) a duty of good faith and fair dealing arising from the contract; 3) conduct that unfairly frustrates the plaintiff's right to receive the benefits of the agreement; and 4) resulting damages. (Smith v. City & Cnty. of San Francisco (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 38, 49.)

Moving Defendant argues that Plaintiff has failed to allege that they are in a contractual relationship with Plaintiff. (Demurrer, p. 5:12-13.) Instead, Plaintiff has alleged that non-moving Defendant, AIG, issued Plaintiff her insurance. (Compl. ¶ 20.) Moving Defendant, meanwhile, is alleged to be a licensed third-party administrator. (Compl. ¶ 12.) Thus, it is unclear from Plaintiff’s Complaint how they satisfy the first element of this claim as to Moving Defendant.

In Opposition, Plaintiff appears to concede the fact that there is no contractual relationship between the parties. (Opp. p. 5:10-13.)

Given that Plaintiff has failed to allege an essential element of this claim, as to Moving Defendant, the Court SUSTAINS this demurrer as to the first cause of action.

ii)             Cause of Action 2: Negligence

Second, Moving Defendant demurs to Plaintiff’s negligence claim, arguing that Plaintiff has failed to allege the element of duty. (Demurrer, p. 6:13-20.)

The essential elements of a cause of action for negligence are: 1) duty; (2) breach; (3) injury to the plaintiff; and (4) damage to the plaintiff. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 141.)

As mentioned above, Plaintiff has alleged that Moving Defendant is AIG’s licensed third-party administrator. (Compl. ¶ 12.) Under California case law, an independent adjuster hired by an insurer owes no duty of care to a claimant who they have no contract with. (Sanchez v. Lindsey Morden Claims Services, Inc. (1999) 72 Cal. App. 4th 249, 250-251.) Here, Plaintiff has failed to allege a contractual relationship with Moving Defendant. Thus, Plaintiff cannot establish the first element of their negligence claim as a matter of law.

For these reasons, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the second cause of action.

iii)           Cause of Action 3: Unfair Business Practices

Next, Moving Defendant demurs to the third cause of action for unfair business practices. (Demurrer, p. 6:23-27.)

The elements of a cause of action for unfair business practices are: 1) defendant engaged in unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business acts or practices, or unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising; 2) plaintiff's entitlement to restitution and/or injunctive relief. (BPC §§ 17200 & 17203.)

Here, Plaintiff alleges that Moving Defendant’s unfair practices arise from their “unfair claims settlement practices as defined by Insurance Code § 790.03(h).” (Compl. ¶ 41.) As explained above, Moving Defendant is not alleged to be in a contract with Plaintiff, and does not owe Plaintiff a duty as a matter of law. Thus, as alleged, Moving Defendant cannot have engaged in unfair claims practices as a matter of law, as they are simply an independent adjuster working for Plaintiff’s actual insurer, AIG.

For these reasons, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the third cause of action. 

iv)            Cause of Action 4: Declaratory Relief

Finally, Moving Defendant demurs to the fourth cause of action – a request for declaratory relief. (Demurrer, p. 7:16-26.)

Specifically, Plaintiff requests, “a judicial declaration clarifying Defendants’ duties owed to me under the insurance program and governing California law.” (Compl. ¶ 44.) Here, Plaintiff has only alleged that Moving Defendant is a “licensed third-party administrator” and “authorized claims administrator” for Plaintiff’s insurer AIG. (Compl. ¶ 12.) As explained above, this means that Moving Defendant has no underlying duties to Plaintiff, per Sanchez. Thus, based on Plaintiff’s own pleadings, this cause of action fails.

Moreover, under California law, a claim for declaratory relief cannot exist in the absence of a valid underlying claim. (City of Cotati v. Cashman (2002) 29 Cal.4th 69, 80.) Here, the Court has sustained this demurrer as to all other claims brought against Moving Defendant. Thus, Plaintiff does not have a valid underlying claim, meaning the claim for declaratory relief fails as well.

For these reasons, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the fourth cause of action.

v)             Leave to Amend

Next, the Court must determine whether leave to amend is appropriate as to claims two and six for aiding and abetting breach of fiduciary duty, and violations of CORP § 25504.1, respectively. “Although leave to amend is to be liberally granted, it is not error to deny leave to amend when there is no ‘reasonable possibility’ that the plaintiff can state a cause of action.” (Tola v. Bryant (2022) 76 Cal.App.5th 746, 755 [citing Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967].) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)

            Here, the Court’s tentative ruling is to deny leave to amend. The burden rests with Plaintiff to establish that a pleading can be amended to state a valid cause of action. Here, the Court has found that all of Plaintiff’s claims are barred against Moving Defendant as a matter of law. Thus, it is unclear how new, non-contradictory allegations might salvage Plaintiff’s claims against this third-party claims administrator.

            In Plaintiff’s Opposition, she repeatedly requests leave to amend. (Opp. p. 5:23-25; see also, p. 7:1-4.) However, Plaintiff does not elaborate as to any specific facts she has that will allow her to bring a cause of action against Moving Defendant. For instance, Plaintiff asserts that, “I can amend to more specifically allege Toggle’s direct undertaking of claim handling functions, direct communications of claim positions, direct handling of valuation and settlement communications, direct transmission of unsupported or incomplete denial positions, and direct conduct causing foreseeable harm after notice that the vehicle was my domicile and I was displaced.” (Id. p. 6:6-10.) None of these facts are inconsistent with Moving Defendant’s alleged role as a claims administrator. Thus, it is unclear what these facts add that would allow Plaintiff to salvage her claims.

            For these reasons, the Court’s tentative ruling is to deny leave to amend. However, the Could will reserve its ruling on this issue for after the hearing on the matter.

Conclusion

The Court SUSTAINS Moving Defendant’s Demurrer as to all four causes of action. The Court TENTATIVELY DENIES leave to amend.
