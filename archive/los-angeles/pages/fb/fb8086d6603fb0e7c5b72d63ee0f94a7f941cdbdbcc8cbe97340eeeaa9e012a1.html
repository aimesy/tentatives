DEPARTMENT 406 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV15909    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 406

 

RALPH SIBLEY, et al.,

                        Plaintiffs,

            v.

 

FORD MOTOR COMPANY, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV15909

  Hearing Date:  September 11, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant ford motor company’s motion for summary judgment or adjudication

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On May 30, 2025, Plaintiffs Ralph Sibley and Catherine Sibley filed this action against Defendants Ford Motor Company (Ford) and Santa Monica Ford Lincoln (Dealer). The complaint asserts causes of action for violations of the Song-Beverly Act, breach of warranty, negligent repair, and fraud. The fraud claim has since been stricken.

            On June 15, 2026, Ford filed the instant motion for summary judgment or adjudication. Plaintiffs filed an opposition on August 21, 2026. Ford filed a reply on August 31, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” (Leyva v. Garcia (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1095, 1101.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce “substantial responsive evidence.” (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)

DISCUSSION

I. Express Warranty

a. Statute of Repose

Effective January 1, 2025, the Code of Civil Procedure imposes new time limits for actions “seeking restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle pursuant to subdivision (b) or (d) of Section 1793.2, Section 1793.22, or Section 1794 of the Civil Code, or for civil penalties pursuant to subdivision (c) of Section 1794 of the Civil Code, where the request for restitution or replacement is based on noncompliance with the applicable express warranty.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.20(a).) Specifically, the action “shall be commenced within one year after the expiration of the applicable express warranty.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.21(a).)

Here, Plaintiffs purchased the subject vehicle on April 20, 2021. (Def.’s Stmt. of Undisputed Facts (UF) 1.) Ford’s bumper-to-bumper warranty lasts for 3 years or 36,000 miles, whichever occurs sooner. (UF 2.) Thus, the warranty expired on April 20, 2024. Ford argues that the Song-Beverly claims are untimely because Plaintiffs filed this action on May 30, 2025, more than 1 year after the expiration of the bumper-to-bumper warranty. Plaintiffs presented the vehicle for repairs to the driver’s window and rear shock absorbers, which occurred under the bumper-to-bumper warranty. (UF 5, 9, 14.)

Plaintiffs argue that the bumper-to-bumper warranty is not the only applicable express warranty. Specifically, the vehicle also came with a 5-year/60,000-mile powertrain warranty, an 8-year/100,000-mile hybrid/electric vehicle warranty, and emissions warranties. (Plntf.’s Stmt. of Additional Facts (AF) 22-24.) Plaintiffs contend that the vehicle was subject to a recall for an engine defect and that this defect falls under the longer warranties.

In June 2023, Ford issued a recall notice to Plaintiffs, stating that “a defect that relates to motor vehicle safety exists in your vehicle, with the VIN shown above.” (Moawad Decl., Ex. 8, emphasis added.) The recall notice stated that “significant quantities of engine oil and/or fuel vapor may be released into the under hood environment and may migrate to and/or accumulate near ignition sources.” (Ibid.) According to the notice, this may lead to “a reduction in available engine power” and “increasing the risk of a fire.” (Ibid.) This is evidence that an engine defect existed in Plaintiffs’ specific vehicle.

The engine defect potentially falls under the 5-year/60,000-mile powertrain warranty, which expired on April 20, 2026. A claim under this warranty would therefore be timely under the statute of repose. Ford presented no evidence that the faulty window and leaking shock absorbers were the only defects on the subject vehicle. In fact, evidence of the additional engine defect appears in Ford’s own evidence. (Moawad Decl., Ex. 8 [recall notice]; Ex. 9 at 40:2-42:4 [testimony regarding engine recall].) By failing to address all relevant defects and the warranties applicable to them, Ford has failed to satisfy its initial burden of showing that the Song-Beverly claims are time-barred as a matter of law.

In reply, Ford argues that the statute of repose is triggered by the expiration of “applicable” warranties, not all existing warranties. Ford argues that Plaintiffs never presented the vehicle for repair under any warranty except the bumper-to-bumper warranty. However, the engine defect arguably falls under the 5-year/60,000-mile powertrain warranty. Plaintiff Ralph Sibley testified that he waited four months for the engine to be repaired in relation to the 2023 recall notice. (Moawad Decl., Ex. 9 at 40:2-42:4.) This suggests that Plaintiffs did present the vehicle for the engine defect. Ford has not precluded this possibility.

In sum, triable issues remain over a potential engine defect falling within the 5-year/60,000-mile powertrain warranty. Because a claim under this warranty would be timely under the statute of repose, the Court cannot find the claims to be time-barred as a matter of law.

b. Substantial Impairment

A “nonconformity” is one which “substantially impairs the use, value, or safety of the new motor vehicle.” (Civ. Code, § 1793.22(e)(1).)

Ford argues that neither the window nor shock absorber defects substantially impaired the vehicle because Plaintiffs were admittedly able to drive it despite the defects. First, Ford cites no authority suggesting that a defect does not substantially impair a vehicle so long as the vehicle remains drivable. The Act requires substantial impairment, not total impairment. What is considered “substantial” is an inherently factual question. Plaintiff Ralph Sibley testified that the window defect impaired his ability to drive safely. (Moawad Decl., Ex. 9 at 25:1-27:15.)

Second, the additional engine defect may be considered a substantial impairment. In particular, the defect subjected the vehicle to a risk of premature engine failure and fire. (Moawad Decl., Ex. 8.) Plaintiff Ralph Sibley testified that he avoided driving on the freeway due to the risk. (Id., Ex. 9 at 40:8-12.) A reasonable trier of fact may find that the engine defect substantially impaired the use, value, or safety of the vehicle.

Ford’s argument that the recall was a preventative measure, and that no defect actually existed in Plaintiffs’ vehicle, is unsupported by evidence. Ford argues that the vehicle was “asymptomatic.” (Reply 3:15-17.) To the extent Ford is suggesting that a nonconformity did not exist because the engine did not actually catch fire or experience some type of failure, this position is unsupported by law. A “nonconformity” is one which “substantially impairs the use, value, or safety of the new motor vehicle.” (Civ. Code, § 1793.22(e)(1).) A defect which increases the risk of an engine failure or fire arguably impairs the vehicle’s use, value, or safety, even if a failure does not actually occur.

In sum, triable issues remain over whether the vehicle suffered from nonconformities which substantially impaired its use, value, or safety.

c. Timely Repairs

Under the Song-Beverly Act, “service and repair shall be commenced within a reasonable time by the manufacturer or its representative.” (Civ. Code, § 1793.2(b).) “Unless the buyer agrees in writing to the contrary, the goods shall be serviced or repaired so as to conform to the applicable warranties within 30 days.” (Ibid.)

Here, Ford argues that the window and shock absorber repairs were completed within one or two days. However, as discussed above, the evidence indicates that the vehicle suffered from an additional engine defect. Ford has presented no evidence regarding when it commenced or completed the repair of this defect, thus leaving open a triable issue. The existing evidence also raises a triable issue. The recall notice stated that repairs could not be commenced until parts were available in October 2023. (Moawad Decl., Ex. 8.) Plaintiff Ralph Sibley testified that he waited four months for repairs. (Id., Ex. 9 at 40:2-42:4.)

Ford argues that the claim requires evidence “that the Vehicle was out for service (i.e. out for repair at the shop) for more than 30 days or that the servicing dealership kept the vehicle longer than necessary because they lacked sufficient parts/service literature.” (Reply 4:7-9.) Ford cites no authority for this position. If Ford’s argument were true, a manufacturer could delay repairs for any amount of time and avoid liability under section 1793.2 so long as it does not physically keep the vehicle for more than 30 days. This would defy the purpose of the Act. Moreover, even if Ford’s legal argument were correct, Ford presents no evidence regarding how long it took to repair the engine defect. Thus, Ford has not precluded the possibility that it took longer than 30 days to repair the engine and accordingly fails to meet its initial burden on the motion.  

Moreover, the statute specifically states that repair must be “commenced within a reasonable time.” (Civ. Code, § 1793.2(b).) Ford’s argument that there is no independent duty to commence repairs within a reasonable time is unsupported by citation to authority and is contradicted by the plain language of the Act. (See also CACI 3205 [identifying two potential ways to violate section 1793.2(b)—failure to begin repairs in a reasonable time or failure to repair within 30 days].) Ford presents no evidence that it commenced repairs within a reasonable time, and the existing evidence suggests that repairs were not commenced within a reasonable time.

In sum, triable issues remain over Ford’s obligation to perform timely repairs.

d. Sufficient Parts and Literature

The Song-Beverly Act requires a manufacturer to “[m]ake available to authorized service and repair facilities sufficient service literature and replacement parts to effect repairs during the express warranty period.” (Civ. Code, § 1793.2(a)(3).)

Ford argues that “Plaintiffs’ document production and disclosures identified no facts nor evidence supporting any violation of Civil Code § 1793.2(a)(3) let alone any damages from such a violation.” (Mtn. 15:25-26.) However, Ford’s motion ignores the engine defect and presents no evidence regarding the provision of parts and literature pertaining to that defect. Therefore, Ford has failed to meet the summary judgment burden in relation to sufficient parts and literature.  

II. Implied Warranty

            Ford argues that the only defect which manifested during the one-year duration of the implied warranty (Civ. Code, § 1791.1(c)) was the window defect, which Plaintiffs admitted did not hinder their ability to drive. First, as discussed above, drivability is not the sole test for impairment. Plaintiff Ralph Sibley testified that the window defect presented a safety hazard. Second, Ford does not address the engine defect, presenting no evidence that this defect manifested outside of the one-year implied warranty period.

Ford also argues that Plaintiffs suffered no damages because they presented no evidence of the vehicle’s diminution in value as a result of the window defect. However, the engine defect arguably impaired the value of the vehicle. Ford presents no evidence to the contrary.

            Therefore, triable issues remain over Ford’s breach of the implied warranty.

CONCLUSION

            Defendant Ford Motor Company’s motion for summary judgment or adjudication is DENIED.

 


Case Number: 25STCV20125    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 406

 

DAVID CORRIEA, et al.,

                        Plaintiffs,

            v.

 

GENERAL MOTORS, LLC,

                        Defendant.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV20125

  Hearing Date:  September 11, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant’s motion to compel deposition

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On June 30, 2025, Plaintiffs David Corriea and Diana Corriea filed this action against Defendant General Motors, LLC for violations of the Song-Beverly Act, breach of warranty, and fraudulent concealment. Plaintiffs filed the operative First Amended Complaint on October 21, 2025.

            On August 7, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to compel Plaintiffs’ depositions. Plaintiffs filed an opposition on August 28, 2026. Defendant filed a reply on September 3, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure sections 871.20 to 871.30 set forth specific rules applying to actions for restitution or replacement of motor vehicles under the Song-Beverly Act. With regards to discovery, the Code provides that      “[w]ithin 120 days after the filing of the answer or other responsive pleading, all parties have the right to conduct initial depositions.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26(c).) The statute additionally sets forth a schedule of sanctions which “shall” be imposed “[u]nless the party failing to comply with this section shows good cause.” (Id., § 871.26(j).)          

DISCUSSION

            Here, Defendant filed its responsive pleading—a demurrer—on October 9, 2025. Thus, Defendant was entitled to depose Plaintiffs by February 6, 2026. Defendant served a meet-and-confer letter along with its demurrer in an attempt to obtain Plaintiffs’ availability for deposition. (Stark Decl. ¶ 4.) However, to date, Defendant has been unable to ascertain Plaintiffs’ availability, and Plaintiffs have not indicated their availability. (Id., ¶¶ 6-8.) Defendant’s counsel wrote to and called Plaintiffs’ counsel, to no avail. (Ibid.)

            Plaintiffs argue that they have never refused to be deposed and merely objected to Defendant’s unilaterally noticed date. However, Plaintiffs do not dispute ignoring Defendant’s repeated communications seeking their availability. Plaintiffs point out that they have been conferring on dates since August 28, 2026, after this motion was filed. (Smith Decl. ¶ 11.) This is nearly a year after Defendant sent its initial communication seeking Plaintiffs’ availability. The motion was clearly necessary to compel Plaintiffs’ compliance.

            Sanctions are also warranted, as Plaintiffs have not demonstrated good cause for their failure to appear for deposition within the statutory deadline. The personnel change that Plaintiffs’ counsel went through in June 2026 (Smith Decl. ¶ 10) does not justify Plaintiffs’ failure to provide their availability since October 2025. The statute mandates a $1,500 sanction against the plaintiff’s attorney, paid within 15 business days, for violation of the deposition requirements. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26(j)(2).)

CONCLUSION

            Defendant’s motion to compel depositions is GRANTED. Plaintiff David Corriea shall appear for deposition on _____________________. Plaintiff Diana Corriea shall appear for deposition on _____________________.

            The Court sanctions Plaintiffs’ counsel in the amount of $1,500, to be paid within 15 business days.

 


Case Number: 26STCV04635    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 406

 

MARIA ESPARZA,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

ALTAMED HEALTH SERVICES CORPORATION,

                       

                         Defendant.

 

  Case No.:  26STCV04635

  Hearing Date:  September 11, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant’s motion to compel arbitration

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On February 11, 2026, Plaintiff Maria Esparza filed this action against Defendant Altamed Health Services Corporation, asserting claims for harassment, discrimination, retaliation, and failure to prevent.

            On June 4, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to compel arbitration. Plaintiff filed an opposition on August 28, 2026. Defendant filed a reply on September 3, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

“On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party to the agreement refuses to arbitrate that controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists….” (Code Civ. Proc, § 1281.2.) “The party seeking arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement, and the party opposing arbitration bears the burden of proving any defense, such as unconscionability.” (Pinnacle Museum Tower Assn. v. Pinnacle Market Development (US), LLC (2012) 55 Cal.4th 223, 236.)

DISCUSSION

“As to the existence of an agreement, [the moving party bears] the ultimate burden of proof, but the court [is] obliged to resolve the dispute using a three-step burden-shifting process.” (Iyere v. Wise Auto Group (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 755.)  

a. Defendant’s Initial Burden

“The moving party ‘can meet its initial burden by attaching to the motion or petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the opposing party's signature.’” (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.) “At this step, a movant need not ‘follow the normal procedures of document authentication’ and need only ‘allege the existence of an agreement and support the allegation.’” (Iyere, supra, 87 Cal.App.5th at p. 755.)

Here, Defendant relies on the declaration of its HR director, Elizabeth Martinez, who avers that “Plaintiff electronically signed the binding Arbitration Agreement on June 3, 2021.” (Martinez Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. B.) The agreement provides: “I further agree and acknowledge that the Company and I will utilize binding arbitration as the sole and exclusive means to resolve all disputes that may arise out of or be related in any way to my employment, including but not limited to the termination of my employment and my compensation.” (Ibid.) An electronic signature bearing Plaintiff’s name appears at the bottom of the agreement underneath an acknowledgment that reads: “MY SIGNATURE BELOW ATTESTS TO THE FACT THAT I HAVE READ, UNDERSTAND, AND AGREE TO BE LEGALLY BOUND TO ALL OF THE ABOVE TERMS.” (Ibid.)

This evidence satisfies Defendant’s initial burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement.

 

b. Plaintiff’s Burden to Raise a Factual Dispute

“If the movant bears its initial burden, the burden shifts to the party opposing arbitration to identify a factual dispute as to the agreement's existence—in this instance, by disputing the authenticity of their signatures. To bear this burden, the arbitration opponent must offer admissible evidence creating a factual dispute as to the authenticity of their signatures. The opponent need not prove that his or her purported signature is not authentic, but must submit sufficient evidence to create a factual dispute and shift the burden back to the arbitration proponent, who retains the ultimate burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, the authenticity of the signature.” (Iyere, supra, 87 Cal.App.5th at p. 755.)

Here, Plaintiff states: “I deny ever signing an arbitration agreement during my employment with Defendant, including the one at issue. I also deny ever electronically signing any documents at any time during my employment with Defendant.” (Esparza Decl. ¶ 4.) Plaintiff further denies ever being presented with an arbitration agreement. (Id., ¶¶ 4-5.) This raises a factual dispute regarding Plaintiff’s signature, shifting the burden back to Defendant.

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s declaration is insufficient to raise a factual dispute because Plaintiff focuses on the period “during” her employment, whereas the purported agreement was signed as part of pre-employment onboarding. However, Plaintiff expressly denies signing “an arbitration agreement during my employment with Defendant, including the one at issue.” (Esparza Decl. ¶ 4.) Thus, Plaintiff has denied signing the specific arbitration agreement at issue. Defendant’s semantic argument elevates form over substance and does not detract from the factual dispute raised by Plaintiff’s declaration.

Plaintiff also denies “ever typing my name, or selecting the checkmark on this agreement.” (Esparza Decl. ¶ 5.) This is another specific denial regarding Plaintiff’s signature on the purported agreement. Defendant contends this is not a sufficient denial because Plaintiff “does not state that [she] did not check the checkbox, which is the equivalent to a handwritten signature.” (Reply 3:25-26.) There is no substantive difference between “selecting the checkmark” and “check the checkbox.” Plaintiff clearly conveyed that she did not electronically sign the agreement, and minor semantic differences do not detract from this denial.

To meet her burden, Plaintiff is not required to definitively prove that the agreement is invalid; she need only “submit sufficient evidence to create a factual dispute.” (Iyere, supra, 87 Cal.App.5th at p. 755.) Taken together, the denials in Plaintiff’s declaration are sufficient to raise a factual dispute over the purported signature on the agreement. Therefore, the burden shifts to Defendant to prove the agreement by a preponderance of the evidence.

c. Defendant’s Ultimate Burden

An electronic signature has the same legal effect as a handwritten signature. (Civ. Code, § 1633.7(a).) An “electronic signature is attributable to a person if it was the act of the person. The act of the person may be shown in any manner, including a showing of the efficacy of any security procedure applied to determine the person to which the electronic record or electronic signature was attributable.” (Id., § 1633.9(a).) “[T]he burden of authenticating an electronic signature is not great.” (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 844.)

In the face of a plaintiff’s denial, the mere fact that an agreement has an electronic signature on it bearing the plaintiff’s name, with a date and time stamp, is insufficient to show that the signature was “the act of” the plaintiff. (Ruiz, supra, 232 Cal.App.4th at p. 844.) Rather, a moving defendant needs to present evidence to “show that only [the plaintiff] could have placed the electronic signature on the arbitration agreement.” (Garcia v. Stoneledge Furniture LLC (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 41, 53, emphasis in original.) “For example, a party may present evidence that the signatory was required to use a unique, private login and password to affix the electronic signature, along with evidence detailing the procedures the person had to follow to electronically sign the document and the accompanying security precautions.” (Ibid.)

Here, Defendant makes no attempt at the requisite showing. Ms. Martinez’s declaration, while sufficient to meet Defendant’s initial burden, has been rebutted by Plaintiff’s specific denials. Ms. Martinez simply concludes that “Plaintiff electronically signed the binding Arbitration Agreement on June 3, 2021” without showing how she knows that. (See Martinez Decl. ¶ 5; see also Garcia, supra, 102 Cal.App.5th at p. 53 [finding no arbitration agreement where defendant “summarily concluded that Garcia electronically signed and acknowledged the agreement before exiting out of Taleo, with the only purported evidence being her name on the agreement”].) The fact that employees are generally presented with an arbitration agreement during onboarding is not evidence that a specific individual signed a specific document. (See Martinez Decl. ¶¶ 7-8.) Ultimately, there is insufficient evidence that the particular electronic signature at issue here was “the act of” Plaintiff.

In sum, Defendant has failed to prove an agreement to arbitrate by a preponderance of the evidence.

CONCLUSION

            Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is DENIED.
