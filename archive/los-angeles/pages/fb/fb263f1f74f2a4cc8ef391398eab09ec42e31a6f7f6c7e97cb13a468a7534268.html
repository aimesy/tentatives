VACATE ORDER

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff, Albert Agdaian

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendant, Renaissance Maintenance Corporation

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion to Vacate Order

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Albert Agdaian sued Defendant Renaissance Maintenance Corporation after $67,153 was deducted from the sale proceeds of his property pursuant to a demand on escrow by Defendant to claim reimbursement for landscaping work on Plaintiff’s property. Defendant moved for summary judgment on February 2, 2024 to which Plaintiff did not file an opposition. On April 18, 2024, the Court granted summary judgment in favor of Defendant and judgment was entered on May 16, 2024. Unbeknownst to the parties and the Court, Plaintiff’s lawyer passed away on March 1, 2024. On July 24, 2024, Plaintiff filed a motion to vacate summary judgment, which was granted on November 6, 2024. Defendant again moved for summary judgment on December 31, 2024 and Plaintiff did not oppose this motion either. Summary judgment was subsequently entered in favor of Defendant on July 3, 2025. On October 3, 2025, Plaintiff filed another motion to vacate summary judgment, which was denied.

 

RULING: Denied.

Request for Judicial Notice: Granted in part.

The Court takes judicial notice of the existence of Plaintiff’s complaint filed in the instant action. (Lockley v. Law Office of Cantrell, Green, Pekich, Cruz & McCort (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 875, 882.) Judicial notice is also taken of the existence of the Court’s February 4, 2026 minute order and the truth of the results reached therein. (Id. at pp. 885-86.) The Court does not take judicial notice of the remaining documents as they are immaterial to disposition of the motion.

 

Plaintiff seeks both mandatory and discretionary relief from the Court’s February 4, 2026 order denying his motion to vacate summary judgment. Plaintiff argues that relief is warranted because the microphone on his computer was not working during the hearing and he was unable to present oral argument. Defendant opposes the motion, contending that granting relief would prejudice it because Plaintiff has been dilatory in prosecuting the case and that ultimately vacating the summary judgment order would be futile. As explained in the Court’s prior ruling, Plaintiff, as a self-represented litigant, may not seek mandatory relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b). (McClain v. Kissler (2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 399, 428-29.) The sole issue is thus whether Plaintiff is entitled to discretionary relief. The Court concludes that he is not, and the motion to vacate is denied.

 

Assuming that the malfunctioning of Plaintiff’s microphone constitutes excusable neglect, the Court nonetheless declines to grant him relief from the order. Plaintiff does not elaborate what further argument he would have made at the hearing or how such argument may have impacted the Court’s prior order denying him relief. The Court considered Plaintiff’s reasons for his failure to oppose the summary judgment motion and concluded that they did not constitute excusable neglect under the statute. Plaintiff’s motion is therefore denied.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.   

 

 


Case Number: 24STCV32788    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 617

Dept. 617

DEMURRER

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendants, Corewell Partners Inc. and M. Aaron Yashouafar

RESPONDING PARTY: Unopposed/Plaintiff, Aggreko LLC

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Demurrer to First Amended Complaint

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Aggreko LLC sued Defendants Corewell Partners Inc. and M. Aaron Yashouafar after Defendants failed to pay the outstanding amount on a contract for leased equipment. Plaintiff asserts causes of action for: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Open Book Account; (3) Account Stated; and (4) Quantum Meruit.

 

RULING: Sustained in part.

Request for Judicial Notice: Granted.

The Court takes judicial notice of the existence of the filings with the secretary of state. (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (c).)

 

Defendants demur to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint arguing that Plaintiff fails to state its claims and the complaint is uncertain. They further demur to Plaintiff’s alter ego allegations to the extent the allegations form the basis for any cause of action. Plaintiff does not oppose the demurrer. Because a general demurrer does not lie to a portion of a cause of action, the alter ego allegations are not subject to demurrer. (Daniels v. Select Portfolio Servicing, Inc. (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1150, 1167.) The Court’s inquiry is thus focused on Defendants’ challenges to the entire complaint and Plaintiff’s individual causes of action. The demurrer on grounds of uncertainty is overruled. Defendants’ general demurrer to the breach of contract claim is overruled and the demurrer to the remaining claims is sustained. Plaintiff is granted leave to amend with the exception of the account stated claim.

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.” (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.) “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law . . . .” (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.) In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated. (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.) “If the complaint states a cause of action under any theory, regardless of the title under which the factual basis for relief is stated, that aspect of the complaint is good against a demurrer.” (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.)

 

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of Cal., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616; Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2 [“[U]nder our liberal pleading rules, where the complaint contains substantive factual allegations sufficiently apprising defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet, a demurrer for uncertainty should be overruled or plaintiff given leave to amend”].)

 

The Court first rejects Defendants’ uncertainty arguments. Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s use of “dbas” and its allegations regarding Corewell Partners’ entity form and jurisdiction, which contradict secretary of state filings, fail to provide them fair notice of who Plaintiff contends incurred the debt and on what theory. But the complaint clearly alleges that Plaintiff seeks to hold Defendants liable on a contract they entered into using their alleged fictitious business name “Lennox Equities.” [FAC ¶¶ 2-3.] And while Plaintiff alleges that Corewell Partners is an LLC “duly organized and existing under the laws of the State of California,” even though the statement of information indicates it is a Delaware corporation, this is not a defect that renders the complaint fatally uncertain and can be cured on amendment. [Id. ¶ 2; RJN, Ex. 2.] Defendants’ uncertainty arguments are therefore unavailing, and the Court proceeds to the challenges to the specific causes of action.

 

First Cause of Action: Breach of Contract

The demurrer to the breach of contract cause of action is overruled. Defendants argue this claim fails because the attached contract only identifies Lennox Equities LLC as the contracting entity. [FAC ¶ 9, Ex. A.] But as explained above, Plaintiff alleges that Defendants’ fictitious business name is Lennox Equities. The demurrer to the breach of contract claim is therefore overruled.

 

Second Cause of Action: Open Book Account

The demurrer to the open book account claim is sustained. As Defendants argue, “monies which become due under an express contract (such as rent due under a lease) cannot, in the absence of a contrary agreement between the parties, be treated as items under an open book account . . . .” (Tsemetzin v. Coast Federal Savings & Loan Assn. (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1334, 1343.) Here, Plaintiff attempts to treat the outstanding amount on the contract as an item under an open book account, but it does not allege an agreement between the parties that the sum would be treated as such. Because there is a reasonable possibility that Plaintiff can cure this defect, it is granted leave to amend.

 

Third Cause of Action: Account Stated

The demurrer to the account stated claim is sustained. Defendants correctly argue that “a debt which is predicated upon the breach of the terms of an express contract cannot be the basis of an account stated.” (Moore v. Bartholomae Corp. (1945) 69 Cal.App.2d 474, 477.) Here, an account stated claim does not lie because the basis of Plaintiff’s claim is the amount owed under the parties’ contract. [FAC ¶¶ 16-18.] The demurrer to this claim is therefore sustained without leave to amend.

 

Fourth Cause of Action: Quantum Meruit

Defendants’ demurrer to the quantum meruit claim is sustained. As they argue, a plaintiff may not “pursue or recover on a quasi-contract claim if the parties have an enforceable agreement regarding a particular subject matter.” (Klein v. Chevron U.S.A., Inc. (2012) 202 Cal.App.4th 1342, 1388.) Because quantum meruit may be pled in the alternative, there exists a reasonable possibility that Plaintiff can cure this pleading defect. (Id. at p. 1389.) It is thus granted leave to amend.

 

Plaintiff is granted 30 days’ leave to amend. Plaintiff may only add facts in support of the successfully challenged claims. (Harris v. Wachovia Mortgage, FSB (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1018, 1023.) If Plaintiff declines or fails to file an amended complaint, Defendants shall answer the operative complaint within 10 days of the lapsed deadline. Any new causes of action outside the scope of the order may be addressed via a motion to strike.

 

Defendants to give notice.

SANCTIONS

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff, Kinya Carr

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendant, Rahel Shamash, Landmark Realtors, Inc., and Baroukh Shamash, as Co-Trustee of the Solomon Family Trust

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Sanctions

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Kinya Carr sued Defendants Rahel Shamash, Landmark Realtors, Inc., and Baroukh Shamash, as Co-Trustee of the Solomon Family Trust, asserting claims under the Fair Employment and Housing Act, among others. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants “constructively denied” Plaintiff’s rental application after they discovered she was eligible for rental assistance through the Section 8 Housing Choice Voucher Program.

 

RULING: Denied.

Plaintiff moves for sanctions against Defendants under Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7 arguing that the previously filed motion to dismiss is “a procedurally unauthorized pleading” and frivolously relied on the “one-satisfaction rule” to delay the proceedings. Defendants oppose the motion, contending that their motion was not an unauthorized pleading because the Court treated it as a demurrer and that their one-satisfaction rule theory was not frivolous.[1] Defendants further move for attorney’s fees and costs incurred in opposing the instant motion. Plaintiff’s motion is denied because Defendants’ motion was not frivolous or brought for an improper purpose.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7 requires that an attorney sign all pleadings, petitions, notices of motions, and other similar papers.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7, subd. (a).) Thus, by presenting a pleading or other similar paper to the court, an attorney is certifying that to the best of his or her knowledge, information, and belief, “formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances,” all of the following conditions are met:

 

 (1) It is not being presented primarily for an improper purpose, such as to harass or to cause unnecessary delay or needless increase in the cost of litigation.

 

(2) The claims, defenses, and other legal contentions therein are warranted by existing law or by a nonfrivolous argument for the extension, modification, or reversal of existing law or the establishment of new law.

 

(3) The allegations and other factual contentions have evidentiary support or, if specifically so identified, are likely to have evidentiary support after a reasonable opportunity for further investigation or discovery.

 

(4) The denials of factual contentions are warranted on the evidence or, if specifically so identified, are reasonably based on a lack of information or belief.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7, subd. (b).) 

 

The section “authorizes trial courts to impose sanctions to check abuses in the filing of pleadings, petitions, written notices of motions or similar papers.” (Musaelian v. Adams (2009) 45 Cal.4th 512, 514.) A violation of any of these certifications may give rise to sanctions. (Eichenbaum v. Alon (2003) 106 Cal.App.4th 967, 976.) An objective standard of review applies to sections 128.5 and 128.7. (San Diegans for Open Government v. City of San Diego (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 1306, 1318; Bockrath v. Aldrich Chemical Co., Inc. (1999) 21 Cal.4th 71, 82.) Whether a claim is meritless or for the sole purpose of harassment must be evaluated by examining whether the factual allegations of the claim had evidentiary support. (580 Folsom Associates v. Prometheus Development Co. (1990) 223 Cal.App.3d 1, 22.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7 imposes a lower threshold for sanctions than is required under Code of Civil Procedure section 128.5. This is because Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7 requires only that the conduct be ‘objectively unreasonable,’ while Code of Civil Procedure section 128.5 also requires ‘a showing of subjective bad faith.’” (Guillemin v. Stein (2002) 104 Cal.App.4th 156, 167.)

 

Sanctions are not warranted because the motion was not an unauthorized pleading nor was it frivolous or brought for an improper purpose of delay. The Court construed the motion to dismiss as a demurrer and considered it on the merits. (Sole Energy Co. v. Petrominerals Corp. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 187, 193.) On the current record, the Court cannot conclude that Defendants’ one-satisfaction theory, which the Court did not reach in ruling on the demurrer, was frivolous or brought to delay the proceedings. Defendants’ argument rested on a plausible theory that Plaintiff’s injury in her inability to obtain housing during a certain timespan constituted a single injury, which she was compensated for in different suits. “[O]ur adversary system requires that attorneys and litigants be provided substantial breathing room to develop and assert factual and legal arguments” and “sanctions should not be routinely or easily awarded even for a claim that is arguably frivolous.” (Peake v. Underwood (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 428, 448.) Plaintiff’s motion is therefore denied.

 

Defendants may separately move for attorney’s fees and costs. The instant ruling in no way decides Defendants’ entitlement to fees and costs.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.

 


[1] While Plaintiff filed proof of service of her reply, she did not file the reply itself.
