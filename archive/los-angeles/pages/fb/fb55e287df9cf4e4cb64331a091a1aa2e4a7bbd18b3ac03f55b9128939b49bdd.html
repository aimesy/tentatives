24STCV21452 Rivera v. Anastasia Beverly Hills, LLC, et al.

Tuesday, August 4, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANT’S MOTION TO SEAL EXHIBITS IN SUPPORT OF ITS OPPOSITION TO PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO QUASH OR MODIFY SUBPOEAS FOR PLAINTIFF’S EMPLOYMENT RECORDS (1597)

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANT’S MOTION TO SEAL EXHIBITS IN SUPPORT OF ITS OPPOSITION TO PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO QUASH OR MODIFY SUBPOEAS FOR PLAINTIFF’S EMPLOYMENT RECORDS (4668)

 

I.       BACKGROUND

      This action arises from alleged retaliation, discrimination, and sexual harassment in violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act and the Labor Code. Plaintiff also alleges (1) retaliation in violation of the Labor Code; violations under the Fair Employment and Housing Act (“FEHA”)  for (2) sex discrimination, (3) sexual harassment, (4) hostile work environment, (5) retaliation, (6) failure to prevent harassment, discrimination, or retaliation; and tort claims for (7) negligent supervision, hiring, and retention; (8) intentional infliction of emotional distress; and (9) wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

A.     Res. No. 1597

      Defendant requests that the court seal selected exhibits conditionally filed under seal in support of its opposition to Plaintiff’s Motion to Quash or Modify Subpoenas for Plaintiff’s Employment Records. The selected exhibits are:

1.     Exhibit 2: Excerpts from the confidential portion of the transcript from Plaintiff’s November 8, 2025, deposition;

2.     Exhibit 3: Plaintiff’s therapy notes from March 13, 2023, from MDLive;

3.     Exhibit 4: Plaintiff’s therapy notes from March 2, 2022, from MDLive; and

4.     Exhibit 9: Plaintiff’s June 21, 2021, Separate Authorization for Release of Information and Waiver of Potential Claims.

      Defendant argues the exhibits contain Plaintiff’s personal and private information including medical information. The court signed a protective order for confidential information on December 16 and 19, 2025. An order to seal the selected exhibits will uphold the court’s order to protect confidential information.

      No opposition has been filed.

B.     Res. No. 4668

      Defendant files a second motion to seal exhibits in response to Plaintiff’s Motion to Quash or Modify Subpoenas for Plaintiff’s Education Records and Pharmacy Records. The selected exhibits are:

1.     Exhibit 1: Plaintiff’s medical records from Dr. Khedia on March 10, 2022;

2.     Exhibit 2: Plaintiff’s medical records from Dr. Khedia on March 10, 2023;

3.     Exhibit 3: Plaintiff’s therapy records from MD Live on April 17, 2023;

4.     Exhibit 5: Plaintiff’s therapy records from MD Live on February 4, 2022;

5.     Exhibit 6: Plaintiff’s medical records from Dr. Khedia on October 21, 2022;

6.     Exhibit 7: Plaintiff’s medical records from Dr. Khedia on January 18, 2023;

7.     Exhibit 9: Excerpts from the transcript from the confidential portion of Plaintiff’s

November 8, 2025, deposition;

8.     Plaintiff’s therapy records from MD Live on November 9, 2021

9.     Exhibit 11: Plaintiff’s therapy records from MD Live on March 3, 2023.

II.     DISCUSSION

      Court records are presumed to be open. (Cal. Rules of Court, 2.550 subd. (c).) The burden is on the moving party to show compelling reasons for sealing records. (Mary R. v. B. & R. Corp. (1983) 149 Cal.App.3d 308, 317.) To support an order to seal records, the court must find that “(1) there is an overriding interest supporting sealing records; (2) there is a substantial probability that the interest will be prejudiced absent sealing; (3) the proposed sealing is narrowly tailored to serve the overriding interest; and (4) there is no less restrictive means of achieving the overriding interest." (McNair v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn. (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 25, 29; Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 2.550 subd. (d).)

      Defendant has shown that the protection of private and personal information may serve as an overriding interest that outweighs the public’s right to court records. (Pettus v. Cole (1996) 49 Cal.App.4th 402, 440. ["The right to control circulation of personal information is fundamental."].) The right of privacy is protected by the California Constitution and includes informational privacy such as sensitive or confidential information, the dissemination of which is precluded. (Ruiz v. Podolsky (2010) 50 Cal.4th 838, 850.)

      The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored to serve an overriding interest. There is no less restrictive means of protecting Plaintiff’s overriding privacy interests.

III.    CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s two motions to seal selected exhibits are GRANTED.

 

 


Case Number: 24STCV30344    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: 414

24STCV30344 Berry v. Los Angeles Community College District

Tuesday, August 4,  2026

 

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING DEFENDANT’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO FORM INTERROGATORIES- GENERAL (0957)

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING DEFENDANT’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO FORM INTERROGATORIES- EMPLOYMENT LAW (0814)

 

 

I.       BACKGROUND

      The second amended complaint alleged Plaintiff is a sociology professor for Defendant and has been employed since 2013. Plaintiff alleges that the Vice President overseeing all educational programs engaged in acts including termination that Plaintiff claims were motivated by racial discrimination. The Vice President allegedly interfered with Plaintiff’s medical leave. Plaintiff alleges seven causes of action for various violations of the Fair Employment and Housing Act, the California Family Rights Act, and a tort claim for wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

      Defendant propounded two sets of discovery, one for general form interrogatories, and a second set for employment law form interrogatories. Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s responses to each set were not verified, the answers were evasive or incomplete, and the objections were without merit and too general. Defendant requests imposition of sanctions.

      In opposition to both motions, Plaintiff argues that Defendant failed to request an IDC. The single meet and confer letter sent by email was not reasonable or in good faith. Plaintiff contends the motion is moot because Plaintiff served verified supplemental responses. There is nothing for the Court to compel. The requests for imposition of sanctions are unjustified.

      Defendant contends in reply that the motion is not moot because Plaintiff maintained boilerplate objections and some responses remain deficient.

II.     LEGAL STANDARDS

      A motion to compel further responses to special interrogatories, is proper where the requesting party believes the responses are evasive and incomplete, that an objection is without merit, or an exercise of the option to produce documents is unwarranted. (Code Civ. Proc., sections 2030.300 subd. (a).) The motion must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration. (Id. subd. (b).)

      Sanctions may be imposed against the person, party, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion unless the court finds that the sanctioned party acted with substantial justification, or imposition of sanctions would be unjust. (Id. subd. (d).)     

III.    DISCUSSION

      While an informal discovery conference with the court is not mandatory, the motion must be accompanied by a declaration describing the parties’ meet and confer attempts.

      Defendant sent a meet and confer letter which Plaintiff ignored, and Defendant did not follow up. The meet and confer requirement is designed to encourage the parties to resolve their issues informally and "lessen the burden on the court and reduce the unnecessary expenditure of resources by litigants.” (Townsend v. Superior Court (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 1431, 1435. ["[T]he parties must present to each other the merits of their respective positions with the same candor, specificity, and support during informal negotiations as during the briefing of discovery motions. Only after all the cards have been laid on the table, and a party has meaningfully assessed the relative strengths and weaknesses of its position in light of all available information, can there be a 'sincere effort' to resolve the matter.”].)

      A discussion never took place between the parties. Plaintiff does not show substantial justification for failing to respond to Defendant’s meet and confer letter. Instead, Plaintiff filed supplemental responses after the motion was filed which Defendant believes remain deficient. This leaves the burden on the Court to rule on matters which no one discussed or attempted to resolve informally. Motion practice is not how meet and confer is accomplished.

 

IV.   CONCLUSION

      The Court finds that Plaintiff served supplemental responses to the discovery at issue. To the extent Defendant believes the responses remain deficient, the parties are ordered to meet and confer and discuss each parties’ positions before filing a motion as required by statute.

      Because both parties failed to uphold their obligations under the Discovery Code, the Court does not award sanctions to either party.

      Defendant’s motions to compel further responses to Form Interrogatories -- General, and Form Interrogatories – Employment, are DENIED.

       

 

 

 


Case Number: 25STCV18416    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: 414

25STCV18416 Alfa Prosmarttech PTE, LTD, et al. v. Izrailbekova, et al.

Tuesday, August 4, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING DEMURRER OF DEFENDANT, NIGINA IZRAILBEKOVA, TO PLAINTIFFS’ SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT IN PART AND OVERRULING IN PART (0219)

 

I.        BACKGROUND

       The second amended complaint (“SAC”) seeks recovery of more than $9 million dollars in funds allegedly stolen by Nigina Izrailbekova (“Defendant”), from the five corporate entities (“Plaintiffs”) she was employed to serve. Plaintiffs allege seven causes of action for breach of contract, misrepresentation, conversion, and other claims arising from these alleged facts.

       Defendant demurs to all seven causes of action.

II.      LEGAL STANDARDS

       A demurrer tests the sufficiency of a complaint as a matter of law and raises only questions of law. (Schmidt v. Foundation Health (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1702, 1706.) In testing the complaint’s sufficiency, the court must assume the truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations as well as facts that can be reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded facts. The court may also consider matters properly subject to judicial notice. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

       The court may not consider contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law. (Moore v. Conliffe (1994) 7 Cal.4th 634, 638.) Plaintiff is required to allege facts sufficient to establish every element of each cause of action. (Rakestraw v. California Physicians Service (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 39, 43.) Where the complaint fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, courts should sustain the demurrer. Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(e); Zelig v. County of Los Angeles (2002) 27 Cal.4th 1112, 1126.)

       Sufficient facts are the essential facts of the case stated "with reasonable precision and with particularity that is sufficiently specific to acquaint the defendant with the nature, source, and extent of his cause of action.” (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 643-644.)  Whether the Plaintiff will be able to prove the pleaded facts is irrelevant. (Stevens v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 605, 609–610.)

       A demurrer may be sustained if a complaint is “uncertain.” Uncertainty exists where a complaint’s factual allegations are so confusing, vague, or ambiguous that they do not sufficiently apprise a defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2; Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(f).)

III.    DISCUSSION

A.      Joinder

       Plaintiffs raise the issue of joinder in opposition to Defendant’s argument that the pleading combines claims of five different Plaintiffs and transactions which renders the pleading defective. Defendant does not demurrer on grounds of misjoinder of parties.  Defendant’s reply clarifies that it does not dispute that joinder is permissible by statute. (Reply 3:20-21.) Rather it is the purported uncertainty that results from the collective pleading combining different transactions for each Plaintiff and different damage and for different time periods. Each cause of action is analyzed from that perspective and not against principles of joinder.

 

B.      Capacity to sue and standing. Sustained.

       Defendant takes issue with the following allegation: “To the extent any Plaintiff is

deemed to transact intrastate business in California within the meaning of California Corporations Code §§ 191 and 2105, such Plaintiff has complied, or will comply, with all requirements of Corporations Code § 2203 to maintain this action.” (SAC ¶ 17.)

       Defendant argues this allegation raises the issue that some or all of the Plaintiffs may not be qualified to maintain this action until they comply with all requirements under the Corporations Code. Foreign corporations cannot engage in intrastate business in the State of California unless they "hold a valid certificate from the Secretary of State.” (Corp. Code, § 2203.)

       Section 2203 also states that foreign corporations doing unauthorized business in California "shall not maintain any action or proceeding upon any intrastate business so transacted in any court of this state, …  until it has complied with the provisions thereof and has paid to the Secretary of State a penalty of two hundred fifty dollars ($250) in addition to the fees due for filing the statement and designation required by Section 2105 and has filed with the clerk of the court in which the action is pending receipts showing the payment of the fees and penalty and all franchise taxes and any other taxes on business or property in this state that should have been paid for the period during which it transacted intrastate business.” (Corp. Code, § 2203 (c).)

       Plaintiffs argue that having a bank account in California is not “transacting interstate business.” However, the pleading alleges that Plaintiffs hired Defendant to serve as director of Plaintiffs’ U.S. operations, allowing an inference of intrastate business. (SAC ¶ 48.) Defendant was given significant responsibility and authority over their respective U.S. based financial affairs. (SAC ¶ 20.)

       Defendant observes in reply that only some of the five Plaintiffs have been qualified to transact business by the California Secretary of State. The court grants Plaintiffs’ request for judicial notice of Secretary of State records, showing that Plaintiff Technopike PTE, Ltd.; and  Prime Tech Solutions EOOD, LLC;  are qualified to transact intrastate business in California. (RJN Ex. 1-2.) The remaining certificates are from the State of New York and do not clearly show that they are authorized to transact business in the State of California.

       Plaintiffs need not qualify in order to commence an action. Section 2203 requires compliance to maintain an action. A foreign corporation transacting intrastate business which has failed to qualify with the Secretary of State may also commence an action in state court. (Ward Land etc. Co. v. Mapes (1905) 147 Cal. 747, 753, 82 P. 426.) “A foreign corporation transacting intrastate business which has failed to qualify may not, however, maintain an action commenced prior to qualification, except upon the satisfaction of certain conditions.” (United Medical Management Ltd. v. Gatto (1996) 49 Cal.App.4th 1732, 1739.)

       Defendant properly raises the issue upon demurrer based on lack of capacity. It is a matter of abatement of the action. (Id. at 1740 [“Ordinarily, the matter should be stayed to permit the foreign corporation to comply. If the foreign corporation plaintiff complies with section 2203, subdivision (c), by qualifying and paying fees, penalties and taxes, it may maintain the action. If the foreign corporation fails to comply, the matter should be dismissed without prejudice."] .)

 

C.      Breach of contract. Overruled.

       The elements of a claim for breach of contract are: "(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff's performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant's breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.) As Defendant acknowledges, where the contract is oral, the complaint must allege the substance of its terms with sufficient certainty to show a binding agreement and permit a responsive pleading. (Dem  6:12-13.)

       Plaintiffs have met this standard. Notwithstanding that five Plaintiffs collectively allege the same claims, the terms of the alleged employment contracts are not complex or uncertain. Plaintiffs separately engaged Defendant to serve as director of Plaintiffs’ United States Operations. (SAC ¶ 20.) The employment agreements were oral in nature. (SAC ¶ 48.) Each employment agreement was “substantially similar” and required Defendant to “(a) faithfully, honestly, and competently manage the respective Plaintiff’s U.S. based financial affairs, including its corporate bank accounts;  (b) act at all times in the best interests of the Plaintiff she was serving and exercise a high degree of care, loyalty, and good faith, (c) provide each Plaintiff with full, complete, and accurate access to its own financial records, including bank statements, upon request and as a matter of course; (d) refrain from making any withdrawals, transfers, or payments from a Plaintiff’s accounts without that Plaintiff’s consent and authorization, and to safeguard the assets and property that each Plaintiff entrusted Defendant to control.” (SAC ¶ 49.)

       Defendant’s breach began in October 2023 and consisted of systematically misappropriating funds Plaintiffs’ funds. (SAC ¶ 51.)

       To the extent Defendant requires more specifics to render the claim more certain, that is an issue better left to discovery. Demurrers for uncertainty are disfavored and are strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, “because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly's of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616; Chen v. Berenjian (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 811, 822.)

 

D.     Penal Code § 496. Overruled.

       Penal Code § 496 criminalizes the buying or receiving of stolen property knowing it was stolen or obtained “in any manner constituting theft or extortion.” (Pen. Code, § 496.) “Theft” includes taking personal property “by any false or fraudulent representation or pretense." (Pen. Code, § 484(a).)

       Defendant argues that this claim does not articulate a legally distinct statutory theory that is “separable from the alleged initial taking.” (Dem 11:16-21.).) Defendant contends it is uncertain whether Plaintiffs seek to hold Defendant for direct theft, as a later concealer or withholder, or both, and “what conduct supposedly satisfies each statutory element.”

       Defendant’s do not articulate why both an initial obtaining by Alexsander Semerdgian (“Semerdgian”), and a subsequent transfer of that alleged stolen property by Defendant renders the pleading fatally defective.   

       The pleading alleges that Defendant Semerdgian received, possessed, and controlled portions of the stolen funds. (SAC ¶ 56.) Defendant transferred the stolen funds into bank accounts owned by Semerdgian. (SAC ¶ 56.) Defendant unlawfully took the money from Plaintiffs’ accounts, then concealed, withhold and aided in concealing and withholding stolen from Plaintiffs know it to be stolen. (SAC ¶ 57.)

       The claim is adequately alleged and is not uncertain.       

E.      Negligent misrepresentation and Intentional misrepresentation. Overruled.

       A claim for fraud requires facts to support the following elements: (1) a misrepresentation, (2) made with knowledge of its falsity, (3) Defendant intended to defraud Plaintiff, i.e., induce Plaintiff’s reliance, (4) Plaintiff justifiably relied on the misrepresentation, (5) causing damage. (Nagy v. Nagy (1989) 210 Cal.App.3d 1262, 1268.)

       Fraud claims are subject to strict requirements of particularity in pleading. (Id.) The particularity requirements necessitate pleading facts showing “how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered." (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 73.) The requirement “applies not only to the alleged misrepresentation, but also to the elements of causation and damage." (Moncada v. West Coast Quartz Corp. (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 768, 776.)

       Where fraud is alleged against a corporation, the pleading standards are even greater. Plaintiff must allege “the names of the persons who made the allegedly fraudulent representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written." (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal. 4th 631, 645.)

       Defendant argues that at a minimum, Plaintiffs should identify, who made each statement, to whom it was made, when it was made, by what means it was made, what exactly was said, and what each plaintiff did in reliance on it.

       Plaintiffs allege that in or around August and September 2023, Izrailbekova made positive assertions of material fact including that Plaintiffs’ respective U.S. bank accounts had been frozen or otherwise restricted by the banks. (SAC ¶64.) The statements were made with intent to induce Plaintiff to rely on these statements, delay their inquiries, and prevent Plaintiffs from discovering her ongoing embezzlement. (SAC ¶ 64.) Defendant falsely told Plaintiffs that Defendant had been confronted by the FBI and was instructed to cease all communications. These representations were not true. (SAC ¶ 65.)

       In reliance on Defendant's representations, Plaintiffs allege they refrained from immediately investigating their accounts, securing their funds, and terminating Izrailbekova’s access. As a result of that delay,  Izrailbekova allegedly continued to misappropriate funds from the accounts, causing Plaintiffs damages of $9,024,157.38. (SAC, ¶ 67.)

       Plaintiffs' opposition has merit. Although fraud ordinarily must be pleaded with specificity, that requirement is relaxed where "it appears from the nature of the allegations that the defendant must necessarily possess full information concerning the facts of the controversy." (Bradley v. Hartford Acc. & Indem. Co. (1973) 30 Cal.App.3d 818, 825.) Likewise, "less particularity is required when the facts lie more in the knowledge of the opposite party." (Committee On Children's Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 217.)

       Here, Plaintiffs allege Defendant made the misrepresentations during telephone calls and email communications that she initiated. At the pleading stage, the content and circumstances of those communications are alleged to be matters more readily within Defendant's knowledge than Plaintiffs'. Defendant argues in reply that this contention "inverts the burden." (Reply 6:1-2.) The argument is unpersuasive. California law recognizes that the heightened specificity requirement is relaxed where, as alleged here, the pertinent facts are peculiarly within the defendant's knowledge.

 

F.       Conversion. Overruled.

       Defendant argues Plaintiff should clearly identify the particular transactions or specific sums allegedly converted by transfer, or other concrete transactional detail. Defendant does not cite any authority requiring this detail in the pleading.

       To prevail on a claim for conversion, Plaintiffs must establish their ownership interest at the time of the conversion and Defendants’ wrongful act or dispossession of Plaintiffs’ property rights. (Moore v. Regents of University of California (1990) 51 Cal.3d 120, 136.) The basic elements are (1) the plaintiff's ownership or right to possession of personal property; (2) the defendant's disposition of the property in a manner that is inconsistent with the plaintiff's property rights; and (3) resulting damages. (Fremont Indemnity Co. v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119.)

       Plaintiffs have alleged facts to support each element.

G.     Breach of fiduciary duty. Overruled.

       Defendant challenges a portion of this cause of action, because it involves non fiduciary Defendants. Defendant argues that to the extent the pleading attempts to extend liability through broad abetting and conspiracy allegations directed to the nonfiduciary defendants, those allegations are general and conclusory.

       However, only Izrailbekova is alleged to have owed a fiduciary duty to Plaintiffs, and she is the only demurring party. Additionally, demurrer does not lie to a part of a cause of action. (Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1047 ["a demurrer cannot rightfully be sustained to part of a cause of action or to a particular type of damage or remedy.”].)

H.     Trade name misappropriation and Unfair Competition. Overrule.

       Defendant argues Plaintiffs blend two theories in this cause of action. Plaintiffs are not entitled to damages under Business and Professions Code, section 17200. Defendants do not cite any authority that articulates the necessary elements of either a claim for misappropriation or for violation of Business and Professions Code, section 17200.

       Plaintiffs argue that this claim represents a common law trade name misappropriation claim and a violation of the Unfair Competition Law. Plaintiffs cite A.J. Fistes Corp. v. GDL Best Contractors, Inc. (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 677 for the proposition that a plaintiff is not required to separate claims into separate causes of action. The court ignores "erroneous or confusing labels ... if the complaint pleads facts which would entitle the plaintiff to relief.’”]; Saunders v. Cariss (1990) 224 Cal.App.3d 905, 908, 274 Cal.Rptr. 186 [“[T]he trial court is obligated to look past the form of a pleading to its substance.” (Id. 695.) 

       This cause of action is not fatally uncertain. Here, Plaintiffs allege that Defendant knowingly and wrongfully misappropriated Alfa’s trade name by filing documents with the California Secretary of State to register a new, separate business entity in California under the identical name, “Alfa Prosmarttech PTE. LTD.” (SAC ¶ 93.) In these fraudulent filings, Defendant is alleged to have falsely designated herself as the Chief Executive Officer, Secretary, and Chief Financial Officer. (Id.)

       Plaintiffs allege that the unauthorized use of Alfa’s trade name was intended to allow Defendant to trade on Alfa’s goodwill and reputation, and was likely to cause confusion in the marketplace among customers, vendors, and financial institutions and to facilitate the opening of fraudulent bank accounts or the obtaining of credit in Alfa’s name. (SAC ¶ 94.)

       Plaintiffs allege that this conduct constitutes an unlawful, unfair, and fraudulent, business act or practice. (SAC ¶ 95.) The UCL claim is clearly predicated on the common law trade name misappropriation claim.

IV.    CONCLUSION

       Based on the foregoing, demurrer to the second amended complaint is SUSTAINED only as to Plaintiffs’ capacity to maintain an action under Corp. Code, § 2203. Defendant has not cited any authority that Plaintiffs have to allege compliance with the Code in the pleadings. Rather, the statute provides that Plaintiffs cannot maintain the action until they have complied with the statutory requirements by paying the "Secretary of State a penalty of two hundred fifty dollars ($250) in addition to the fees due for filing the statement and designation required by Section 2105 and has filed with the clerk of the court in which the action is pending receipts showing the payment of the fees and penalty and all franchise taxes and any other taxes on business or property in this state that should have been paid for the period during which it transacted intrastate business." (Corp. Code, § 2203.)

       Accordingly, the Court sets an Order to Show Cause for September 18, 2026, for Plaintiffs to file the necessary receipts showing compliance with statute. Demurrer to all other causes of action is OVERRULED.  Defendant shall file an answer 10 days after Plaintiffs file the receipts showing compliance.
