APPLICATIONS

Admitted Pro Hac Vice

APPLICANTS

(1)   Alexandra R. French

(2)   Caitlyn Fields

 

            Alexandra R. French and Caitlyn Fields of Barnes & Thornburg, LLP, apply to be admitted pro hac vice as counsel for Cross-Defendants Quanex Building Products Corporation and Quanex IG Systems, Inc.  The applications are unopposed.           

 

ANALYSIS

 

            California Rules of Court rule 9.40 provides that an attorney in good standing in another jurisdiction may apply to appear as counsel pro hac vice in the State of California by filing a verified application together with proof of service by mail of a copy of the application and notice of hearing on all parties who have appeared in the case and on the State Bar of California at its San Francisco office, with payment of the application fee, so long as that attorney is not a resident of the State of California, and is not regularly engaged in substantial business, professional, or other activities in the State of California. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40.)

 

The application must state: (1) the applicant’s residence and office addresses; (2) the courts to which the applicant has been admitted to practice and the dates of admission; (3) that the applicant is a member of good standing in those courts; (4) that the applicant is not currently suspended or disbarred in any court; (5) the title of each court and cause in which the applicant has filed an application to appear as counsel pro hac vice in this state in the preceding two years, the date of each application, and whether or not it was granted; and (6) the name, address, and telephone number of the active member of the State Bar of California who is attorney of record in the local action. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d).)

 

            The Verified Applications provide most this information, and indicate that the requisite application fees have been paid. 

 

            However, instead of indicating the title of each court and cause in which the applicant has filed an application to appear as pro hac vice in this State in the preceding two years, the date of each application, and whether or not such application as granted, each moving Counsel simply indicates the cases in which they have been admitted to practice in California in the preceding two years.  That does not satisfy the requirement. 

 

Furthermore, the proofs of service indicate counsel for all partes were served electronically.  However, there is no proof of service demonstrating service on the State Bar by mail at its San Francisco office, as required by California Rules of Court, Rule 9.40(c), which provides:  “A person desiring to appear as counsel pro hac vice in a superior court must file with the court a verified application together with proof of service by mail in accordance with Code of Civil Procedure section 1013a of a copy of the application and of the notice of hearing of the application on all parties who have appeared in the cause and on the State Bar of California at its San Francisco office. The notice of hearing must be given at the time prescribed in Code of Civil Procedure section 1005 unless the court has prescribed a shorter period.”   (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(c)(1), emphasis added.) 

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

The Court denies both applications without prejudice as procedurally defective. 

 

Counsel shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

MATTER

Request for Default Judgment

 

This case arises from a dispute concerning private education loans. 

 

On May 28, 2025, Plaintiff Rock Creek Capital, LLC (“Plaintiff”) filed suit against Defendants Quazi Rahman a/k/a Quazi J Rahman and Quazi Rahman a/k/a Quazi Z Rahman and Does 1 through 15, alleging two causes of action for breach of contract, pertaining to two separate accounts.

 

Defendant Quazi Rahman a/k/a Quazi J Rahman (“Defendant”) was served with a copy of the summons and complaint via substitute service on September 27, 2025.  Default was entered against Defendant on May 6, 2026, and the Doe defendants were dismissed on May 8, 2026.  Defendant Quazi Rahman a/k/a Quazi Z Rahman remains named in the litigation. The Court cannot enter judgment while another party remains named in the litigation without an application for separate judgment.  (California Rules of Court, rule 3.1800(a)(7).)

 

Plaintiff now requests default judgment against Defendant in the amount of $74,515.59, representing $73,683.77 as demanded in the complaint and costs in the amount of $831.82.

 

A.    Damages

 

             Plaintiff’s Complaint seeks $73,683.77 in special damages. (See Compl.)  Therefore, Plaintiff does not seek damages that are in excess of what is pled in the Complaint.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 580, subd. (a) [“The relief granted to the plaintiff, if there is no answer, cannot exceed that demanded in the complaint”]; Levine v. Smith (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 1131, 1136-1137 [“when recovering damages in a default judgment, the plaintiff is limited to the damages specified in the complaint”].) 

 

            In support of the request, Plaintiff advances the Declaration of Chad Welch, demonstrating damages in connection with account 1 in the amount of $31,671.88, representing $28,825.97 in unpaid principal, $2,765.84 in accrued interest, and $80.07 late fee (Welch Decl. ¶ 41) and damages in connection with account 2 in the amount of $42,011.89, representing unpaid principal in the amount of $37,718.18, accrued interest in the amount of $4,174.04, and a late fee of $119.67, for a grand total of $73,683.77.

 

            Therefore, the Court finds that Plaintiff is entitled to the requested $73,683.77 in damages.

 

B.    Costs

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5 outlines recoverable costs to a prevailing party under Code of Civil Procedure section 1032.  The Code of Civil Procedure defines the “prevailing party” as follows:

 

[T]he party with a net monetary recovery, a defendant in whose favor a dismissal is entered, a defendant where neither plaintiff nor defendant obtains any relief, and a defendant as against those plaintiffs who do not recover any relief against that defendant. If any party recovers other than monetary relief and in situations other than as specified, the “prevailing party” shall be as determined by the court, and under those circumstances, the court, in its discretion, may allow costs or not and, if allowed, may apportion costs between the parties on the same or adverse sides pursuant to rules adopted under Section 1034.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (a)(4).)

           

            Plaintiff requests $831.82 in costs composed of $435 in filing fees, $340 in process server fees, and $56.82 in e-filing fees. (CIV-100.)  

 

            The Court grants Plaintiff’s request for costs as Plaintiff is the prevailing party in this action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (a)(4).)

 

CONCLUSION

 

            The Court cannot enter judgment while Defendant Quazi Rahman a/k/a Quazi Z Rahman remains named in the litigation.  Otherwise, Plaintiff has demonstrated entitled to the requested default judgment regarding Defendant Quazi Rahman a/k/a Quazi J Rahman. 

 

            If Plaintiff submits on the Court’s Tentative Ruling and makes an Oral Motion to Dismiss Defendant Quazi Rahman a/k/a Quazi Z Rahman (which the Court is inclined to grant), the Court will enter the proposed Default Judgment in conformity with the ruling.

MOTION

Motion to Quash Defendant Beverly Hills Hotel Corporation’s Subpoenas for Plaintiff’s Medical Records

MOVING PARTY

Plaintiff Terrence Morgan

OPPOSING PARTY

none

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from a slip and fall.  On July 16, 2025, Plaintiff Terrance Morgan (“Plaintiff”) filed suit against Defendants Beverly Hills Hotel Corporation (“BHHC”); Sajahtera, Inc. (“Sajahtera”) and Beverly Hills Hotel (“BHH”) (together, “Defendants”) alleging two causes of action for (1) general negligence and (2) premises liability.

 

Plaintiff now moves to quash a subpoena for business records propounded by BHHC on various health providers seeking Plaintiff’s medical records.  The motion is unopposed.

 

ANALYSIS

 

I.                MOTION TO QUASH

 

If a subpoena requires the production of documents, the court may quash the subpoena entirely or modify it.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)  In ruling on a motion to quash, “the court may in its discretion award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonable attorney's fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.2, subd. (a).)

 

Current discovery standards hold that, “any party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged,[1] that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action . . . if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.”  (Board of Registered Nursing v. Superior Court (2021) 59 Cal.App.5th 1011, 1039 (hereafter Board of Nursing).)  “To meet this [test], a party seeking to compel [the] production of records . . . must articulate specific facts justifying the discovery sought; it may not rely on mere generalities.”  (Ibid.; see Johnson v. Superior Court (1968) 258 Cal.App.2d 829, 837 [finding that a subpoena was “insufficient” to compel production because it was “based wholly on the [party’s] alleged information and belief without any statement of supporting facts”].)  However, “[e]ven if information is otherwise discoverable, it may be protected by a constitutional or statutory privilege[,] [including] the right to privacy . . . .”  (Board of Nursing, supra, 59 Cal.App.5th at p. 1039.) 

 

            “The state Constitution expressly grants Californians a right to privacy.  Protection of informational privacy is the provision's central concern.  .  .  .  The party asserting a privacy right must establish a legally protected privacy interest, an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and a threatened intrusion that is serious.[2]  The party seeking information may raise in response whatever legitimate and important countervailing interests disclosure serves, while the party seeking protection may identify feasible alternatives that serve the same interests or protective measures that would diminish the loss of privacy.  A court must then balance these competing considerations.”  (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552 [cleaned up].)

            “Legally recognized privacy interests [include] interests in precluding the dissemination or misuse of sensitive and confidential information (‘informational privacy’) .  .  .  .”  (Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1, 35.)  “A particular class of information is [sensitive or confidential] when well-established social norms recognize the need to maximize individual control over its dissemination and use to prevent unjustified embarrassment or indignity.”  (Ibid.) 

 

            It is “well-settled” that “patients have a right to privacy with respect to information contained in . . . [their] medical records.”  (Grafilo v. Wolfsohn (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 1024, 1034.)  Indeed, a patient’s right to privacy “is protected by case law as well as state and federal statutes and regulations.”  (County of Los Angeles v. Superior Court (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 621, 641 (hereafter County of Los Angeles).)  Additionally, “[t]he privacy interest in psychiatric records is particularly strong, and in some respects, entitled to more robust protection than other types of medical records.”  (Grafilo v. Soorani (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 497, 507.) 

            “As one court explained in discussing the examination of medical records vis-à-vis the right to privacy:  the information that may be recorded in a doctor’s files is broad-ranging.  The chronology of ailments and treatment is potentially sensitive.  Patients may disclose highly personal details of lifestyle and information concerning sources of stress and anxiety.  These are matters of great sensitivity going to the core of the concerns for the privacy of information about an individual.”  (County of Los Angeles, supra, 65 Cal.App.5th at pp. 641–642 [cleaned up].) 

 

            In Britt v. Superior Court (1978) 20 Cal.3d 844, 859, it was recognized that “the filing of a lawsuit may implicitly bring about a partial waiver of one’s constitutional right of . . . privacy.”  However, the California Supreme Court held that “the scope of such ‘waiver’ must be narrowly rather than expansively construed.”  (Ibid.)  In other words, “while [a plaintiff] may not withhold information which relates to any physical or mental condition which they have put in issue by bringing this lawsuit, they are entitled to retain the confidentiality of all unrelated medical or psychotherapeutic treatment they may have undergone in the past.”  (Ibid.)  Therefore, it follows that a patient cannot reasonably expect certain matters to remain private if they are related to the specific issues that the patient has himself brought before a court.  On the other hand, it is objectively reasonable to expect health care records that are unrelated to a current legal dispute to remain private.

 

            Here, on or about April 22, 2026, BHHC served subpoenas to Elite Medical Clinic; Savas Health Desert Clinic Palm Springs; The Chiropractor Center; Adam Zweig, M.D.; Douglas Roger, M.D.; Radnet (Billing Dept.); Radnet Management Inc.; California Sports & Spine Center- Santa Ana; California Sports & Spine Center- Santa Ana – Medical Records; and California Sports & Spine Center- Billing seeking “all documents and records” for Plaintiff from January 1, 2016 to present. 

 

            On May 13, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel objected on the grounds that the subpoenas should be limited to Plaintiff’s neck, bilateral shoulders, and lower back, as those are the only body parts at issue in this case, and that the request should be limited to a five-year period preceding the incident.

 

            Following meet and confer efforts, the parties agreed to limit the time frame to a seven-year period preceding the accident, but the parties did not come to an agreement regarding the scope of body parts at issue.

 

            Because a plaintiff waives their right to privacy only as to the specific injuries or conditions for which she seeks redress, the Court agrees that the scope of the subpoenas should be limited to only those body parts at issue in this case, namely Plaintiff’s neck, bilateral shoulders, and lower back, and no party has opposed the motion to demonstrate otherwise. 

 

II.             REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS

In ruling on a motion to quash, “the court may in its discretion award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonable attorney's fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.2, subd. (a).)  “Unreasonable or oppressive demands” include “unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)

 

Plaintiff seeks monetary sanctions against BHHC and/or its attorneys of record, Lewis Brisbois Bisgaard & Smith, in the amount of $6,110 for attorneys’ fees and costs incurred in bringing the instant motion, comprised of 11 hours of attorney time at $550 per hour plus the $60 filing fee.

 

The Court notes that BHHC did not oppose the motion.  As such, there is no opposition made in bad faith or without substantial justification.

 

However, because the Court ultimately agrees with Plaintiff that the subpoenas should be limited to the body parts at issue, the subpoenas as drafted constitute an unreasonable violation of Plaintiff’s right to privacy.  Further, because the motion was necessitated by BHHC’s counsel’s outright failure to respond to Plaintiff’s counsel’s request to limit the scope of the subpoenas at issue to the relevant body parts, the Court finds that monetary sanctions are appropriate against BHHC’s counsel of record.

 

Notwithstanding, the Court finds the amounts Plaintiff seeks to be excessive and awards monetary sanctions in the amount of $1,710, representing 3 hours of attorney time plus the $60 filing fee.

 

Further, there is no evidence in the record to demonstrate that BHHC, as opposed to its counsel, unreasonably sought the additional records or that BHHC thwarted its counsel from responding to Plaintiff’s counsel.  As such, the record does not support issuing monetary sanctions against BHHC.

CONCLUSION AND ORDERS

 

The Court grants in part and denies in part Plaintiff’s unopposed motion to quash and quashes the above-referenced subpoenas. 

Further, the Court awards Plaintiff monetary sanctions against BHHC’s counsel of record, Jay B. Lake of Lewis Brisbois Bisgaard & Smith LLP in the amount of $1,710.00, payable to Plaintiff, by and through Plaintiff’s counsel, within thirty (30) days of notice of this order.

 

Further, the Court will enter the proposed Order lodged on June 1, 2026 in conformity with the ruling. 

 

 

Plaintiff shall provide notice of the Court’s Order/ ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

CASE NUMBER

MOTION

Demurrer

MOVING PARTY

Defendant Ryan Shuler

OPPOSING PARTY

Plaintiff Richard Abbitt

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from a dispute between real estate business partners. 

 

On April 1, 2026, Plaintiff Richard Abbitt (“Plaintiff”) brought suit against Defendant Ryan Shuler (“Defendant”) alleging five causes of action for (1) fraud; (2) breach of fiduciary duty; (3) breach of contract; (4) conversion; and (5) constructive trust.

 

Defendant now demurs to the first, second, fourth, and fifth causes of action on the grounds that they fail to state facts sufficient to constitute causes of action pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e).  Plaintiff opposes the demurrer and Defendant replies.

 

ANALYSIS

 

1.     DEMURRER

 

“It is black letter law that a demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.”  (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.)  In testing the sufficiency of a cause of action, a court accepts “[a]s true all material facts properly pled and matters which may be judicially noticed but disregard contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.  [A court also gives] the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.”  (290 Division (EAT), LLC v. City & County of San Francisco (2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 439, 450 [cleaned up]; Hacker v. Homeward Residential, Inc. (2018) 26 Cal.App.5th 270, 280 [“in considering the merits of a demurrer, however, “the facts alleged in the pleading are deemed to be true, however improbable they may be”].)

 

Further, in ruling on a demurrer, a court must “liberally construe” the allegations of the complaint “with a view to substantial justice between the parties.”  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)  “This rule of liberal construction means that the reviewing court draws inferences favorable to the plaintiff, not the defendant.”  (Perez v. Golden Empire Transit Dist. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1228, 1238.)  

 

In summary, “[d]etermining whether the complaint is sufficient as against the demurrer on the ground that it does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, the rule is that if on consideration of all the facts stated it appears the plaintiff is entitled to any relief at the hands of the court against the defendants the complaint will be held good although the facts may not be clearly stated, or may be intermingled with a statement of other facts irrelevant to the cause of action shown, or although the plaintiff may demand relief to which he is not entitled under the facts alleged.”  (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 639.)

 

A.    FAILURE TO STATE A CAUSE OF ACTION

 

                                                         i.          First Cause of Action – Fraud by Concealment

 

“In California, fraud must be pled specifically; general and conclusory allegations do not suffice.”  (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.)  “This particularity requirement necessitates pleading facts which show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.”  (Ibid.) 

 

“One of the purposes of the specificity requirement is notice to the defendant, to furnish the defendant with certain definite charges which can be intelligently met.”  (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn., Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384.)  As such, less specificity is required “when it appears from the nature of the allegations that the defendant must necessarily possess full information concerning the facts of the controversy[.]”  (Ibid.)  “Even under the strict rules of common law pleading, one of the canons was that less particularity is required when the facts lie more in the knowledge of the opposite party.”  (Ibid.)

 

“The required elements for fraudulent concealment are (1) concealment or suppression of a material fact; (2) by a defendant with a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff; (3) the defendant intended to defraud the plaintiff by intentionally concealing or suppressing the fact; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he or she did if he or she had known of the concealed or suppressed fact; and (5) plaintiff sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact.”  (Hambrick v. Healthcare Partners Medical Group, Inc. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 124, 162.)

 

“There are four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.”  (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 311.)  In the absence of a fiduciary duty, “[a] duty to disclose facts arises only when the parties are in a relationship that gives rise to the duty, such as seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual arrangement.”  (Ibid.)

29. Abbitt further alleges that the representation of Antares in its efforts to lease the Prairie Property, and to expand the Initial Lease, were expressly part of the joint venture and/or partnership between Abbitt and Shuler, in that the commissions for the Initial Lease and the first expansion were pursuant to jointly executed agreements, and were fully shared in compliance with the then longstanding agreement to share those commissions 50%/50% between Abbitt and Shuler.

 

30. Abbitt further alleges that Shuler, as a member of the joint venture with Abbitt on the Antares lease opportunity, was in a confidential relationship with Abbitt, owed fiduciary duties to Abbitt and was bound to act in the highest good faith toward Abbitt and could not obtain any advantage over him in the partnership affairs by the slightest misrepresentation or concealment.

 

31. Abbitt further alleges that the opportunity to work with Antares to secure the final expansion of the Initial Lease into the entire Prairie Property, was an opportunity belonging to the joint venture/partnership, and Shuler could not undertake to exploit that partnership opportunity for his own personal benefit and to the resulting detriment of Abbitt.

 

32. Abbitt further alleges that as a result of his confidential and fiduciary relationship with Abbitt, Shuler had a duty to disclose the opportunity to earn additional commissions, by assisting Antares in its efforts to further expand the Initial Lease, and Shuler deliberately breached that duty.

 

33. Abbitt further alleges that while the partnership/joint venture was ongoing, Shuler actively concealed the expansion of the Antares Initial Lease from Abbitt, actively, deliberately and maliciously concealed the fact that substantial additional commissions could be earned, were earned and actually paid, and misappropriated the entirety of said commission to himself, and to the exclusion of Abbitt.

 

34. In deliberately concealing the final expansion of the Antares Initial Lease, and misappropriating the commissions earned on that concealed transaction for his exclusive benefit, Shuler knew that Abbitt was unaware of what he was doing, would have intervened if he had found out, was ready, willing and available to assist on said transaction, and was intentionally deprived of what would have been 50% of the net commission earned, but for Shuler’s deceit and concealments.

 

35. That as a direct and proximate result of Defendants’ concealment and deceit as herein alleged, the Plaintiff has suffered, and will continue to suffer, general and special damages in an amount no less than $500,000, plus interest at the legal rate for the period between the date Plaintiffs provided said time, capital, design skill, leasing and management of the Property in reliance on the Defendants misrepresentations and the date the judgment requested herein is entered. Until this matter is fully resolved, Plaintiff will not know the full extent and nature of the damages he has suffered as a result of Defendants’ fraud and misrepresentations, and Plaintiff hereby reserves the right to amend this Complaint to set forth the full extent and amount of those damages as they become known, through and including the trial in this action.

 

36. Plaintiff further alleges that in deliberately concealing the final Antares transaction and misappropriating Abbitt’s share of the commission earned on the consummation of said transaction, and engaging in the original and continuing deceit alleged herein, the Defendants were acting in a knowing, intentional, willful and malicious manner, and in a conscious disregard of the Plaintiff’s interests, and therefore, Plaintiff should be awarded punitive and exemplary damages against the Defendants.

 

(Complaint ¶¶ 7-36.)

 

Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiff alleges (1) a fiduciary relationship between Plaintiff and Defendant by virtue of their agreement; (2) Defendant’s concealment of the expansion of an existing lease vis-à-vis the Prairie property and retention of Plaintiff’s portion of the resulting commission thereon; (3) Plaintiff’s unawareness of the details of the transaction, having found out about it only through the LinkedIn announcement; and (4) damages (the unpaid portion of the commissions on the Prairie property). 

 

Defendant demurs on the grounds that Plaintiff does not allege the date, amount, or manner of the alleged concealment with particularity.  But the precise date, amount of the transaction, and manner in which Defendant acted are very things Defendant concealed from Plaintiff.  As such, they are naturally more within Defendant’s knowledge than Plaintiff’s.  Plaintiff has alleged, to the best of his knowledge, based on the February 2026 LinkedIn posting, that the transaction consummated sometime in late 2025.  That timing, in conjunction with the other specific facts that the client was Antares, the property is the Prairie property, and the fact that Defendant acknowledged the transaction to Plaintiff but refused to pay Plaintiff his share of the commissions are sufficient to put Defendant on notice as to the specific transaction at issue.

 

Therefore, the Court sustains the demurrer to the first cause of action for fraudulent concealment.

 

                                                       ii.          Second Cause of Action – Breach of Fiduciary Duty

 

“The elements of a claim for breach of fiduciary duty are (1) the existence of a fiduciary relationship, (2) its breach, and (3) damage proximately caused by that breach.”  (O'Neal v. Stanislaus County Employees' Retirement Assn. (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 1184, 1215.) 

 

Defendant argues that no fiduciary duty arises because no joint venture actually exists.  “A joint venture requires (1) a joint interest; (2) an agreement to share profits and losses; and (3) a right to joint control.”  (Weiner v. Fleischman (1991) 54 Cal.3d 476, 482.) 

 

Specifically, Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not alleged a right to control, as required to create a true joint venture.  In support, Defendant cites to Brown v. USA Taekwondo (2019) 40 Cal.App.5th 1077, review granted,[1] (2021) 11 Cal.5th 204 (hereafter Brown).  In Brown, the plaintiffs alleged negligence against the defendants premised upon a joint venture.  The Court of Appeal held that allegations “on information and belief” that the defendants contributed money to the enterprise and “had a right of control in directing the conduct of the enterprise” were insufficient to allege the essential element of a joint venture that the joint venturers share in the profits and losses.  (Id. at p. 1105.)  Further, the appellate court held that denial of leave to amend was appropriate because the plaintiffs failed to articulate facts that they could allege that one of the defendants shared in the profits or had a right of control over any joint venture.  (Ibid.)

 

But Defendant has not cited any authority requiring a heightened pleading requirement to allege a breach of fiduciary duty.  As such, “ultimate facts” suffice.[2]   Yet, as to a joint venture, Plaintiff alleges:

 

9. Since approximately 2018, Abbitt and Shuler have jointly engaged in representing various parties in real estate transactions prior to the transaction at issue, and have split the commissions earned on approximately 100 transactions and Abbitt had trust and confidence in Shuler as a result of these prior dealings.

 

10. Initially, because Shuler was just starting out, and Abbitt had decades of experience, the initial commission split was 90%/10% between them, but by 2020, and due to the low volume of transactions resulting from the Covid disruption, Abbitt and Shuler orally agreed to make the split 50%/50%. Since 2018, Abbitt and Shuler have completed many real estate transactions worth many millions of dollars, and since 2020, have split the commissions earned on those transactions, 50%/50%.

 

11. Initially, Abbitt and Shuler were jointly working as agents at the Santa Monica office of Lee & Associates. Since early 2020, they have been agents at Industry Partners in Santa Monica (“Industry”).

 

12. At Industry, by law and practice, all brokerage relationships, and commission agreements are between Industry – as broker, and the client. All commissions are earned by the broker, and paid by the client to the broker. Industry, as with all brokers, is entitled to, and does retain licensed agents like Abbitt and Shuler, to assist the broker in these transactions. Agents like Abbitt and Shuler, have their contractual relations with the broker, and not with the client, who may not contract directly with the agent. Brokers like Industry, may agree to share the commissions earned with their agents like Abbitt and Shuler. In addition, agents like Abbitt and Shuler, may agree to share in commissions paid by brokers like Industry, as long as such agreements are known to the broker and agreed to by the broker.

 

13. Since starting at Industry, Abbitt and Shuler have, in a written agreement with Industry, to a commission split such that approximately ten percent (10%) of the gross commission would be retained by Industry, with the remaining commission distributed to Abbitt and Shuler split 50/50%. Since beginning at Industry, Abbitt and Shuler have split the real estate commissions in this manner on approximately 25 transactions.

 

14. In late 2024, the team of Abbitt and Shuler attended a SOHO Malibu “Tech Event.” Shuler had been a member of SOHO Malibu, but refused to renew his membership, unless Abbitt paid half of that cost. In order that they could both attend events there, Abbitt reimbursed $7,500 of the membership renewal cost to Shuler, and the two of them participated in several events at SOHO Malibu, including the Tech Event.

 

[…]

 

38. Based upon the facts set forth above, Plaintiff contends that he and Defendants agreed to form and did form a joint venture partnership and/or agreement (“JVA”) in 2018, and since then, have been joint venture partners and as a result, they hold a 50%/50% interest in all net commissions paid by Industry on transactions in which either or both of them were involved. Plaintiff further alleges that he has performed all of his obligations under the JVA, except for such obligations that are excused by Defendants’ breaches of the JVA, as alleged herein.

 

39. As joint venture partners, Defendants, and each of them, knew that they were in a fiduciary relationship with Plaintiff, and have owed and continue to owe Plaintiffs, all fiduciary obligations partners owe to the other members/partners of the joint venture. Pursuant to the JVA, Shuler owes and has fiduciary duties of fidelity and loyalty, and is obligated to put Plaintiff’s interests on a footing at least equal to those of Shuler.

 

(Complaint ¶¶ 9-14; 38-39.)

 

            While Plaintiff has alleged (1) a joint interest in selling real estate (Complaint ¶ 9) and (2) that the parties shared in the profits (Complaint ¶¶ 10, 13, 38) and losses (Complaint ¶ 14); there are no allegations that the parties’ agreement conferred on each of them a joint right to control the venture.

 

            In opposition, Plaintiff argues that even if the Complaint does not adequately allege control, a fiduciary duty nonetheless arises due to the parties’ alleged “confidential relationship.” 

 

“Fiduciary” and “confidential” have been used synonymously to describe any relation existing between parties to a transaction wherein one of the parties is in duty bound to act with the utmost good faith for the benefit of the other party. Such a relation ordinarily arises where a confidence is reposed by one person in the integrity of another, and in such a relation the party in whom the confidence is reposed, if he [or she] voluntarily accepts or assumes to accept the confidence, can take no advantage from his [or her] acts relating to the interest of the other party without the latter's knowledge or consent. Technically, a fiduciary relationship is a recognized legal relationship such as guardian and ward, trustee and beneficiary, principal and agent, or attorney and client, whereas a “confidential relationship” may be founded on a moral, social, domestic, or merely personal relationship as well as on a legal relationship. The essence of a fiduciary or confidential relationship is that the parties do not deal on equal terms, because the person in whom trust and confidence is reposed and who accepts that trust and confidence is in a superior position to exert unique influence over the dependent party. 

 

(Barbara A. v. John G. (1983) 145 Cal.App.3d 369, 382–383, emphasis added {cleaned up].)

 

            Here, although the Complaint alleges that in the past, Defendant was in a superior position to Plaintiff, such that the parties split their profits at a rate of 90/10, since 2020, the parties have split profits and expenses equally.  (Complaint ¶ 10.)  Thus, the Complaint does not allege an unequal dynamic at the time Plaintiff concealed the Prairie property lease expansion, or that by virtue of the parties’ agreement or conduct, Defendant accepted the confidence of Plaintiff and undertook not to exert undue influence over him by virtue of that unequal dynamic, to demonstrate a confidential relationship giving rise to a fiduciary duty.

 

            Therefore, the Court sustains the demurrer to the second cause of action.

 

                                                     iii.          Fourth Cause of Action – Conversion

 

“Conversion is the wrongful exercise of dominion over the property of another.

The elements of a conversion are the plaintiff's ownership or right to possession of the property at the time of the conversion; the defendant's conversion by a wrongful act or disposition of property rights; and damages.”  (Spates v. Dameron Hospital Assn. (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 208, 221.)  However, “[a] mere contractual right of payment, without more, will not suffice to support a claim for conversion.”  (Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 221, 233, emphasis added.)  Here, Plaintiff alleges:

 

49. As alleged herein, and by virtue of his membership in the JVA and pursuant to his agreements with Defendants, Plaintiff owned and/or had a right to receive 50% of the net commission earned on the final expansion of the Initial Lease, which always belonged to Plaintiff, and never belonged to Defendants, although Defendants, as alleged herein, have diverted, misappropriated and improperly taken possession of said commission.

 

50. Defendants substantially interfered with Plaintiff’s right to, use and possess his property by knowingly and/or intentionally taking possession of said share of the net commission, by misappropriating and or diverting said commission, and/or by refusing to return the commission after Plaintiff demanded its return.

 

51. Plaintiff did not consent to Defendant’s interference with Plaintiff’s possession of the commission, as described above.

 

52. Defendant’s conduct was a substantial factor in causing Plaintiffs’ harm.

 

53. That as a direct and proximate result of Defendants’ original, continuing and intentional conversion of the commission as alleged, the Plaintiff has suffered, and will continue to suffer, general and special damages in an amount no less than $500,000.00, plus interest at the legal rate for the period between the date Defendants converted the commission and the date the judgment requested herein is entered. Until this matter is fully resolved, Plaintiff will not know the full extent and nature of the damages he has suffered as a result of Defendants’ conversion of the commission, and Plaintiff hereby reserves the right to amend this Complaint to set forth the full extent and amount of those damages as they become known, through and including the trial in this action.

 

(Complaint ¶¶ 49-53, emphasis added.)

 

            Thus, because the Complaint alleges only a right to a contractual payment, Plaintiff has not adequately alleged a cause of action for conversion.

 

                                                     iv.          Fifth Cause of Action – Constructive Trust

 

Defendant demurs to the fifth cause of action on the grounds that constructive trust is not itself a cause of action, but merely a remedy.  (See, e.g. Glue-Fold, Inc. v. Slautterback Corp. (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 1018, 1023, fn. 3.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff concedes this is true, but points out that Courts permit parties to plead constructive trust as a cause of action where the underlying equitable basis is adequately stated.  (See, e.g. Burlesci v. Petersen (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 1062, 1069.)

 

Indeed, the elements for the imposition of constructive trust are: (1) the existence of a res; (2) the right of the complaining party to that res; and (3) some wrongful acquisition or detention of the res by another party not entitled to it.  (Communist Party v. 522 Valencia, Inc. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 980, 990.)

 

But here, as demonstrated by the Court’s sustaining of Defendant’s demurrer to the fourth cause of action for conversion, Plaintiff has not adequately alleged the existence of a res requiring the imposition of a constructive trust.  Rather, money damages suffice to remedy Plaintiff’s only remaining cause of action for breach of contract.

 

2.     LEAVE TO AMEND

 

A plaintiff has the burden of showing in what manner the complaint could be amended and how the amendment would change the legal effect of the complaint, i.e., state a cause of action. (See The Inland Oversight Committee v. City of San Bernardino (2018) 27 Cal.App.5th 771, 779; PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Int'l, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 156, 189.) A plaintiff must not only state the legal basis for the amendment, but also the factual allegations sufficient to state a cause of action or claim. (See PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Int'l, Inc., supra, 14 Cal.App.5th at p. 189.) Moreover, a plaintiff does not meet his or her burden by merely stating in the opposition to a demurrer or motion to strike that “if the Court finds the operative complaint deficient, plaintiff respectfully requests leave to amend.” (See Major Clients Agency v Diemer (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1133; Graham v. Bank of America (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 594, 618 [asserting an abstract right to amend does not satisfy the burden].)

 

Here, Plaintiff argues that he can amend the complaint to allege:

 

(1) specific facts regarding the joint decision-making and day-to-day operation of the joint venture to further support joint control; (2) the specific gross and net amount of the final Antares commission; and (3) additional detail regarding the timeline and mechanics of Shuler’s concealment.

 

(Opposition at p. 13:21-24.)

 

            While these proposed facts could cure the deficiencies for the breach of fiduciary duty and fraud causes of action, they would not cure the deficiencies vis-à-vis the causes of action for conversion or constructive trust. 

 

In opposition, Plaintiff relies on Voris v. Lampbert (2019) 7 Cal.5th 1141, 1151 as standing for the general proposition that once Plaintiff can identify the exact amount of money owed, his contractual right to commission payments can similarly constitute property for purposes of a conversion claim. 

 

            But in Voris, the Supreme Court specifically denied the plaintiff employee’s right to maintain a conversion claim on the basis of unpaid wages, explaining, “where the money or fund is not identified as a specific thing the action is to be considered as one upon contract or for debt—or perhaps upon some other appropriate theory—but not for conversion.”  (Voris v. Lampert (2019) 7 Cal.5th 1141, 1151, citing Baxter v. King (1927) 81 Cal.App. 192, 194.) 

 

            Indeed, “California cases permitting an action for conversion of money typically involve those who have misappropriated, commingled, or misapplied specific funds held for the benefit of others.”  (PCO, Inc. v. Christensen, Miller, Fink, Jacobs, Glaser, Weil & Shapiro, LLP (2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 384, 396, emphasis added.)

 

            For example, in Fischer v. Machado (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 1069, the defendant converted funds from the sale of a farm the defendant sold on behalf of defendant as defendant’s agent.  The underlying real property and resulting funds from the sale were always the plaintiff’s property, which the defendant only acquired in his capacity as the plaintiff’s agent.  Thus, the defendant’s retention of those funds was the wrongful conversion of the plaintiff’s property.

 

Similarly, in Weiss v. Marcus (1975) 51 Cal.App.3d 590, 599, the litigant’s new counsel failed to pay former counsel the amounts owed from a settlement received, despite having notice of former counsel’s lien.  The lien established former counsel’s legal right to a portion of the settlement funds received, which new counsel wrongfully converted by disbursing it to the client instead of to former counsel.

 

            Thus, the core problem is not that Plaintiff has failed to allege the specific dollar amount owed, but rather that Plaintiff has alleged only a contractual right to commission payments, not the wrongful retention of a fund or account held in trust that can be properly characterized as Plaintiff’s property.  Plaintiff has proffered no facts that can cure this fundamental deficiency with his conversion and constructive trust claims.  Rather, Plaintiff alleges only a claim for money damages.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the reasons stated, the Court sustains without leave to amend Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s fifth and sixth causes of action, and sustains with leave to amend Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s first and second causes of action.

 

Further, the Court orders Plaintiff to file and serve the amended complaint in conformance with this ruling on or before July 6, 2026.

 

Further, the Court will enter the proposed Order lodged on May 19, 2026 in conformity with the ruling. 

 

Defendant shall provide notice of the Court’s Order/ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

Motion for Order to Remove Inadvertently Filed Public Documents

Plaintiff Thomas Soliz

OPPOSING PARTY

MOTION

 

This case arises from a dispute concerning the alleged breach of a settlement agreement. 

 

On May 1, 2026, Plaintiff Thomas Soliz (“Plaintiff”) brought suit against Defendants Scott Chaplan and Del Rey Urban Brokerage, Inc. (“Defendants”) alleging three causes of action for (1) breach of contract; (2) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing; and (3) unjust enrichment.

 

On May 12, 2026, the Court initially denied Plaintiff’s application for leave to file under seal Exhibit A to the Complaint (the parties’ settlement agreement) and references thereto in the Complaint, on the grounds that Plaintiff had not lodged with the Court the document proposed to be sealed and that request to seal the entire settlement agreement was unlikely to be sufficiently narrowly tailored.

 

On June 8, 2026, the Court denied Plaintiff’s renewed application for leave to file Exhibit A under seal, finding that although Plaintiff had cured the prior procedural deficiency of failing to lodge the exhibit with the Court, Plaintiff’s request was not narrowly tailored, and indeed, the agreement did not appear to contain any private financial information (such as bank account numbers) warranting any sealing.

 

Plaintiff now moves for an order to remove Exhibit A from the public docket as having been inadvertently filed.  The motion is unopposed.

 

ANALYSIS

 

            “In general, the moving party must carry the initial burden of informing its opponent and the court of the specific basis for its motion.  If the rule were otherwise, then the party opposing the motion would have to try to guess, and then refute, every possible basis for the motion, which would always be inefficient and would often produce arbitrary results.”  (People v. Williams (1999) 20 Cal.4th 119, 129, citation omitted.)        

 

            On that premise, the Court finds that Plaintiff has not advanced any authority in his moving papers which would warrant the relief sought in violation of California Rules of Court, rule 3.1113(b) which provides: “The memorandum must contain a statement of facts, a concise statement of the law, evidence and arguments relied on, and a discussion of the statutes, cases, and textbooks cited in support of the position advanced.”  (See, e.g., Chavez v. Netflix, Inc. (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 43, 52 [trial court was justified in denying the motion on procedural grounds due to  deficient memorandum in support of motion].) 

 

            Similarly, the Second District Court of Appeal, in affirming the trial court’s denial of post-trial motions on procedural grounds, observed:  “Rule 3.1113 rests on a policy-based allocation of resources, preventing the trial court from being cast as a tacit advocate for the moving party's theories by freeing it from any obligation to comb the record and the law for factual and legal support that a party has failed to identify or provide.”  (Quantum Cooking Concepts, Inc. v. LV Associates, Inc. (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 927, 934.) 

 

            Accordingly, the Court denies will deny Plaintiff’s motion as procedurally defective. 

 

Notwithstanding, to the extent that Plaintiff is again seeking to “seal” Exhibit A from the public record, the Court finds that endeavor to be equally flawed because Plaintiff does not raise any new arguments as to why some or all of Exhibit A is subject to sealing by the Court.  (See Minute Orders of May 12, 2026 and June 8, 2026 which are incorporated by this reference.)

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

            For the reasons stated, the Court denies Plaintiff’s motion without prejudice as procedurally defective.
