[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: PLAINTIFF MICHAEL BRIAN SMITH’S MOTION TO SET ASIDE/VACATE JUDGMENT [CRS #8855] Dept. 731 8:30 a.m. July 21, 2026

Moving Party: Plaintiff, Michael Brian Smith

Opposing Party: Defendant, Michael Kors Inc.

Notice: OK

Plaintiff Michael B. Smith brought this suit against his former employer, Defendant Michael Kors Inc., alleging race discrimination in his employment and a subsequent unlawful layoff.

On March 26, 2026, the Court granted a motion for judgment on the pleadings filed by Defendant, which disposed of the then-operative March 19, 2024, Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”). Plaintiff filed a motion for reconsideration on March 30, 2026, which was opposed by Defendant, and was ultimately denied by the Court on May 20, 2026. The Court entered judgment in Defendant’s favor that same day.

Now before the Court is Plaintiff’s motion to set aside/vacate the May 20, 2026, judgment on the grounds that another action affecting the outcome of the proceedings before this Court is purportedly presently pending before the Compton, California branch of the Los Angeles Superior Court as 22CMCV00753, Smith v. Michael Kors Inc. (the “Santa Monica action”). (See Motion, p. 2, ¶ 4.)

The motion is DENIED, with prejudice.

Plaintiff’s motion is noticed as being premised on Code of Civil Procedure sections 473, subdivision (d), and 1008, subdivisions (a), (b), and (e). (Motion, pp. 1-2.) Both premises fail.

As argued in Defendant’s opposition, there is no applicable provision of Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d), here. (See Opposition pp. 11-12.) Plaintiff is asking that the Court vacate a final judgment based on the purported pendency of the Santa Monica action. This is not a valid ground for relief under section 473, subdivision (d), which is limited to clerical errors and void judgments and orders, including those voided by extrinsic fraud or mistake, neither of which Plaintiff’s paper show. (See Kramer v. Traditional Escrow, Inc. (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 13, 29 [discussing § 473, subd. (d)].)

Similarly, none of the grounds for party-requested reconsideration—new or different facts, circumstances, or law (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a))—appear here, as the requested relief is based on the purported pendency of the Santa Monica action rather than some fact, circumstance, or new law relating to this action.

Last, the Court takes judicial notice of Exhibit A attached to Defendant’s opposition, which shows a June 18, 2026, minute order for the Santa Monica action, in which the court in that case denied a similar motion by Plaintiff in this case to vacate or set aside the judgment in that case, which was entered on November 6, 2025. (Opposition, Request for Judicial Notice, Ex. A, pp. 6-7.)

Plaintiff’s motion is accordingly DENIED. No further motions seeking identical relief shall be entertained absent a proper statutory basis.

  1. Background Facts
    1. Pleadings

Plaintiff Ruben Vera sues Defendants University of Southern California (“USC”), Contemporary Services Corporation (“CSC”), Apex Security Group, Inc. (“Apex”), Live Nation, Inc. (“Live Nation”), Live Nation Worldwide, Inc. (“LNW”), and Does 1 to 50 pursuant to an August 15, 2024, Complaint alleging claims of Negligence, Negligent Infliction of Emotional Distress, and Assault and Battery.

The claims arise from allegations that, on August 20, 2023, while attending a live “Los Bukis” concert with members of his family at the USC Coliseum located at 3911 S Figueroa St, Los Angeles, CA 90037, Plaintiff was suddenly and without provocation approached and assaulted by security officers employed by Apex, a company which alongside CSC was providing security services for the Los Bukis concert, with Apex security then handcuffing Plaintiff, dragging him away from the concert goers, climbing on top of Plaintiff (thus preventing Plaintiff from breathing), cursing at Plaintiff ordering him to keep his head down, and eventually escorting Plaintiff to a detainment center, where Plaintiff was held until after the concert ended, having sustained traumatic injuries to his person, including but not limited to a fractured clavicle, neck strain, musculoskeletal chest pain, and wrist contusions.

    1. Background Facts

Now before the Court is CSC and Apex’s June 25, 2026, joint motion for summary adjudication of the Complaint’s second cause of action for Negligent Infliction of Emotional Distress against these Defendants and the Complaint’s prayer for punitive damages against Apex.

Plaintiff filed an opposition on September 4, 2026, against which CSC and Apex filed a reply on September 11, 2026.

 

  1. Motion for Summary Adjudication

b.     Legal Standard

A motion for summary judgment shall be granted if all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact for trial or that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law. (Code of Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) A party may also seek summary adjudication of select causes of action, affirmative defenses, claims for damages, or issues of duty, which may be made by a standalone motion or as an alternative to a motion for summary judgment and proceeds in all procedural respects like a motion for summary judgment, but which must completely dispose of the challenged cause of action, affirmative defense, claim for damages, or issue of duty. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subds. (f)(1)-(2), (t).)

The moving party bears the initial burden of production to make prima facie showing no triable material fact issues. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850 (Aguilar).) This burden on summary judgment or adjudication “is more properly one of persuasion rather than proof, since he must persuade the court that there is no material fact for a reasonable trier of fact to find, and not to prove any such fact to the satisfaction of the court itself as though it were sitting as the trier of fact.” (Id. at p. 850, fn. 11.) If the moving party meets this burden, the burden shifts to the opposing party to make a rebuttal prima facie showing that a triable issue of material fact exists. (Id. at p. 849.)

Where a defendant seeks summary judgment or adjudication, the defendant must show that either “one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to that cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (o)(2).) A defendant may satisfy this burden by showing that the claim “cannot be established” because of the lack of evidence on some essential element of the claim. (Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 574, 590.) Once the defendant meets this burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a “triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or defense thereto.” (Ibid.)

“[I]n ruling on motions for summary judgment courts are to ‘“liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.”‘ [Citations].” (Cheal v. El Camino Hospital (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 736, 760.)

    1. Discussion

                                          i.    Complaint, Second Cause of Action, Negligent Infliction of Emotional Distress

As a matter of law—rather than on burden shifting based on evidence—and as raised for first time in reply—permissible here due to the clear applicability of the argument, in response to which the Court permits oral argument and may permit further briefing as necessary—the Court finds that the NIED claim fails on summary adjudication.

Under California law, negligent infliction of emotional distress is not an independent tort, but rather, is instead subsumed within the tort of negligence. (Burgess v. Superior Court (1992) 2 Cal.4th 1064, 1072 (Burgess); Huggins v. Longs Drug Stores California, Inc. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 124, 129 (Huggins).) Consequently, a plaintiff seeking to recover for negligent infliction of emotional distress (“NIED”) must establish the traditional elements of negligence: duty, breach of duty, causation, and damages. (Burgess, supra, 2 Cal.4th at p. 1072; Huggins, supra, 6 Cal.4th at p. 129.)

California courts have addressed these duplicative claims in the context of summary disposition and post-trial challenges, concluding that separate claims for NIED cannot stand alongside general negligence claims because they are redundant and risk creating double recovery.

In Los Angeles Cellular Tel. Co. v. Superior Court (1998) 65 Cal.App.4th 1013, 1016, fn. 1 & 1018-1019 (“L.A. Cellular”), in the context of a writ of mandate from a denied motion for summary adjudication, the court of appeal treated its analysis of a negligence claim and of a separate negligent infliction of emotional distress claim as a single claim for negligence, specifically noting that “[f]or reasons we do not understand, [plaintiff’s] complaint includes a separate cause of action entitled ‘negligent infliction of emotional distress’ (a cause of action generally limited to bystanders and other third parties),” where “negligent infliction of emotional distress is not an independent tort but simply the tort of negligence, with the traditional elements of duty, breach and causation.” (Quoted content at fn. 1.)

In Plotnik v. Meihaus (2012) 208 Cal.App.4th 1590, 1608-1609 (Plotnik), in the context of appeals to jury verdicts resulting in judgment, the court of appeal reversed judgment in favor of plaintiff as to the negligent infliction of emotional distress claim, reasoning that, even though distinct awards were given for claims of breach of settlement agreement and NIED, “the duty underlying each cause of action [wa]s identical,” for which reason the court of appeal “s[aw] no basis for distinguishing between the emotional distress plaintiffs suffered because of [the defendant’s] breach of the settlement and his liability in tort for negligent infliction of emotional distress.”

In Belen v. Ryan Seacrest Productions, LLC (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 1145, 1165, within the context of discussing prong 2 of the anti-SLAPP analysis on appeal, the court of appeal noted the correct finding by a trial court that the NIED claim in that action was duplicative of a negligence claim otherwise alleged in that action, but modified the judgment in the action to strike the cause of action for negligent infliction of emotional distress because “the trial court neglected to strike the cause of action for negligent infliction of emotional distress,” where “no such independent tort exists.” (Emphasis added.)

Several federal courts have reached similar conclusions with regard to the relationship between negligence and NIED claims. (See, e.g., Lopez v. Johnson and Johnson (2023) 654 F.Supp.3d 997, 1016 [Federal district court granting motion to dismiss negligent infliction of emotional distress claim based on California law determining that there is no independent NIED claim beyond the tort of negligence]; White v. Social Security Administration (2015) 111 F.Supp.3d 1041, 1054 [same].)

Here, because the first cause of action for Negligence and the second cause of action for NIED are alleged equally against all Defendants, the NIED claims add nothing to the pleadings beyond the Negligence claim, is redundant, and under the above authorities, should either be struck or considered subsumed into the negligence claim.

Based on this reasoning, as a matter of law, summary adjudication is GRANTED as relates to the second cause of action.

                                         ii.    Complaint, Prayer, Punitive Damages

CSC and Apex next seek summary adjudication of the Complaint’s prayer for punitive damages as against Apex, which the moving papers correctly note that the Complaint expressly confines to Apex. (Complaint, ¶¶ 54, 57 & Prayer, ¶ 3.)

In support of this position, CSC and Apex argue that “Plaintiff cannot meet his burden to show by clear and convincing evidence that any corporate officer, director, or managing agent of Apex committed, or knowingly ratified any of the Apex security guards’ alleged, malicious or oppressive conduct toward Plaintiff,” as required by Civil Code section 3294 and controlling case law. (Motion, pp. 6-7, citing Motion, Separate Statement, Undisputed Material Fact (“UMF”) Nos. 4-8.)

Established case law exists relating to this argument. When the defendant is a corporation, ‘[a]n award of punitive damages … must rest on the malice of the corporation’s employees’” specifically, “the oppression, fraud, or malice perpetrated, authorized, or knowingly ratified by an officer, director, or managing agent of the corporation,” where a managing agent “include[s] only those corporate employees who exercise substantial independent authority and judgment in their corporate decisionmaking so that their decisions ultimately determine corporate policy.’” (Wilson v. Southern California Edison Co. (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 123, 164, citations omitted.) 

For factual support, CSC and Apex cite discovery responses served by Plaintiff to Apex’s Special Interrogatories, Set One, Nos. 70-72, in which Plaintiff respectively identifies the factual bases for punitive damages, witnesses supporting punitive damages, and documents supporting punitive damages. (See Motion, Separate Statement, UMF Nos. 4-8, citing Motion, Index of Exhibits, Ex. 4, Responses to Special Interrogatories, Set One, Nos. 70-72, as verified in Motion, Doumar Decl., 2:20-21 as Exhibit 4.)

The Court notes that it has overruled objections to this evidence.

The Court also notes that where a defendant moves for summary judgment or adjudication on the ground that the plaintiff does not possess, and/or cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence, the defendant cannot simply make a bare argument to that effect but must instead support the motion with evidence. (Professional Collection Consultants v. Lauron (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 958, 965, citing to Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 855; Rio Linda Unified School Dist. v. Superior Court (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 732, 735 [party seeking summary adjudication may “establish its prima-facie entitlement to judgment by demonstrating its opponent’s discovery responses are devoid of evidence to support an element of the opponent's case”].)

Here, a review of Plaintiff’s discovery responses confirms the proposition for which the evidence is advanced, i.e., nowhere in Plaintiff’s responses to special interrogatories specifically asking Plaintiff to identify his bases for punitive damages did Plaintiff identify the conduct or omissions of any Apex corporate officer, director, or managing agent. (See Motion, Index of Exhibits, Ex. 4, Responses to Special Interrogatories, Set One, Nos. 70-72.)

Nevertheless, the Court determines that CSC and Apex fail to carry their burden on summary adjudication of the prayer for punitive damages against Apex.

In the context of a summary judgment or adjudication motion, if a party contends some particular issue of fact has no support in the record, it must set forth all the material evidence on the point and not merely the evidence favorable to it. (Rio Linda Unified School Dist. v. Superior Court (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 732, 740, citing Foreman & Clark Corp. v. Fallon (1971) 3 Cal.3d 875, 881.) Moreover, the moving party must also produce evidence that the opponent cannot reasonably expect to produce evidence supporting the infirm element. (Addy v. Bliss & Glennon (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 205, 214.)

Here, the moving papers only address whether Plaintiff’s responses to Apex’s Special Interrogatories, Set One, Nos. 70-72 contain any information relating to conduct or omissions by a corporate officer, director, or managing agent at Apex and fail to mention whether other discovery propounded on Plaintiff relates to punitive damages, what the responses thereto were, or why Plaintiff cannot be reasonably expected to obtain discovery showing corporate liability to Apex by way of the conduct or omissions of any corporate officer, director, or managing agent.

Summary adjudication of the prayer for punitive damages, expressly confined by the pleadings to Apex, is accordingly DENIED.

d.     Conclusion

Contemporary Services Corporation and Apex Security Group, Inc.’s motion for summary adjudication is GRANTED in part and DENIED in part as follows:

(1) GRANTED as to the Complaint’s second cause of action; and

(2) DENIED as to the Complaint’s prayer for punitive damages against Apex.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO SUBSTITUTE SUCCESSOR IN INTEREST FOR DECEASED PLAINTIFF

 

Dept. 731

September 24, 2026

8:30 A.M.

Calendar No.16

 

Moving Party:               Plaintiff’s natural adult son, DAVID KOHAN as the successor in interest for his deceased mother, PLAINTIFF, FELIZE KOHAN

Opposing Party:           None

Notice:                         OK

 

  1. Background Facts

Now before the Court is a motion by David Kohan, natural adult son of Plaintiff Felize Kohan, Deceased, for an order permitting him to substitute into this action as a successor-in-interest plaintiff to his late mother. The motion was properly served via email on counsel for Defendants, who have failed to file an opposition to the motion.

 

  1. Motion to Substitute Plaintiff

a.     Legal Standard

 “Every action must be prosecuted in the name of the real party in interest, except as otherwise provided by statute.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 367.) “The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a)(1).) For example, “[u]nder certain circumstances, a complaint may be amended to substitute a new plaintiff where it is determined the named plaintiff is not the proper party to maintain the alleged claims, so long as the amendment does not present an entirely new set of facts and the defendant is not prejudiced.” (Demetriades v. Yelp, Inc. (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 294, 305-306.) Such an amendment may take place after trial has been concluded, judgment entered, and a motion for new trial denied, or even after an appeal has been taken. (Erickson v. Boothe (1949) 90 Cal.App.2d 457, 459.) California courts should liberally allow such amendments to substitute as plaintiffs the true real parties in interest. (CashCall, Inc. v. Superior Court (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 273, 287, citing Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 1004.)

A personal representative has standing to enforce the rights of the decedent’s estate. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 369, subd. (a)(1); Olson v. Toy (1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 818, 823-824 (Olson); see also Roes v. Wong (1999) 69 Cal.App.4th 375, 378 [executor of deceased borrower’s estate has standing to assert usury].) A “personal representative” ordinarily is (1) an executor named in the will, (2) a successor to that executor, called an “administrator-with-the-will-annexed,” or (3) an administrator when the decedent died without naming an executor. (Estate of Hilton (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 890, 894, fn.1; see Prob. Code, § 58, subd. (a).) Generally, an heir does not have standing to sue on behalf of the estate. (Holland v. McCarthy (1918) 177 Cal. 507, 510; Olson, supra, at pp. 823-824; see, e.g., Amundson v. Catello (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 817, 827-828 [personal representative’s stipulation permitting heirs to handle estate litigation on representative’s behalf did not confer standing on heirs to participate in suit]; see also Prob. Code, § 44 [“‘Heir’ means any person, including the surviving spouse, who is entitled to take property of the decedent by intestate succession under this code.”].)

    1. Discussion

After review, the Court finds insufficient grounds for relief.

Simply, the moving papers consist of a brief declaration from David Kohan that fails to establish, either by verified paper or by evidence, that David Kohan is a real party in interest suitable to take over the place of Plaintiff Felize Kohan in this litigation. This is because the papers fail to show that David Kohan is any of (1) the executor in a named will of Felize Kohan, (2) a successor to initial executor, or (3) an/the administrator of the estate of Felize Kohan.

At most, the unverified moving papers represent that David Kohan is Felize Kohan’s natural adult son and that he is the sole beneficiary of his mother’s estate pursuant to intestate succession. (Motion, 4:5-8.)

However, the verified declaration attached to the moving papers does not make the same representations, limiting itself to describing David Kohan as Felize Kohan’s natural son, describing the circumstances leading to Felize Kohan’s death and attaching a death certificate therefor, and indicating David Kohan seeks to become the plaintiff in place of his late mother. (Motion, Kohan Decl., ¶¶ 1-5 [full contents of declaration].)

This is defective motion work given that a memorandum of points and authorities must include (1) a statement of facts (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1113(b)) supported by admissible evidence and followed by a reference to the supporting evidence accompanying the memorandum (see Smith, Smith & Kring v. Superior Court (1997) 60 Cal.App.4th 573, 577-578), and (2) a concise statement of the law, arguments, and evidence relied on and a discussion of the statutes, cases, and textbooks cited in support of the movant’s position (Cal. Rules of Court, 3.1113(b), emphasis added; see, e.g., Nationwide Ins. v. Tipton (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 1355, 1365 [trial court had discretion to ignore arguments that Ds did not properly present in their memorandum of points and authorities in opposition to P’s petition]).

c.     Conclusion

The Court accordingly DENIES non-party David Kohan’s motion, without prejudice.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION OF DEFENDANT CALIFORNIA AUTOMOBILE INSURANCE COMPANY TO STRIKE PLAINTIFF’S FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT [CRS NO. 410505430714]

 

Dept. 731

Matter No. 13

 

 

Moving Party:               Defendant, California Automobile Insurance Company

Opposing Party:           Plaintiff, Kevin Thanh Cao

Notice:                         OK

 

  1. Background Facts

This action arises from an insurance coverage dispute concerning alleged wind damage to a residential roof.

    1. Pleadings

Specifically, Plaintiff Kevin Thanh Cao (“Plaintiff”) sues California Automobile Insurance Company (“CAIC”) under a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging claims for Breach of Contract, Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing, and Violations of Business and Professions Code Section 17200, et seq.

The FAC’s claims arise from allegations that wind caused significant roof damage to a home owned by Plaintiff and covered under an insurance policy by CAIC requiring full replacement of three slopes of an integrated roof system, but that CAIC paid only $3,562.40 for partial repairs using non-matching shingles despite knowing the original shingles were discontinued, ignored its own ITEL report confirming that replacement materials could not provide a reasonably uniform appearance, and failed to investigate whether the entire roof constituted the damaged area, as required by law and the Policy, as a result of which the roof remains structurally compromised, visually non-uniform, and unable to be returned to pre-loss condition, causing unreimbursed costs, diminished property value, and further exposure to damage.

    1. Motions Before the Court

Now before the Court are CAIC’s demurrer challenging the FAC’s three causes of action and motion to strike three distinct groups of allegations: (1) allegations beyond the leave to amend from this Court’s June 4, 2026, Order sustaining a demurrer to Plaintiff’s original Complaint; (2) allegations related to and prayers for punitive damages; (3) damages disguised as restitution vis-à-vis the Business and Professions Code claim; and (4) prejudgment interest vis-à-vis the Business and Professions Code claim. Both the demurrer and motion to strike are opposed by Plaintiff.

 

  1. Demurrer
    1. Meet and Confer

The moving papers establish that a sufficient meet and confer process preceded the filing of CAIC’s demurrer. (Demurrer, Duque Decl., ¶¶ 3-12, Exs. A-C; see Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3)(A).)

b.     Legal Standard

                                          i.    Sufficiency

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; see Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).)

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) “[E]ach evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)

In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-67.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) A demurrer, however, “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

A demurrer may only be used to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318 (Blank).) The face of the complaint includes exhibits attached to the complaint. (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94.) If facts appearing in the exhibits contradict those alleged, the facts in the exhibits take precedence. (Holland v. Morse Diesel Intern., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447, superseded by statute on other grounds as stated in White v. Cridlebaugh (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 506, 521.)

                                         ii.    Lack of Clarity of as to Type of Contract

In a contract action, a defendant can file a special demurrer to a complaint on the ground that it cannot be determined from the pleadings whether the contract is written, oral, or implied by conduct. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (g); see, e.g., Holcomb v. Wells Fargo Bank (2007) 155 Cal.App.4th 490, 500-501 (Holcomb) [affirming trial court order sustaining, with leave to amend, a demurrer challenging breach of contract claim, reasoning that the pleadings failed to adequately allege the nature of the contract, particularly where the pleadings alleged an express contract from which certain terms were to be implied and moreover failed to allege the specific terms breached].)

    1. Discussion

                                          i.    Demurrer, First Cause of Action, Breach of Contract

After review, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the first cause of action, with leave to amend.

Plaintiff’s first cause of action alleges a Breach of Contract claim against CAIC.

“A contract is a voluntary and lawful agreement, by competent parties, for a good consideration, to do or not to do a specified thing.” (Robinson v. Magee (1858) 9 Cal. 81, 83.)

“To prevail on a cause of action for breach of contract, the plaintiff must prove (1) the contract, (2) the plaintiff’s performance of the contract or excuse for nonperformance, (3) the defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damage to the plaintiff.” (Richman v. Hartley (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1182, 1186.) Implicit in the element of damage is that the defendant’s breach caused the plaintiff’s damage.” (Troyk v. Farmers Group, Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1305, 1352, italics omitted.)

“‘Contracts may be express or implied. These terms, however, do not denote different kinds of contracts, but have reference to the evidence by which the agreement between the parties is shown. If the agreement is shown by the direct words of the parties, spoken or written, the contract is said to be an express one. But if such agreement can only be shown by the acts and conduct of the parties, interpreted in the light of the subject-matter and of the surrounding circumstances, then the contract is an implied one.’” (Marvin v. Marvin (1976) 18 Cal.3d 660, 678, fn. 16, citation omitted.)

A contract must be pled verbatim in the body of the complaint, be attached to the complaint and incorporated by reference, or be pled according to its legal effect. (Construction Protective Services, Inc. v. TIG Specialty Ins. Co. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 189, 198-99 [“In an action based on a written contract, a plaintiff may plead the legal effect of the contract rather than its precise language.”]; Bowden v. Robinson (1977) 67 Cal.App.3d 705, 718.) “In order to plead a contract by its legal effect, plaintiff must ‘allege the substance of its relevant terms.’” (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 993.)

Here, Plaintiff’s first cause of action alleges that CAIC breached the Policy between the parties by (1) proposing a repair that could not restore the roof to its pre-loss condition, (2) failing to replace all items in the damaged area to achieve a reasonably uniform appearance, (3) relying on discontinued shingles that could not match the existing roof, (4) creating structural and integration problems through partial replacement, and (5) limiting its inspection to visible shingle damage without assessing system-wide wind impact. (See FAC, ¶¶ 53-53(e).)

In relevant part, CAIC argues that the demurrer to the FAC’s breach of contract claim should be sustained based on the failure to verbatim allege the relevant terms of the Policy, to attach the Policy to the FAC, or to allege the substance of the relevant terms of the Policy.

The Court agrees.

As argued by CAIC, a review of the FAC fails to show any verbatim quotations of the Policy, let alone terms breached, and moreover fails to show attachment of a copy of the Policy.

This leaves pleading by legal effect, which requires allegations providing the substance of the relevant terms of the breached clauses. However, here, the breaches of contract alleged in the FAC at paragraph 53 are vague as to the substance of the relevant terms supporting the breach (¶ 53(a) and ¶¶ 53(c)-(e)) or do not provide sufficient information about the substance of the Policy’s terms as to sufficiently allege automatic incorporation of the California Code of Regulations into the Policy (¶ 53(b)). (Cf. Holcomb, supra, 155 Cal.App.4th at pp. 500-501 [“The [allegations supporting the breach of contract claim] do[] not describe the contractual standards to be applied to the bank’s actions nor how these standards might vary from those imposed by the [California Uniform Commercial Code],” where the Uniform Commercial Code allows a bank and its customers to alter the terms by agreement, within certain limits”].)

 Based on the above reasoning, the Court sustains the demurrer as to the FAC’s first cause of action, with leave to amend.

                                         ii.    Demurrer, Second Cause of Action, Breach of Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

As a matter of law, without a cause of action for breach of contract, there is no cause of action for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. (Love v. Fire Ins. Exchange (1990) 221 Cal.App.3d 1136, 1151 & fn 10.)

Here, having sustained CAIC’s demurrer as to the first cause of action, the Court likewise SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the FAC’s second cause of action, with leave to amend.

                                        iii.    Demurrer, Third Cause of Action, Violations of Business and Professions Code, § 17200, et seq.

As with the second cause of action, the third cause of action for violations of the Business and Professions Code is derivative of the first cause of action, for which reason the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the FAC’s third cause of action, with leave to amend.

d.     Conclusion

Defendant California Automobile Insurance Company’s demurrer is SUSTAINED, with leave to amend.

 

  1. Motion to Strike
    1. Meet and Confer

The moving papers establish that a sufficient meet and confer process preceded the filing of CAIC’s motion to strike. (Motion, Duque Decl., ¶¶ 3-12, Exs. A-C; see Code Civ. Proc., § 435.5, subd. (a)(3)(A).)

b.     Legal Standard

The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (a) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (b) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc. § 436, subds. (a), (b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].)

For the purposes of a motion to strike pursuant to Sections 435 to 437 of the Code of Civil Procedure, the term “pleading” generally means a demurrer, answer, complaint, or cross-complaint, (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (a)), and an immaterial allegation or irrelevant matter in a pleading entails (1) an allegation that is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense, (2) an allegation that is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense, or (3) a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint or cross-complaint (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subds. (b)(1)-(3), (c)).

    1. Discussion

                                          i.    Motion to Strike, Allegations Beyond June 4, 2026, Order

The Court DENIES CAIC’s motion insofar as it seeks to strike allegations in the FAC relating to how, because the roof is an integrated system, damages to a portion of the roof can be extended to the whole roof.

Specifically, CAIC argues that such allegations extend beyond the Court’s June 4, 2026, Order relating to the scope of leave to amend.

The Court finds this position unavailing.

First, the Court’s discussion as to the breach of contract claim did not frame leave to amend as confined to California Code of Regulations section 2695.9, subdivision (a)(2), even if the Court’s reasoning relied on interpretations of that subdivision. (See June 4, 2026, Order, pp. 5-6.)

Second, arguendo, even if the Court accepted CAIC’s reading of the June 4th Order, and the FAC contains allegations beyond the leave of the court permitted on June 4th, the amendments would be permissible insofar as they “directly respond” to the trial court’s reasoning in sustaining CAIC’s demurrer to the Breach of Contract claim as alleged in Plaintiff’s original Complaint. (See, e.g., Patrick v. Alacer Corp. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 995, 1015 [amendment of derivative action against corporation that failed on demurrer to declaratory relief claim in new pleading permitted where demurrer was sustained based on issues related to standing properly raised in the declaratory relief claim].)

                                         ii.    Motion to Strike, Punitive Damages

The Court having sustained CAIC’s demurrer to all three causes of action, the Court GRANTS CAIC’s motion to strike allegations and prayers of punitive damages, which depend on Plaintiff’s claims for support.

                                        iii.    Motion to Strike, Damages Disguised as Restitution

The Court DENIES CAIC’s motion as relates to purported damages disguised as restitution within the third cause of action.

It is true, insofar as it goes, that the remedies available to private individuals for violation of the Unfair Competition Law codified at Business and Professions Code section 17200, et seq., are limited to restitution and injunctive relief, such that damages cannot be recovered. (Graham v. Bank of America, N.A. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 594, 609 (“Graham”), citations omitted.)

However, here, the motion to strike seeks to characterize the following allegation in paragraph 71 of the FAC as damages disguised as restitution: “restitution of all insurance premiums paid to CAIC for coverage that was not adequately honored, and restitution of all amounts wrongfully withheld by CAIC that are necessary to restore Plaintiff to the position he occupied before CAIC’s unlawful, unfair, and deceptive conduct.” (Motion, pp. 18-19.)

However, the Court reads the same allegations as disgorgement of profits to CAIC during the time that CAIC did not provide benefits due to Plaintiff under the Policy, thus disfavoring relief.

                                        iv.    Motion to Strike, Prejudgment Interest

Last, the Court DENIES CAIC’s motion to strike allegations related to prejudgment interest specifically as connected to the second cause of action.

While, again, remedies available under the UCL are limited to equitable relief and not money damages (Graham, supra, 226 Cal.App.4th at p. 609), California courts have held that a trial court has the discretion in equity to award prejudgment interest as a component of restitution (Espejo v. The Copley Press, Inc. (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 329, 375; M&F Fishing, Inc. v. Sea-Pac Ins. Managers, Inc. (2012) 202 Cal.App.4th 1509, 1539) and have moreover found that a trial court erred in failing to consider its equitable powers to award prejudgment interest as a component of restitution (see, e.g., Tufeld Corp. v. Beverly Hills Gateway, L.P. (2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 12, 33, citing Espejo and M&F Fishing).

d.     Conclusion

Defendant California Automobile Insurance Company’s motion to strike is DENIED in part and GRANTED in part as follows:

(1) DENIED as to striking allegations in the FAC purportedly beyond the authority provided in the Court’s June 4, 2026, Order, allegations and/or prayers related to damages purportedly disguised as restitution vis-à-vis the second cause of action, and to allegations and/or prayers related to prejudgment interests vis-à-vis the second cause of action; and

(2) GRANTED, with leave to amend, as to allegations and/or prayers of punitive damages, as noticed in the moving papers. (See Motion, 2:23-3:22.)
