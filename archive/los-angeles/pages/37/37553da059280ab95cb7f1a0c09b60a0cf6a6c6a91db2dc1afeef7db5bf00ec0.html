DEPARTMENT 400 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV33648    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO COMPEL

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Ramon Rincon v. General Motors, LLC, Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 24STCV33648, set for hearing on July 27, 2026. Ramon Rincon (Plaintiff) moves for an order compelling General Motors, LLC (Defendant) to produce its person most knowledgeable (PMK) for deposition and produce the records identified in the deposition notice. Defendant opposes the motion. Plaintiff’s motion is denied.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

An oral deposition must be scheduled for at least 10 days after the service of the deposition notice. (Id., § 2025.270, subd. (a).) “If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action . . . without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document . . . the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document . . . described in the deposition notice.” (Id., 2025.450, subd. (a).) The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration or declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about their nonappearance. (Id., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Plaintiff initially served a notice of deposition on Defendant’s PMK along with a request for production on March 7, 2025, setting a deposition date of March 19 and providing an invitation to accommodate Defendant regarding the deposition date. (Barry Decl. ¶ 4.) On March 12, Defendant served objections to the deposition notice. (Id. ¶ 6.) Throughout April and May 2025, the parties met and conferred regarding Defendant’s objections but failed to secure a different date for deposition. (Id. ¶¶ 7–11.)

 

A motion to compel deposition pursuant to section 2025.450 “shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).) A meet and confer declaration “shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Id., § 2016.040.) Here, Plaintiff has not demonstrated sufficient meet and confer efforts. The last communication cited between the parties was from over a year ago in May 2025 and did not mention the possibility of a motion to compel being filed. The Court does not find that over a year of silence between the parties constitutes a good faith attempt at informal resolution. Thus, Plaintiff’s motion is improper. Accordingly, the motion is denied.

 

C.     Conclusion


The Court denies Plaintiff’s motion.

Case Number: 25STCV17509    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO COMPEL

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Cornell Fontaine McTier v. Robert Aguirre Casanova, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 25STCV17509, set for hearing on July 27, 2026. Spectrum Pacific West LLC (Defendant) moves for an order compelling Cornell Fontaine McTier (Plaintiff) to present for deposition within 15 days. Plaintiff opposes the motion. Defendant’s motion is granted.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

An oral deposition must be scheduled for at least 10 days after the service of the deposition notice. (Id., § 2025.270, subd. (a).) “If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action . . . without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document . . . the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document . . . described in the deposition notice.” (Id., 2025.450, subd. (a).) The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration or declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about their nonappearance. (Id., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).)

 

B.     Discussion

 

As a threshold matter, Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s motion is procedurally defective for multiple reasons. First, Plaintiff asserts that Defendant’s motion is premised on the incorrect statute; however, Defendant’s notice of motion properly references Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.450, not solely section 2032.310 like Plaintiff suggests. Thus, the notice properly appraises Plaintiff of the contents of the motion as required under section 1010.

 

Second, Plaintiff argues that section 2025.450 is inapplicable because Plaintiff never failed to appear for a deposition. Indeed, motions to compel pursuant to that section apply if a party fails to appear for a deposition without having served a valid objection. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (a).) Here, Defendant noticed Plaintiff’s deposition unilaterally on April 16, 2026. (Gezukarayan Decl. ¶ 2.) An amended notice set the deposition for May 28, 2026. (Ibid.) Plaintiff canceled this deposition 2 days before it was set to occur, and it was taken off calendar. This did not constitute a proper objection under section 2025.410 as the objection was lodged less than three days before the date of deposition. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.410, subd. (a).) Thus, Plaintiff failed to appear for deposition without lodging a proper objection and section 2025.450 is the proper section to invoke.

 

Third, Plaintiff argues that Defendant failed to meet and confer in good faith. However, Defendant properly requested on June 4, 2026, that Plaintiff provide alternative dates for deposition within two weeks. (Morris Decl. ¶ 4.) Plaintiff never responded to this communication. (Gezukarayan Decl. ¶ 6.) Thus, Defendant’s meet and confer efforts were sufficient.

 

Fourth, Plaintiff argues that the motion is moot because Plaintiff offered Defendant potential deposition dates preceding the hearing. However, those dates — July 14, 20, 22, 23, and 24 — were offered on June 30, 2026, twelve days after the present motion was filed. No deposition has occurred in advance of this hearing.

 

Plaintiff offers no further substantive argument in opposition. Plaintiff failed to appear for deposition without serving a valid objection and did not timely respond to Defendant’s efforts at informal resolution. Thus, the present motion to compel deposition attendance is proper. Accordingly, Defendant’s motion is granted.

 

If a motion pursuant to section 2025.450 is granted “the court shall impose a monetary sanction . . . in favor of the party who noticed the deposition and against the deponent . . . unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (g)(1).) Plaintiff’s arguments were meritless and the Court does not find that he has acted with substantial justification. Thus, sanctions are appropriate. The Court credits Defendant’s counsel’s hourly rate of $350. (Morris Decl. ¶ 7.) Given the present motion’s simplicity and the lack of any reply brief from Defendant, the Court only credits counsel with 2 hours for drafting the motion and declaration and 1 hour for attending the hearing on this matter. (Ibid.) Additionally, the Court credits the $60 filing fee for the present motion. (Ibid.) Accordingly, $1,110 are imposed on Plaintiff’s counsel ([$350 per hour] x [3 hours] + [$60 filing fee] = $1,110).

 

C.     Conclusion


The Court grants Defendant’s motion and imposes $1,110 in monetary sanctions on Plaintiff’s counsel.

Case Number: 26STCV16707    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO SEAL

 

The Court tenders the following tentative ruling in the matter Mohammed Shaikh v. Quinn Emanuel Urquhart & Sullivan, LLP, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 26STCV16707, set for hearing on July 27, 2026. Mohammad Shaikh (Plaintiff) moves for an order to file his opposition to Quinn Emanuel Urquhart & Sullivan, et al.’s (collectively, Defendants) motion to compel arbitration and certain supporting documents under seal. The motion is unopposed. The motion is granted.

 

A.    Legal Standard


Pursuant to California Rule of Court, rule 2.550(d), the court may order that a record be sealed if it finds facts that establish the following: (1) There exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) The overriding interest supports sealing the record; (3) A substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and (5) No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest.

A “reasoned decision about sealing or unsealing records cannot be made without . . . (1) identifying the specific information claimed to be entitled to such treatment; (2) identifying the nature of the harm threatened by disclosure; and (3) identifying and accounting for countervailing considerations. The burden of presenting information sufficient to accomplish the first two steps is logically placed on the party seeking the sealing of the documents, who is presumptively in the best position to know what disclosures will harm and how.” (H.B. Fuller Co. v. Doe (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 879, 894.) The burden of showing there are less restrictive means of achieving the overriding interest lies with one who is seeking disclosure of the allegedly confidential information.  (NBC Subsidiary (KNBC-TV), Inc. v. Superior Court (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1178, 1224.) 

 

B.     Discussion


Plaintiff has met all the requirements for sealing under rule 2.550(d). First, Plaintiff demonstrates that there is an overriding interest in sealing the relevant records because of the existence of a confidentiality agreement between the parties. Second, he argues that this interest supports sealing the records because Defendants would suffer financial hardship if they were released. Third, Plaintiff argues that Defendant will suffer prejudice if the opposition brief remains unsealed. Fourth, Plaintiff contends that the proposed sealing is narrowly tailored, and Defendants have not offered any alternative means of achieving the same result. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion is granted.

 

Conclusion:


            The Court grants the motion.
