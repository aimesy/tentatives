 IV.          Legal Standard

Standard for Motion for Summary Judgment – Summary judgment is proper “if all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Code Civ. Proc., §437c, subd. (c).) The moving party bears the initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.) A defendant moving for summary judgment must show either (1) that one or more elements of the cause of action cannot be established or (2) that there is a complete defense to that cause of action. (Id at §437c, subd. (p).) A defendant may discharge this burden by furnishing either (1) affirmative evidence of the required facts or (2) discovery responses conceding that the plaintiff lacks evidence to establish an essential element of the plaintiff's case. If a defendant chooses the latter option, he or she must present evidence “and not simply point out that plaintiff does not possess and cannot reasonably obtain needed evidence.” (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at 865-66.)

Under the latter approach, a defendant's initial evidentiary showing may ‘consist of the deposition testimony of the plaintiff's witnesses, the plaintiff's factually devoid discovery responses, or admissions by the plaintiff in deposition or in response to requests for admission that he or she has not discovered anything that supports an essential element of the cause of action.’ [Citation.] In other words, a defendant may show the plaintiff does not possess evidence to support an element of the cause of action by means of presenting the plaintiff's factually devoid discovery responses from which an absence of evidence may be reasonably inferred. [Citation.]

(Leyva v. Garcia (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1095, 1103 (internal citations omitted).)

Until the moving defendant has discharged its burden of proof, the opposing plaintiff has no burden to come forward with any evidence. Once the moving defendant has discharged its burden as to a particular cause of action, however, the plaintiff may defeat the motion by producing evidence showing that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(p)(2).) On a motion for summary judgment, the moving party's supporting documents are strictly construed and those of his opponent liberally construed, and doubts as to the propriety of summary judgment should be resolved against granting the motion.  (D’Amico v. Board of Medical Examiners (1974) 11 Cal.3d 1, 21.)

The court must grant a motion for summary judgment if, after all admissible evidence is considered, there is no triable issue as to any material fact. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(c).) The court has no discretion to deny a summary judgment motion where the moving party has met their burden and the opposing party has failed to establish any triable issue of material fact exists, and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (Krasley v. Superior Court (1980) 101 Cal.App.4th 59, 70.)

 

A party may also move for summary adjudication as to one or more causes of action within an action, one or more claims for damages, or one or more issues of duty if the party contends the cause of action has no merit, that there is no merit to a claim for damages, or that one or more defendants did not owe a duty to the plaintiff. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).) A motion for summary adjudication may be made by itself or as an alternative to a motion for summary judgment, shall proceed in all procedural respects as a motion for summary judgment, and shall only be granted if it completely disposes of a cause of action, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty. (Id at § 437c, subd. (f)(1)-(2).)

    V.          Discussion

 

Application – Rivas moves for summary judgment, or in the alternative summary adjudication. The causes of action asserted against Rivas are: (1) Professional Medical Negligence (Medical Malpractice); (2) Willful Misconduct; (4) Fraudulent Concealment; (5) Lack of Informed Consent; and (8) Negligent Hiring, Training, Supervision, and Retention. 

 

a.     Rivas’ Immunity From Suit and Liability Pursuant to the PREP Act

 

                                                  i.     PREP Act

 

The Public Readiness and Emergency Preparedness Act, known as the PREP Act, provides that "a covered person shall be immune from suit and liability under Federal and State law with respect to all claims for loss caused by, arising out of, relating to, or resulting from the administration to or the use by an individual of a covered countermeasure." (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d, subd. (a)(1).) PREP Act immunity applies to any claim "that has a causal relationship with the administration to or use by an individual of a covered countermeasure, including a causal relationship with the design, development, clinical testing or investigation, manufacture, labeling, distribution, formulation, packaging, marketing, promotion, sale, purchase, donation, dispensing, prescribing, administration, licensing, or use of such countermeasure." (42 U.S.C., § 247d-6d, subd. (a)(2).)

 

The PREP Act is invoked when "the [HHS] Secretary makes a determination that a disease or other health condition or other threat to health constitutes a public health emergency, or that there is a credible risk that the disease, condition, or threat may in the future constitute such an emergency.” (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d, subd. (b)(1).) The Secretary "controls the scope of immunity through the declaration and amendments, within the confines of the PREP Act." (Estate of Maglioli v. Alliance HC Holdings LLC (2021) 16 F.4th 393, 401.) The Secretary's declaration "may specify[ ] the manufacture, testing, development, distribution, administration, or use of one or more covered countermeasures." (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d, subd. (b)(1).) The PREP Act created the Covered Countermeasure Process Fund to compensate "eligible individuals for covered injuries directly caused by the administration or use of a covered countermeasure pursuant to such declaration." (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6e, subd. (a).)

 

The PREP Act provides that "the sole exception to the immunity from suit and liability of covered persons ... shall be for an exclusive Federal cause of action against a covered person for death or serious physical injury proximately caused by willful misconduct ... by such covered person." (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d, subd. (d)(1).) Such an action "shall be filed and maintained only in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia." (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d, subd. (e)(1).) The term "willful misconduct" is defined as an act or omission that is taken to achieve a wrongful purpose, knowingly without justification, and in disregard of a known or obvious risk that is so great as to make it highly probable that the harm will outweigh the benefit. (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d, subd. (c)(1)(A)(i)-(iii).) The “willful misconduct” standard shall be construed as establishing a standard for liability that is more stringent than a standard of negligence in any form or recklessness. (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d, subd. (c)(1)(B). An individual may not bring a suit under 42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d, subd. (d)(1) unless the individual has exhausted the remedies available under § 247d-6e, subd. (a), the Covered Countermeasure Process Fund. (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6e, subd. (d)(1).)

 

                                                ii.     COVID-19

 

On March 10, 2020, the Secretary issued a declaration under the PREP Act "to provide liability immunity for activities related to medical countermeasures against COVID-19." (Rivas RJN, Ex. A; Declaration Under the Public Readiness and Emergency Preparedness Act for Medical Countermeasures Against COVID-19, 85 Fed. Reg. 15,198, 15,198 (Mar. 17, 2020).) The declaration provided immunity for covered persons for the use of covered countermeasures.

 

Covered countermeasures include "any antiviral, any other drug, any biologic, any diagnostic, any other device, or any vaccine, used to treat, diagnose, cure, prevent, or mitigate COVID-19… or any device used in the administration of any such product." (Id. at 15,202.) When used in respect to the administration of a covered countermeasure, “covered person” includes any person or entity that is a distributor of such countermeasure or a qualified person who administered or dispensed such countermeasure and any employee thereof. (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d, subd. (i)(2)(B)(ii)-(v).) A “distributor” means a person or entity engaged in the distribution of drugs, biologics, or devices, including retail pharmacies. (42 U.S.C., § 247d-6d, subd. (i)(3).) “A qualified person means a licensed health professional or other individual authorized to prescribe, administer, or dispense Covered Countermeasures under the law of the state in which the Covered Countermeasure was prescribed, administered, or dispensed; or a person within a category of persons identified as qualified in the Secretary’s Declaration.” (Rivas RJN, Ex. A, 15,199.)

 

The March 10, 2020 Secretary’s Declaration under the PREP Act discusses administration of covered countermeasures as follows:

 

The PREP Act does not explicitly define the term ‘‘administration’’ but does assign the Secretary the responsibility to provide relevant conditions in the Declaration. In Section IX of the Declaration, the Secretary defines ‘‘Administration of a Covered Countermeasure,’’ as follows:

 

Administration of a Covered Countermeasure means physical provision of the countermeasures to recipients, or activities and decisions directly relating to public and private delivery, distribution, and dispensing of the countermeasures to recipients; management and operation of countermeasure programs; or management and operation of locations for purpose of distributing and dispensing countermeasures.

 

The definition of ‘‘administration’’ extends only to physical provision of a countermeasure to a recipient, such as vaccination or handing drugs to patients, and to activities related to management and operation of programs and locations for providing countermeasures to recipients, such as decisions and actions involving security and queuing, but only insofar as those activities directly relate to the countermeasure activities. Claims for which Covered Persons are provided immunity under the Act are losses caused by, arising out of, relating to, or resulting from the administration to or use by an individual of a Covered Countermeasure consistent with the terms of a Declaration issued under the Act. Under the definition, these liability claims are precluded if they allege an injury caused by a countermeasure, or if the claims are due to manufacture, delivery, distribution, dispensing, or management and operation of countermeasure programs at distribution and dispensing sites.

 

Thus, it is the Secretary’s interpretation that, when a Declaration is in effect, the Act precludes, for example, liability claims alleging negligence by a manufacturer in creating a vaccine, or negligence by a health care provider in prescribing the wrong dose, absent willful misconduct. Likewise, the Act precludes a liability claim relating to the management and operation of a countermeasure distribution program or site, such as a slip-and-fall injury or vehicle collision by a recipient receiving a countermeasure at a retail store serving as an administration or dispensing location that alleges, for example, lax security or chaotic crowd control. However, a liability claim alleging an injury occurring at the site that was not directly related to the countermeasure activities is not covered, such as a slip and fall with no direct connection to the countermeasure’s administration or use. In each case, whether immunity is applicable will depend on the particular facts and circumstances.

 

(Rivas RJN, Ex. A; Declaration Under the Public Readiness and Emergency Preparedness Act for Medical Countermeasures Against COVID-19, 85 Fed. Reg. 15,198, 15,200 (Mar. 17, 2020).)

 

                                              iii.     Rivas’ Burden

 

Rivas moves for summary judgment on the grounds that he is immune from suit and liability under the PREP Act. To establish immunity, Rivas must establish that he was acting as covered person, engaged in the administration of a covered countermeasure, and that Plaintiff’s injury was caused by, arose out of, related to, or resulted from said administration.

 

It is undisputed that pursuant to the statutory framework set forth, administration and distribution of COVID-19 vaccines are designated as “covered countermeasures” under the PREP Act. (Plaintiff’s UMF (“PSUMF”) No. 2.) Plaintiff presented to the CVS on April 7, 2021 for the purpose and intent of receiving a COVID-19 vaccine. (PSUMF, No. 10.)

 

As to Rivas, however, the parties dispute two key issues. First, Plaintiff disputes that Rivas is a “covered person” under the PREP Act. Second, the parties dispute whether the first insertion of a needle during the vaccination process that did not contain or administer any dose of the COVID-19 vaccine and Plaintiff’s harm stemming therefrom is causally connected to the administration of a covered countermeasure of administering the vaccine, where Plaintiff alleges the needle contained hazardous substance or was previously used. Defendant argues the injection is expressly covered by the PREP Act, as the first needle was injected and intended for administration of the vaccine, but was by human error not pre-dosed with the vaccination, while Plaintiff argues the first injection “had nothing to do with a Covid 19 vaccine.” (UMF, No. 11.)

 

1.     Rivas is a Covered Person Under the PREP Act

 

Turning to the first disputed fact regarding Rivas’ PREP immunity, Rivas argues he is a covered person under the PREP Act because at all relevant times, Rivas was acting as the store manager for CVS in connection with vaccine preparation and administration. (Motion, p. 5:9-12, 7:1-4.)

 

To reiterate, a “covered person” includes any person or entity that is a distributor, manufacturer, or program planner of such countermeasure, a qualified person who administered or dispensed such countermeasure, or any official, agent, or employee of a covered person or entity described. (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d, subd. (i)(2)(B)(ii)-(iv); See also Rivas RJN, Ex. A, 15,201, section V.) A “distributor” means a person or entity engaged in the distribution of drugs, biologics, or devices, including retail pharmacies. (42 U.S.C., § 247d-6d, subd. (i)(3).) “A qualified person means a licensed health professional or other individual authorized to prescribe, administer, or dispense Covered Countermeasures under the law of the state in which the Covered Countermeasure was prescribed, administered, or dispensed; or a person within a category of persons identified as qualified in the Secretary’s Declaration.” (Rivas RJN, Ex. A, 15,199.)

 

It is undisputed is that Defendants CVS and CVS Health were authorized to administer the COVID-19 vaccine and constitute “covered persons” for the purposes of the PREP Act. (Plaintiff’s UMF No. 6.) Rivas argues that he is not a licensed or certified healthcare professional, and therefore, concedes he is not a qualified person for purposes of the PREP Act. (Motion, p. 9:9-25; Rivas Decl., ¶¶ 5-15.) However, Rivas declares that in April 2021, he was employed as the General Manager of the CVS store located at 2006 West Avenue J, Lancaster, CA 93536 where the incident occurred, and that he primarily worked in the front retail portion of the store, overseeing front-store operations and employees who worked in the area. (Rivas Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.)

 

Rivas also provides the Declaration of Sneha Patel, a District Leader for CVS. (Patel Decl., ¶¶ 1-2.) Patel declares that her responsibilities included overseeing the Palmdale CVS location where Plaintiff received her vaccine and where Rivas and Ifejika were employed in April of 2021. (Patel Decl., ¶ 3.) Patel asserts that prior to April 7, 2021, she was unaware of any complaints regarding Rivas’ mishandling of customer complaints or performance as store manager relating to Covid-19 issues, policies, or procedures, and did not receive, review, or become aware of any reports, documentation, or communications to suggest that Mr. Rivas engaged in conduct that would make him unfit for his role as store manager. (Patel Decl., ¶¶ 5-7.)

 

Rivas provides portions of Plaintiff’s deposition testimony from October 23, 2025. Plaintiff testified to the following relevant facts: (1) after the subject incident, Plaintiff returned to the CVS and requested to speak to a manager, and an employee informed her a manager was unavailable and wrote down the name of the manager and provided it to Plaintiff (Ex. 8, p. 27:3-9); (2) Plaintiff subsequently returned to the CVS and requested to speak to a manager, at which point she came into contact with Rivas (Ex. 8, p. 27:10-15); and (3) Rivas, described by Plaintiff as “the manager,” brought Plaintiff to an office and placed a call to make a report regarding the incident (Ex. 8, p. 27:21-28:2). Plaintiff again testified on February 27, 2026 to the following relevant facts: (1) Plaintiff is suing the technician, Ifejika, the store manager, Rivas, and the company, CVS (Ex. 12, p. 122:10-13), and (2) at no point prior to, during, or after the incident did Plaintiff know whether Rivas was a medical professional (Ex. 12, p. 122:14-19).

 

Finally, the Court notes that Plaintiff’s SAC defines Rivas as an “employee, manager, supervisor, agent of Defendants CVS HEALTH and CVS PHARMACY, and DOES 3 through 100, and manager, employer and/or supervisor of Defendant ILEKA IFEJIKA.” (SAC, ¶ 4.) The SAC states that “Defendant CVS HEALTH…was at all times relevant to the incident herein…the employer of Defendant ANTINO RIVAS.” (SAC, ¶ 5.)

 

The evidence provided by Rivas is sufficient to meet his burden of making a prima facie showing that at all relevant times, Rivas was an employee of CVS. As CVS’s status as a covered entity is undisputed, Rivas is therefore a covered person under the PREP Act.

 

2.     Rivas Was Engaged in a Covered Countermeasure

 

Plaintiff’s allegations against Rivas specifically consist of the following: (1) Rivas disregarded and showed no concern for the first needle being injected into Plaintiff’s arm (SAC, ¶ 61(u)), and (2) Rivas failed to obtain Plaintiff’s consent prior to injection of either needle, disposed of the needles, failed to inform Plaintiff of what had happened or remedy the error, and was unfit and incompetent to perform his duties (SAC, ¶ 88.) Otherwise, the SAC alleges actions against CVS Defendants and Ifejika, all of which are entirely and exclusively related to injections of the first and second syringes. (See SAC generally.)

 

As a covered person, Rivas is only immune under the PREP Act under certain circumstances. As stated, covered countermeasures include any vaccine or device used to treat, diagnose, cure, prevent, or mitigate Covid-19, or any device used in the administration of such a product. (Rivas RJN, Ex. A, 15,202.) However, the scope of immunity is sweeping. As delineated in the Secretary’s Declaration, “[c]laims for which Covered Persons are provided immunity under the Act are losses caused by, arising out of, relating to, or resulting from the administration to or use by an individual of a Covered Countermeasure ….these liability claims are precluded if they allege an injury caused by a countermeasure, or if the claims are due to manufacture, delivery, distribution, dispensing, or management and operation of countermeasure programs at distribution and dispensing sites….the Act precludes a liability claim relating to the management and operation of a countermeasure distribution program or site.” (Rivas RJN, Ex. A, 15,200.)

 

Returning to Rivas’ Declaration, he states his first encounter with Plaintiff occurred on April 7, 2021, when she returned to the CVS location and expressed concerns over her vaccination encounter. (Rivas Decl., ¶ 17.) Rivas declares he assisted Plaintiff by documenting her complaint and completing an incident report so the matter could be reviewed by CVS Risk Management, and that his role in the incident was limited to documenting the customer complaint and following appropriate internal reporting procedures. (Rivas Decl., ¶ 18.)

 

As discussed above, Plaintiff testified in her deposition that she returned to the CVS for the sole purpose of speaking with a manager regarding her COVID-19 vaccination appointment, and that the scope of her meeting with Rivas was limited to reporting the interaction and injection of the syringes by Ifejika to an individual in a management capacity. (Ex. 8, p. 27:13- 28:2.)

 

Rivas has sufficiently established that at all relevant times, he was acting in the scope of his employment as a CVS store manager, that his role was limited to reporting Plaintiff’s incident complaint related to CVS’s administration of the COVID-19 vaccine, and of the few allegations pertaining to Rivas alleged in the SAC, all are specifically related to either Rivas’ operation, management, internal reporting, and handling of Plaintiff’s complaints regarding administration of the COVID-19 vaccine, or to his allegedly concealing evidence of the first injection. Accordingly, at all times relevant to this action, Rivas was engaged in conduct related to the management and operation of the specific CVS location for the purpose of distributing and dispensing countermeasures, and was therefore engaged in a covered countermeasure.

 

3.     Plaintiff’s Injury Related to Administration of a Covered Countermeasure

 

Rivas contends that Plaintiff’s claims against him arise from or relate to the administration of a covered countermeasure. Rivas submits the Declaration of Defendant Ifejika in support. Ifejika declares that he was employed as a COVID-19 Travel Vaccine Pharmacist for Defendants beginning in January of 2021 to assist in vaccination clinics during the pandemic. (Ifejika Decl., ¶ 2.) He states that during the relevant period in April of 2021, the customary protocol for administering COVID-19 vaccines involved one pharmacist administering vaccines and one nurse assisting by pre-drawing doses from multi-dose vials into single-use, retractable syringes for efficient and safe administration. (Id at ¶ 4.) Ifejika also provides that both of the syringes used were VanishPoint Retractable syringes with an automated retraction technology used for the administration of the COVID-19 vaccine, which are single-use and designed such that once the plunger is depressed after medication delivery, the needle automatically retracts into the barrel, rendering it unusable and eliminating the possibility of reuse or accidental needle sticks. (Id at ¶ 5.) He states that if the plunger is already fully depressed before insertion and the needle has not been retracted, the syringe contains no medication and cannot inject any substance into a patient. (Ibid; Defendants’ Ex. 10.)

 

On April 7, 2021, Ifejika, based on his prior experience at that vaccination location, assumed the nurse on staff had pre-batched and pre-filled the syringes in preparation for the vaccination line, and when Plaintiff arrived for her appointment, he selected what he believed as a pre-filled VanishPoint syringe from the prepared batch, but noticed for the first time upon insertion that the plunger was fully depressed and no liquid was present. (Id at ¶¶ 6-9.) Ifejika states that no air or any other substance could have been delivered

into Plaintiff's arm as the plunger was fully depressed. (Id. at ¶ 10.) He further states that

the syringe could not have previously been used as the needle had not retracted. (Id.)

 

Ifejika states he then removed the empty syringe, properly discarded it in a Sharps container per standard protocol, cleaned Plaintiff’s arm, and retrieved a new, pre-filled VanishPoint syringe containing the COVID-19 vaccine and administered the vaccine to Plaintiff. (Id at ¶ 11.) Ifejika further declares that the single-use retractable VanishPoint syringes used to administer the vaccine eliminate the possibility of cross-contamination or reuse of a needle, and that to a reasonable degree of medical probability, there was no circumstance under which Plaintiff could have been injected with a used needle or needle containing any substance at all, including air. (Id at ¶ 17.)

 

Rivas also provides the Declaration of Dr. Kimberly Moore, a retained expert and licensed registered pharmacist with extensive experience administering the COVID-19 vaccine in retail pharmacy settings. (Moore Decl., ¶¶ 1, 4; Defendants’ Exhibit 11.) Moore declares that based on her experience, in early 2021, COVID-19 vaccines were only available in multi-use vials that required an individual dose to be drawn into a single-use syringe to then be injected into the patient’s deltoid muscle. (Moore Decl., ¶¶ 8, (d)-(e).) Moore states that an empty-needle insertion during a high-volume administration setting of a vaccine, such as the administration of the COVID-19 vaccine during the pandemic, is a recognized occurrence, which may occur due to syringe malfunction, mechanical defect, plunger positioning, or human factors, even where appropriate care is exercised, and that empty needle insertion is not a breach of the standard of care, but rather, is a known risk inherent in the vaccination process during the pandemic. (Moore Decl., ¶ 8 (f), (g).) Moore further contends that if a pharmacist reasonably believes an empty-needle insertion occurred and the vaccine dose was not delivered, the appropriate and acceptable standard of care is to administer a replacement dose to ensure the patient receives the intended dose as such action is medically appropriate, reasonable, and consistent with CDC guidance and pharmacy vaccination protocols. (Moore Decl., ¶ 8 (h), (i).) Moore also states that failing to administer a second injection when the pharmacist believes the initial injection did not deliver the vaccine would fall below the standard of care. (Moore Decl., ¶ 8 (j).)

 

As to the case at hand, Moore states that Plaintiff presented to CVS and consented to receive the COVID-19 vaccine on April 7, 2021, the needles used on Plaintiff were the VanishPoint syringes with automated retraction technology, and that to a reasonable degree of medical probability, on review of the video evidence of Plaintiff receiving the injection, the first injection was empty, and because the needle was not retracted, to a reasonable degree of medical probability, the first injection could not have been a used needle. (Moore Decl., ¶ 8 (k), (l).) Therefore, Moore states that to a reasonable degree of medical probability, the second injection administered contained the COVID-19 vaccine and ensured Plaintiff received the intended vaccination. (Moore Decl., ¶ 8 (n), (o).)

 

Rivas further submits the Declaration of Mark Richard Needham, M.D., M.B.A., a physician  with experience in vaccination procedures and the mechanisms, risk and medical evaluations of needlestick injuries, and adverse events related to the COVID-19 vaccine. (Needham Decl., ¶¶ 1-2, 6.) Needham states that after review of the records and video evidence of the vaccination encounter, the plunger of the first syringe used to administer the vaccine was fully depressed at the time of insertion, which from a medical and mechanical standpoint, indicates the syringe was empty, no medication or substance was injected, and that an empty, single-use syringe cannot transmit medication, infectious material, or contaminants. (Needham Decl., ¶ 11 (a)-(c).)

 

Rivas submits transcripts of Plaintiff’s depositions. Plaintiff testified to the following relevant facts:

 

Plaintiff presented to the CVS Premises for the purpose of receiving the COVID-19 vaccine after scheduling the vaccination appointment in advance. (Ex. 8, p. 20:25, 22:11-12.) Plaintiff scheduled an appointment to receive the COVID-19 vaccine and received confirmation from CVS of the appointment. (Ex. 8, p. 20:20-25.) Plaintiff arrived for the vaccine at approximately 8:50 a.m., 10 minutes before her 9:00 a.m. appointment, and waited approximately 15 minutes. (Ex. 8, p. 22:9-21.) Plaintiff was then escorted to the back of the pharmacy to the cubicles where the person in charge of doing the vaccine was located. (Ex. 8, p. 22:22-23:4.) Plaintiff asked another customer in line for vaccination to take a photograph of her receiving the injection, this individual asked Ifejika at the time of injection “two shots?”, and Plaintiff felt two needles inserted in her arm. (Ex. 8, p. 25:1-3, 30:5-25, 31:7-11.) Plaintiff did not see either the first or second shot being prepared, sealed, or prefilled, did not see whether the syringes appeared new or used, and did not see anything regarding the source or disposal of either needle. (Ex. 8, p. 31:7-33:21.) Plaintiff did not see what Ifejika did with the first needle after injection, disposal or otherwise. (Ex. 8, p. 38:2-16.) The second shot Plaintiff received was a COVID-19 vaccine, and Plaintiff states the reason she was there was to receive the COVID-19 vaccine. (Ex. 8, p. 40:23-12.) At her later deposition, Plaintiff stated she does not know whether the second syringe contained the COVID-19 vaccine. (Ex 12, p. 118:17-119:7.) Attached to Exhibit 8 and 12 are photographs of Plaintiff receiving both injections.

 

There is little published case law on PREP immunity. However, as to summary judgment motions specifically, non-binding, but persuasive decisions from various jurisdictions have established the need for the defendant to evidence a causal connection between a plaintiff’s loss and a covered countermeasure. (See Austbo v. Greenbriar Nursing Home No. Two, Inc. (2025) OK 85, 20, 23; Wilhelms v. ProMedica Health Sys., Inc. 2023- Ohio 143, 205 N.E.3d 1159, 1161 (Ohio Ct. App. 2023); Ashley v. Anonymous Physician 1, 245 N.E.3d 658, 2024 WL 4142508, at *1 (Ind. Ct. App. 2024).)

 

Here, the Court finds that Rivas’ evidentiary showing is sufficient to satisfy his burden of showing that the injection at issue was directly related to CVS’s administration of countermeasure activities, that any injury Plaintiff allegedly suffered as a result of the first injection was caused in the process and for the purpose of administration of the COVID-19 vaccine, and that her allegations against Rivas are exclusively related to his capacity as manager of the approved countermeasure administration location. The covered countermeasure at issue is the COVID-19 vaccine. Plaintiff made an appointment to get the vaccine; she showed up for her appointment; she met with Ifejika to get the vaccine; and she in fact received a vaccination shot from Ifejika (as even she admitted at her first deposition). The PREP Act does not require that a covered countermeasure be successfully delivered by every needle insertion; it immunizes any loss “arising out of, relating to, or resulting from the administration” of the countermeasure, and the management and operation of a location administering countermeasures. (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d, subd. (a)(1).) Ifejika’s selection and use of a syringe he believed contained the vaccine, drawn from the batch designated for its administration, is an activity and decision directly relating to the delivery and dispensing of that vaccine. Plaintiff then met with Rivas after attempting numerous times to meet with a manager regarding the administration of the vaccine and reported her complaints. Any acts taken by Rivas were taken in his role as a manager in responding to COVID-19 vaccine-specific customer complaints, making hiring decisions for the staffing specifically of the vaccine administration program located and operated at the Lancaster CVS, general management and operation of the premises, decisions and policies regarding patient consent, and policies and practices of vaccine administration and disposal of syringes. All of these activities directly relating to the management and operation of an authorized countermeasure location.  

 

The plain language of the PREP Act is broad and sweeping in scope. As discussed, administration of a covered countermeasure includes not only the physical provision of a countermeasure to a recipient, such as the direct injection of the vaccine itself, but also includes activities related to management and operation of locations for providing countermeasures, including decisions and actions made, so long as they directly relate to the countermeasure activities.  (Rivas’ RJN, Ex. A, 15,200.)

 

In Maney v. Brown (2024) 91 F.4th 1296, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed that PREP Act immunity is not limited to the actual injection of the vaccine itself. (Maney, supra, 91 F.4th at 1301.) The Court reasoned:

 

The phrasing "administration to…an individual of a covered countermeasure" could refer only to the act of physically giving a countermeasure to a particular person—for example, injecting someone with a vaccine shot. However, in § 247d-6d(a)(2)(B), the Act provides that the scope of immunity under paragraph (1) includes various activities with a causal relationship to the administration of a countermeasure beyond injecting someone with a vaccine. Most significantly, subsection (a)(2)(B) lists several terms, including "administration," without reference to "an individual." This is consistent with the expansive causal relationship the subsection provides; for example, the "design, development," "manufacture," and "distribution" of a vaccine are multiple links removed in the chain of events from the ultimate injecting of an individual with a vaccine. By referring to "administration . . . of [a covered] countermeasure," in the context of a list that expands the conduct within the Act's scope of immunity, and without requiring a direct link to an individual, subsection (a)(2)(B) broadens the scope of immunity to administrative activities other than the physical act of directly injecting a particular person with a vaccine.

 

(Ibid at 1300-01 (emphasis added); See also Hampton v. California (9" Cir. 2023) 83 F.4th

754, 764 [“At the very least, then, for PREP Act immunity to apply, the underlying use or

administration of a covered countermeasure must have played some role in bringing about

or contributing to the plaintiff's injury. It is not enough that some countermeasure's use

could be described as relating to the events underpinning the claim in some broad sense.”].)

 

The Secretary’s interpretation of “administration of a protected countermeasure” for COVID-19 precludes liability even for dangerous conditions on a property, immunizing covered persons for incidents such as vehicle collisions and premises liability claims, such as slip-and-fall incidents, if there is a direct connection to the countermeasure’s administration, such as where an individual suffers such harm while being on the premises for a vaccine and the conditions were created by the operation of a countermeasure program. (Defendants’ RJN, Ex. A, 15,200.)

 

Here, Rivas has submitted competent evidence that the first injection was an activity and decision directly relating to the delivery, distribution, and dispensing of the countermeasures to Plaintiff. Therefore, Plaintiff’s allegations against Rivas as the manager of the location directly relate to management and operation of CVS’s countermeasures. First, Ifejika’s Declaration establishes that the first injection was given in error in the process of administering the COVID-19 vaccine based on his assumption, in accordance with standard vaccination practice at the location, that the syringe had been pre-filled. Second, Dr. Moore declares that empty shots which have not been prefilled were a recognized occurrence in administration of the COVID-19 vaccines in large-scale vaccination centers at the time Plaintiff received the injections. Third, Plaintiff’s deposition testimony corroborates a causal link between the first injection and Defendants’ administration of the vaccine as a licensed retail pharmacy. The sole purpose for her presence was to receive the vaccination as advertised by the pharmacy. Fourth, the circumstances set forth in the SAC and Rivas’ evidence support this finding. Both injections were given by Ifejika, who is a COVID-19 Travel Vaccine Pharmacist, whose role at CVS was to assist and administer the COVID-19 vaccines. Both injections were given successively in the same seat within the pharmacy designated for vaccine administration and in close temporal proximity, almost in immediate succession. The first shot was given during Plaintiff’s scheduled appointment to receive the vaccine and was given with the intent and belief that it contained the vaccine. The same type of syringe was used for both injections, which was the standard syringe used at the time for COVID-19 vaccine administration, and Ifejika declares the first syringe came from a batch of what he believed were pre-filled COVID-19 vaccine syringes in accordance with the standard administration practices of that specific CVS location. Finally, the SAC, Rivas’ Declaration, and Plaintiff’s testimony confirm that her allegations against Rivas, including that he disregarded her reported concerns, concealed evidence, failed to obtain Plaintiff’s consent, and was negligent in permitting Ifejika to remain employed by CVS all relate directly to the administration efforts at that particular CVS location, Ifejika’s injection of two syringes, and Rivas’ responsibility in managing administration efforts at the location.

 

The purpose of the PREP Act is to immunize and shield protected persons from negligence, improper administration, improper management and operation of a countermeasure location, and other tort liability in connection with administration of the COVID-19 vaccine. Rivas’ evidence has established that the first injection was an empty, unused, single-use syringe, that Ifejika mistakenly believed the syringe was pre-filled with the vaccine, inserted the needle without realizing the syringe did not contain the vaccine, and promptly thereafter disposed of the syringe and injected a second syringe containing the intended dose. Whether these facts give rise to negligence in the management or operation of the vaccination program premises or administration of the program generally is not relevant, as the PREP Act was expressly designed to preclude actions based thereon.

 

Therefore, Rivas has presented sufficient evidence to show that the first injection was an act in relation to the administration of a covered countermeasure and that Rivas’ alleged conduct is directly related to the management and operation of a countermeasure program. Rivas has established the requisite causal relationship between Plaintiff’s allegations against him and his protected conduct in managing a countermeasure program location. The burden shifts to Plaintiff to establish a triable question of fact exists as to whether the injury has a causal connection to the administration of the vaccine.

 

                                               iv.     Plaintiff’s Burden

 

Plaintiff argues Rivas is not a covered person under the PREP Act because he does not fit the definition of a “qualified person” under the PREP Act. (Opp., p. 16:23-17:6.) However, Rivas need not be a “qualified” person to be a “covered” person. A “covered person” includes both (1) a qualified person who administered or dispensed such countermeasure, and (2) any official, agent, or employee of a covered person or entity described, including distributors such as CVS. (42 U.S.C. § 247d-6d, subd. (i)(2)(B)(ii)-(iv); See also Rivas RJN, Ex. A, 15,201, section V.) Plaintiff’s SAC concedes that Rivas was an employee and manager of CVS at the time of the incident. Plaintiff relies on Rivas’ status as employee and manager throughout her responses to Rivas’ statement of undisputed material facts. (See, e.g., PSUMF, Nos. 16, 19-23.) Plaintiff’s deposition testimony and filings refer to Rivas exclusively as a manager or employee of CVS. Plaintiff argues Rivas was employed by CVS at all relevant times. (See PSUMF, p. 20, No. 3.) Plaintiff’s present motion also argues that Rivas acted in the capacity of an ostensible agent, which if true, would also render Rivas a covered person under the PREP Act as an agent of either CVS or Ifejika, both of whom are “covered persons.”

 

Plaintiff next argues that application of the PREP Act to these circumstances is too attenuated, as “administrative activities” as described by the Maney Court, discussed supra, cannot reasonably reach a blank injection of a syringe not containing the vaccine itself. (Opp., p. 12:12-26.) Plaintiff argues that the phrase “related to” must be viewed with a targeted meaning and based on a clear causal relationship establishing that the use or administration of a covered countermeasure played some role in bringing about or contributing to Plaintiff’s injury, not merely that the countermeasure’s use could be described as relating to the events underpinning the claim in a broad sense. (Opp., p. 13:2-12.) Plaintiff avers that if the Court were to find that Rivas is entitled to PREP Act immunity merely because Plaintiff eventually received the vaccine after being injected with an empty syringe, the holding would be akin to ruling that anything that could have caused Plaintiff injury the day of her COVID-19 injection therefore relates to the administration of a covered countermeasure. (Opp., p. 13:13-22.)

 

The problem with Plaintiff's argument lies in her definition of “administration.” As discussed above, “administration” encompasses actions, decisions, operations, and general management in relation to covered countermeasures. Plaintiff's premise, that because the first syringe was empty no covered countermeasure was involved, misapprehends the statute. The covered countermeasure is the vaccine Plaintiff came to receive, not the contents of any specific syringe. The immunity reaches any loss relating to or arising out of its administration. That an individual dose went undelivered no more removes the pharmacist’s conduct from the administration of the vaccine than would a mismeasured or wrong dose; indeed, the Secretary’s Declaration expressly identifies negligence by a health care provider in prescribing the wrong dose as conduct for which the Act precludes liability. (Defendants’ RJN, Ex. A; 85 Fed. Reg. at 15,200.)

 

In Plaintiff’s response to Rivas’ Statement of Undisputed Material Facts, Plaintiff does not expressly dispute that “[a]ll of Plaintiff’s alleged injuries arise from, relate to, or are connected with the administration and use of a COVID-19 vaccine during the COVID-19 public health emergency,” but merely disputes the appropriate legal standard. (See PSUMF, No. 22.)

 

In opposition to the motion, Plaintiff has provided no evidence refuting Rivas’ evidence that the syringe and needle used to administer the first shot was a VantagePoint syringe, that the first syringe was taken from a batch of syringes specifically designated for administration of the COVID-19 vaccine, or that any other injections or vaccinations were being given, to anyone, in the designated location on the designated date. Plaintiff has also submitted no evidence establishing that the first injection was not given in the process of COVID-19 vaccine administration or that there was no causal connection between Ifejika’s administration of the vaccine and the injection. The Declaration of Ruwanthi Campano, MD does not put forth any evidence that refutes Defendant’s showing that they were engaged in a covered countermeasure. Campano’s Declaration actually appears to substantiate this characterization. (Campano Decl., ¶ 9 [“This has nothing to do with the injection of a covid-19 vaccine. This has to do with the injection technique and the lack of informed consent and duty to relate an unforeseen occurrence in the administration of a vaccine by this pharmacist on April 7, 2021, that is what is being called into dispute.”].) Dr. Campano’s Declaration is silent as to Rivas. Further, Dr. Campano’s opinions on the alleged negligence of Ifejika in administering the two injections (id.) are largely immaterial. The point of Rivas’ PREP argument is not that he is blameless of negligence, but that the PREP Act immunizes his actions.

 

Similarly, the Declaration of John Peterson PharmD speaks to Ifejika’s conduct falling below the standard of care, but is silent as to any evidence or facts bearing on PREP immunity. Again, Peterson also appears to characterize the first injection as occurring in the process of “vaccine administration.” (Peterson Decl., ¶ 5 (a) [“The pictures of the vaccine administration on April 7, 2021 show the pharmacist injecting the plaintiff with what appears to be an empty syringe and the plaintiff begins to bleed. The pharmacist then immediately wipes the plaintiff's arm with a paper towel and immediately injects the patient with another syringe.”].) Peterson makes no mention of Rivas’ conduct.

 

Plaintiff argues that this lawsuit “is about injecting Plaintiff SALAZAR with two needles, the first of which was clearly not part any Covid 19 vaccine administration.” (See, e.g., PSUMF, p. 131, No. 17.) However, Plaintiff simultaneously characterizes the first injection as being given in the administration of the vaccine by a pharmacist. (See PSUMF, Nos. 15-19 [“This has nothing to do with the injection of a covid-19 vaccine. This has to do with the injection technique and the lack of informed consent and duty to relate an unforeseen occurrence in the administration of a vaccine by this pharmacist on April 7, 2021, that is what is being called into dispute.”]; PSUMF p. 20, No. 3 [“The pictures of the vaccine administration on April 7, 2021 show the pharmacist injecting the plaintiff with what appears to be an empty syringe and the plaintiff begins to bleed.”]; PSUMF, p. 28, No. 7 [in response to Defendants’ statement of fact that “Defendants [] did not intend to harm Plaintiff in the administration of her COVID-19 vaccine,” Plaintiff does not dispute that the first injection was given in the administration of her vaccine, but disputes on the grounds that “IFEJIKA never looked at the first syringe before injecting it.”].)

 

As to Rivas specifically, Plaintiff’s motion is silent. The SAC does not assert any causes of action against Rivas for any conduct separate and apart from his role as manager and his handling of the first syringe and the incident after it was reported. Plaintiff alleges that Rivas did not properly handle a complaint related to administration of a protected countermeasure as the manager of the location, that he concealed evidence of the first syringe, that he failed to obtain Plaintiff’s consent for the first or second injection, and that he failed to provide an incident report following Plaintiff’s complaint. Plaintiff opposes Rivas’ separate statement in part by arguing that Rivas engaged in further wrongdoing by permitting Ifejika to remain employed at the Lancaster CVS location as a COVID-19 travel nurse following her reporting of the incident. (See, e.g., PSUMF, No. 25.) This assertion, as with the others against Rivas, is directly related to Rivas’ management and operation of a designated CVS location for administering covered countermeasures, arises entirely from complaints related to vaccine administration, and falls squarely within immunized conduct under the PREP Act.

 

Plaintiff provides excerpts from Rivas’ deposition testimony, in which he states every employee of CVS is expected to report injuries and incidents, that he emailed his District Leader, Patel, to report the incident, and that his personal custom and practice as store manager is to report incidents occurring in his CVS location by the end of the day it was reported. (Plaintiff’s Ex. J, p. 133:14-24, 147:16-148:16.) This testimony is irrelevant to the issue of PREP immunity, and no other evidence regarding Rivas’ conduct is presented.

 

Finally, as to Plaintiff’s argument that her claims arise from non-administration of the vaccine, it is true that many Courts have held that in actions arising from failure to administer covered countermeasures do not confer PREP immunity on defendants. These cases are factually distinguishable. The cases specifically deal with failure to implement any protective measures at all. In Adler, the New York County Supreme Court reiterated that where a plaintiff’s claims “pertain only to the defendant's failures to act, [ ] such allegations do not amount to the administration of countermeasures,” but PREP is triggered where the plaintiff brings suit for improper administration of countermeasures. (Adler v. Dewitt Rehab. & Nursing Ctr., 2026 N.Y. Misc. LEXIS 5116, *28.)

 

In Eaton v. Big Blue Healthcare (2020) 480 F.Supp.3d 1184, 1195, the plaintiff brought a wrongful death action after his mother died of COVID-19, contracted at a rehabilitation facility. Plaintiff alleged that the facility was negligent in not taking measures to protect his mother from COVID-19. The facility removed the case to federal court, claiming that the PREP Act applied to its conduct. The district court rejected the argument as the complaint alleged defendant had not so much injured the decedent in the course of administration of countermeasures but had failed to protect decedent from COVID-19. (Id. at 1194.) “The PREP Act still requires a causal connection between the injury and the

use or administration of covered countermeasures, and that link is not present under

Defendants’ interpretation.” (Ibid.) Here, unlike the decedent in Eaton, Plaintiff was a

recipient of countermeasures under the PREP Act, and suffered her alleged injury – the

first injection – as part of that administration.

 

In Dupervil v. Alliance Health Operations, LCC (E.D.N.Y. 2021) 516 F.Supp.3d 238, 256, the Court determined the PREP Act did not apply because (1) failing to administer countermeasures to protect against COVID-19 does not constitute administration of countermeasures for purposes of the Act, and (2) the Court rejected defendant’s argument that a facility is per se covered for all claims arising from the facility merely because it was using covered countermeasures somewhere in the facility, where there was no showing of causation and no allegation that the harm suffered was the result of the administration or use of countermeasures on or by the injured party.

 

In Coleman v. Sharp Memorial Hosp., No. 37-2023 00033307-CU-PO-CTL, 2024 Cal. Super. LEXIS 10893 (Cal. Sup. Ct. March 29, 2024), the Court overruled Defendant’s demurrer regarding PREP immunity where the Plaintiff’s complaint may have been based on administration of a covered countermeasure, but also, could be read to be based on fraudulent concealment of life-saving treatments separate and apart from any COVID-19 countermeasure. Because multiple interpretations of the complaint’s allegations were possible, the Court could not determine as a matter of law whether PREP immunity applied at the pleadings stage. (Ibid.)

 

In Hampton v. California (2023) 83 F.4th 754, 763, the Court found that defendants were not entitled to PREP immunity where the plaintiff alleged that the defendants’ failure to administer COVID tests to inmates in the days prior to their transfer to another prison was the cause of death of a non-transferred inmate at the receiving prison. The Court rested its ruling on two grounds: (1) allegations regarding failure to test are not covered under the PREP Act, as the Act only reaches injury in the active administration of a countermeasure, and (2) to the extent that the complaint alleged the tests used before transfer were so outdated as to be ineffective, the complaint also alleged that the test results came back after the inmates who tested positive had already been transferred and housed in the same unit as the decedent, the defendants failed to establish a causal relationship between the test results and their decision to transfer the inmates, and therefore administration of COVID tests did not ultimately cause the decedent’s death. (Id. at 763-64.)

 

Here, Plaintiff is not bringing suit for Rivas’ failure to take any response in reaction to the danger posed by COVID--19. Rather, based on the unrefuted evidence provided by Defendants, Plaintiff brings suit for Defendants’ improper injection in the course of administration of a protected countermeasure, Rivas’ subsequent concealment of evidence, Rivas’ retention of Ifejika as an employee, and Rivas’ negligent management of the Lancaster CVS location in administration of a protected countermeasure. Defendants, who are protected persons under PREP, were actively engaging in distribution, administration, and management of the COVID-19 vaccine and location of administration of a protected countermeasure. Plaintiff was only on site to receive the vaccine. Plaintiff was seated in the designated COVID-19 vaccination area. Plaintiff received both the first and second shots while seated to receive a scheduled COVID-19 vaccine. Plaintiff received both shots from Ifejika, who was employed as a COVID-19 Travel Vaccine Pharmacist, whose role was to administer COVID-19 vaccines. Plaintiff lodged a complaint with Rivas, manager of the location, solely related to this encounter. As discussed, this evidence is sufficient to show that a covered person, engaged in administration of covered countermeasures, caused the harm alleged. The Court cannot find based on the evidence presented that Rivas’ conduct related to the first injection was unrelated to the management and operation of a location engaged in the administration of a covered countermeasure.

 

Accordingly, Defendant Rivas, as an employee of CVS, is entitled to PREP Act immunity, and Rivas’ motion for summary judgment is therefore GRANTED.

 

Because the sole exception to PREP Act immunity is an exclusive federal cause of action that may be pursued only after the plaintiff exhausts the Countermeasures Injury Compensation Program and then only in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia (42 U.S.C. §§ 247d-6d, subds. (d)(1), (e)(1), 247d-6e, subd. (d)(1)), this Court lacks jurisdiction to adjudicate a claim of willful misconduct. Plaintiff's second cause of action for willful misconduct therefore cannot proceed in this forum, and the Court does not reach whether Plaintiff could otherwise satisfy that heightened standard.

 III.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Taxing Costs Under the Song-Beverly Act – Pursuant to California Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), a prevailing buyer in a Song-Beverly action is entitled to recover a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, as determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of the action. A prevailing party includes the party with a net monetary recovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (a)(4).)

 

California Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5 sets forth the specific categories of costs that a prevailing party may or may not be entitled to recover as a matter of right in civil litigation.

 

California Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5 provides, in relevant part: 

 

(a) The following items are allowable as costs under Section 1032:

(1) Filing, motion, and jury fees.

…

(3)(A) Taking, video recording, and transcribing necessary depositions, including an original and one copy of those taken by the claimant and one copy of depositions taken by the party against whom costs are allowed.

…

(14) Fees for the electronic filing or service of documents through an electronic filing service provider if a court requires or orders electronic filing or service of documents.

…

(b) The following items are not allowable as costs, except when expressly authorized by law:

(1) Fees of experts not ordered by the court.

(2) Investigation expenses in preparing the case for trial.

(3) Postage, telephone, and photocopying charges, except for    

      exhibits.

(4) Costs in investigation of jurors or in preparation for voir dire.

(5) Transcripts of court proceedings not ordered by the court.

(c) An award of costs shall be subject to the following:

(1) Costs are allowable if incurred, whether or not paid.

(2) Allowable costs shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of

      the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its

      preparation.

(3) Allowable costs shall be reasonable in amount.

(4) Items not mentioned in this section and items assessed upon

      application may be allowed or denied in the court's discretion.

 

Generally, the Court has discretion to award recovery of costs that are not listed as recoverable in subdivision (a) and not barred by subdivision (b). (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(4).) However, in Song-Beverly actions the Court must permit the prevailing buyer to recover such costs and expenses if reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of the action. (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).) “[I]n enacting Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) the Legislature intended the phrase costs and expenses to cover items not included in the detailed statutory definition of costs set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5.” (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 42.) Thus, a prevailing buyer in a Song-Beverly action may be entitled to recover costs and expenses otherwise typically barred by Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (b), provided that they were reasonably incurred in commencement and prosecution of the action.

 

The party filing a memorandum of costs is not required to attach any supporting documents. (Jones v. Dumrichob (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1267.) “[A] verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of the propriety of the items listed on it, and the burden is on the party challenging these costs to demonstrate that they were not reasonable or necessary.” (Bender v. County of Los Angeles (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 968, 989, internal citations omitted; see also Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131-132 [“trial court erred in requiring additional proof from” the party claiming costs]; Santantonio v. Westinghouse Broadcasting Co. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 102, 116, 121 [“where a party shows a prima facie entitlement to costs, the burden is on an objector to prove the costs should be disallowed”].)

 

“[T]he mere filing of a motion to tax costs may be a "proper objection" to an item, the necessity of which appears doubtful, or which does not appear to be proper on its face.” (Nelson, supra, 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131.) However, “[i]f the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary. (Ladas v. California State Automotive Assoc. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 773-74.) The court's first determination, is whether the statute expressly allows the particular item and whether it appears proper on its face. If so, the burden is on the objecting party to show it to be  unnecessary or unreasonable. (Nelson, supra, 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131.) Unreasonable costs include costs that were not reasonably necessary to the conduct of litigation or were not reasonable in amount. (Id at 130.)

 

If an item of cost does not appear proper on its face, the burden of showing that it is reasonable and necessary shifts to the party claiming the cost. (Nelson, supra, 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131; Jones v. Dumrichob (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1267; but see Bach v. County of Butte (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 294, 308 [“Only after such costs are challenged by a motion to tax do the parties need to justify their claims by submitting documentation of the costs they have incurred.”].)

 

-----

 

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendant moves to strike or tax costs from Plaintiff’s Memorandum in the amount of $875.77 on the grounds that certain costs requested are unreasonable and unnecessary.

 

Plaintiff’s Memorandum seeks costs totaling $1,420.05, comprised of (1) $775.25 in filing and motion fees, (2) $150.00 in jury fees, (3) $450.10 in deposition costs, (4) $30.90 in service of process costs, and (5) $11.80 for ‘Other’ costs, consisting only of the cost to download the Court’s February 24, 2026 Minute Order.

 

Of these categories, Defendant objects to (1) $275.67 in filing fees, (2) $150.00 in jury fees, and (3) $450.10 in deposition fees.

 

a.     Applicable Standard

 

The Court first addresses the argument raised by Defendant both in its motion and on reply that Plaintiff bears the burden of proving the requested costs are reasonable and necessary. (See Motion, p. 3:24-4:17; Reply, p. 2:16-3:19.) However, Defendant’s reliance on the burden-shifting mechanism is misplaced.

 

As discussed at length above, where a cost appears to be proper, the burden is on the moving party to make a showing that the costs are unreasonable or unnecessary. Only once Defendant makes such a showing, or if the costs appear facially improper, does the burden shift to Plaintiff to establish reasonableness or necessity of the costs. Defendant appears to argue that upon its filing of the motion to strike or tax the Memorandum and identification of specific charges objected to, it has properly lodged an objection to those costs, that no more is required, and the burden automatically shifts back to Plaintiff to demonstrate that the costs are allowable, reasonably necessary to litigation, and reasonable in amount. (Motion, p. 4:10-16.) However, Defendant omits that the burden only shifts upon proper objection, which includes a showing that the costs were not reasonable or necessary where they appear proper on their face, not merely putting forth an argument to that effect. (See Ladas, supra, 19 Cal.App.4th at 773-74 [“[i]f the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary”].)

 

Defendant’s motion does procedurally comply with California Rules of Court rule 3.1700, which requires that where objection is made to less than the entire Memorandum, a motion to strike or tax costs must refer to each item objected to by the same number and appear in the same order as the corresponding cost item claimed on the memorandum of costs and state why the item is objectionable. However, merely complying with the procedural requirements for filing an objection does not shift the burden back to Plaintiff if Defendant does not establish that the costs are unreasonable or unnecessary. (See Reply, p. 3:7-10 [“GM’s motion did more than offer bald assertions—it pointed out specific costs that appeared excessive or unwarranted. This shifts the burden. The question is whether Counsel have now met their burden to justify those costs with competent evidence of necessity. They have not.”].)

 

Plaintiff’s Memorandum serves as prima facie evidence of the propriety of the items listed on it. Plaintiffs are under no obligation to provide supporting documentation in support of the Memorandum. California Rule of Court rule 3.1700(a)(1) requires that “the memorandum of costs must be verified by a statement of the party, attorney, or agent that to the best of his or her knowledge the items of cost are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case.” Plaintiff’s counsel Bobby C. Walker verified the memorandum of costs by signing, under penalty of perjury, a statement which reads: “I am counsel for the party who claims the costs listed above. To the best of my knowledge, the items of costs are correct and were necessarily incurred in this case.” (Memorandum of Costs, p. 1.) Nothing more is required.

 

Therefore, unless the costs are facially unreasonable or Defendant makes a showing that the costs are unreasonable or unnecessary, the costs are recoverable. “[M]ore than a bare objection is needed to rebut the presumption that fees were reasonable and necessarily incurred.” (State of California v. Meyer (1985) 174 Cal.App.3d 1061, 1075.)

 

b.     Filing and Motion Fees

 

Defendant first objects to $275.67 in filing fees related to “miscellaneous overhead costs,” including the filing of Plaintiff’s CMC Statement, Notice of Posting of Jury Fees, and Notice of Settlement, motions, oppositions, and a reply. (Motion, p. 5:1-3.) Defendant argues the Notice of Settlement is a purely administrative notice filed to inform the Court that the case has resolved and requires no judicial decision, opposition, or argument, and is not a motion within the meaning of Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(1), that the costs associated with Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel the Deposition of Defendant’s PMQ, the reply, and the motions for leave to amend were optional costs that counsel elected to incur for one-sided and unnecessary motion practice that did not advance Plaintiff’s case, and that for all of these filings, Plaintiff’s counsel has not provided any receipts or backup documentation to substantiate these costs. (Motion, p. 5:3-8.)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(1) expressly authorizes recovery of filing fees, and separately, motion fees. There is no language in Section 1033.5 that supports Defendant’s interpretation that a filing fee is only recoverable if it was incurred in the filing of a motion requiring opposition or argument.

 

As to the Notice of Settlement, the § 998 Settlement Offer does not contain any limitations on recoverable costs. (See Figura Decl., Ex. 4, ¶ 4.) California Rules of Court rule 3.1385(a)(1) requires that a plaintiff must immediately file written notice of the settlement with the Court once an entire case is settled. Plaintiff’s Notice of Settlement is not a mere administrative or overhead cost, but is a required filing integral to the prosecution of the action, as it serves to inform the Court of the resolution of the case and effectively concludes the prosecution of Plaintiff’s claims.

 

As to Plaintiff’s CMC Statement, this is a filing required by the Court. On September 24, 2024, the Court set a CMC for February 2, 2025. California Rules of Court rule 3.725(a) requires each party to file a CMC Statement no later than 15 days before the CMC. This cost is expressly permitted by Section 1033.5(a), Section 1794(d), and was necessarily incurred in the prosecution and conduct of litigation.

 

As to Plaintiff’s Notice of Posting of Jury Fees, Code of Civil Procedure section 631, subdivisions (b)-(c) requires a party demanding a jury in a civil action to pay a nonrefundable fee of $150.00 on or before the date scheduled for the initial CMC. Failure to do so waives that party’s right to a jury. (Code Civ. Proc., § 631, subd. (f)(5).) Plaintiff’s filing of the Notice of Posting of Jury Fees served to inform the Court and Defendant, and to place on the record, that the fees were paid to avoid waiver of Plaintiff’s right to a jury trial. Defendant has made no showing that this filing was unreasonably or unnecessarily incurred in the conduct of litigation or that recovery of this is precluded.

 

As to Plaintiff’s motions for leave to amend, these costs are expressly permitted by statute and clearly relate to the prosecution and conduct of litigation. The pleadings control the scope of the action, and the motions were targeted to address the specific claims brought against Defendant. That costs must be “necessarily incurred” in litigation does not mean that the underlying filing must be “required.” That these motions were “optional” does not remove them from Sections 1033.5 or 1794(d). Indeed, commencement of civil litigation, generally speaking, is in and of itself “optional.”

 

As to the Motion to Compel the Deposition of Defendant’s PMQ, Defendant argues it already agreed to produce documents subject to a protective order and produce its PMQ to testify on some of the requested topics, and the filing of the motion did nothing to advance Plaintiff’s case, rendering it unnecessary. The Motion was never heard or ruled on, as the case settled prior to the hearing. However, on review of the Motion itself, it appears that Plaintiff noticed the deposition of Defendant’s PMQ on February 18, 2025, and on April 8, 2025, Defendant objected to the NOD but agreed to produce its PMQ on some of the requested categories on a mutually agreed upon date. Plaintiff’s counsel requested dates of availability on September 22, 2025, December 8, 2025, January 6, 2026, January 15, 2026, February 2, 2026, and February 4, 2026, along with leaving messages with Defense counsel requesting a phone call to meet and confer about the deposition. (See Figura MTC Depo Decl., ¶¶ 7-15.) It appears from the moving papers that Defendant never provided dates for its PMQ. Defendant makes no showing to the contrary. Defendant’s agreement to produce its PMQ for some requested categories does not show that this motion was unnecessary or unreasonable where Defendant thereafter failed to provide dates of availability for roughly 10 months and ignored repeated efforts to schedule the deposition.

 

Accordingly, Defendant has failed to show that these costs are not reasonable, necessary, permitted, and recoverable. The Court declines to strike Plaintiff’s request for these fees.

 

c.      Jury Fees

 

Defendant next argues that it should not have to reimburse Plaintiff for the jury fees posted, as under California Law, jury fees are only recoverable when a trial occurs, and permitting recovery would be inconsistent with the statutory purpose of cost recovery as the case never went to trial. (Motion, p. 5:9-14.)

 

The case relied on by Defendant, Perko's Enterprises, Inc. v. RRNS Enterprises (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 238, 245 does not support Defendant’s argument. The Perko’s Court held that under Section 1033.5, subdivision (c)(2), a trial court is authorized to disallow recovery of costs otherwise allowable as a matter of right if the trial court determines that the costs were unnecessarily incurred. (Ibid.) Here, as discussed, it does not matter whether the case ultimately advanced to trial. Plaintiff was required to post $150.00 in jury fees before the initial CMC to preserve her right to a jury trial. Section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(1) authorizes recovery of jury fees. Section 1794, subdivision (d) permits recovery of all costs reasonably and necessarily incurred in the course of litigation. Plaintiff’s posting of jury fees to avoid waiver was necessary and reasonable in prosecuting her case.

 

The Court declines to strike Plaintiff’s request for jury fees.

 

d.     Deposition Fees for the Non-Appearance of Defendant’s PMQ

 

Finally, Defendant moves to strike Plaintiff’s request for the deposition fees incurred in documenting the nonappearance of Defendant’s PMQ. (Motion, p. 5:15-19.) Defendant argues the fees were entirely unnecessary, as Defendant had already provided hundreds of pages of Bates-stamped documents describing the vehicle’s repair history, and that the documentation of non-appearance did nothing to advance Plaintiff’s case.

 

Defendant’s argument is unpersuasive. Compliance with one form of discovery does not alleviate a party’s obligation to comply with another form of discovery to which the requesting party is statutorily entitled. Under Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26, subdivision (c)(2), Plaintiff was entitled, within 120 days after Defendant filed its answer, to conduct her initial deposition of Defendant’s PMQ on the topics set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26, subdivision (i), which includes questions relating to the nature and extent of the entire service history, warranty history, and repairs of the vehicle and questions relating to relevant repair procedures consulted and followed during the repairs. This right to conduct a deposition of Defendant’s PMQ is in addition to Defendant’s statutory obligation to provide, within 60 days of filing its answer, an initial disclosure of specifically defined documents, including the entire warranty transaction history for the vehicle, communications records related to repair orders or claims involving the vehicle, service manuals related to the vehicle’s nonconformities, and motor vehicle information reports. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (b), (h).) Moreover, documentary and testimonial evidence are fundamentally different, and it cannot be said that the deposition of a PMQ is rendered useless merely because Plaintiff was in receipt of certain documents related to the categories sought for deposition.

 

Defendant has not shown that the costs associated with obtaining a certificate of non-appearance for Defendant’s PMQ were not reasonably incurred in the prosecution of the action.

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s motion is DENIED.

    V.          Conclusion

 

Defendant General Motors LLC’s Motion to Strike or Tax Costs is DENIED.

   II.          Legal Standard

Standard for Relief From Admissions – Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.300 provides that “[a] party may withdraw or amend an admission made in response to a request for admission only on leave of court granted after notice to all parties.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.300, subd. (a).)  “The court may permit withdrawal or amendment of an admission only if it determines that the admission was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect, and that the party who obtained the admission will not be substantially prejudiced in maintaining that party’s action or defense on the merits.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.300, subd. (b).) This includes admissions deemed admitted by court order under Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.280, subdivision (b). (See Wilcox v. Birtwhistle (1999) 21 Cal.4th 973, 979–80.)

The terms "mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect" under section 2033.300 have the same meanings as the terms used in Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b). (New Albertsons, Inc. v. Superior Court (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1419.) Therefore, the mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect must be reasonable, in that a reasonably prudent person under similar circumstances might have made the same error. (Austin v. Los Angeles Unified School District (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 918, 929.) 

The court may impose conditions on the granting of the motion that are just, including, but not limited to: (1) “[a]n order that the party who obtained the admission be permitted to pursue additional discovery related to the matter involved in the withdrawn or amended admission,” or (2) “[a]n order that the costs of any additional discovery be borne in whole or in part by the party withdrawing or amending the admission.”(Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.300, subd. (c).) 

“Section 2033.300 eliminates undeserved windfalls obtained through requests for admission and furthers the policy favoring the resolution of lawsuits on the merits.” (New Albertsons, supra, 168 Cal.App.4th at 1418.) “Because the law strongly favors trial and disposition on the merits, any doubts in applying section 2033.300 must be resolved in favor of the party seeking relief.” (Id. at 1420.) “Accordingly, the court’s discretion to deny a motion under the statute is limited to circumstances where it is clear that the mistake, inadvertence, or neglect was inexcusable, or where it is clear that the withdrawal or amendment would substantially prejudice the party who obtained the admission in maintaining that party’s action or defense on the merits.” (Id. at 1420-21.) 

 

-----

 

 III.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendants move for relief and withdrawal of their admissions to Plaintiffs’ RFAs, Set One pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.300 on the grounds that the failure to provide responses to the discovery and oppose the motion to deem the RFAs admitted was the result of attorney mistake, inadvertence, and excusable neglect between Defendants’ two law firms, and because Plaintiffs will not be prejudiced by granting relief.

 

Defendants set forth the following circumstances leading to the admissions:

 

On September 17, 2025, Plaintiffs served 4 sets of discovery on each Defendant, including RFAs, Set One, Requests for Production, Set One, Special Interrogatories, Set One, and Form Interrogatories – General, Set One. At the time of service, Defendants were represented by Park & Lim, LLP (“PL”). On October 24, 2025, Ecoff Campain & Kay, LLP (“ECK”) associated in as defense counsel along with PL in over 25 cases for Defendants Excel and Jogani, including this case. The work was then divided between the two firms. PL was initially responsible for responding to discovery, and exchanged communications with Plaintiff’s counsel regarding the timeline for responding. PL and ECK later agreed that ECK would take the lead on this matter and prepare Defendants’ discovery responses when they became due on January 2, 2026, but PL still continued to work on this file as the firms transitioned the cases from PL to ECK.

 

When Defendants’ responses were due, Defendants’ counsel at ECK, Lawrence C. Ecoff (“Ecoff”), confused this matter with another and believed PL was responsible for responding. At the time the responses were due, two of the four ECK attorneys assigned to this case were engaged in a three month trial in Orange County, which commenced on October 20, 2025 and concluded on January 26, 2026. Plaintiffs’ counsel then served a meet and confer letter and their motions to compel the 12 sets of discovery to both PL and Ecoff, but Ecoff still believed PL was responsible for the discovery responses and took no action. Ecoff’s mistake resulted in failure to serve Defendants’ discovery responses, oppose the motions to compel, and appear at the hearing to argue the motions. Following the May 7, 2026 hearing, Plaintiff’s counsel notified Defense counsel about the orders, and on May 8, 2026, Defense counsel realized their error, contacted Plaintiff’s counsel regarding a stipulation to allow Defendants to withdraw the admissions, and Plaintiff’s counsel refused to set aside the order.

 

(See Motion, p. 4:11-5:18, p. 7:16-19; Ecoff Decl., ¶¶ 3-10; Kay Decl., ¶ 3.)

 

Plaintiffs supplement the factual circumstances as follows:

 

On October 15, 2025, Defense counsel requested an extension for the responses to November 14, 2025, which Plaintiffs granted. On November 14th, Defense counsel requested a further three week extension citing illness and association of new counsel, which Plaintiffs granted. On December 4, 2025, following a telephone conference, Defense counsel requested an extension to January 2, 2026, which Plaintiffs granted with the caveat that they would not grant any further extension. No responses were served. Plaintiff’s counsel sent a meet and confer letter to PL and Ecoff on January 19, 2026 demanding responses without objection by February 2, 2026. Defense counsel did not respond to this letter. In the eleven weeks from conclusion of Ecoff’s Orange County trial on January 26, 2026 to April 14, 2026, the date the motion to deem the RFAs admitted was filed, Defendants did not serve any responses. On May 26, 2026, Defendants served responses to the Special Interrogatories, Form Interrogatories, and Requests for Production, but did not serve, and have never served, any response to the RFAs.

 

(See Opp., p. 3:7-4:11; Jacobsen Decl., ¶¶ 4-10.)

 

For the reasons set forth below, the Court finds that for the purposes of Section 2033.300, Defendants have sufficiently established that their mistake, inadvertence, and neglect was not clearly inexcusable, and Plaintiffs have failed to establish substantial prejudice would result from granting the motion.

 

To start, Plaintiffs misstate the general standard for mistake, inadvertence, excusable neglect of counsel in holding Defense counsel to a reasonably prudent attorney standard. Generally, for non-attorney error, the mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect must be reasonable, in that a reasonably prudent person under similar circumstances might have made the same error. (Austin v. Los Angeles Unified School District (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 918, 929.) The same standard applies to error of counsel. (See McClain v. Kissler (2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 399, 414 [“the discretionary relief provision of section 473 only permits relief from attorney error ‘fairly imputable to the client, i.e., mistakes anyone could have made.’ [Citation.] ‘Conduct falling below the professional standard of care, such as failure to timely object or to properly advance an argument, is not therefore excusable.’”].) Here, Defense counsel is not seeking relief for a legal error, but on the grounds that ECK mistakenly believed the preparation and service of Defendants’ responses were not their responsibility. Therefore, the Court holds ECK to a reasonably prudent person standard, i.e., a mistake anyone could have made and which is attributable to the client.  

 

The Court does find some of the conduct on behalf of ECK and PL inexcusable. For example, on service of Plaintiffs’ counsel’s January 19, 2026 meet and confer letter regarding failure to provide RFA responses, PL and ECK’s failure to communicate to ensure their clients’ discovery responses were completed is not excusable. Nor is the failure to verify which firm would be responsible for responding to Plaintiffs’ motions to compel. Both PL and ECK were on clear notice that their clients’ discovery responses had not been completed or provided, including their RFA responses, and that failure to respond could, and did, ultimately result in admissions that are likely fatal to their clients’ defense. It is inexcusable for both firms to agree to joint representation, undertake such representation on multiple cases, and fail to communicate with one another or adequately manage and track the individual cases. This conduct, on its own, would warrant denial. However, the Court must analyze the circumstances as a whole, and determine ultimately whether Ecoff’s belief that he was not the responsible attorney underlying his failure to act was clearly inexcusable, and the Court cannot base its ruling solely on the failure of PL and ECK to communicate and properly monitor all of their clients’ cases.

 

Plaintiffs rely heavily on the factual circumstances regarding Defense counsel’s numerous extension requests and Plaintiffs’ correspondence in support of finding inexcusable neglect, but their exhibits provided in opposition reinforce Ecoff’s series of events. Exhibit B establishes that PL requested an extension to respond to discovery on October 15, 2025, and neither ECK nor Ecoff are copied. Exhibit C establishes that the same is true for the November 14, 2025 request. Exhibit D establishes that PL requested a telephone call with Plaintiff’s counsel on December 3, 2025, that Plaintiff’s counsel engaged in a telephone call with PL on December 4, 2025, and that PL requested an extension to January 2, 2026. Based on Plaintiffs’ evidence and Ecoff’s undisputed version of events, neither ECK nor Ecoff were copied on or involved in this chain of correspondence. Exhibit E establishes that the January 19, 2026 meet and confer letter was sent via email to four members of PL, and Ecoff was the only employee of ECK served with the meet and confer letter. Pages two and three of the letter include “PARK & LIM LLP” in the top lefthand corner with no mention of ECK. The Court also notes that Ecoff’s October 24, 2025 Notice of Association of Counsel states that all correspondence should be provided to Ecoff.

 

This is not to say that Plaintiffs’ counsel acted improperly or negligently in serving these documents or in attempting to meet and confer with LP to obtain responses to the RFAs, particularly where there is nothing on the record indicating that either firm informed Plaintiffs’ counsel that all discovery-related matters would be handled by ECK or provided alternate service addresses for ECK. However, these circumstances do, to some extent, substantiate Ecoff’s claims that this matter was in the process of being transferred from PL to ECK at or around the time the discovery responses were due in early January and that PL initially took the lead on discovery in this action. They also do not directly show that Ecoff’s belief that PL was the firm responsible for serving Defendants’ discovery responses was clearly unsupported, as the meet and confer correspondence appears primarily directed to PL.

 

The circumstances, taken as a whole, establish the following: when Plaintiffs’ RFAs were served, PL was the only firm representing Defendants. ECK did not associate as counsel until after the RFA responses were initially due. As of December 4, 2025, PL was the only firm engaged in correspondence with Plaintiffs’ counsel regarding the parties’ discovery in this case. The January 2, 2026 responsive deadline was set by PL without ECK’s involvement. At some point between December 4, 2025 and January 2, 2026, this matter, along with over 25 other related matters involving the same Defendants, was in the process of being transferred from PL to ECK, and ECK agreed to handle discovery in this case. Ecoff mistakenly mixed this case up with another transferred case and believed that PL remained responsible for the discovery responses in this action. Ecoff was engaged in a three month long trial that commenced before ECK’s association as counsel and concluded after the responses were due and after Plaintiffs’ meet and confer letter was sent. Ecoff received no documents or correspondence from Plaintiffs’ counsel related to discovery in this case before the January 2, 2026 deadline. Ecoff received three documents related to discovery in this case after the responsive deadline had passed: (1) Plaintiffs’ meet and confer correspondence on January 19, 2026, directed to four members of PL and one from ECK, Ecoff, (2) Plaintiffs’ discovery motions on April 14, 2026, and (3) the Court’s Orders granting Plaintiffs’ motions on May 7, 2026. On May 8, 2026, ECK requested a stipulated withdrawal of the admissions, and Plaintiff’s counsel refused.

 

Under these circumstances, taken together, the Court does not find Ecoff’s belief that ECK was not responsible for Defendants’ discovery responses clearly inexcusable. These circumstances are similar to those in which Courts have found relief warranted. In New Albertsons, a defendant supermarket admitted a photograph of the scene of the plaintiff’s injury showed a bag of ice on the ground. The defendant subsequently moved to withdraw the admission on the grounds that the defendant mistakenly thought the photograph was a bag of ice, and counsel believed the plaintiff’s claims were not predicated on the existence of a bag of ice and mistakenly thought admission made no difference in the case’s outcome. The Court found that the trial court erred in denying the motion, as although the defendant and its attorneys had reason to believe it was not a bag of ice and understood the significance of the admission, it was arguably excusable, and therefore, not clearly inexcusable. (See New Albertsons, supra, 168 Cal.App.4th at 1421.) Here, Ecoff had some reason to believe PL was not responsible for the discovery responses, evidenced by Plaintiffs’ meet and confer letter, motions to compel, and ECK’s apparent agreement to handle the discovery. However, the Court cannot say under the circumstances that this belief was blatantly inexcusable.

 

Even more on point is Elston. The Court found relief warranted where counsel failed to respond to RFAs, but stated he was busy with other matters during the responsive period, his office was understaffed, the papers were misplaced, and he was unaware of the RFAs until the answers were overdue. (Elston v. City of Turlock (1985) 38 Cal.3d 227, 234-35 (disapproved on other grounds).) The Supreme Court noted in Elston that “[w]here an attorney states that he was unaware of his duty to appear or answer because his employees misplaced papers or misinformed him as to the relevant date, relief is routinely granted.” (Id. at 234.) Here, Ecoff was clearly aware of the RFAs and his failure to respond was not due to a calendaring error or misplacement of documents. However, he has set forth sufficient information to support his contention that he was unaware of his duty to appear or answer because he mistakenly mixed the present case up with another, believed the responses were to be completed by PL, was the only ECK employee copied on any correspondence or service emails, was engaged in a three month long trial, and did not calendar the deadline or otherwise recognize his responsibility for the discovery in the case.

 

Unless inexcusable neglect is clear, the policy favoring trial on the merits prevails, and any doubts must be resolved in favor of the party moving for relief. Therefore, the Court finds Ecoff’s conduct was not, as a whole, clearly inexcusable, and relief is warranted.

 

Plaintiffs have not shown that they will suffer substantial prejudice in granting this motion. As Plaintiffs note, the relevant question is “whether permitting withdrawal would impair Plaintiffs’ ability to prove the matters now conclusively established by the admissions.” (Opp., p. 5:11-12.) Here, Plaintiff has not established that their ability to prove the admitted matters will be impaired or prejudiced.

 

Plaintiffs assert that significant prejudice will result because counsel has already relied on the Orders and structured their discovery plan, expert workup, and case strategy around the admissions that Defendants left unchallenged for almost a month, and withdrawal would require Plaintiffs to reconstruct from scratch at their own expense a discovery record that Defendants were obligated to provide in October 2025[1]. (Opp., p. 9:8-12.) However, Defendants were not obligated to provide these RFA responses in October 2025 in light of Plaintiffs’ counsel’s granting Defendants’ extension requests. Plaintiffs do not dispute that the day after the Order was issued, on May 8th, Defense counsel contacted Plaintiffs’ counsel requesting a stipulation to withdrawal the admissions. Defense counsel then filed the present motion less than one month after the Order was issued on June 5th. Plaintiffs appear to state that they crafted a discovery plan entirely from scratch in reliance on the admissions in the less than four weeks following the Court’s Order, but do not identify how it changed from any prior discovery plan they had in place, or how any such modification was reasonable, given that they were almost immediately on notice that Defendants were seeking withdrawal of the admissions. Moreover, the same date the RFAs were deemed admitted, the Court also granted Plaintiffs’ nine other motions to compel further discovery responses, indicating that prior to the Order, Plaintiffs had, to some extent, a discovery plan in place and sought targeted information underlying their claims. Modification of a discovery plan over a year into the case does not establish substantial prejudice where there is no evidence that Plaintiffs initial plan has been prejudiced resulting in loss of evidence.

 

Plaintiffs’ argument that loss of discovery presents substantial prejudice is unpersuasive. Plaintiffs do not point to any piece of evidence that has been lost or any discovery route that has been impeded. For example, Plaintiffs refer to RFA Nos. 27, 39, 43, 45, and 47 regarding testimonial evidence, which they contend goes “directly to the identity and knowledge of the individuals who inspected, maintained, and repaired the Premises and who authorized the painting-over of mold. Those are precisely the witnesses whose memories fade and whose employment ends. Defendants have refused for nearly a year to identify them.” (Opp., p. 9:3-7.) However, these RFAs do not request the identities, contact information, or scope of knowledge of any witness. These RFAs request admission that (1) at least one person employed by or affiliated with Defendants a) was responsible for inspecting, maintaining, or repairing the Property during Plaintiffs’ tenancy, b) is most knowledgeable regarding tenant habitability complaints at the Property, and c) authorized or approved painting over mold at the Property, (2) that Defendants hired, retained, or contracted persons or entities to provide pest control services at the Property during Plaintiffs’ tenancy, and (3) that at least one inspection of the Property was conducted by Defendants during Plaintiffs’ tenancy.

 

Plaintiffs do not establish, and the Court does not recognize, how withdrawing admission to these requests prejudices Plaintiffs or precludes them from identifying and contacting potential witnesses to prove their case. Whether these RFAs are admitted or denied, Plaintiffs still must undertake further discovery to identify these witnesses, obtain substantiating documentation, and conduct further discovery as necessary to obtain relevant and admissible evidence to prove their case.

 

Moreover, as Defendants note, Plaintiffs allege their tenancy ended in September of 2024, this case was filed in April of 2025, the RFAs were deemed admitted in May of 2026, and Defendants filed this motion in June of 2026. Plaintiffs fail to identify what evidence has been lost from May 7, 2026 to date that would have been obtained but was not due to the admissions, which was not already lost between September of 2024 and May 7, 2026. Plaintiffs do not identify what discovery, if any, they would have conducted from May 7, 2026 to date had the RFAs not been deemed admitted, or identify any reason they cannot still conduct such discovery. Plaintiffs do not identify any witnesses that have been made unavailable since May 7, 2026 due to the admissions.

 

Plaintiffs further assert that they have been prejudiced in that they have incurred monetary expense in filing twelve motions to compel and in filing an opposition to this motion. Expenses incurred in filing motions to compel responses to discovery are irrelevant to this motion as they concern other forms of discovery.. However, the Court determines that the current motion would have been unnecessary had ECK been paying sufficient attention to this matter. The Court finds that it is reasonable to grant Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions in the amount of $5,000 for the litigation that have had to engage in due to ECK’s neglect..

 

Otherwise, Plaintiffs have identified no substantial prejudice that would result from granting relief. These RFAs were included in the first round of discovery served in the matter and Plaintiffs have not established that any significant discovery was forfeited or propounded in reliance on the admissions. No identifiable evidence has been lost. Moreover, trial is set for July 2, 2027, with a discovery cutoff of June 2, 2027 and expert designation deadline of May 13, 2027. Plaintiffs have not established any inability to conduct discovery and have every opportunity, and ample time, to do so.

 

To address Plaintiff’s remaining arguments, the Court notes that there is no requirement for Defendants to serve or attach proposed amended responses after RFAs have been deemed admitted by Court Order. The Court does not need to make a determination at this stage whether the RFA responses would be code-compliant, verified, or served without objection before granting relief from admissions.

 

Plaintiffs also argue that Defendants have failed to show that they acted diligently. While section 2033.300 shares the definitions of “mistake, inadvertence, and excusable neglect” with Section 473(b), the standards for relief under the two statutes are not identical. There is no express diligence requirement for relief under Section 2033.300. (See Wilcox, supra, 21 Cal.4th at 980 [“[b]y eliminating the time limits on obtaining relief, [Section 2033.300, formerly section 2033(m)] is much less draconian than the previous practice--which only gave a nonresponding party 30 days after receiving notice of the deemed admissions to obtain relief”]; New Albertsons, supra, 168 Cal. App. 4th at 1421 [“delay alone without a showing of substantial prejudice cannot justify the denial of a motion under the statute”].)

 

Finally, the Court finds relief is supported by the strong policy favoring trial on the merits, which would be all but eliminated in the present case, and the general purpose of RFAs, which do not serve as a method to determine disputed issues between the parties, but “seek to eliminate the need for proof: ‘[T]he purpose of the admissions procedure … is to limit the triable issues and spare the parties the burden and expense of litigating undisputed issues.’” (St. Mary v. Superior Court (2014) 223 Cal. App. 4th 762, 775.)

 

The record establishes that Defense counsel’s mistakes in failing to respond to the RFAs, failing to oppose Plaintiffs’ motion, and failing to appear at the hearing to argue the motion resulting in the admissions are, to an extent, substantiated by the circumstances presented and were not clearly inexcusable. Plaintiffs have not made a showing that they would be substantially prejudiced by withdrawal.

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ motion is GRANTED. Defendants are to serve code-compliant, verified responses to Plaintiffs’ RFAs, Set One, without objection within ten (10) days of the issuance of this Order.  Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions in the amount of $5,000 is GRANTED, to be paid by ECK within 30 (thirty) days of issuance of this Order.

 IV.          Conclusion

 

Defendants’ Motion to Withdraw Requests for Admission Deemed Admitted and Permitting Defendants’ Amended Responses to Requests for Admission, Set One is GRANTED. Defendants are to serve code-compliant, verified responses to Plaintiffs’ RFAs, Set One, without objection within ten (10) days of the issuance of this Order.  Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions in the amount of $5,000 is GRANTED, to be paid by ECK within 30 (thirty) days of issuance of this Order.

      I.          Background

 

This is a negligence action. Defendants Damian Demetrius Coborn (“Coborn”) and Altavesse Correa (“Altaveese”) (together “Defendants”) move to compel the deposition of Narinder S. Grewal, M.D. (“Grewal”) and for monetary sanctions.

 

On May 8, 2025, Plaintiff Antoinette Sims (“Plaintiff”) filed her form Complaint against Defendants, asserting two causes of action for (1) Motor Vehicle Negligence and (2) General Negligence. Plaintiff alleges that she suffered injury following a motor vehicle collision on December 9, 2024 involving a vehicle leased or rented by Defendants.

 

 On June 27, 2025, Defendants filed their Answer.

 

On June 22, 2026, Defendants filed their Motion to Compel Grewal’s deposition. This motion is unopposed.

 

-----

 

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Meet and Confer Requirement – A motion to compel deposition shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450 subd. (b)(2).) 

 

Defense counsel Derick Hovsepian declares that he has made several attempts to contact Grewal’s office regarding the deposition, but the office has failed to return his messages or provide him with any alternative scheduling availability for Grewal’s deposition. This is sufficient to satisfy the meet and confer requirement.

 

-----

 

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Compelling Non-Party Depositions – Any party may obtain discovery, subject to restrictions, by taking the oral deposition of any person.¿¿(Code of Civ. Proc., § 2025.010.) A party may obtain discovery within the state of California from a non-party individual by way of oral deposition and a deposition for production of business records, upon issuance of a deposition subpoena. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2020.010.) A defendant may serve a deposition notice without leave of court at any time after he or she has been served or has appeared in the case. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.210, subd. (a).)

The attendance and testimony of any deponent who is not a party or an officer, director, managing agent, or employee of a party, as well as the production by the deponent of any document or electronically stored information requires the service on the deponent of a deposition subpoena under Chapter 6, commencing with Section 2020.010. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.250, subd. (b).) If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of documents or electronically stored information at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by a party to the case, may make an order directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a)-(b).) In making an order pursuant to motion made under Section 1987.1, the court may in its discretion award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonable attorney's fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (c).)

-----

 

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendants move to compel the deposition of non-party witness Grewal pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 2025.430, 2025.280, 2020.010, 1987.1, 2025.450, subdivision (g) and 2023.010, subdivisions (d) and (i) on the grounds that Grewal failed to appear for deposition and produce documents.

 

Defendants assert that on April 28, 2026, they served their notice of deposition for Grewal and served a subpoena for his deposition and production of Plaintiff’s medical records and bills, noticing the deposition and production for May 12, 2026. (Motion, p. 3:12-16.) On May 12, 2026, Defendants proceeded with the deposition, but no witness appeared, and counsel took a Certificate of Non-Appearance. (Motion, p. 3:16-18.) Despite repeated attempts to reach Grewal’s office, Defendants assert that he has failed to respond to the subpoena or provide alternate dates of availability. (Motion, p. 3:19-23.)

 

On review of the unopposed moving papers and the exhibits provided by Defendants, the Court cannot compel Grewal’s deposition or production of documents.

 

To start, some of the statutes under which Defendants move are not applicable to the issue at hand. Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.430 applies if the party noticing the deposition fails to attend or appear with it. Here, Defendants noticed the deposition and proceeded with it, and this section does not apply to Grewal. Section 2025.450 only applies where the non-compliance is on behalf of a party to the action or an officer, director, managing agent, or employee of a party, and does not apply to a nonparty witness like Grewal.

 

However, the most notable issue here is the fact that Grewal was not properly served. Defendants’ motion, at times, appears to reference Grewal’s production of a witness, rather than his own personal appearance. Regardless, it is clear from the context and subpoena itself that Defendants are seeking to compel the deposition of Grewal, an individual, and not of Advanced Pain Management, the business he works for. The NOD provided to Plaintiff notices the taking of deposition of Grewal. Grewal alone is the named deponent, and the NOD makes no reference to any categories or topics for deposition or otherwise request designation of a person most knowledgeable as would be expected if serving an NOD on a non-person business or entity. The deposition subpoena itself is directed to Grewal and orders him to appear to testify as a witness. Section 4, directed to representatives of a business or other entity, is left blank.

 

Therefore, because the deposition subpoena is clearly directed solely to Grewal, not as a representative of Advanced Pain Management but as an individual treating physician, the only authorized method of service of a deposition subpoena is personal service. Specifically, Code of Civil Procedure section 2020.220, subdivision (b)(1) provides: “Any person may serve the subpoena by personal delivery of a copy of it as follows: (1) If the deponent is a natural person, to that person.” Only if the deponent is an organization may personal service be effected on an individual authorized to accept service of a subpoena. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2020.220, subd. (b)(2).)

 

Here, the proof of service of the deposition subpoena states that Grewal was served on May 1, 2026 through an individual named “Virginia Barajas, MA / Authorized to Accept.” This method of service is improper, and for Grewal to be legally obligated to respond to the deposition subpoena, and for the Court to have any authority to compel his compliance, he must be personally served. Because service was defective, Grewal is under no legal obligation to appear or produce the requested documents, and the Court cannot compel his appearance or production of records.

 

The same is true for service of Defendants’ motion to compel a nonparty deponent’s testimony and production of records. California Rules of Court rule 3.1346 states: “A written notice and all moving papers supporting a motion to compel an answer to a deposition question or to compel production of a document or tangible thing from a nonparty deponent must be personally served on the nonparty deponent unless the nonparty deponent agrees to accept service by mail or electronic service at an address or electronic service address specified on the deposition record.” Defendants filed a proof of service on September 11, 2026, which states that Grewal was served with the moving papers through “Yvonne ‘C.,’ Medical Assistant, Authorized to Accept.” There is no evidence Grewal has agreed to accept service by mail or electronic service. Service is not authorized on an agent authorized to accept service where the target of the motion is a named individual. Therefore, service of this motion was not proper as to Grewal.

 

Moreover, the deposition subpoena is defective for failure to provide a Notice to Consumer to both Plaintiff and Grewal. Notwithstanding the failure to properly serve the deposition subpoena, this defect alone presents grounds for denial.

 

“Personal records” include any documents, copies, writings, or electronically stored information pertaining to a “consumer” and maintained by any “witness” who is a physician, chiropractor, or other health care professional as defined by the statute. (Code Civ Proc., § 1985.3, subd. (a)(1).) A “consumer” includes any individual who has used the services of any such witness, including a treating physician. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1985.3, subd. (a)(2).) The deposition subpoena here requests records from the last 10 years pertaining to Plaintiff, including but not limited to her examinations, medical history, diagnosis, treatment, testing, radiology, prognosis, prescriptions, and billing (including, but not limited, to payments, adjustments, reimbursements, and explanations of benefit). (Hovsepian Decl., Ex. 1.) These records are held by Grewal, Plaintiff’s treating physician.

 

Where a deposition subpoena commands attendance, testimony, and production of documents, and the subpoenaed party is a “witness,” and the business records described in the deposition subpoena are “personal records” pertaining to a “consumer,” the service of the deposition subpoena must be accompanied either by (1) a copy of the proof of service of the Notice to Consumer as described by Section 1985.3(e) or (2) the consumer’s written authorization to release personal records described in Section 1985.3(c)(2). (Code Civ. Proc., § 2020.510, subd. (c).) The notice of privacy rights requirement is set forth by Code of Civil Procedure section 1985.3, subdivision (e) as follows: “Every copy of the subpoena duces tecum…shall be accompanied by a notice, in a typeface designed to call attention to the notice, indicating that (1) records about the consumer are being sought from the witness named on the subpoena; (2) if the consumer objects to the witness furnishing the records to the party seeking the records, the consumer must file papers with the court or serve a written objection as provided in subdivision (g) prior to the date specified for production on the subpoena; and (3) if the party who is seeking the records will not agree in writing to cancel or limit the subpoena, an attorney should be consulted about the consumer’s interest in protecting his or her rights of privacy. If a notice of taking of deposition is also served, that other notice may be set forth in a single document with the notice required by this subdivision.”

 

There is nothing on the record indicating that Plaintiff was ever served with a notice of privacy rights. From the copy of the subpoena provided, it also does not appear that Grewal was served with either a proof of service of the Notice to Consumer to Plaintiff or Plaintiff’s written authorization to release her personal records. The subpoena, therefore, is defective in this regard as well.

 

Furthermore, the notice of deposition served on Plaintiff is also defective. The notice states: “NOTICE IS FURTHER GIVEN that the Most Knowledgeable Person at ELITE AUTO BODY COLLISION, INC., shall produce” the requested documents related to Plaintiff’s medical records as detailed above. (Hovsepian Decl., Ex. 1, p. 2:22-24.) The deposition subpoena for personal appearance and production of documents, however, identifies Grewal as associated with Advanced Pain Management, not Elite Auto Body Collision, Inc. Additionally, as discussed, Defendants must serve on Plaintiff not only a notice of the deposition, but also a notice of privacy rights and a copy of the deposition subpoena. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.240.) Here, there is no evidence that Plaintiff was served with any document other than the NOD and a copy of the deposition subpoena. The NOD does not contain the information required by Section 1985.3(e) and no separate Notice to Consumer has been provided.

 

Finally, Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.270, subdivision (c) states that if “the party giving notice of the deposition is a subpoenaing party, and the deponent is a witness commanded by a deposition subpoena to produce personal records of a consumer or employment records of an employee, the deposition shall be scheduled for a date at least 20 days after issuance of that subpoena.” Here, per Defendants, the notice of deposition was issued on April 28, 2026, served on Grewal on May 1, 2026, and set the deposition and date of production for May 12, 2026. Pursuant to Section 2025.270(c), Defendants were required to set the deposition for a date at least 20 days after the subpoena and deposition notice were issued, rendering the earliest possible date for which Defendants could set this deposition May 18, 2026.

 

Accordingly, Defendants did not properly serve the deposition subpoena and required Notice to Consumer on either Grewal or Plaintiff. Defendants’ motion is DENIED without prejudice. Defendants may properly issue, notice, and serve a deposition subpoena in accordance with the governing authorities, and this Order shall not be construed to preclude Defendants’ from seeking enforcement through the Court in the event of proper issuance and subsequent noncompliance, including as to non-party witness Grewal.

    V.          Conclusion

 

Defendants Damian Demetrius Coborn and Altaveese Correa’s Motion to Compel the Deposition of Narinder S. Grewal, M.D.’s Deposition and Request for Sanctions are DENIED. This Order is made without prejudice to Defendants’ right to seek relief for any future noncompliance with a properly issued subpoena.

      I.          Background

 

This is a breach of contract action. Defendants Fredd Garcia (Garcia) and Bo Kim (Kim) move to stay the case and compel arbitration.

 

On September 24, 2025, Plaintiff Muideen Ganiyu (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendants Garcia, Kim, and Manuel Tambunting (Tambunting) asserting fourteen causes of action for (1) breach of contract, (2) violation of Civil Code section 1102, (3) violation of Civil Code section 1102.6, (4) violation of Civil Code section 1102.13, (5) violation of Civil Code section 2079, (6) violation of Business and Professions Code section 10176(a), (7) fraudulent concealment (Civil Code sections 1709, 1710), (8) negligent misrepresentation, (9) professional negligence, (10) intentional infliction of emotional distress, (11) unjust enrichment, (12) fraud, (13) fraudulent inducement, and (14) negligence per se.

 

Plaintiff alleges that on September 14, 2024, Plaintiff entered into a Residential Purchase Agreement to purchase the real property located at 37643 Barrinson Street, Palmdale, CA 93550 (the Property) for $469,000. (Compl., ¶ 14.) Garcia acted as the seller’s agent, while Kim served as the supervising broker, who was allegedly on notice of Garcia’s misconduct and failed to take action. (Compl., ¶ 15.) Plaintiff claims the Property was falsely marketed and sold as a single family home suitable for immediate residential use, and that Defendants together coordinated a scheme to defraud Plaintiff by failing to disclose extensive defects in the Property. (Compl., ¶¶ 17-20.)

 

On December 18, 2025, litigation in the present case was stayed pending completion of mediation. The stay was lifted on June 22, 2026.

 

On August 10, 2026, Defendants Garcia and Kim filed a motion to stay the case and compel arbitration. This motion is unopposed.

 

-----

 

   II.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Compelling Arbitration – California law incorporates many of the basic policy objectives contained in the Federal Arbitration Act, including a presumption in favor of arbitrability. (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 971-72.) Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2 permits a party to file a motion to request that the Court order the parties to arbitrate a controversy.

 

The party seeking to enforce the arbitration agreement bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence. (Giuliano v. Inland Empire Personnel, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1276, 1284.) The trial court first decides whether an enforceable arbitration agreement exists between the parties, then determine whether the plaintiff’s claims are covered by the agreement. (Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961.) The party opposing the petition to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense. (Giuliano v. Inland Empire Personnel, Inc., supra, at 1284.) In these summary proceedings, the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination. (Id.)

 

Barring certain circumstances set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2, the Court must compel arbitration upon a finding that: (1) there was a valid agreement to arbitrate between the parties; and (2) said agreement covers the controversy or controversies in the parties’ dispute. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; See Omar, supra,¿118 Cal.App.4th at 961.) Because public policy strongly favors arbitration, “any doubts regarding the arbitrability of a dispute are resolved in favor of arbitration.”  (Coast Plaza Doctors Hospital v. Blue Cross of California (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 677, 686.)

 

-----

 

 III.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendants Kim and Garcia (hereinafter “Defendants”) move to compel arbitration and stay this case pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 1281, 1281.2, 1281.4, and 1281.7 on the grounds that the purchase sale agreement requires arbitration and that Defendants are permitted to compel arbitration. (Motion, p. 2:3-12.)

 

On September 14, 2024, Defendant Tambunting entered into a purchase sale agreement and Joint Escrow Instructions (the Agreement) with Plaintiff for the sale of the Property. Plaintiff was represented by non-party realtor Reza Mohajer of Premier Agent Network, Inc.

 

Defendants attach as Exhibit A to their Motion a copy of the Agreement. Paragraph 28 of the Agreement contains an “Arbitration of Disputes” provision dated September 14, 2024 and initialed by Plaintiff. The provision provides, in pertinent part:

 

(A) The Parties agree that any dispute or claim in Law or equity arising out of this Agreement or any resulting transaction, which is not settled through mediation, shall be decided by neutral, binding arbitration. The parties also agree to arbitrate any disputes or claims with Agents(s), who, in writing, agree to such arbitration prior to, or within a reasonable time after, the dispute or claim is presented to the Agent.

…

(E) NOTICE: BY INITIALING IN THE SPACE BELOW YOU ARE AGREEING TO HAVE ANY DISPUTE ARISING OUT OF THE MATTERS INCLUDED IN THE ‘ARBITRATION OF DISPUTES’ PROVISION DECIDED BY NEUTAL ARBITRATION AS PROVIDED BY CALIFORNIA LAW AND YOU ARE GIVING UP ANY RIGHTS YOU MIGHT POSSESS TO HAVE THE DISPUTE LITIGATED IN A COURT OR JURY TRIAL. BY INITIALING IN THE SPACE BELOW YOU ARE GIVING UP YOUR JUDICIAL RIGHTS TO DISCOVERY AND APPEAL, UNLESS THOSE RIGHTS ARE SPECIFICALLY INCLUDED IN THE ‘ARBITRATION OF DISPUTES’ PROVISION. IF YOU REFUSE TO SUBMIT TO ARBITRATION AFTER AGREEING TO THIS PROVISION, YOU MAY BE COMPELLED TO ARBITRATE UNDER THE AUTHORITY OF THE CALIFORINIA CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE. YOUR AGREEMENT TO THIS ARBITRATION PROVISION IS VOLUNTARY.

 

(Garcia Decl., p. 14, ¶ 28.)

 

The Agreement is signed only by Plaintiff and Defendant Tambunting. However, “[n]onsignatory defendants may enforce arbitration agreements ‘where there is sufficient identity of parties.’” (Marenco v. DirecTV LLC (2015) 233 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1417.) Enforcement by a nonsignatory is permitted where the nonsignatory is the agent for a party to an arbitration agreement, is a third party beneficiary of the agreement, or establishes right to enforce under the doctrine of equitable estoppel. (Ibid.) Equitable estoppel “applies where, for example, a signatory plaintiff sues a nonsignatory defendant for claims that are based on an underlying contract. In such instance, the plaintiff may be equitably estopped to deny the nonsignatory defendant's right to enforce an arbitration clause that is contained within the contract that the plaintiff has placed at issue.” (Ibid.)

 

Here, Defendants are entitled to enforce arbitration under the Agreement. Defendants were the agents of Defendant Tambunting, a signatory of the Agreement and arbitration provision. (Garcia Decl., ¶ 1.) The first page of the Agreement states “[t]he following agency relationships are hereby confirmed for this transaction,” and lists Leader One Realty, Inc., Kim’s brokerage firm, as Seller’s Brokerage Firm, and names Garcia as the Seller’s Agent. (Ex. A, p. 1, ¶ 2.) The express language of the arbitration provision states that the parties agree to arbitrate all disputes or claims with the Agents who agree in writing to arbitrate, either prior to or within a reasonable time after the dispute or claim is presented.

 

Plaintiff’s complaint was filed in September of 2025. Defendants were served in October of 2025. On December 18, 2025, the parties stipulated to stay the case pending completion of mediation in accordance with section 27(a) of the Agreement. Defendants assert that on June 4, 2026, the parties mediated with Rose Nazarin, Esq., but the case did not settle. (Motion, p. 4:3-4; Weiss Decl., ¶ 1.) On June 26, 2026, Defense counsel Richard Weiss promptly sent a letter via email to Plaintiff’s counsel demanding arbitration. (Motion, p. 4:4-10; Weiss Decl., ¶ 2.) Plaintiff’s counsel has since been relieved as counsel of record.

 

Under the express terms of the Agreement, namely the Agency relationship created in paragraph 2, the mediation prerequisite described in paragraph 27(a), the language of the arbitration agreement in paragraph 28, and Defendants’ clear agreement to mediate and arbitrate the dispute under the Agreement within two months of service, Defendants are entitled to enforce the terms of this Agreement and compel arbitration. (See also Dryer v. Los Angeles Rams (1985) 40 Cal.3d 406, 418.)

 

Defendants argue that they are also agents of signatory Defendant Tambunting under general California agency law. In a real property transaction, an “agent” is “a person acting under provisions of Title 9 (commencing with Section 2295) in a real property transaction, and includes a person who is licensed as a real estate broker under Chapter 3 (commencing with Section 10130) of Part 1 of Division 4 of the Business and Professions Code, and under whose license a listing is executed or an offer to purchase is obtained.” (Civ. Code, § 2079.13, subd. (a).) “When a salesperson or broker associate owes a duty to any principal, or to any buyer or seller who is not a principal, in a real property transaction, that duty is equivalent to the duty owed to that party by the broker for whom the salesperson or broker associate functions.” (Civ. Code, § 2079.13, subd. (a).) “[A]n associate licensee does not have an independent agency relationship with the clients of his or her broker, but rather an agency relationship that is derived from the agency relationship between the broker and the client. Against that backdrop, Civil Code section 2079.13, subdivision (b), is most naturally read to mean that the associate licensee owes the parties to that transaction the same duties as the broker on whose behalf he or she acts.” (Horiike v. Coldwell Banker Residential Brokerage Co. (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1024, 1037.) Here, there is a clear agency relationship, with Kim serving as broker and Garcia as an agent under Kim’s brokerage. Therefore, general agency law creates a clear agency relationship between Tambunting and Defendants, and for the reasons discussed above, Defendants are entitled to enforce the Agreement.

 

Finally, Defendants argue they are also entitled to enforce the Agreement under the doctrine of equitable estoppel. Under both California and federal law, “a nonsignatory defendant may invoke an arbitration clause to compel a signatory plaintiff to arbitrate its claims when the causes of action against the nonsignatory are ‘intimately founded in and intertwined’ with the underlying contract obligations.” (Molecular Analytical Systems v. Ciphergen Biosystems, Inc. (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 696, 706.) “By relying on contract terms in a claim against a nonsignatory defendant, even if not exclusively, a plaintiff may be equitably estopped from repudiating the arbitration clause contained in that agreement.” (Ibid.)

 

The gravamen of Plaintiff’s case against Defendants is their breach of the Agreement and their duties thereunder to deliver the Property in habitable condition suitable for residential use. (Compl., ¶ 31.) Further allegations include inadequate disclosures in the Seller Property Questionnaire (Compl., ¶ 36), failure to disclose structural modifications on the Transfer Disclosure Statement (Compl., ¶ 42), failure to amend disclosures after execution of the Agreement (Compl., ¶¶ 47-48), breach of professional duty as licensed real estate professionals (Compl., ¶ 73), and fraudulently inducing Plaintiff into entering into the Agreement by knowingly making false and misrepresentative statements of fact and concealing material facts (Compl., ¶¶ 54, 57, 63, 69, 78, 85, 90). These claims against Defendants all arise out of the Agreement for sale of the Property, Defendants’ duties thereunder, the breach of those duties, and the alleged fraud, concealments, and misrepresentations in connection with the sale of the Property. These claims rely on the existence of the Agreement and Plaintiff’s agreement to purchase the Property and are so intimately founded in and intertwined with the underlying Agreement that equitable estoppel applies and provides another basis for Defendants’ enforcement of the arbitration provision.

 

Accordingly, Defendants are entitled as non-signatories to compel arbitration based on the express language in the Agreement, general California agency law, and equitable estoppel. The language of the arbitration agreement encompasses any dispute or claim in law or equity arising out of the Agreement or any resulting transaction that is not settled through mediation, including all claims against Agents. Such broad language unequivocally encompasses Plaintiff’s claims, which all arise out of the sale of the Property. Plaintiff has not opposed this motion and previously submitted to mediation pursuant to section 27(a) of the same Agreement, and mediation was unsuccessful.

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ motion to compel is GRANTED.

 IV.          Conclusion

 

Defendants Bo Kim and Fredd Garcia’s Motion to Stay This Case and Compel Arbitration is GRANTED.

On September 9, 2026, Plaintiff filed her opposition.

 

On September 15, 2026, Defendant filed its reply.

 

-----

 

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Meet and Confer Requirement – A motion to compel further responses must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration showing “a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2016.040, 2030.300, subd. (b)(1), 2031.310, subd. (b)(2).)

 

Defendant’s counsel Susan S. Arnall provides her declaration, detailing the meet and confer efforts between the parties, including a telephone conversation, after which Plaintiff’s counsel agreed to provide further responses. (Arnall Decl., ¶¶ 5-13.) This requirement is satisfied.

 

-----

 

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Motions to Compel Further Discovery Responses - Interrogatories – In responding to a set of interrogatories, a party must provide one of the following: (1) an answer “containing the information sought to be discovered”; (2) “an exercise of the party’s option to produce writings”; or (3) an objection to the particular interrogatory. (Code Civ. Proc.,  § 2030.210, subd. (a).) Each answer to an interrogatory “shall be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220, subd. (a).) If any objections are made, “the specific ground for the objection shall be set forth clearly in the response.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.240, subd. (b).) 

Upon receipt of responses to interrogatories, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if they deem the responses to be “evasive or incomplete” or if “[a]n objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a)(1), (a)(3).) The motion must also be accompanied by a separate statement containing the text of the interrogatories at issue, the text of each response at issue, and a statement of the reasons why a further response is required, though the court may permit a party to submit “a concise outline of the discovery request and each response in dispute” in lieu of a separate statement. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (b)(2); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345.)  

A motion to compel further response lies where the party to whom the interrogatories were directed gave responses deemed improper by the propounding party; e.g., objections, or evasive or incomplete answers. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300.) Unlike a motion to compel further responses to a request for production, there is no “good cause” requirement applicable to a motion to compel further responses to interrogatories. “The ruling usually is based on consideration of the following factors:  the relationship of the information sought to the issues framed in the pleadings; the likelihood that disclosure will be of practical benefit to the party seeking discovery; the burden or expense likely to be encountered by the responding party in furnishing the information sought.” (Weil, et al., Cal. Prac. Guide, Civil Procedure Before Trial, Ch. 8F-7, ¶ 8:1181; see also Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc v. Superior Court (1968) 263 Cal.App.2d 12, 18–19 [“[I]n deciding a motion under [former] section 2030 the trial court must, of necessity, consider not only the stated objections to the interrogatories, but also the interrogatories themselves, as well as the pleadings, and the contentions of the interrogating party as to the purpose and validity of the interrogatories. If the interrogatories stray too far and seek information which cannot reasonably serve the acknowledged purposes of pretrial discovery, the motion should be denied.”].) If a timely motion to compel has been filed, the burden is on the responding party to justify any objection or failure to fully answer. (Coy v. Superior Court of Contra Costa County (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220–221.) 

If a party fails to serve a timely response to interrogatories, the party waives the right to exercise the option to produce writings under Section 2030.230 and waives any objection to the demand, including objections based on privilege or work product. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).) The Court may relieve a party of this waiver only if the party has subsequently served a substantially code-compliant response and the party’s failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a)(1)-(2).)

-----

 

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendant moves to compel further responses to Special Interrogatories, Set Two from Plaintiff pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300 and California Rules of Court rule 3.1345 on the grounds that Plaintiff’s responses are not as complete and straightforward as required.

 

Defendant served Plaintiff with its Special Interrogatories, Set Two on May 19, 2026, and Plaintiff served her response on June 23, 2026. (Arnall Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) On July 24, 2026, counsel for the parties conferred, and Plaintiff’s counsel agreed to provide further code-compliant responses by July 31, 2026, but did not do so. (Arnall Decl., ¶¶ 11-12.) At issue are Interrogatory Nos. 44, 45, and 46. Defendant argues Plaintiff’s responses to these objections consisted of boilerplate objections without merit, and that the responses are not code-compliant.

 

Interrogatory No. 44 reads: “Do YOU contend that the LIFTED PIECE OF CONCRETE was located at a seam between two adjacent concrete sidewalk panels on the date of the INCIDENT?”  

 

Interrogatory No. 45 reads: “If YOU contend that the LIFTED PIECE OF CONCRETE was located at a seam between two adjacent concrete sidewalk panels, state the HEIGHT DIFFERENTIAL, in inches, between the LIFTED PIECE OF CONCRETE and the lower, adjacent concrete sidewalk panel on the date of the INCIDENT.”

 

Interrogatory No. 46 reads: “If the LIFTED PIECE OF CONCRETE was located at a seam between two adjacent concrete sidewalk panels, state the HEIGHT DIFFERENTIAL between those two adjacent concrete sidewalk panels that constituted the DANGEROUS CONDITION OF PUBLIC PROPERTY.”

 

The terms “lifted piece of concrete,” “height differential,” and “dangerous condition of public property” are defined.

 

Plaintiff objected on the grounds that the interrogatories are vague, ambiguous, oppressive, harassing, burdensome, seek Plaintiff’s counsel’s legal analysis, invade the attorney’s work product privilege, call for an expert opinion, call for improper lay witness opinion, call for speculation, and violate Code of Civil Procedure, Section 2030.060(d). Plaintiff further responded that as to Interrogatory Nos. 44 and 45, she was unable to make such a contention at the time of response, and as to all three Interrogatories, deferred to her experts’ opinions, and states that discovery and investigations are continuing and ongoing.

 

Defendant argues these interrogatories are authorized, straightforward, and address specific allegations in Plaintiff’s complaint, while her responses were evasive, ambiguous, and not code-compliant.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that no further response should be required, as the Interrogatories seek retrospective technical opinions regarding the sidewalk’s historical configuration and precise measurements to be rendered by Plaintiff, a lay witness, based on her personal firsthand knowledge that she encountered lifted concrete. (Opp., p. 3:9-13.) Plaintiff argues that a forced “yes” or “no” answer would require Plaintiff to guess at a technical classification, and that a forced, unqualified statement that Plaintiff “does not know” would be misleading and risks portraying her response as an admission that she does not know what condition caused her fall and subsequent injury. (Opp., p. 4:3-6.)

 

“An interrogatory may relate to whether another party is making a certain contention, or to the facts, witnesses, and writings on which a contention is based,” but an interrogatory “is not objectionable because an answer to it involves an opinion or contention that relates to fact or the application of law to fact, or would be based on information obtained or legal theories developed in anticipation of litigation or in preparation for trial.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.010, subd. (b).) Moreover, “[a] party may not deliberately misconstrue a question for the purpose of supplying an evasive answer,” and “where the question is somewhat ambiguous, but the nature of the information sought is apparent, the proper solution is to provide an appropriate response.” (Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 783.)

 

Answers to interrogatories must be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits, shall be answered to the extent possible if it cannot be answered in full, and if the responding party does not have personal knowledge sufficient to respond fully, that party must state as much after making a reasonable and good faith effort to obtain the information by inquiry to other natural persons except where the information is equally available to the propounding party. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.220.) If a party can provide partial answers, the answers must be provided and must reveal “all information then available to the party.” (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at 782.) “If a person cannot furnish details, he should set forth the efforts made to secure the information,” and “cannot plead ignorance to information which can be obtained from sources under his control.” (Ibid.)

 

Here, Plaintiff’s responses that she cannot make the requested contention and defers to her experts are insufficient. Interrogatory No. 44 asks whether Plaintiff contends the lifted concrete was at a seam between two adjacent sidewalk panels, which Plaintiff argues embeds a technical premise, as Defendant does not define the phrase or specify whether “seam” refers to a formed construction joint, expansion or control joint, visible scoring line, crack, or some other interface. While the term “seam between two adjacent concrete sidewalk panels” may present some ambiguity in the technical sense, in light of the images referenced in the Interrogatories and the common understanding of the phrase, the information sought by Interrogatory No. 44 is apparent, and Plaintiff cannot evade providing an appropriate response based on personal knowledge and information available to her based on the limitless technical interpretations one could impose on the word “seam.” Plaintiff herself concedes that a pedestrian can perceive and identify raised or lifted concrete surface. (Opp., p. 6:8-9.) It is based in part on this perception and identification that Plaintiff must respond, and as Defendant asserts, the Interrogatory is not asking for a technical evaluation, determination of the boundary between constructed sidewalk panels, or specialized knowledge.

 

Therefore, the Court finds that the information requested is clear enough that the objections based on vagueness and ambiguity are not warranted. If Plaintiff has a contention one way or the other, she must provide it. If, after reasonable inquiry, Plaintiff maintains that she has insufficient information to formulate any contention regarding the lifted concrete on the date of the incident, or if any such contention is solely based on her expert(s) reports, measurements, technical analysis, opinion, and inspection, Plaintiff may so state, but her present response is nonetheless inadequate, as she still must affirm that a reasonable and good faith effort was made to obtain the information and set forth the efforts made to secure the information. She has not done so, and the Court therefore agrees that further response is required.

 

The same is true as to Interrogatory Nos. 45 and 46, Defendant requests that Plaintiff state a precise measurement - the height differential between the lifted and non-lifted portions of concrete on the date of the incident. If Plaintiff does not have this information or otherwise only has information contained in currently protected expert reports and writings, she must still affirm that a reasonable and good faith effort was made to obtain the information and set forth the efforts made to secure the information in addition to asserting her objections.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion is GRANTED, and Plaintiff shall provide code-compliant responses to Interrogatory Nos. 44-46.

 

Sanctions – “The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct. The court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney who advised that assertion, or on both.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (a).)

 

Defendant requests sanctions in the amount of $3,465.00, consisting of 1.5 hours engaging in meet and confer efforts, 2.5 hours preparing the motion, 1.0 hour preparing the separate statement, and an anticipated 3.0 hours for preparing and attending the hearing on the motion, for a total of 11.0 hours of work at $315/hour. (Arnall Decl., ¶ 14.)

 

Here, the Court finds that sanctions are not warranted. While Plaintiff’s responses are not code-compliant, as discussed above, the Court also finds that they are not necessarily evasive. Defendant requests specific measurements, in inches, from April 4, 2025, and Plaintiff’s contentions regarding the location of lifted concrete. It is not apparent here that Plaintiff is lodging boilerplate objections without reasonable basis. Moreover, Plaintiff’s responses, while they do not assert that reasonable inquiry has been made or state what efforts were made to secure the information aside from retained expert evaluation, inspection, and measurements, do state that Plaintiff cannot make the requested contention, and that the contention is based on her retained experts’ findings, which will be disclosed as and when required. Moreover, it appears the parties engaged in good faith meet and confer efforts, and while Plaintiff’s counsel failed to provide supplemental responses, Plaintiff’s three deficient responses do not rise to the level of misuse of the discovery process warranting the requested sanctions.

 

Accordingly, the Court declines to award monetary sanctions at this time, and Defendant’s request is DENIED.

    V.          Conclusion

 

Defendant City of Palmdale’s Motion to Compel Further Responses to Special Interrogatories, Set Two is GRANTED. Plaintiff shall provide code-compliant responses to Interrogatory Nos. 44, 45, and 46 within ten (10) days of this Order.

 

Defendant’s Request for Monetary Sanctions is DENIED.

On August 31, 2026, Williams filed her Oppositions.

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Formatting of Wiliams’ FACC – The FACC is not in compliance with the pleading requirements of the California Rules of Court, which require for all documents filed with the Court specific line spacing, 12 point font size, a footer with the requisite specifications and containing the title of the paper, specific information to be contained on the first page, and compliance with various other formatting rules. (See Cal. Rules of Court rules 2.104 – 2.111.) The causes of action in the FACC do not specifically state the party or parties to whom they are directed. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.112.)

 

Evidence Submitted by Williams – Williams submits a number of exhibits of which the Court has not been asked to take judicial notice. A ruling on a demurrer or motion to strike may not be based on extrinsic evidence and must be based on the face of the pleading and properly judicially noticed documents. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  The Court therefore declines to consider the declaration or evidence submitted by Williams in opposition.

 

Meet and Confer Requirement – Before filing a demurrer or motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due, in person, by telephone, or by video conference, with the party who filed the pleading for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer or motion to strike. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a), 435.5, subd. (a).).) The demurring or moving party shall file and serve with the demurrer or motion to strike a declaration stating the means by which the parties met and conferred and that the parties did not reach an agreement to resolve the issues raised, or that the party who filed the pleading demurred or objected to failed to respond or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith. (Ibid.) 

 

Glendale provides the Declaration of Counsel Alana Anaya, who states she sent Williams a meet and confer letter on May 27, 2026 regarding the deficiencies raised on demurrer. Williams responded the same day that she disagreed, and declined to amend the FACC. The Court finds this requirement satisfied.

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Demurrer – A party against whom a complaint has been filed may object by demurrer to the pleading on the ground that the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action or that the pleading is uncertain. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e), (f).) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations reasonably, liberally, and in context. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) Courts must “treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at 318.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 126.) The threshold for meeting this burden is low, and “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

 

Standard for Motion to Strike – A motion to strike lies where either (1) there is “irrelevant, false or improper matter inserted in any pleading,” or (2) where a pleading or part thereof is “not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule or order of court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The Court has the authority to strike out any part of a pleading not filed in conformity with an order of the court. (See Ibid; Ferraro v. Camarlinghi (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 509, 528.) 

When the pleadings are defective, “the defect may be raised by demurrer or motion to strike, or by motion for judgment on the pleadings.” (Coyne v. Krempels (1950) 36 Cal.2d 257, 262.) The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or form any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (a).) A matter to be judicially noticed shall be specified in the notice of motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (b).) The court then may strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading and strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend. (Perlman v. Municipal Court (1979) 99 Cal.App.3d 568, 575.)

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Glendale demurs to the FACC in its entirety pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivisions (e)  and (f) on the grounds that the FACC fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action and is uncertain.

 

a.     Glendale’s Demurrer to the FACC

 

                                                  i.     Uncertain, Ambiguous, and Unintelligible

 

Glendale demurs to the FACC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (f) on the basis that the FACC and allegations within are uncertain, ambiguous, and unintelligible.

 

“A defendant may demur to a complaint… on the ground that the ‘pleading is uncertain.’ (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) The term ‘uncertain’ includes ‘ambiguous” and ‘unintelligible.’ (Ibid.).” (Van Horn v. Dep't of Toxic Substances Control (2014) 231 Cal. App. 4th 1287, 1295.) A “[d]emurrer for uncertainty is directed at the allegations actually made. [Citation.] Demurrer for uncertainty does not lie as to immaterial matter. [Citation.] Nor will a demurrer for uncertainty lie where the facts are presumptively within the knowledge of the demurring parties. [Citation.]” (Dumm v. Pac. Valves (1956) 146 Cal. App. 2d 792, 799.) “A special demurrer on the ground that an answer is (a) ambiguous, (b) unintelligible, or (c) uncertain is insufficient unless the demurrer points out specifically wherein the pleading is ambiguous, uncertain or unintelligible. [Citation.].” (Coons v. Thompson (1946) 75 Cal. App. 2d 687, 690 (citation omitted).)

 

Glendale argues the FACC is uncertain because it is devoid of specific facts, dates, or identification of parties, does not specifically identify agreements referred to, and does not define the content of certain statements. (Demurrer, p. 5:2-17.) These arguments are more akin to a demurrer on the grounds that the FACC has failed to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action under subsection (e). Moreover, the Court finds that the FACC is not so unambiguous or unintelligible that Glendale cannot adequately respond, particularly where it has filed a detailed, multi-page demurrer addressing each cause of action asserted. While there are deficiencies in the FACC and identification of parties, discussed, infra, aside from the Doe Cross-Defendants, the only named Cross-Defendant is Glendale, and the FACC is clear enough to provide notice of the causes of action asserted.

 

Glendale’s Demurrer is OVERRULED to the extent it is brought pursuant to Section 430.10, subdivision (f) for ambiguity.

 

                                                ii.     First Cause of Action – Breach of Contract

 

"To prevail on a cause of action for breach of contract, the plaintiff must prove (1) the contract, (2) plaintiff's performance of the contract or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant's breach, and (4) resulting damage to the plaintiff." (Richman v. Hartley (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1182, 1186.)

 

Glendale argues the first cause of action is subject to demurrer pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (g) because it is unascertainable whether the contract is written, oral, or implied by conduct. (Demurrer, p. 6:8-9.) Glendale did not demur pursuant to subdivision (g), so the Court cannot sustain a demurrer on that ground, and therefore declines to analyze the argument. However, Glendale also argues this claim fails because the FACC does not specify what agreements were breached, what party breached, or otherwise plead the existence of a contract. (Demurrer, p. 6:1-5.) The Court agrees.

 

The FACC alleges that Cross-Defendants materially breached agreements relating to the installation, servicing, operation, financing, and completion of the SES by failing to provide a functional SES, complete installation, obtain Permission to Operate (PTO), provide promised services, ensure the SES became operational, properly complete inspection and permitting requirements, and continuing enforcement efforts despite unresolved installation defects and lack of PTO. (FACC, ¶ 32.) The FACC names a number of entities, including Enium Capital Group, Voltaic Construction, Flex Energy Solar, and their related agents, and appears to refer to these entities as their corporate names, “Defendants,” and “Cross-Defendants” interchangeably throughout the FACC, despite none of these companies being named as parties in this action. However, the FACC does not allege the existence of any contract with Glendale, does not attach or define the terms of a contract, does not allege that Glendale ever agreed to install, permit, obtain PTO for, operate, or inspect any SES on the Property, and does not allege that Glendale’s right to recover on the loan was ever contingent on the SES becoming operational. The FACC also does not allege that Williams performed under a contract or was otherwise excused from performance.

 

In opposition, Williams refers to extrinsic exhibits to reinforce her claim, none of which may be considered by the Court on demurrer. (Opp., p. 3:7-8.) Williams also argues that all reasonable inferences must be accepted as true. (Opp., p. 2:7-8.) However, on the FACC’s factual allegations, there is no reasonable inference that Glendale, a state credit union and financer, entered into a contract with Williams, the borrower, for installation, obtaining of permits, servicing, or operation of an SES. There is also no reasonable inference that Plaintiff was in any way discharged from making payments on her loan based on the project’s non-operational and incomplete status, which Williams appears to attribute to third party entities.

 

The FACC fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for breach of contract against Glendale, and the Demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the first cause of action. The Court will grant leave to amend in the event that Williams can sufficiently plead a breach of contract claim against Glendale.

 

                                              iii.     Second Cause of Action – Promissory Estoppel

 

“The elements of a promissory estoppel claim are (1) a promise clear and unambiguous in its terms; (2) reliance by the party to whom the promise is made; (3) the reliance must be both reasonable and foreseeable; and (4) the party asserting the estoppel must be injured by his reliance.” (Flintco Pacific, Inc. v. TEC Management Consultants, Inc. (2016) 1 Cal.App.5th 727, 734, quotation marks and brackets omitted.)

 

The FACC does not state facts sufficient to state a cause of action for promissory estoppel against Glendale. Specifically, the second cause of action states that “Representatives associated with Enium made promises and representations” and that Williams reasonably relied on these promises. (FACC, ¶¶ 35-36.) This allegation does not relate to Glendale. There are no allegations of any clear and unambiguous promise made by Glendale to Williams, reasonable and foreseeable reliance, or injury therefrom. There are also no allegations that Enium is associated with Glendale, that Glendale ever entered into an agreement with Williams and/or Enium regarding payments, or that Glendale ever adopted Enium’s alleged promise to continue payments until the SES reached PTO status. At best, Williams may have a cause of action against Enium, who is not a party to this case, but there is nothing to support a claim against Glendale.

 

Glendale’s Demurer is SUSTAINED as to the second cause of action. The Court will grant leave to amend in the event that Williams can plead this cause of action against Glendale based on some other ground.

 

                                               iv.     Third Cause of Action – Negligent Misrepresentation

 

The elements of a cause of action for negligent misrepresentation are “[m]isrepresentation of a past or existing material fact, without reasonable ground for believing it to be true, and with intent to induce another’s reliance on the fact misrepresented; ignorance of the truth and justifiable reliance on the misrepresentation by the party to whom it was directed; and resulting damage.” (Hydro-Mill Co., Inc. v. Hayward, Tilton & Rolapp Ins. Associates, Inc. (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 1145, 1154.) The facts constituting the alleged misrepresentation must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element as with a general fraud claim, and must plead facts that show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645; SI 59 LLC v. Variel Warner Ventures, LLC (2018) 29 Cal.App.5th 146, 155.) “California courts have recognized a cause of action for negligent misrepresentation, i.e., a duty to communicate accurate information, in two circumstances. The first situation arises where providing false information poses a risk of and results in physical harm to person or property. The second situation arises where information is conveyed in a commercial setting for a business purpose.” (Friedman v. Merck & Co. (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 454, 477.)

 

Glendale reiterates for all remaining causes of action that there are insufficient facts pled to state a cause of action. As to misrepresentation and fraud, Glendale argues the FACC fails to state facts with the requisite particularity. (Demurrer, p. 8:9-11.) Williams reiterates her opposition and contention that the remaining causes of action state sufficient facts, relying on extrinsic evidence not appropriate for consideration on demurrer.

 

The FACC fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for negligent misrepresentation. The FACC references representations made by “[r]epresentatives associated with Enium, Voltaic, and/or other Cross-Defendants” regarding the SES’s operational status, completion of installation, PTO approval, payment handling, inspection compliance, and expected completion and usability of the SES. (FACC, ¶ 39.) There are no facts regarding any representation made by Glendale, what that representation was, who made it, in what manner it was made, or when it was made. There are no facts alleged regarding the whether reasonable grounds existed for Glendale believing it to be true, or Glendale’s intent to induce reliance. The FACC does not connect Glendale with any of the non-party entities implicated or establish that Glendale made any representation regarding collection of payment and installation completion, PTO, or operational status of the SES. There are also no allegations to support that Glendale owed a duty to communicate accurate information.

 

Glendale’s Demurer is SUSTAINED as to the third cause of action. The Court will grant leave to amend in the event that Williams can plead this cause of action against Glendale based on some other representation made with the requisite particularity.

 

                                                v.     Fourth Cause of Action – Intentional Misrepresentation and Fraud

 

The essential elements of fraud, generally, are (1) a misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (2) knowledge of falsity; (3) intent to induce reliance; (4) justifiable reliance; and (5) resulting damage. (City of Industry v. City of Fillmore (2011) 198 Ca.App.4th 191, 211; Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Ctr. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) Fraud must be specifically pleaded. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.) “This particularity requirement necessitates pleading facts which 'show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.'” (Id. at 645 (citations omitted).)

 

The FACC alleges facts related to communications from Enium, states that “Cross-Defendants further represented that the solar system would be completed, operational, compliant with permitting and inspection requirements, and capable of providing the promised benefits associated with the solar transaction.” (FACC, ¶¶ 44-45.) There are no specific factual allegations as to any representation or fraud attributable to Glendale. For the same reasons as stated above regarding the third cause of action, the FACC fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for fraud or intentional misrepresentation.

 

Glendale’s Demurer is SUSTAINED as to the fourth cause of action. The Court will grant leave to amend in the event that Williams can plead this cause of action against Glendale based on some other representation made or fraud committed with the requisite particularity.

 

                                               vi.     Fifth Cause of Action – Declaratory Relief

 

“To qualify for declaratory relief, a party would have to demonstrate its action presented two essential elements: (1) a proper subject of declaratory relief, and (2) an actual controversy involving justiciable questions relating to the party’s rights or obligations.” (Jolley v. Chase Home Finance, LLC (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 872, 909.)

 

Glendale argues the fifth cause of action fails to specify which financing obligations the FACC is referring to, and that if a contract for financing exists, then Williams must seek relief under a breach of contract theory, not under a declaratory relief claim. (Demurrer, p. 8:27-9:2.) Williams argues in opposition that the FACC alleges an actual controversy concerning the enforceability of financing obligations for the SES alleged never to have become operational or received PTO. (Opp., p. 4:2-3.) The Court notes that contrary to Glendale’s argument, it does not appear the fifth cause of action is seeking a sum of monetary damages from Glendale. (See Canova v. Trustees of Imperial Irrigation Dist. Employee Pension Plan (2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 1487, 1497.)

 

Nonetheless, the FACC does not plead sufficient facts to maintain this cause of action against Glendale for two reasons. First, it merely pleads that a controversy exists regarding “the enforceability of financing obligations associated with a solar system that allegedly failed to become operational, failed to obtain PTO, and failed to provide the promised benefits contemplated by the parties,” and that Williams seeks judicial determination regarding “responsibility for removal of the solar panels, equipment, mounting hardware, wiring, and related solar components from Cross-Complainant’s property, as well as restoration of the property, due to the alleged failure of the solar system to become operational and obtain PTO” and “the parties’ respective rights, obligations, and liabilities arising from the solar transaction.” (FACC, ¶¶ 52-54.) The FACC does not specify what financial obligations it refers to, who the obligations belong to, what parties are implicated, or what “solar transaction” is referenced. There are no factual allegations connecting Glendale to any of the alleged transactions, representations, agreements, or obligations, and no allegations support a reasonable inference that Glendale is somehow implicated in Williams’ communications, transactions, or agreements with third party entities who are not named in this action.

 

Second, the relief sought and issues raised in the FACC, at least to the extent that they can be attributable to Glendale, are duplicative of the issues that must be determined in Glendale’s underlying action for declaratory relief. As Williams notes in opposition, “Glendale Financial itself is pursing enforcement of the financing obligation.” (Opp., p. 4:3-4.)

 

A cause of action for declaratory relief should not be used as a second cause of action for the determination of identical issues raised in another cause of action. (General of America Insurance Co. v. Lilly (1968) 258 Cal.App.2d 465, 470.) “The availability of another form of relief that is adequate will usually justify refusal to grant declaratory relief” (California Insurance Guarantee Association v. Superior Court (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1617, 1624), and a duplicative cause of action is subject to demurrer (Palm Springs Villas II Homeowners Association, Inc. v. Parth (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 268, 290). “The declaratory relief statute should not be used for the purpose of anticipating and determining an issue which can be determined in the main action. The object of the  statute is to afford a new form of relief where needed and not to furnish a litigant with a second cause of action for the determination of identical issues." (Hood v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal. App. 4th 319, 324.)

 

Glendale’s underlying Complaint asserts a cause of action for declaratory relief, states that an actual controversy has arisen between Williams and Glendale concerning their respective rights to the SES, and seeks judicial declaration of the parties’ rights with respect to the UCC-1 filing recorded by Glendale as security to finance the loan. (Compl., ¶¶ 14-16.) Thus, the fifth cause of action is derivative and seeks determination of identical issues already raised by Glendale. Williams has an adequate form of relief in the main action in defending against Glendale’s claims, and no new form of relief is sought.

 

Glendale’s Demurer is SUSTAINED as to the fifth cause of action without leave to amend. This ruling shall not be construed to implicate Williams’ ability to plead a cause of action for declaratory relief in a subsequent amendment against other non-parties if Williams seeks to name any separate entity as a Cross-Defendant on a separate basis. The Court offers no opinion on the viability of any such claim.

 

                                             vii.     Sixth Cause of Action – Violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200

 

Business and Professions Code section 17200 describes unfair competition as “any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising and any act prohibited by Chapter 1 (commencing with Section 17500) of Part 3 of Division 7 of the Business and Professions Code.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200.) A claim under the California's Unfair Competition Law, Business & Professions Code §§17200 et seq. ("UCL"), requires the allegation of particular facts showing the statutory elements of ongoing unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business acts on the part of the defendant with “reasonable particularity.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 619.) Claims by private plaintiffs can only be brought by “a person who has suffered injury in fact and has lost money or property as a result of the unfair competition.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17204.)

 

Glendale argues this claim fails because Williams has failed to identify any statutory, regulatory, or decisional law that Glendale has violated and fails to allege how any act or omission on the part of Glendale violated Business and Professions Code section 17200. (Demurrer, p. 9:10-11, 9:17-18.)

 

The Court notes that identification and violation of a specific statutory, regulatory, or decisional law is not necessarily a requirement for liability under the UCL, and the passage cited by Glendale from Bernardo v. Planned Parenthood Federation of America (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 322, 351-52, which stated that a plaintiff failed to meet her burden by filing to identify a statutory, regulatory, or decisional law allegedly violated, applies only to “unlawful” business practices. A claim may still proceed under the UCL under “unfair” or “fraudulent” business practices, which do not require identification of a violated law.

 

However, Glendale is correct that Williams has failed to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for unlawful business practices. An “unlawful business act” includes “anything that can properly be called a business practice and that at the same time is forbidden by law.” (People v. McKale¿(1979) 25 Cal.3d 626, 632.) The FACC does not identify any law that was allegedly violated, much less any law violated by Glendale.

 

The FACC also fails to allege any fraudulent or unfair business practices attributable to Glendale. A claim for fraudulent business practice requires a showing that members of the public are likely to be deceived, meaning “members of the public must have had an expectation or an assumption about” the matter in question.” (Daugherty v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc., 144 Cal. App. 4th 824, 838.) “[A]n ‘unfair’ business practice occurs when that practice ‘offends an established public policy or when the practice is immoral, unethical, oppressive, unscrupulous or substantially injurious to consumers.’ [Citation.]” (Smith v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Ins. Co. (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 700, 718–719.)

 

The FACC alleges that “Cross-Defendants” engaged in unlawful, unfair, and/or fraudulent business practices by making misleading representations regarding the SES, attempting to enforce financing obligations tied to a non-operational SES, continuing collection efforts despite notice of defects and disputes, engaging in deceptive servicing and account practices, and reporting adverse account information. (FACC, ¶ 56.) The FACC does not attribute these acts or omissions to Glendale, does not state with the requisite particularity what representations were made and by whom, what financing obligations are being enforced or why enforcement is fraudulent or unfair, who is continuing collection efforts and why the SES’s status renders collection fraudulent or unfair, who is engaging in deceptive servicing and accounting and how any such practices are deceptive under the UCL, or how reporting adverse account information gives rise to a claim under the UCL.

 

The FACC does not allege any facts showing members of the public are likely to be deceived by Glendale’s conduct as defined under the UCL, nor that the initiation of collection efforts and reporting of adverse account information offends public policy or amounts in any way to being ‘unfair.’ This is particularly true here, where there is no allegation that Williams has made payments under the loan, that Glendale does not have the legal authority to foreclose on the collateral when the debt secured by it is not being paid, or that the adverse account information reported is inaccurate. Williams has also failed to allege Glendale deprived her of any specific money or property, or that any loss was a result of Glendale’s business practices.

 

Glendale’s demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the sixth cause of action. The Court will grant leave to amend in the event that Williams can plead this cause of action against Glendale with the requisite particularity.

 

b.     Glendale’s Motion to Strike

 

Glendale moves to strike Williams’ request for punitive damages in the FACC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 436, subdivision (a) on the grounds that the FACC does not plead with specificity any despicable conduct on Glendale’s part. Specifically, Glendale moves to strike the following from the FACC:

 

1.     ¶ 50 – ““The conduct alleged herein was carried out with oppression, fraud, and/or malice, including conscious disregard for Cross-Complainant’s rights and supports an award of punitive damages pursuant to California Civil Code section 3294”; and

2.     Prayer for Relief, ¶ 3 (FACC, p. 7:10-11) – “For punitive damages pursuant to California Civil Code section 3294.”

 

As the Court has sustained Glendale’s demurrer to the FACC in its entirety, the Court DENIES the motion to strike as moot. This denial is without prejudice to file a subsequent motion to strike to a subsequently amended Cross-Complaint.
