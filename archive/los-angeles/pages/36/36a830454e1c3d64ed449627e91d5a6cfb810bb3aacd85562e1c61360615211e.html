DEPARTMENT 316 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV12495    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 316

BACKGROUND

This case is a breach of contract dispute. Plaintiff Fryman Management, Inc. served as a subcontractor for Defendant Essential Vegetation Management Services, LLC, on a construction project for Caltrans. Plaintiff alleges that it performed all its obligations under the parties’ agreement, but Defendant refused to pay.

On May 21, 2025, Plaintiff filed its first amended complaint against Defendants Essential Vegetation Management Services, LLC, the State of California, and the Hudson Insurance Company. The first amended complaint alleges ten causes of action stemming from the facts alleged above.

On July 21, 2026, Defendant Essential Vegetation Management Services, LLC filed a Motion to Compel Release of Funds Withheld Under Stop Payment Notice.

On August 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.

On August 28, 2026, the State of California, acting by and through the Department of Transportation (“Caltrans”) filed a notice of non-opposition to the motion.

On September 3, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

On September 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed a sur-reply.

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant’s Motion to Compel Release of Funds Withheld Under Stop Payment Notice is DENIED.

LEGAL STANDARD

On receipt of a stop payment notice, a public entity must withhold from the direct contractor sufficient funds to pay the claim stated in the notice. (Civ. Code, § 9358, subd. (a).) A direct contractor may obtain release of funds withheld pursuant to a stop payment notice if:

(a) The claim on which the notice is based is not a type for which a stop payment notice is authorized under this chapter.

(b) The claimant is not a person authorized under Section 9100 to give a stop payment notice.

(c) The amount of the claim stated in the stop payment notice is excessive.

(d) There is no basis for the claim stated in the stop payment notice.

(Civ Code, § 9400, subds. (a)-(d).)

To obtain said release, the direct contractor must serve an affidavit to the public entity. (Civ. Code, § 9402.) Afterwards, “[t]he public entity shall serve on the claimant a copy of the direct contractor’s affidavit, together with a notice stating that the public entity will release the funds withheld, or the portion of the funds demanded, unless the claimant serves on the public entity a counteraffidavit on or before the time stated in the notice.” (Civ. Code, § 9404.) “The time stated in the notice shall be not less than 10 days nor more than 20 days after service on the claimant of the copy of the affidavit.” (Ibid.) “If no counteraffidavit with proof of service is served on the public entity within the time stated in the public entity’s notice, the public entity shall immediately release the funds, or the portion of the funds demanded by the affidavit, without further notice to the claimant, and the public entity is not liable in any manner for their release.” (Civ. Code, § 9406, subd. (b).)

The Legislature designed this scheme for the speedy resolution of disputes over withheld funds. (See Jasper Constr. Inc. v. Univ. Casework Sys., Inc. (1974) 39 Cal. App. 3d 582, 586 [“a speedy resolution of disputes regarding stop notices and bonds on public projects is intended.”].)

DISCUSSION

The Court holds that Plaintiff was not timely put on notice of Defendant’s affidavit and could not file its counteraffidavit by the set deadline. Accordingly, it would be unjust and violate due process if the Court were to order the immediate release of the funds.

Here, on April 30, 2026, Plaintiff submitted a stop payment notice for $823,728. (See Tomaselli Decl., Exh. C, p. 3.) On June 13, 2026, Defendant served Caltrans with an affidavit to release funds withheld pursuant to Plaintiff’s stop payment notice. (See Tomaselli Decl., Exh. A.) Thereafter, Caltrans served Plaintiff with a copy of Defendant’s affidavit and ordered Plaintiff to serve a counter affidavit by July 8, 2026. (See, Tomaselli Decl., Exh. C.) Per Caltrans’ own records, Plaintiff did not file a counter affidavit by said deadline. (See ibid. [records accessed on July 20, 2026, twelve days after the deadline].)

Given the foregoing, the withheld funds would be released as required by Civil Code section 9406, subdivision (b). However, Plaintiff asserts that Caltrans did not serve Defendant’s affidavit to Plaintiff’s correct mailing address. Prior to Defendant’s affidavit, Plaintiff changed its corporate mailing address to P.O. Box 15025, San Diego, California 92175. (See Niccoli Decl., ¶ 10, Exh. A.) Furthermore, Plaintiff reached out to Caltrans and requested that it update Plaintiff’s mailing address to the San Diego address. (See id. at ¶¶ 11-12, Exh. B; Fryman Decl. ¶ 3.) However, Caltrans mailed Defendant’s affidavit to Plaintiff’s former Irvine address. (See id. at ¶¶ 14-15, Exh. C.) Therefore, Caltrans’ service was defective and Plaintiff was not put on notice of Defendant’s affidavit until this present was filed. (See Niccoli Decl., ¶¶ 5-7.) Accordingly, the Court finds that it would be unfair and violate due process if the Court ordered the release of the funds where Plaintiff had no opportunity to timely serve a counteraffidavit. For this reason, the Court shall not release the withheld funds and this motion is denied.

In reply, Defendant contends that Caltrans’ service was not defective simply because Plaintiff did not receive the affidavit. (See Reply, p. 3:14-21 [citing Med. Bd. of California v. Superior Court (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1191, 1195].) However, this principle only applies if the affidavit was properly mailed to Plaintiff. (See AO Alfa-Bank v. Yakovlev (2018) 21 Cal. App. 5th 189, 212 [“Moreover, even if receipt were relevant, proof that a letter was properly mailed ‘creates a presumption that it reached its destination in usual time and was actually received by the person to whom it was addressed.’”].) Here, the affidavit was not properly mailed to Plaintiff; it was mailed to Plaintiff’s former Irvine address after Plaintiff had informed Caltrans that i. (See Niccoli Decl., Exh. C.) Therefore, Caltrans’ service was defective because it sent the affidavit to the wrong address, not because Plaintiff never received the affidavit.

Defendant faults Plaintiff for referencing a separate contract in its email to Caltrans requesting that Plaintiff’s address be updated. (See Reply, pp. 4:1-9.) However, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s request – with a subject line of “Fryman Management – Address Change” and a request in the body of the email that Plaintiff “would like to change Fryman Management’s mailing address to the PO Box below – to be sufficient to put Caltrans on notice of the update address. (See Niccoli Decl., ¶¶ 11-12, Exh. B.) The email referenced a separate contract but did not in any way indicate that the change of address request was limited to that contract.

Finally, Defendant requests that if the Court is not going to order release of the full amount of the funds, that the Court treat this motion as a summary proceeding and determine the rights of the parties. (See Reply, pp. 5:26-6:8.) However, the Court finds that this is not the proper procedure for the Court to determine the rights of the parties. Once the affidavit and counter affidavit are served, either party may commence an action for a declaration of their rights. (Civ. Code, § 9408, subd. (a).) Either party may then move the court for a hearing to determine their rights under the affidavit and counter affidavit. (Id. at subd. (b).)

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant’s Motion to Compel Release of Funds Withheld Under Stop Payment Notice is DENIED.

Case Number: 25STCV30356    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 316

BACKGROUND

This case is a personal injury action. Plaintiff Alexander Estiven Seijas Torrez alleges he was injured in a motor vehicle accident for which Defendants Ryder Truck Rental, Inc., Florexpo LLC, Kendall Floral Supply, LLC, and Jose Enrique Gonzales are liable.

On October 17, 2025, Plaintiff filed a complaint against Defendants. The complaint alleges two causes of action stemming from the motor vehicle accident alleged above.

On March 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Motion to Compel Defendant Florexpo LLC to Produce Documents. Specifically, Plaintiff seeks to compel the production of the “Driver’s Accident Report”, “Claim file,” “Personnel File”, “Record of Road Test”, and “Safety Training” as identified by Defendant in its privilege log.

No opposition or reply has been filed.

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff’s Motion is GRANTED.

Defendant Florexpo LLC must produce the requested documents within twenty (20) days from the date of this hearing.

LEGAL STANDARD

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.310, the Court may order a responding party to serve a further response to a request for production of documents when the Court finds that any of the following apply: (1)¿a statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete; (2)¿a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive; (3)¿an objection in the response is without merit or too general.¿¿¿¿ 

A motion to compel further response to requests for production “shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the inspection demand.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) To establish ‘good cause,’ the burden is on the moving party to show both: (1) relevance to the subject matter (e.g., how the information in the documents would tend to prove or disprove some issue in the case); and (2) specific facts justifying discovery (e.g., why such information is necessary for trial preparation or to prevent surprise at trial). (See¿Glenfed¿Development Corp. v. Superior Court¿(1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 1113, 1117.)¿If¿‘good cause’¿is shown by the moving party, the burden is then on the responding party to justify any objections made to document disclosure. (See¿Coy v. Superior Court¿(1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220-21.)¿¿¿¿¿ 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.310, subdivision (h) authorizes a mandatory issuance of sanctions against “any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a further response to a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”¿ 

DISCUSSION

      I.          Meet and Confer

A motion to compel further responses must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(2).) Said declaration must demonstrate a “reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040, subd. (a).)

Here, the parties discussed Defendant’s discovery responses by a series of letters and emails. (See Laforet Decl., Exhs. 3-4.) However, the parties did not meet in person, by telephone, or by videoconference. Thus, the meet and confer requirement has not been satisfied. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(2); Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040, subd. (a).) Despite this, the Court shall consider the merits of Plaintiff’s motion.

   II.          Plaintiff’s Requests for Production of Documents (“RPD”)

Plaintiff seeks to compel the production of specified documents in response to RPDs Nos. 1, 2, 9, 10, 11, and 15. (See Motion, p. 6:1-21.) Plaintiff contends further responses are necessary because they will help determine the extent of Defendants’ negligence and whether there is any veracity to Defendants’ claim that Plaintiff is partially responsible for the accident. (See id. at pp. 5:20-24, 10:12-15, 14:8-15.)

After consideration of the motion and accompanying documents, the Court finds good cause to compel Defendant’s production of the requested documents. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) Plaintiff has put forth specific facts showing good cause justifying production of the requested discovery. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).)  

Because Defendant did not file an opposition, they did not meet their assertion of privilege, work product protection or other objections to the production of the documents at issue. (See¿Coy v. Superior Court¿(1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220-221.) Accordingly, Defendant’s objections and assertions of privilege are found to be without support and the Court will compel the production of the identified documents.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Further Discovery Responses is GRANTED.

Defendant must serve produce the “Driver’s Accident Report”, “Claim file,” “Personnel File”, “Record of Road Test”, and “Safety Training” as identified by Defendant in its privilege log within twenty (20) days from the date of the Court’s order.

Case Number: 26STCV09423    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 316

BACKGROUND

This is a lemon law matter.

On March 24, 2026, Plaintiff Adolfo Posada filed a complaint against Defendants BMW of North America, LLC and McKenna Motors Torrance, Inc. Plaintiff alleges two causes of action stemming from his purchase of an allegedly defective vehicle.

On July 8, 2026, Defendants filed a Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings.

On August 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.

On September 3, 2026, Defendants filed a reply.

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendants’ Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is DENIED.

LEGAL STANDARD

A defendant may move for judgment on the pleadings when the “complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against the defendant.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subds. (b)(1), (c)(1)(B)(ii).) “A motion for judgment on the pleadings performs the same function as a general demurrer, and hence attacks only defects disclosed on the face of the pleadings or by matters that can be judicially noticed. Presentation of extrinsic evidence is . . . not proper on a motion for judgment on the pleadings.” (Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 999 [citations omitted].)¿¿  

The standard for ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings is essentially the same as that applicable to a general demurrer: that under the state of the pleadings, together with matters that may be judicially noticed, it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (See Schabarum v. California Legislature (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1216.) The court “must accept as true all material facts properly pleaded, but does not consider conclusions of law or fact, opinions, speculation, or allegations contrary to law or facts that are judicially noticed.” (Stevenson Real Estate Services, Inc. v. CB Richard Ellis Real Estate Services, Inc. (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1219-1220.)¿¿  

A motion for judgment on the pleadings may be granted when, “under the state of the pleadings, together with matters that may be judicially noticed, it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Southern California Edison Co. v. City of Victorville (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 218, 227 [citations omitted].) “[J]udgment on the pleadings must be denied where there are material factual issues that require evidentiary resolution.” (Ibid. [quoting Schabarum v. California Legislature (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1216.].) 

DISCUSSION

I.                Meet and Confer

Before filing a motion for judgment of the pleadings (“MJOP”), the moving party must meet and confer in person, by telephone or by video conference with the opposing party. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 439, subd. (a).) “A determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to grant or deny the motion for judgment on the pleadings.” (Id. at subd. (a)(4).)

Here, Defendants attempted to meet and confer by telephone, but Plaintiff’s counsel refused to do so. (See Miller, Decl., ¶ 3.) Thus, the meet and confer requirement has not been satisfied. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 439, subd. (a).) Despite this, the Court shall consider the merits of Defendants’ MJOP. (See id. at subd. (a)(4).)

II.             Breach of Express Warranty (Cause of Action No. 1)

“A plaintiff pursuing an action under the Song-Beverly Act has the burden to prove the following elements: (1) the product had a defect or nonconformity covered by the express warranty; (2) the product was presented to an authorized representative of the manufacturer for repair; and (3) the manufacturer or its representative did not repair the defect or nonconformity after a reasonable number of repair attempts.” (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 798-799.) Thus, to sustain a breach of express warranty cause of action, a nonconformity must have been subject to multiple unsuccessful repair attempts. (Civ. Code § 1793.2; see also Silvio v. Ford Motor Co. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1209 [“A single attempt does not meet the statutory threshold. . . .”].)

The statute also provides that “[i]f the manufacturer or its representative in this state is unable to service or repair a new motor vehicle, as that term is defined in paragraph (2) of subdivision (e) of Section 1793.22, to conform to the applicable express warranties after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer shall either promptly replace the new motor vehicle in accordance with subparagraph (A) or promptly make restitution to the buyer in accordance with subparagraph (B).”¿(Civ. Code, § 1793.2, subd.¿(d)(2).) “‘New motor vehicle’¿includes¿…¿a dealer-owned vehicle and a¿‘demonstrator’ or other motor vehicle sold with a manufacturer’s new car warranty.” (Civ. Code, §¿1793.22, subd.¿(e)(2) [emphasis added].)

In Rodriguez v. FCA US, LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189, the Supreme Court held that “a motor vehicle purchased with an unexpired manufacturer’s new car warranty does not qualify as a¿‘motor vehicle sold with a manufacturer’s new car warranty’¿under section 1793.22, subdivision (e)(2)’s definition of¿‘new motor vehicle’¿unless the new car warranty was issued with the sale.” (Id. at p. 196.) The Rodriguez¿court found that against the backdrop of the Song-Beverly Act, “it would be anomalous to construe¿‘other motor vehicle sold with a manufacturer’s new car warranty’¿(§ 1793.22, subd. (e)(2)) to include any used car sold by a distributor or retailer with a preexisting, unexpired manufacturer’s warranty.” (Id. at p. 202.)¿ 

Lastly, “[n]otwithstanding the provisions of subdivision (a) of Section 1791 defining consumer goods to mean ‘new’ goods, the obligation of a distributor or retail seller of used consumer goods in a sale in which an express warranty is given shall be the same as that imposed on manufacturers under this chapter except: [¶] (a) It shall be the obligation of the distributor or retail seller making express warranties with respect to used consumer goods (and not the original manufacturer, distributor, or retail seller making express warranties with respect to such goods when new) to maintain sufficient service and repair facilities within this state to carry out the terms of such express warranties. (§ 1795.5, subd. (a).)” (Kiluk v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 334, 339.) “But the assumption baked into section 1795.5 is that the manufacturer and the distributor/retailer are distinct entities. Where the manufacturer sells directly to the public, however, it takes on the role of a retailer.” (Id. at 340.) 

A.    Purchase in California

Defendants contend this claim is subject to judgment on the pleadings because Plaintiff does not specifically allege the subject vehicle was purchased in California. (See MJOP, p. 5:15-28.) However, Plaintiff states that he resides in Los Angeles and was offered the subject warranty in the same city. (Complaint, ¶¶ 1, 4.) There is no allegation that the subject vehicle was purchased outside of California. The Court finds there is a reasonable inference that the subject vehicle was purchased in California. Accordingly, there is a factual issue as to where the subject vehicle was purchased which requires further evidentiary resolution. (See Southern California Edison Co. v. City of Victorville (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 218, 227.) The MJOP will not be granted on this ground.

B.     New Car Warranty

Defendants also contend that this claim is subject to judgment on the pleadings because Plaintiff purchased the subject vehicle used.[1] (See MJOP, pp. 6:1-10:5.) However, the Court finds this argument unavailing at this stage of the proceedings for two reasons. First, Plaintiff alleges that “Defendant BMW gave Plaintiff(s) a written warranty contract,” (see Compl., ¶ 8) and there is no indication that this alleged warranty contract was an unexpired new car warranty contract as discussed in Rodriguez. Accepting the allegation as true, the Court finds this is sufficient to state a cause of action as Plaintiff has alleged a new warranty was issued with the sale of the subject vehicle. (See Rodriguez v. FCA US, LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189, 196.) Defendants do not address this allegation in the motion.

Second, Plaintiff alleges that when Plaintiff purchased the subject vehicle, Defendants offered a certified pre-owned warranty in addition to the warranty referenced above. (See Compl., ¶ 8 [“Defendant also gave an additional warranty for the vehicle being certified pre-owned, which included additional coverage for the earlier of 12 Months (1 Year) or Unlimited Miles miles.”].) The Court finds that this qualifies as a new warranty issued with the sale of the subject vehicle. (See Rodriguez v. FCA US, LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189, 196.)

Despite the allegation that Defendant presented Plaintiff with a new certified pre-owned warranty, Defendants contend it is not the equivalent to a new car warranty. Defendants argue that for “the CPO warranty to be deemed a ‘manufacturer’s new car warranty,’ it would—at a minimum—need to provide the same level of coverage as that original manufacturer’s warranty.” (MJOP, p. 8:9-11.) However, Defendants do not provide any binding authority for this proposition,[2] nor does Rodriquez explicitly hold that a certified pre-owned warranty cannot qualify as a “new car warranty”. Thus, Plaintiff’s claim is not barred because it is premised on a certified pre-owned warranty offered at the time of purchase.

C.     Requirements for Liability

Defendants also contend this claim is subject to judgment on the pleadings because Plaintiff did not specifically state how many times the subject vehicle was brought in for repairs. (See MJOP, p. 11:5-11.) However, Plaintiff alleges that there were “a reasonable number of repair attempts.” (Compl., ¶ 19.) When accepted as true, this allegation is sufficient to satisfy the multiple repair attempts requirement at this stage. (See Civ. Code § 1793.2; see also Silvio v. Ford Motor Co. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1209.) Accordingly, Defendants are not entitled to judgment as a matter of law on this ground. (See Southern California Edison Co. v. City of Victorville (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 218, 227.)

For these reasons, the Court finds that Defendants are not entitled to judgment as a matter of law as to the first cause of action.

III.           Negligent Repair

The Court finds that this cause of action is not barred by the economic loss rule.

To state a cause of action for negligence, a plaintiff must allege facts to show that:¿(1) defendant owned plaintiff a duty of care; (2) defendant breached that duty of care; (3)¿defendant’s breach actually and proximately caused plaintiff’s injury; and (4) plaintiff suffered damages. (See¿Achay¿v. Huntington Beach Union High School District¿(2022) 80 Cal.App.5th 528, 535.)¿¿ 

The economic loss rule posits that a purchaser of a product that does not live up to the buyer’s expectations can only recover in contract and not tort, “unless [the purchaser] can demonstrate harm above and beyond a broken contractual promise.” (Food Safety Net Services v. Eco Safe Systems USA, Inc.¿(2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1118, 1130 [quoting¿Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp.¿(2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 988].) “In general, there is no recovery in tort for negligently inflicted ‘purely economic losses,’ meaning financial harm unaccompanied by physical or property damage.” (Sheen v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.¿(2022) 12 Cal.5th 905, 922 (Sheen).) Typically, plaintiffs may only recover in tort for a breach of contract where: “(1) the breach is accompanied by a traditional common law tort, such as fraud or conversion; (2) the means used to breach the contract are tortious, involving deceit or undue coercion or; (3) one party intentionally breaches the contract intending or knowing that such a breach will cause severe, unmitigable harm in the form of mental anguish, personal hardship, or substantial consequential damages.’” (Erlich v. Menezes¿(1999) 21 Cal.4th 543, 553-554.)¿¿ 

Here, Defendants contend this cause of action is subject to judgment on the pleadings because it is barred by the economic loss rule. (See MJOP, p. 11:14-13:2.) However, this cause of action is not based on Defendant South Bay BMW’s alleged failure to repair the subject vehicle¿to conform to the applicable warranty. Rather, it is based on Defendant South Bay BMW’s alleged failure to repair the subject vehicle¿to conform with industry standards. (See Compl., ¶ 30 [“Defendant South Bay BMW owed a duty to Plaintiff(s) to use ordinary care and skill in storage, preparation and repair of the Subject Vehicle in accordance with industry standards.”].) Thus, the economic loss rule does not apply because Plaintiff is not alleging a contractual breach in which he is seeking tort relief. (See Food Safety Net Services v. Eco Safe Systems USA, Inc.¿(2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1118, 1130.)

CONCLUSION¿

For the foregoing reasons, Defendants’ Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is DENIED.

[1] Plaintiff contends there are no allegations suggesting that the subject vehicle was used. However, this is incorrect as evidenced by Plaintiff’s own claim that the vehicle came with a certified pre-owned warranty. (See Compl., ¶ 8 [“Defendant also gave an additional warranty for the vehicle being certified pre-owned, which included additional coverage for the earlier of 12 Months (1 Year) or Unlimited Miles miles.”].)

[2] Defendants refer to a series of unpublished federal court cases to support their position. However, these cases are not binding and do not control the Court’s decision.  


Case Number: 26STCV13240    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 316

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Pattrice Corrine McMichael alleges that the conditions in her leased unit were substandard and the unit was not suitable for habitability. On April 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed a complaint against Defendants Mary Diana Hackland and Robert Allen Hackland. The complaint alleges 15 causes of action stemming from the facts alleged above.

On June 29, 2026, Defendant Mary Diana Hackland filed a Motion to Transfer Venue.

On July 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.

On September 3, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant’s Motion to Transfer Venue is GRANTED.

Defendant shall be awarded $2,125 in attorney’s fees. 

LEGAL STANDARD

“The proper place for trial is fixed by statutory scheme.” (Battaglia Enters., Inc. v. Superior Ct. (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 309, 313 (Battaglia).) Code of Civil Procedure section 395, subdivision (a), provides in part “[e]xcept as otherwise provided by law . . . If the action is for injury to person or personal property or for death from wrongful act or negligence, the superior court in either the country where the injury occurs or . . or the county where the defendants, or some of them reside at the commencement of the action, is the proper court for the trial of the action.”¿   

“Generally, when venue is proper in more than one county, a plaintiff has the choice of where to file the action from among the available options. There is a presumption that the county in which the plaintiff chose to file the action is the proper county.” (Battagalia, supra, at pp. 313–314 [citation omitted].) Where a party is seeking a change of venue, the burden is on the moving party to defeat this presumption. (Id. at p. 314.) 

Regarding transfer of an action, the Code of Civil Procedure provides:  

The court may, on motion, change the place of trial in the following cases: 

(a) When the court designated in the complaint is not the proper court. 

(b) When there is reason to believe that an impartial trial cannot be had therein. 

(c) When the convenience of witnesses and the ends of justice would be promoted by the change. 

(d) When from any cause there is no judge of the court qualified to act. 

(e) When a proceeding for dissolution of marriage has been filed in the county in which the petitioner has been a resident for three months next preceding the commencement of the proceeding, and the respondent at the time of the commencement of the proceeding is a resident of another county in this state, to the county of the respondent's residence when the ends of justice would be promoted by the change. 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 397, subds. (a)–(e).) 

DISCUSSION

I.                Transferring Action to Shasta County

Defendant seeks to transfer this action to the Shasta County Superior Court. Defendant contends that this transfer is proper because: (1) she is a resident of Shata County; (2) the subject property is in Shasta County; and (3) the alleged wrongful acts occurred in Shasta County. (See Motion, p. 2:11-24.)

The Court finds that the Shasta County is the proper court to hear this action and, further, the convenience of witnesses and the ends of justice would be promoted by the transfer of this action to the Shasta County Superior Court. Given that the alleged wrongful conduct and injury occurred in Shasta County and that Defendant currently resides in Shasta County, the Shasta County Superior Court is the proper court to handle this action. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 395, subd. (a); see also Code Civ. Proc., § 397, subd. (a); see Compl., ¶¶ 1, 16-17, 117.) Further, the fact that the alleged wrongful conduct and the property at issue are in Shasta County makes clear that relevant witnesses, evidence, and information to prosecute this case are in Shasta County. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 395, subd. (c).) Lastly, it is preferred to hold actions in the region where the underlying events occurred. (See Rycz v. Superior Court (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 824, 848 [“Generally speaking, conducting trials in the venue most connected to the underlying events contributes to a more fair and efficient system of justice.”]; see also Seybert v. Imperial County (1956) 139 Cal.App.2d 221, 234 [“There is a local interest in having localized controversies decided at home.”].) Accordingly, it is proper to hold this action in Shasta County since that is where the underlying events occurred. (See Compl., ¶¶ 1, 16-17, 117.) For these reasons, Defendant has met her burden in showing that the proper county to hear this action is Shasta County.

In opposition, Plaintiff contends that Defendant has not met her burden in “affirmatively demonstrating that Plaintiff’s selected venue is legally improper under the applicable statutory provisions.” (Opp’n, p. 3:10-12.) However, as explained above, Defendant has met this burden by demonstrating that Shasta County is the proper venue for this action because this is where the alleged injury occurred and where Defendant resides. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 395, subd. (a).) Plaintiff also contends that Defendant has failed to make a requisite showing that Shasta County is the proper venue for this action because she failed to consider the complaint in its entirety. (See Opp’n, pp. 10:5-11:7.) However, Defendant’s analysis of the complaint provides sufficient justification to transfer this action to Shasta County because this where the subject property is located, where the alleged wrongful acts and injury occurred and where defendant resides.

In short, Defendant has met her burden in demonstrating that Shasta County is the proper venue for this action. Accordingly, the Court shall transfer this action to the Shasta County Superior Court.

II.             Attorney’s Fees

In a motion to transfer venue, the Court may award attorney’s fees to the prevailing party. (Code Civ. Proc., § 396b, subd. (b).) In determining whether to award attorney’s fees, the Court must consider: “(1) whether an offer to stipulate to change of venue was reasonably made and rejected, and (2) whether the motion or selection of venue was made in good faith given the facts and law the party making the motion or selecting the venue knew or should have known.” (Ibid.)

Here, Plaintiff rejected a stipulation to transfer this action to Shasta County. (See Nelson Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.) As evidenced by the complaint, Plaintiff also knew that the alleged wrongful acts and injury underlying this action all occurred in Shasta County. (See Compl., ¶¶ 1, 16-17, 117.) Furthermore, Plaintiff’s only justification for pursuing this action in Los Angeles County was because this is where her attorney was located and because she consented to said venue. (See id. at ¶ 9.)

Given the foregoing, the Court shall award attorney’s fees. Not only did Plaintiff reject Defendant’s stipulation, but Plaintiff knew or reasonably should have known that Shasta County was the proper venue. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 396b, subd. (b).) Therefore, attorney’s fees are warranted.

Defendant requests $3,825 in attorney’s fees. Defendant’s counsel seeks an hourly rate of $425 per hour, which the Court finds reasonable. (Nelson Decl., ¶ 5.) Counsel contends he spent five hours on this motion and anticipates an additional four hours to reply to Plaintiff’s opposition and attend the scheduled hearing. (Ibid.) The Court finds those amounts excessive given the motion was relatively straightforward and the reply very brief. Therefore, the Court shall reduce the time spent working on this motion to three hours and the time on the reply brief and remote appearance at the hearing to two hours. Accordingly, Defendant is awarded $2,125 in attorney’s fees.  

CONCLUSION¿

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant’s Motion to Transfer Venue is GRANTED.

Defendant is awarded $2,125 in attorney’s fees to be paid by Plaintiff’s counsel to Defendant within thirty days of the Court’s order.
