ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANT MOHAMAD ANOUTI’S MOTION TO SET ASIDE ENTRY OF DEFAULT

 

I.               BACKGROUND 

Plaintiffs Cory Delpasand and Nadia Sadat (jointly “Plaintiffs”) filed this breach of contract action against Defendants Mohamad Anouti (“Anouti”) and AMN Burnet LLC (“Burnett”). Plaintiffs allege they entered into a Stock Purchase Agreement (“Contract”) whereby Defendant sold the shares in The Green Earth Farmacie, Inc. to Plaintiffs. Plaintiffs allege Defendants breached the Contract by failing to obtain the Certificates of Occupancy.

Anouti moves to set aside default entered against him on January 6, 2026.

///

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On April 9, 2024, Plaintiffs filed a Complaint alleging 1) Breach of Contract; and 2) Fraud.

On May 15, 2024, Plaintiffs filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging 1) Breach Of Contract; 2) Fraud; 3) Intentional Interference With Prospective Economic Advantage; and 4) Negligent Intereference With Prospective Economic Advantage.

On April 4, 2025, the Court granted Plaintiffs’ application for publication.

On May 30, 2025, Plaintiffs filed proof of publication.

On January 7, 2026, the Court entered default against Anouti.

On March 27, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiffs’ ex parte application for order directing service by delivery to Secretary of State.

On June 8, 2026, the Court entered default against Burnett.

On July 2, 2026, Anouti filed the instant Motion.

As of August 12, 2026, no opposition has been filed. However, the Court notes there may be issues with service because the Proof of Service attached to the Motion is not signed and appears to have been effectuated by Anouti himself in violation of Code of Civil Procedure § 414.10.

III.         LEGAL STANDARD

A.             Discretionary or Mandatory Relief – Code of Civil Procedure § 473(b)

“The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken…” (Code Civ. Proc. § 473(b).) [1] 

Code of Civil Procedure § 473(b) “provides for both discretionary and mandatory relief. [Citation.]” (Pagnini v. Union Bank, N.A. (2018) 28 Cal.App.5th 298, 302.)

For discretionary relief, “[t]he court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.”¿(Code Civ. Proc. § 473(b).)  

A moving party need only establish “mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect” by a preponderance of the evidence. (Luz v. Lopes (1960) 55 Cal.2d 54, 62.) Excusable neglect exists where counsel or the party acted as a reasonably prudent person would under the circumstances. (See Hearn v. Howard (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1206; See also Zamora v. Clayborn Contracting Group, Inc. (2002) 28 Cal.4th 249, 258.) Attorney negligence is not a basis for discretionary relief under Code of Civil Procedure § 473. (See Pazderka v. Caballeros Dimas Alang, Inc. (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 658, 672; see also Garcia v. Hejmadi (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 674, 682.) 

An application for discretionary or mandatory relief must be made no more than six months after entry of the judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding from which relief is sought. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 473(b); see also English v. IKON Bus. Solutions (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 130, 143.)

 

B.             Void Judgment or Order – Code of Civil Procedure § 473(d)

Code of Civil Procedure § 473(d) provides, “[t]he court may… on motion of either party after notice to the other party, set aside any void judgment or order.” (Code Civ. Proc. §473(d); see also Zisk v. City of Roseville (1976) 56 Cal.App.3d 41, 47; accord Bastajian v. Brown (1941) 19 Cal.2d 209, 216; Baird v. Smith (1932) 216 Cal. 408, 411.) Once brought to the trial court’s attention, it is error to refuse to set a void order or judgment aside. (See Lovato v. Santa Fe Int’l Corp. (1984) 151 Cal.App.3d 549, 554-555.) “[I]nclusion of the word ‘may’ in the language of section 473, subdivision (d) makes it clear that a trial court retains discretion to grant or deny a motion to set aside a void judgment [or order]. However, the trial court “has no statutory power under section 473, subdivision (d) to set aside a judgment [or order] that is not void....” (Pittman v. Beck Park Apartments Ltd. (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1009, 1020 [internal citations omitted].)

In determining whether an order is void for purposes of § 473(d), a distinction is made between orders that are void on the face of the record and orders that appear valid on their face but are shown to be invalid by extrinsic evidence. (See id.)

C.             Lack of Actual Notice - Code of Civil Procedure § 473.5

Code of Civil Procedure § 473.5  permits the Court to vacate a default when service of a summons has not resulted in actual notice to a party in time to defend the action and a default or default judgment has been entered against him. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(a).) “The notice of motion shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of: (i) two years after entry of a default judgment against him or her; or (ii) 180 days after service… of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.” (Id.)

The phrase actual notice means genuine knowledge and does not include constructive or imputed notice to the client. (See Tunis v. Barrow (1986) 184 Cal.App.3d 1069, 1077.) “A defendant seeking vacation of a default judgment entered against him must further show that his lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by his inexcusable neglect or avoidance of service.” (Id. at 1077-1078, citing Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(c).) Additionally, “the party shall serve and file with the notice a copy of the answer, motion, or other pleading proposed to be filed in the action.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(b).)

IV.          ANALYSIS

Anouti moves for relief pursuant to §§ 473(d), 473.5, and 473(b).

A.             Void Order

A judgment may be void for lack of improper service of summons. A motion for relief from a judgment valid on its face, but void for improper service, must be brought with a “reasonable” time after the default judgment. (See California Capital Ins. Co. v. Hoehn (2024) 17 Cal.5th 207, 212, 225-226 [disapproving prior authority that applied 2-year time limit to § 473(d) motions to vacate].) In contrast, there is no time limit on a motion for relief under § 473 where it is clear from the face of the record that the judgment should not have been entered. (See Plotitsa v. Superior Ct. (Kadri) (1983) 140 Cal.App.3d 755, 761.)

Anouti argues the entry of default against him is void because Plaintiffs failed to exercise reasonable diligence before serving him by publication. (See Mot. at 6:16-20.) Anouti contends Plaintiffs’ showing of reasonable diligence comprises attempted service at two units he did not occupy, one unit he vacated in November 2021, and another unit where he never resided. (See Declaration of Mohamad Anouti filed July 2, 2026 (“Anouti Decl.”), ¶¶ 3-5, 9.) Anouti argues Plaintiffs’ investigator had information including potential current addresses and phone numbers they did not explore as service avenues. (See id., ¶ 9) Anouti also contends Plaintiffs were concurrently involved in another action with Burnett and thus Plaintiffs’ counsel could have asked Burnett’s counsel in that action to accept service under § 415.30 on behalf of Burnett’s manager, Anouti, or at least advised him of this action. (See Mot., at 6:21-27; Anouti Decl., ¶¶ 13-15)

The Court finds the entry of default is void due to improper service of summons. After reviewing the service attempts documented in Plaintiffs’ Application for Publication, the Court finds Anouti’s evidence shows the entry of default is void because Plaintiffs’ publication order rested on an inadequate diligence showing. Anouti’s evidence shows Plaintiffs could have reached Anouti at a possible current address, as listed in Plaintiffs’ skip tracing report, contacted Anouti at the phone numbers listed in the skip tracing report to confirm his address, or communicated to Burnett’s counsel in the parties’ ongoing action about this action.

Thus, the Court GRANTS Anouti’s Motion to Set Aside Entry of Default. Accordingly, the Court will not address whether Anouti is entitled to relief pursuant to § 473(b) or 473.5.

V.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS Anouti’s Motion to Set Aside Entry of Default.

          IT IS SO ORDERED.

ORDER GRANTING IN PART DEFENDANT’S MOTION TO TAX COSTS

 

I.               BACKGROUND 

This lemon law action arises out of Plaintiff Ritza S. Turcios Serrano’s (“Plaintiff”) September 20, 2021, purchase of a 2019 Honda Civic ( “Subject Vehicle”). (See Compl., ¶ 8.) Plaintiff brings this action against Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.(“Defendant”), alleging the Subject Vehicle contained or developed various engine and suspension system defects. (See id. at ¶ 10.)  

Defendant moves to strike Plaintiff’s costs.

 

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On January 23, 2025, Plaintiff filed her Complaint alleging (1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act - Breach of Express Warranty; (2) Violation of Song-Beverly Act - Breach of Implied Warranty; and (3) Violation of The Song-Beverly Act Section 1793.2.

On January 23, 2025, the parties were served with the Lemon Law Addendum.

On March 5, 2025, Defendant filed an Answer.

On April 2,2026, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Settlement.

On July 16, 2026, Defendant filed the instant Motion.

As of August 10, 2026, no opposition has been filed.

III.         LEGAL STANDARD

The Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act authorizes recovery of costs “reasonably incurred by the buyer.” (Civ. Code § 1794(d).)[1] “If the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary.” (Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal. App. 4th 761, 774.) “On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue, and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.” (Id.) 

“A prevailing party who claims costs must serve and file a memorandum of costs within 15 days after the date of service of the notice of entry of judgment or dismissal by the clerk under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5 or the date of service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, or within 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is first. The memorandum of costs must be verified by a statement of the party, attorney, or agent that to the best of his or her knowledge the items of cost are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case.” (Rule of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).)

“Any notice of motion to strike or to tax costs must be served and filed 15 days after service of the cost memorandum. If the cost memorandum was served by mail, the period is extended as provided in Code of Civil Procedure section 1013. If the cost memorandum was served electronically, the period is extended as provided in Code of Civil Procedure section 1010.6(a)(4)”. (See id., rule 3.1700(b)(1).) 

“If items on a memorandum of costs appear to be proper charges on their face, those items are prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses, and services are proper and necessarily incurred. [citations.] The burden then shifts to the objecting party to show them to be unnecessary or unreasonable.” (Doe v. Los Angeles County Dept. of Children & Family Services (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 675, 693.) “In ruling upon a motion to tax costs, the trial court's first determination is whether the statute expressly allows the particular item and whether it appears proper on its face. If so, the burden is on the objecting party to show the costs to be unnecessary or unreasonable.” (Foothill De Anza Cmty. College Dist. v. Emerich (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 11, 2930.) “Whether a cost item was reasonably necessary to the litigation presents a question of fact for the trial court.” (Ladas v. Cal. State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 774.) 

“[T]he mere filing of a motion to tax costs may be a ‘proper objection’ to an item, the necessity of which appears doubtful, or which does not appear to be proper on its face. [citation.] However, ‘[i]f the items appear to be proper charges, the verified memorandum is prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses and services therein listed were necessarily incurred by the defendant [citations], and the burden of showing that an item is not properly chargeable or is unreasonable is upon the [objecting party].’” (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131.)  

IV.          ANALYSIS

Defendant argues Plaintiff’s costs should be stricken in their entirety because Plaintiff’s sole attestation to the veracity of the Memorandum of Costs is upon knowledge and belief but they submit no supporting affidavit. (See Mot. at 3:15-18, 4:27-5:2.)

The Court declines to strike Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs in its entirety for failure to submit a supporting affidavit as the verified Memorandum of Costs is prima facie evidence the costs listed were necessarily incurred. (See Doe, 37 Cal.App.5th at 693; Nelson, 72 Cal.App.4th at 131; Adams v. Ford Motor Co.¿(2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 1475, 1486-1487.)

Alternatively, Plaintiff moves to strike the following costs:

A.             Post 998-Offer Costs

Defendant moves to strike deposition costs of $1,581.25 from April 6, 2026, and expert witness fees of $1,031.25 from April 3, 2026. (See Mot. at 5:18-22.) Defendant argues per the parties 998 Offer, Defendant was to pay “Plaintiff’s statutory costs and expenses under California Civil Code l794(d), including reasonable attorneys’ fees…in connection with the commencement and prosecution of this action to, and including, the date of this Offer.” (See Declaration of Ashleigh K. Gideon filed July 16, 2026, ¶ 2, Ex. A at ¶ 2.) Defendant argues per these terms, costs incurred after the April 2, 2026, 998 Offer are not recoverable and should be stricken.

The Court will strike deposition costs of $550.00[2] from item 4 and expert witness fees of $1,031.25 from item 8 as Defendant has shown they are not recoverable pursuant to the parties’ 998 Offer, which was signed by Plaintiff on April 2, 2026.

The Court GRANTS the Motion as to deposition costs of $550.00 from item 4 and expert witness fees of $1,031.25 from item 8.

///

///

B.             Item 4: Deposition Costs

“Taking, video recording, and transcribing necessary depositions, including an original and one copy of those taken by the claimant and one copy of depositions taken by the party against whom costs are allowed.” (Code. Civ. Proc. § 1033.5(a)(3)(A).)

Defendant moves to strike costs of $450.00 in connection with Plaintiff’s deposition transcript and $1,195.40 in connection with court reporter fees for the deposition of Kaneisha Jones from item 4. (See Mot. at 6:7-8.) Defendant argues these costs are not recoverable under § 1033.5(b).

The Court finds Defendant has not shown these costs are unrecoverable or unreasonable. Defendant’s reliance on § 1033.5(b) is misplaced. In relevant part, Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5(b) provides “The following items are not allowable as costs, except when expressly authorized by law: (1) Fees of experts not ordered by the court…(5) Transcripts of court proceedings not ordered by the court.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1033.5(b).) This section does not provide that transcripts of a plaintiff’s deposition or transcription of depositions are not recoverable as costs. On the contrary, § 1033.5(a)(3)(A) expressly provides that transcribing necessary depositions are allowable costs. Accordingly, transcripts of Plaintiff’s deposition and court reporter fees incurred to transcribe the deposition of Kaneisha Jones are recoverable and reasonably necessary to litigating this action.

Thus, the Court DENIES Defendant’s Motion to strike these costs.

C.             Item 15: Other

“Costs are allowable if incurred, whether or not paid.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1033.5(c).) “Allowable costs shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation…Items not mentioned in this section and items assessed upon application may be allowed or denied in the court’s discretion.” (Id., § 1033.5(c)(1)-(4).)

          Defendant moves to strike $199.00 in hotel accommodation costs in relation to the vehicle inspection cost of $2,148.48 from February 10, 2026. (See Mot. at 6:22-26.) Defendant argues Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs includes a screenshot of an invoice that does not appear to be a formal invoice and lacks pertinent information such as the hotel and guest name. (See id., at 6:18-21.)

The Court finds Defendant properly objects to this cost. Routine expenses for local travel, including parking fees, cab fares, and mileage fees, incurred by attorneys, paralegals, or other law firm employees are not reasonably necessary to conduct of litigation and, thus, cannot be allowed as reimbursable costs. (See Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 775 [finding the supporting declaration of attorney failed to demonstrate how any of these charges were necessary to conduct the litigation, as opposed to being merely convenient].) The Court finds travel and hotel costs for a vehicle inspection are routine to lemon law litigation and thus not recoverable. Further, Plaintiff submits no evidence showing this expense was reasonably necessary to litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 1033.5(c)(2), (4).)

Thus, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s Motion to strike $199.00 in hotel accommodation costs from the February 10, 2026, vehicle inspection cost totaling $2,148.48.

V.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS in part Defendant’s Motion to Tax Costs in the amount of $1,780.25.

The total amount in costs recoverable to Plaintiffs will be $5,328.98.

          IT IS SO ORDERED.
