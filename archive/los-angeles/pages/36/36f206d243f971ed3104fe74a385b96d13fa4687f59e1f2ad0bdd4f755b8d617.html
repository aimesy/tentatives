23STCV26122 Steve Wallace v. Bruce Marder, et al.

Thursday, August 13, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER CONTINUING MOTION TO APPROVE FINAL SETTLEMENT AND DISMISS CASE (9932)

 

I.       BACKGROUND

      The first amended complaint alleges direct claims by Plaintiff and derivative claims on behalf of the entity CAPO, LLC, which is controlled by Defendant, Bruce Marder. The derivative claims are based on Defendants’ failure to distribute revenues to members who invested in a new restaurant in Santa Monica.

      On October 3, 2025, the Court granted Defendant PTSF’s motion to compel arbitration. The parties jointly move for an order approving the final settlement and to dismiss the case as required in derivative actions.

II.     DISCUSSION

      Plaintiff’s counsel reports that Plaintiff Steve Wallace passed away on June 12, 2026, and his estate is now handling the litigation. (Supp. Decl of Steven Morris.) An action for or against a decedent is not lost by reason of that person’s death. (Code Civ. Proc., § 377.21.) The claims survive subject to the applicable limitations period. (Code Civ. Proc., § 377.20.) The court “shall allow a pending action or proceeding that does not abate to be continued by the decedent's personal representative or, if none, by the decedent's successor in interest pursuant to a noticed motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 377.31.)

      The person who seeks to continue a pending action or proceeding as the decedent's successor in interest under this article, shall provide a certified copy of the decedent’s death certificate and shall execute and file an affidavit or a declaration stating the decedent’s name, date, and place of death, that no proceeding is pending in California for administration of the decedent’s estate, that the proposed successor is decedent’s successor-in-interest, and that no other person has a superior right to continue the decedent’s claims. (Code Civ. Proc., § 377.32.)

III.    CONCLUSION

      The Court continues the hearing to  December 4, 2026, at 8:30 a.m., to permit Plaintiff’s counsel to move to appoint a successor in interest, comply with the foregoing statutory requirements, and to file an amended pleading bearing the name of the real party in interest.

25STCV06899 Hernandez v. Aly, et al.

Thursday, August 13, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL RESPONSES TO SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES, SET ONE, AS TO DEFENDANT, TAREK ALY; REQUEST FOR IMPOSITION OF SANCTIONS (4424)

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR

PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS, SET ONE, AS TO DEFENDANT TAREK ALY; REQUEST FOR

MONETARY SANCTIONS (0849)

 

I.         BACKGROUND

      This is an action for breach of implied warranty of habitability and related claims for Defendants’ alleged failure to remedy conditions in Plaintiff’s residential unit. Plaintiff brings two motions to compel responses to special interrogatories and request for production of documents by Defendant, Tarek Aly (“Aly”). Plaintiff requests imposition of sanctions for fees incurred in preparing two motions. Plaintiff served all discovery at issue on Defendant on April 11, 2026. Defendant did not respond by May 13, 2026, when responses were due.

      Both motions refer to a property inspection noticed to take place on April 11, 2026, at which Defense counsel failed to appear and later claimed he had never received the notice. Defense counsel asked to reschedule but did not ask for the notice to be sent again which suggests to Plaintiff’s counsel that defense counsel is not acting in good faith. Plaintiff argues this is consistent with Defendant’s broader pattern of non-responsiveness.

      Plaintiff does not request an order compelling Defendant to appear for inspection. Rather, Plaintiff asks for an order to compel Defendant to serve initial responses without objection. Defendant did not file oppositions, and Plaintiff did not file corresponding replies.

II.        DISCUSSION

      Where a party fails to timely respond to interrogatories and a document request, the court has authority to compel a response. (Code Civ. Proc., §2030.290 subd. (b), §2031.300 subd. (b).) Untimely responses result in a waiver of objections. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290 subd. (a), §2031.300 subd. (a). As Defendant did not serve responses, all objections are waived.

      Imposition of sanctions is warranted for Defendant’s failure to show substantial justification for failing to comply with discovery obligations. However, the Court denies Plaintiff’s request for imposition of additional monetary sanctions on Defendant pursuant to Civil Procedure §2023.050. That section permits additional sanctions of $1,000 if the Court finds that a party did not respond in good faith to the document request or an inspection demand. There is no evidence that Defendant acted in bad faith in failing to respond to discovery to the document request. The request for inspection is not the discovery at issue in these motions.

III.       CONCLUSION       

      The Court GRANTS both motions. Defendant, Tarek Aly, is ordered to provide verified responses to Set One of Request for Production of Documents and Special Interrogatories without objections within 10 days.

Case Number: 25STCV11887    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: 414

25STCV11887 Aaron Brody v. Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc. et al.

Thursday, August 13, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANTS’ MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION (6251)

 

I.         BACKGROUND

      The complaint alleges that Toyota Motor Sales, USA, Inc. (“Defendant”) issued a warranty contract to Plaintiff with the purchase of a 2022 Lexus UX. Defects and nonconformities developed that impaired the use of the vehicle. Defendant did not promptly repurchase or replace after failing to conform the vehicle to the terms of the express warranty within a reasonable number of attempts. Plaintiff alleges violations of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act and a claim for negligent repair against Lexus Santa Monica.

      Defendants move to summarily adjudicate the sixth cause of action for fraudulent inducement-concealment because there are no facts demonstrating Defendant had a duty to disclose or that Defendant failed to disclose a material fact, and therefore, the punitive damages claim should be adjudicated in Defendants’ favor.

      Defendants argue the fifth cause of action for negligent repair is barred by the economic loss rule.

      Plaintiff files a notice of non-opposition.

II.        LEGAL STANDARDS

      Summary adjudication can be granted “as to one or more causes of action within an action, one or more affirmative defenses, one or more claims for damages, or one or more issues of duty, if the party contends that the cause of action has no merit, that there is no affirmative defense to the cause of action, that there is no merit to an affirmative defense as to any cause of action, [or] that there is no merit to a claim for [punitive] damages. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c (f)(1).) A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty. (Id.)

      The court applies the three-step analysis to motions for summary judgment or adjudication: (1) identify the issues framed by the pleading, (2) determine whether the moving party established facts which negate the opponents’ claim, (3) if the moving party meets its threshold burden of persuasion and the burden shifts, determine whether the opposing party has controverted those facts with admissible evidence. (Torres v. Reardon (1992) 3 Cal.App.4th 831, 836.)

III.       DISCUSSION

  1. Fraudulent concealment

      The elements of a fraud claim based on concealment are: “(1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.” (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 310–311.)

      There are “four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.” (Id. at 311.) If a fiduciary relationship does not exist, but the latter three circumstances are present, plaintiff must still show “the existence of some other relationship between the plaintiff and defendant from which a duty to disclose can arise.” (Id. at 311; LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336.)

      Plaintiff alleges Defendants actively concealed the engine defect. (Complaint, ¶ 57.) There is no dispute that Plaintiff did not experience an engine defect. (UF 7.) At his deposition, Plaintiff was asked to describe the recurring problems. He described bubble in the trims, right blinker malfunction, turn signal, USB port doesn’t work, power locks failed, a rattling antenna, and a “sway bar”, seeping oil, clicking in the front end. (Ex. J, p. 47:22-49:3.)

      The complaint alleges the car had engine, transmission and electrical defects. (Complaint, ¶ ¶ 12.) There is no dispute that the car did not suffer an engine defect. (UF 6- 8.)

      Plaintiff admitted he did not have direct dealings with Defendant. (UF 9, 12). He did not rely on anything in deciding to buy the vehicle. He did not research the internet or rely on marketing materials. (UF 10-11.) Therefore, there is no evidence of a direct relationship with Defendant from which a duty to disclose could arise.

      The fraudulent concealment claim serves as predicate to support recovery of punitive damages. Without a fraud claim, there is no basis for recovery of punitive damages which requires clear and convincing evidence of Defendant’s malice, fraud, or oppression.(Civ. Code, § 3294 subd. (a).).

B.     Negligent repair

      The fifth cause of action for negligent repair alleges Defendant Lexus Santa Monica (“Lexus”) owed a duty to use ordinary care and skill in storing and repairing the vehicle, which Lexus allegedly failed to do, causing Plaintiff damage. (Complaint, ¶ 48-50.)

      The economic loss rule bars tort recovery in a transaction where the plaintiff suffers only economic losses. (Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 988.) Such losses consist of “damages for inadequate value, costs of repair and replacement of the defective product or consequent loss of profits—without any claim of personal injury or damages to other property. (Robinson at 988.)

      Plaintiff did not suffer any physical injuries or property damage as a result of any alleged improper repairs. (UF 13, 14).

IV.  CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication of the fifth and sixth causes of action and the claim for punitive damages is GRANTED.


Case Number: 25STCV26714    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: 414

25STCV26714 Verginia Petikyan v. Dianna Zohrabyan, et al.

Thursday, August 13, 2026

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING VERGINIA PETIKYAN’S DEMURRER TO DIANNA ZOHRABYAN’S CROSS-COMPLAINT (0706)

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING ONIK PETIKYAN, MARY PETIKY AN, AND MAGDALENA PETIKYAN’S DEMURRER TO DIANNA ZOHRABYAN'S CROSS-COMPLAINT (8326)

 

I.         BACKGROUND

      Plaintiff, Verginia Petikyan, filed her complaint in this action on September 11, 2025, alleging that Defendants exploited Plaintiff and induced Plaintiff to lend Defendants more than $1.4 million between 2020 and 2024. The complaint alleges that Defendants also induced Plaintiff to open a credit card account that Defendants used to accumulate debt. Plaintiff asserts 11 causes of action for financial elder abuse, breach of contract, fraud, conversion, and related claims.

      On April 17, 2026, Defendant, Diana Zohrabyan, (“Cross-Complainant” or “Diana”)[1] filed a Cross-Complaint against Verginia Petikyan (“Verginia” or “Cross-Defendant”), Onik Petikyan, Mary Petikyan, and Magdalena Petikyan (collectively, “Cross-Defendants” or “Petikyans”) asserting affirmative relief for (1) involuntary servitude, (2) intentional infliction of emotional distress, and (3) intentional misrepresentation.

      Cross-Defendant Verginia demurs to the Cross-Complaint (0706) on grounds of uncertainty and failure to state a claim.

II.        LEGAL STANDARDS

      A demurrer tests the sufficiency of a complaint as a matter of law and raises only questions of law. (Schmidt v. Foundation Health (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1702, 1706.) In testing the complaint’s sufficiency, the court must assume the truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations as well as facts that can be reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded facts. The court may also consider matters properly subject to judicial notice. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

      Sufficient facts are the essential facts of the case "with reasonable precision and with particularity that is sufficiently specific to acquaint the defendant with the nature, source, and extent of his cause of action.” (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 643-644.) Whether the Plaintiff will be able to prove the pleaded facts is irrelevant. (Stevens v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 605, 609–610.)

      A demurrer may also be sustained if a complaint is “uncertain.” Uncertainty exists where a complaint’s factual allegations are so confusing, they do not sufficiently apprise a defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2; Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(f).)      

III.       DISCUSSION

A.     Uncertainty on grounds of group pleading. Sustained.

      Cross-Defendant Verginia argues that the Cross-Complaint is uncertain because Diana alleges claims against Cross-Defendants as a group, without specifying which Cross-Defendant engaged in the wide-ranging wrongful acts.

      The Cross-Complaint alleges that Verginia is the mother of Mary and Magdalena (also Cross-Defendants.) Cross-Complainant Diana worked for Verginia at Verginia’s Greek Restaurant. (Cross-compl ¶ 6-.) It was through that relationship that Verginia allegedly knew about Dianna and her family situation, immigration status, and other personal information. (Cross-compl ¶ 9-10.)

      The Cross-Complaint is uncertain. While the general allegations identify Verginia’s role in certain acts, the claims are alleged against all four Cross-Defendants as a group, although Cross-Complainant was allegedly only employed by Verginia. While the factual allegations lay out alleged misconduct by Verginia specifically in some cases and more obliquely in others, the inclusion of all of the factual allegations, and their relationship with which cause of action cannot be discerned. Cross-Complainant alleges wide-ranging grievances from paragraphs 6 through 35, such that one cannot determine the basis for liability to Cross-Complainant for each alleged claim and based on which particular alleged misconduct.

      For example, the first cause of action for involuntary servitude alleges Dianna started working for Verginia but someone forced Dianna to perform labor or services. The claim alleges coercion, deception, withholding of immigration and information and status, threats, and debt bondage among other things, sometimes identifying what Verginia is alleged to have done and sometimes referring to all Cross-Defendants as a group. (Cross-compl ¶¶ 35-70.)

      Verginia cites a federal court case that requires the plaintiff to differentiate allegations when suing more than one defendant. (Swartz v. KPMG LLP (9th Cir. 2007) 476 F.3d 756, 764.) While Swartz construed the federal rules of pleading, the reason for the rule applies equally here as Verginia cannot determine what her alleged participation was in a scheme to commit “slavery, bondage, or threats.”

      Cross-Complainant is required to allege claims in "ordinary and concise language.” (Brown v. Sweet (1928) 95 Cal.App. 117, 123 [it is a general rule that material matters must be distinctly stated, and may not be left to inference to be drawn from doubtful or obscure language.”].)

B.     Involuntary Servitude. Sustained.

      Cross-Complainant brings this action pursuant to 18 U.S.C.A. § 1584 (West). As Verginia observes, this is a criminal statute. Verginia identifies the federal statute that permits a private right of action, however, that action must be brought "in an appropriate district court of the United States.” (18 U.S.C.A. § 1595 (West).)

      Dianna acknowledges section 1595 but argues she can bring a state claim through Civ Code § 52.5, which permits a civil action for damages for victims of human trafficking (not involuntary servitude). (Civ. Code, § 52.5.) This falls under the Tom Bane Civil Rights act, which Dianna does not allege.  Human trafficking requires facts showing that a person deprived or violated the personal liberty of another with the intent to obtain forced labor or services." (Pen. Code, § 236.1.) The elements of the offense are “(1) the defendant either deprived another person of personal liberty or violated that other person's personal liberty; and (2) when the defendant did so, he or she intended to obtain forced labor or services from that person." (People v. Halim (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 632, 643.) Dianna alleges she was hired as a kitchen worker to assist with washing dishes. (Cross-compl ¶ 7.) Whatever payments Verginia made to Dianna were for the job that Dianna performed. (Cross-compl ¶ 13.)

      Dianna alleges she did more than kitchen work, such as accounting and food delivery applications, among other things, and concludes this is involuntary servitude. (Cross-compl ¶ 16, 22.) These bare allegations do not support involuntary servitude, or human trafficking.

      Dianna points to a number of federal statutes prohibiting peonage, involuntary servitude,

forced labor, and trafficking for peonage, slavery, involuntary servitude, or forced labor. However, the alleged claim is limited to section 1584, not any of the other cited statutes, and civil action violations “under this chapter” for peonage, slavery, and trafficking must be brought in federal Court. (Civ. Code § 1595.)

      The Court takes judicial notice of Dianna’s verified answer to the complaint. (Evid. Code § 452(d). As Verginia points out, Dianna’s verified answer to the complaint admits that Verginia paid Dianna for the work she performed, which undermines the contention that she involuntarily provided slave labor. She cannot be both a paid employee and an involuntary worker doing work against her will. (Answer ¶¶ 10- 13 and throughout.)

      The Court is not obliged to accept allegations as true if they conflict with other pleadings, in this case Dianna’s verified answer. (Del E. Webb Corp. v. Structural Materials Co. (1981) 123 Cal.App.3d 593, 604 [“A pleading valid on its face may nevertheless be subject to demurrer when matters judicially noticed by the court render the complaint meritless. In this regard the court passing upon the question of the demurrer may look to affidavits filed on behalf of plaintiff … .”].

C.     Intentional infliction of emotional distress. Sustained.

      To prevail on this claim, plaintiff must allege facts showing (1) extreme and outrageous conduct with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing emotional distress, and (2) plaintiff suffered severe or extreme emotional distress as a proximate result. (Christensen v. Superior Court (1991) 54 Cal.3d 868, 903.) Conduct is “extreme and outrageous” where a defendant (1) abuses a relation or position which gives him power to damage the plaintiff’s interest; (2) knows the plaintiff is susceptible to injuries through mental distress; or (3) acts intentionally or unreasonably with the recognition that the acts are likely to result in illness through mental distress.” (Agarwal v. Johnson (1979) 25 Cal.3d 932, 946.)

      To the extent this claim is based on involuntary servitude, it is derivative of the first cause of action which is defective.

      The outrageous conduct alleged consists of allegations that Cross-Defendants threatened to report her to immigration, used profanity, called her names, misrepresented facts, and sent text messages, or made them orally. “Extreme” conduct “exceeds all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community." (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1051.)

      The claim is uncertain as to Verginia’s participation in extreme and outrageous conduct and the Court cannot determine which of the alleged misconduct alleged in narrative form is the outrageous conduct supporting the claim. Cross-Complainant must allege facts to support each element described above.

      Dianna argues that she alleges “a years-long course of coercive conduct, threats involving her children, exploitation of immigration status, financial pressure, humiliation, compelled labor, late-night demands, emotional trauma, hospitalization, and medical and therapy expenses.” (Opp.  4:15-17. Dianna’s statement in opposition are conclusions. Dianna must allege the outrageous conduct in a concise manner and in a way that informs Verginia how she is involved in the misconduct.

      Additionally, the claim appears to be a combination of making statements and making false statements with intent to deceive. (Cross-compl ¶ 82-3.) If this claim is based on fraud, this is alleged as a third cause of action and will be discussed below.

D.     Intentional misrepresentation. Sustained.

      A claim for fraud requires facts to support the following elements: (1) a misrepresentation, (2) made with knowledge of its falsity, (3) Defendant intended to defraud Plaintiff, i.e., induce Plaintiff’s reliance, (4) Plaintiff justifiably relied on the misrepresentation, (5) causing damage. (Nagy v. Nagy (1989) 210 Cal.App.3d 1262, 1268.)

      Fraud claims are subject to strict requirements of particularity in pleading. (Id.) The particularity requirements necessitate pleading facts showing “how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered." (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 73.) The requirement “applies not only to the alleged misrepresentation, but also to the elements of causation and damage." (Moncada v. West Coast Quartz Corp. (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 768, 776.)

      Dianna alleges: “Cross Defendants repeatedly misrepresented the facts concerning the truthful information for applying various government assistance programs, forced Diana to work for each and every Cross Defendants by performing tasks unelated to her initial employments, and taking leverage of Diana’s immigration status and her inability to reunited with her sons, and her financial hardship, by forcing her to involuntary servitude.” (Cross-compl ¶ 89.)

      Dianna’s opposition confuses more than it clarifies. Dianna argues the claim is based on the fact that “Verginia gave false information to Dianna, caused Dianna to complete and submit applications and paperwork based on that information, intended Dianna to rely on the information so that she would perform work for Verginia’s benefit, and that Dianna later discovered the falsity and suffered damages.” (Opp.  4:22-26.) What damage did Dianna suffer because she was allegedly involved in someone else’s fraud?

      The Court cannot discern what the misrepresentation is that Dianna relied upon that induced her reliance, facts showing reliance was justifiable, or when the misrepresentation was made, by whom, when, and by what means and resulting damage to Dianna.

      Accusations about why Verginia should know better are irrelevant. (Cross-compl ¶ 92.) What this claim appears to allege is that Verginia allegedly defrauded the government with respect to assistance programs. This does not state a claim for fraud against Dianna. Additionally, recycling preceding allegations of generally bad conduct obscures the purported fraud claim causing damage to Dianna. The Cross-Complaint should allege facts material to the cause of action in order to put Verginia on notice of the claims alleged against her. 

E.     Statute of limitations

      The Court is unable to resolve the statute of limitations issues with respect to each claim, because the Court cannot identify precisely the wrongful conduct supporting each claim and when it was made, or what claim is being made.

      In order for the court to sustain demurrer to the complaint based on a statute of limitations defect, “the defect must clearly and affirmatively appear on the face of the complaint… . A demurrer will not lie where the action may be but is not necessarily barred.” (Citizens for a Responsible Caltrans Decision v. Department of Transportation (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 1103, 1117; Marshall v. Gibson, Dunn & Crutcher (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1397, 1398.)

       IV.       CONCLUSION

      Dianna has not persuasively argued that she has a claim for involuntary servitude on the statute she relies on, or that she was made to work involuntarily if she was also Verginia’s paid employee, or that she was “trafficked” under California law.  Nor has Diana demonstrated she has a fraud claim against Verginia if Verginia defrauded the government.

      If there is a reasonable possibility that the defect in a complaint can be cured by amendment, it is an abuse of discretion to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend. [Citation.] The burden is on the plaintiff, however, to demonstrate the manner in which the complaint might be amended ...  [appellant ‘must show in what manner he can amend his complaint and how that amendment will change the legal effect of his pleading"].’ (Association of Community Organizations for Reform Now v. Department of Industrial Relations (1995) 41 Cal.App.4th 298, 302.)

      Dianna’s opposition does not meet that burden. She should be prepared to address at the hearing how these defects can be cured.

      Cross-Defendant’s demurrer to the entire Cross-Complaint is SUSTAINED. 

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING ONIK PETIKYAN, MARY PETIKYAN, AND MAGDALENA PETIKYAN’S DEMURRER TO DIANNA ZOHRABYAN'S CROSS-COMPLAINT (8326)

 

I.       BACKGROUND

      To reiterate, on April 17, 2026, Defendant, Diana Zohrabyan, (“Cross-Complainant” or “Diana”)[2] filed a Cross-Complaint against Verginia Petikyan (“Verginia” or “Cross-Defendant”), Onik Petikyan, Mary Petikyan, and Magdalena Petikyan (collectively, “Cross-Defendants” or “Petikyans”) asserting affirmative relief for (1) involuntary servitude, (2) intentional infliction of emotional distress, and (3) intentional misrepresentation.

      The Petikyan Cross-Defendants demur to the entire Cross-Complaint. The Court’s electronic filing system does not reflect that Dianna opposed this demurrer.

II.     LEGAL STANDARDS

      The Court incorporates the legal standards identified in the ruling on Verginia’s demurrer to the Cross-Complaint.

III.    DISCUSSION

A.     Uncertainty on grounds of “mob” pleading. Sustained.

      The Cross-Complaint is uncertain because Diana alleges claims against Cross-Defendants as a group, without specifying which Cross-Defendant engaged in the wide-ranging wrongful acts. The only allegation against Cross-Defendant Onik is that he is a Los Angeles resident. (Cross-compl ¶ 3.) Mary and Magdalena are Verginia’s daughters who allegedly called Dianna names. (Cross-compl ¶ 10.)

      Mary and Magdalena are alleged to be a part of the alleged fraud on the U.S. government because they “used Diana” by providing false information. (Cross-compl ¶ 11.) Dianna does not allege a factual basis for liability against the Petikyans. The existence of a duty owed may properly be challenged at the demurrer stage, as it is generally a question of law. (Osornio v. Weingarten (2004) 124 Cal.App.4th 304, 316.)

B.     Involuntary Servitude. Sustained.

      Cross-Defendants argue there are no facts to support liability against them for involuntary solitude. The Cross-Complaint alleges Verginia employed Dianna. (Cross-compl ¶ 7.)  Cross-Complainant must allege facts to support the legal conclusion that Dianna was the slave for all Cross-Defendants over 10 years. (Cross-compl ¶ 44.)

      Cross-Defendants evaluate this claim under 18 U.S.C.A. § 1589 (West). However, Cross-Complainant brings this action pursuant to 18 U.S.C.A. § 1584 (West) (Sale into involuntary servitude.) The claim fails because Dianna’s civil action must be brought in federal Court.

(18 U.S.C.A. § 1595 (West).)

      18 U.S.C. § 1589, raised by Cross-Defendants, precludes a person from obtaining labor or service by force, threats, physical restraint or threats of physical restraint. (18 U.S.C.A. § 1589 (West).) Dianna did not allege such acts against Cross-Defendant.

      There are no facts to support liability against the Cross-Defendants for money lent to Dianna. (Cross-compl ¶¶ 14, 39, 47-50.) That Cross-Defendants called Dianna names does not state a claim for involuntary servitude.

      Dianna can bring a civil action for involuntary servitude pursuant to section 1595 but as Cross-Defendants observe, this claim is not alleged.

      Cross-Defendants argue that if Dianna is trying to allege a claim as a victim of human trafficking under Penal Code § 236.1 and Civ. Code § 52.5, the claim is still defective under the alleged facts and Dianna does not allege a claim under Civil Code § 52.5. Human trafficking requires facts showing that a person deprived or violated the personal liberty of another with the intent to obtain forced labor or services." (Pen. Code, § 236.1.) The elements of the offense are “(1) the defendant either deprived another person of personal liberty or violated that other person's personal liberty; and (2) when the defendant did so, he or she intended to obtain forced labor or services from that person." (People v. Halim (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 632, 643.)

      The Cross-Complaint does not allege Cross-Defendants engaged in such conduct.

C.     Intentional infliction of emotional distress. Sustained.

      To prevail on this claim, Cross-Complainant must allege facts showing (1) extreme and outrageous conduct with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing emotional distress, and (2) plaintiff suffered severe or extreme emotional distress as a proximate result. (Christensen v. Superior Court (1991) 54 Cal.3d 868, 903.) Conduct is “extreme and outrageous” where a defendant (1) abuses a relation or position which gives him power to damage the plaintiff’s interest; (2) knows the plaintiff is susceptible to injuries through mental distress; or (3) acts intentionally or unreasonably with the recognition that the acts are likely to result in illness through mental distress.” (Agarwal v. Johnson (1979) 25 Cal.3d 932, 946.)         Dianna does not allege any facts showing Onik engaged in actionable behavior. Mary and Magdalena are alleged to have called Dianna profane names. (Cross-compl ¶ 10.) “Outrageous conduct” may be considered “irritating, insulting or even distressing but it is not actionable and must simply be endured without resort to legal redress.” (Yurick v. Superior Court (1989) 209 Cal.App.3d 1116, 1129 ["there can be no recovery for mere profanity, obscenity, or abuse, without circumstances of aggravation, or for insults, indignities or threats which are considered to amount to nothing more than mere annoyances. The plaintiff cannot recover merely because of hurt feelings."].)

D.     Intentional misrepresentation. Sustained.

      A claim for fraud requires facts to support the following elements: (1) a misrepresentation, (2) made with knowledge of its falsity, (3) Defendant intended to defraud Plaintiff, i.e., induce Plaintiff’s reliance, (4) Plaintiff justifiably relied on the misrepresentation, (5) causing damage. (Nagy v. Nagy (1989) 210 Cal.App.3d 1262, 1268.)

      Fraud claims are subject to strict requirements of particularity in pleading. (Id.) The particularity requirements necessitate pleading facts showing “how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered." (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 73.) The requirement “applies not only to the alleged misrepresentation, but also to the elements of causation and damage." (Moncada v. West Coast Quartz Corp. (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 768, 776.)

      Dianna alleges: “Cross Defendants repeatedly misrepresented the facts concerning the truthful information for applying various government assistance programs, forced Diana to work for each and every Cross Defendants by performing tasks unelated to her initial employments, and taking leverage of Diana’s immigration status and her inability to reunited with her sons, and her financial hardship, by forcing her to involuntary servitude.” (Cross-compl ¶ 89.)

       As Cross-Defendants argue, the introductory factual allegations are mostly directed at Verginia. The claim is otherwise uncertain as to Cross-Defendants’ involvement. Verginia’s alleged perpetration of fraud on the government does not translate into a private right of action for fraud against Cross-Defendants. The paragraph cited above does not provide the specific facts supporting each element of the claim much less, how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered by Cross-Defendants. (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 73.)

F.      Statute of limitations

      The Court is unable to resolve the statute of limitations issues with respect to each claim, because the Court the Cross-Complaint does not specifically identify actionable conduct against Cross-Defendants and when they were made.    In order for the court to sustain demurrer to the complaint based on a statute of limitations defect, “the defect must clearly and affirmatively appear on the face of the complaint… . A demurrer will not lie where the action may be but is not necessarily barred.” (Citizens for a Responsible Caltrans Decision v. Department of Transportation (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 1103, 1117; Marshall v. Gibson, Dunn & Crutcher (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1397, 1398.)

IV.   CONCLUSION

      Cross-Defendants demurrer to the Cross-Complaint is SUSTAINED. If there is a reasonable possibility that the defect in a complaint can be cured by amendment, it is an abuse of discretion to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend. [Citation.] The burden is on the plaintiff, however, to demonstrate the manner in which the complaint might be amended ...  [appellant ‘must show in what manner he can amend his complaint and how that amendment will change the legal effect of his pleading"].’ (Association of Community Organizations for Reform Now v. Department of Industrial Relations (1995) 41 Cal.App.4th 298, 302.)

      Cross-Complainant did not oppose this demurrer. Accordingly, the Court is not inclined to grant leave to amend.

Case Number: 26STCV03749    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: 414

26STCV03749 Team Elite Genetics, LLC v. Broadway Alliance, LLC, et al.

Thursday, August 13, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING DEMURRER IN PART AND OVERRULING IN PART AS TO THE SIXTH, NINTH, TWELFTH, FIFTEENTH, TWENTY FIFTH, TWENTY-SIXTH, AND TWENTY SEVENTH CAUSES OF ACTION IN FIRST AMENDED CROSS-COMPLAINT (4745)

 

 

I.       BACKGROUND

      The complaint alleges that the parties are in the cannabis industry. The action arises from Defendants’ alleged breach of the parties’ Services Agreement, wherein Plaintiff Team Elite Genetics, LLC (“Team Elite” or “Plaintiff”) would cultivate its strains at its facilities, and Defendants would distribute Plaintiff’s products. Plaintiff alleges claims for injunctive relief, breach of contract, and related contract claims, trade secret misappropriation, conversion, and unfair competition.

      On May 18, 2026, Defendants, Broadway Alliance, LLC; Broadway Alliance Cultivation, LLC; and Danny Abdelmalak (collectively, “Broadway”) filed a first amended Cross-Complaint against Team Elite; Steven Castillo; Paet Real Estate, LLC, dba Stella Farms; Garrett Sullivan; and David Maldonado alleging 30 causes of action for breach of contract, breach of fiduciary duty, breach of the implied duty of good faith and fair dealing, interference with contract claims, trespass, and related claims.

      Cross-Defendant, Paet Real Estate, LLC dba Stella Farms (“Stella Farms” or Cross-Defendant) demurs to the following claims:

1.     (6) Intentional interference with prospective economic advantage

2.     (9) Negligent interference with prospective economic advantage

3.     (12) Intentional interference with contractual relations

4.     (15) Negligent interference with contract relations

5.      (25) Civil conspiracy

6.     (26) Negligence

7.     (27) Unfair competition

      Stella Farms argues it was not a party to the Services Agreement at the heart of this action. Stella Farms is alleged only to have commenced a commercial relationship with Team Elite for

the sale and purchase of cannabis products at some unspecified later date. Stella Farms is not alleged to owe a duty to Cross-complainants, such that its alleged interference with Broadway’s relationship was independently wrongful other than for the alleged interference.

II.     LEGAL STANDARDS

      A demurrer tests the sufficiency of a complaint as a matter of law and raises only questions of law. (Schmidt v. Foundation Health (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1702, 1706.) In testing the complaint’s sufficiency, the court must assume the truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations as well as facts that can be reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded facts. The court may also consider matters properly subject to judicial notice. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

III.    DISCUSSION

A.     Material alleged facts

      Cross-Complainant Broadway entered into a consulting Services Agreement with Cross-Defendant Team Elite wherein Team Elite provided a system of cannabis cultivation and production for Broadway’s use and licensed all necessary genetic stock and plants to Broadway. (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 15.) Team Elite granted Broadway an exclusive and irrevocable royalty-free right and license to use, market, and distribute for sale the top five highest selling cannabis strains and products developed by Team Elite Genetics, LLC (“TEG”). (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 14-16.) 

      Broadway and Team Elite jointly developed “Rosin” products including vape products that could not be provided to any third party. (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 21.)

      Broadway alleges Team Elite breached the Agreement by in part, providing Broadway’s exclusive distribution rights to distribution and sale of  TEG products to a separate entity. (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 27.)  Broadway sent a termination notice to Team Elite and its owner Steven Castillo (“Castillo”). (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 29.)

B.     (6) Intentional interference with prospective economic advantage. Sustained.

      To prevail on a claim for intentional interference with prospective economic advantage, plaintiff must prove "(1) the existence, between the plaintiff and some third party, of an economic relationship that contains the probability of future economic benefit to the plaintiff; (2) the defendant's knowledge of the relationship; (3) intentionally wrongful acts designed to disrupt the relationship; (4) actual disruption of the relationship; and (5) economic harm proximately caused by the defendant's action.” (Roy Allan Slurry Seal, Inc. v. American Asphalt South, Inc. (2017) 2 Cal.5th 505, 512.)

      Additionally, the plaintiff must show that that defendant’s conduct was “wrongful by some legal measure other than the fact of interference itself.” (Ixchel Pharma, LLC v. Biogen, Inc. (2020) 266 Cal.Rptr.3d 665.) An act is independently wrongful if it is unlawful, ie., "it is proscribed by some constitutional, statutory, regulatory, common law, or other determinable legal standard.” (Id. at 1142.)

      The first amended Cross-Complaint alleges that Stella Farms was aware of and aided Team Elite’s breach of the Agreement with Broadway. (1st Am. Cross-compl.¶ 42.) Stella Farms was allegedly aware that Broadway had expected relationships with third party clients in the commercial cannabis field, in which Broadway had a probability of future economic benefit. (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 78.) Stella Farms intentionally interfered with those relationships by in part, purchasing and reselling the TEG and Rosin products to which Cross-Complainants Broadway had an exclusive right under the Agreement to sell and distribute resulting in Broadway’s ability  to process and sell quality products to potential clients in this business field. (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 79.)

      Stella Farms argues that Cross-Complainant is required to plead a specific economic relationship with a particular third party that carries a sufficient probability of future economic benefit. This is not supported by the case cited. The “third party” does not have to be identified by name; “it was enough that the defendant was aware its actions would frustrate the legitimate expectations of a specific, albeit unnamed" third party. (Westside Center Associates v. Safeway Stores 23, Inc. (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 507, 527, citing Ramona Manor Convalescent Hospital v. Care Enterprises (1986) 177 Cal.App.3d 1120, 1133 [“We are not persuaded knowledge of the injured party's specific identity or name is a prerequisite to recovery … It is sufficient that he is identified in some manner.”].)

      Cross-Complainant describes the third parties with whom it had a prospective economic relationship as “various clients in the commercial cannabis field with a probability of economic benefit from those relationships” and “third party clients in the commercial cannabis field, and Cross-Complainants’ probability of future economic benefit from those relationships.” (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 77, 78.) Stella Farms’s reply to this argument is instructive. The third-party relationship has to exist. (Westside at 527 [“Without an existing relationship with an identifiable buyer, WCA's expectation of a future sale was ‘at most a hope for an economic relationship and a desire for future benefit’”]; Westside at 526 ["These requirements presuppose the relationship existed at the time of the defendant's allegedly tortious acts lest liability be imposed for actually and intentionally disrupting a relationship which has yet to arise."].) An essential element of the tort of intentional interference with prospective business advantage is the existence of a business relationship with which the tortfeasor interfered." (Roth v. Rhodes (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 530, 546. Elsewhere in the Cross-Complaint, Broadway characterizes the relationship with third parties as “potential clients.” (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 79.) The facts are insufficient to support an existing relationship with a third party.   
      Stella Farms is alleged to have interfered with Broadway’s prospective economic advantage by in part, “purchasing and reselling the TEG Products and Rosin products to which Cross-Complainants Broadway Alliance and Broadway Cultivation have an exclusive right under the Agreement, such that these actions will make it – and has made it – difficult to process and sell quality products to potential clients in this business field.” (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 79.)

      The allegations do not support that Stella Farms’ interference was “wrongful by some legal measure other than the fact of interference itself.” (Ixchel Pharma, LLC v. Biogen, Inc. (2020) 266 Cal.Rptr.3d 665.) Broadway argues it had an exclusive agreement to license, sell, and distribute the products, “establishing determinable legal rights in favor of Broadway.” (Opp.  11:23-26.) As Stella Farms points out, it was not a party to the agreement between Team Elite and Broadway, and therefore, did not owe Broadway a legal duty arising from contract. The allegations demonstrate that Broadway allegedly had legal rights in its favor vis-à-vis Team Elite arising from their Agreement, to which Stella Farms was not a party. (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 12.)  

      Broadway argues that Stella Farms aided and abetted Team Elite’s breach of the Agreement, and therefore, interfered with Broadway’s ability to resell thereby knowingly exploiting Broadway’s exclusive license. (Opp.  12:1-6.) The “aiding and abetting” Team Elite’s breach of its contractual obligations does not make the interference independently wrongful if Stella Farms is not a party to the Agreement, as Stella Farms observes. That Stella Farms allegedly knew of and intentionally interfered with Broadway’s prospective economic advantage does not cure the lack of an “independently wrongful” act by Stella Farms. The “intentional” aspect of the interference is not at issue as Stella Farms argues in reply.

      Stella Farms’ reply cites Applied Equipment Corp. v. Litton Saudi Arabia Ltd. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 503 for the proposition that aiding and abetting another’s wrongful conduct does not make the interference independently wrongful. Applied Equipment held that conspiracy is not a cause of action but a legal doctrine imposing liability on persons who did not actually commit a tort but shares liability for a common plan or design. (Applied Equipment at 510-511.) Applied Equipment observed that "[c]ontract and tort are different branches of law. Contract law exists to enforce legally binding agreements between parties; tort law is designed to vindicate social policy." (Applied Equipment at 514.)

C.     (9) Negligent interference with prospective economic advantage. Sustained.

      Stella Farms argues that this claim is viable only if Stella Farms owes Broadway a duty of care. (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 348.) Stella Farms may be found liable for negligent interference for loss of expected economic advantage if "a special relationship exists between the parties … although the parties were not in contractual privity." (J'Aire Corp. v. Gregory (1979) 24 Cal.3d 799, 804.) In opposition, Broadway argues that every case is governed by the general rule that all persons are required to use ordinary care to prevent others from being injured. (Opp. 13:7-9.) However, as both parties acknowledge, under J’Aire , Stella Farms’ alleged interference must have intended to and did directly affect Broadway. (Id. at 804.)

      Broadway cites the first amended Cross-Complaint alleging that Stella Farms failed to act with reasonable care.” (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 97-103.) Failure to act is not the same as an act intending to affect Broadway. Moreover, a claim for negligent interference with prospective economic advantage also requires that the interference be independently wrongful. (National Medical Transp. Network v. Deloitte & Touche (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 412, 439. [The trial court erred in omitting the “independently wrongful” jury instruction for the claim for negligent interference with prospective economic advantage.].)

D.     (12) Intentional interference with contract relations. Sustained.

      The elements of this claim require showing "(1) a valid contract between plaintiff and a third party; (2) defendant's knowledge of this contract; (3) defendant's intentional acts designed to induce a breach or disruption of the contractual relationship; (4) actual breach or disruption of the contractual relationship; and (5) resulting damage." (Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. Bear Stearns & Co. (1990) 50 Cal.3d 1118, 1126.

      Stella Farms argues that the allegation of a valid existing contract with third party clients to provide goods and service is insufficient to support the claim; rather Broadway must identify the contract and the third party. The first amended Cross-Complaint alleges that Broadway entered into valid and existing contracts with third party clients to provide goods and services, namely commercial cannabis products. (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 114.) The specific identification of the party and contract is not required under Pacific Gas.

      In reply, Stella Farms focuses on the third element (also raised in the moving papers) that its alleged intentional acts must be designed to induce a breach or disruption of the contractual relationship. The first amended Cross-Complaint alleges that Stella Farms acts were to purchase and resell the TEG and Rosin products infringing on Broadway’s exclusive rights although Stella Farms knew that Broadway had exclusive rights to sell the product.  (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 115-116.) The pleading does not allege that Stella Farms engaged in any act designed to induce a third party to breach its contract with Stella Farms. Purchasing inventory from Team Elite is not an act directed to breach a third party’s contract with Broadway. Engaging in competition in this manner is not actionable. (Imperial Ice Co. v. Rossier (1941) 18 Cal.2d 33, 36–37 ["A person is likewise free to carry on his business, including reduction of prices, advertising, and solicitation in the usual lawful manner although some third party may be induced thereby to breach his contract with a competitor in favor of dealing with that person." (Id.)

E.     (15) Negligent interference with contractual relations

      Broadway agrees to dismiss this claim.

F.      (25) Civil Conspiracy. Sustained.

      Broadway argues it has alleged facts to support this claim.  Cross-Complainant must allege to show “the formation and operation of a conspiracy, the wrongful act of any of the conspirators, thereto and damage resulting therefrom." (117 Sales Corp. v. Olsen (1978) 80 Cal.App.3d 645, 649.) The pleading alleges that Stella Farms and others were aware that Team Elite planned to commit the wrongful act of breach of contract and interference with Broadway’s economic relations. (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 201.) Stella Farms and Team Elite agreed that Team Elite intended to breach its contract with Broadway by selling the TEG strains and interfering with Broadway’s economic and contractual relationships. (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 202.)

      Aiding and abetting Team Elite in breaching its contract with Broadway is not an independent wrong since Stella Farms is not a signatory to the contract as noted previously.  This claim also depends on the viability of the claims for negligent and intentional interference with prospective economic advantage, and interference with contract relations, all of which are defective as alleged. Accordingly, this claim is equally defective.

      The claim also alleges a conspiracy between Team Elite and Stella Farms to permanently destroy the crops harvested under the agreement in retaliation for Broadway’s termination of the agreement. (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 33.) The alleged purpose of this conspiracy was to ensure that Broadway would no longer hold the exclusive rights in the products in violation of the Agreement, thereby intentionally depriving Broadway of its expected economic advantage, market position, reputation and total loss of the crop’s commercial value. (1st Am. Cross-compl. ¶ 202.)

      Stella Farms argues that Broadway failed to identify participants in the conversation, a date, or terms of the agreement supposedly reached, and therefore lacks specificity. This purported requirement is not supported by any authority. Stella Farms requires evidentiary facts that are not required at the pleading stage. General rules of pleading do not require that the plaintiff plead evidentiary facts supporting the allegation of ultimate fact. (Careau & Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1371, 1390 [“It is both improper and insufficient for a plaintiff to simply plead the evidence by which he hopes to prove such ultimate facts."].

      Regardless, this claim remains defective to the extent it is derived from the underlying claims.

G.    (27) Unfair Competition. Sustained.

      As defined by statute, “unfair competition” includes “any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200). Its purpose is to protect both consumers and competitors by promoting fair competition in commercial markets for goods and services.” (Gutierrez v. Carmax Auto Superstores California (2018) 19 Cal.App.5th 1234, 1265).

      The UCL is stated in the disjunctive; Plaintiff need only allege a claim that is unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent. Under Section 17200, a practice is prohibited as ‘unfair’ or ‘deceptive’ even if not ‘unlawful’ and vice versa.” (Cel-Tech Communications, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Co. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 163, 180.) The scope of section 17200 is broadly construed and stated in “sweeping language to permit tribunals to enjoin on-going wrongful business conduct in whatever context such activity might occur. Indeed, ... the section was intentionally framed in its broad, sweeping language, precisely to enable judicial tribunals to deal with the innumerable ‘new schemes which the fertility of man's invention would contrive.’" (Cel-Tech at 181.)

      Section 17200 “borrows” violations of other laws and treats them as unlawful practices, however, "a practice may be deemed unfair even if not specifically proscribed by some other law." (Cel-Tech at 180.)

      Stella Farms argues that because this is a derivative claim, it fails with the underlying causes of action. Stella Farms also contends that Broadway did not allege facts to support its standing to assert the claim. In opposition, Broadway contends the underlying causes of action are adequately alleged.

      As discussed, previously the underlying causes of action from which the unfair competition claim is derived are defective as alleged.

      To meet standing requirements, Cross-Complainant must allege that it “suffered injury in fact and has lost money or property as a result of the unfair competition." (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17204.) Assuming the underlying claims can be cured, the standing requirement is met.

IV.   CONCLUSION

      Accordingly, demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the following: (6) Intentional interference with prospective economic advantage; (9) Negligent interference with prospective economic advantage; (12) Intentional interference with contractual relations; (15) Negligent interference with contract relations (no dismissal of this cause of action has been filed) ; (25) Civil conspiracy and (27) Unfair competition.

      While the notice includes a demur to the 26th cause of action for negligence, it is not addressed in Stella Farms’s points and authorities, and therefore, demurrer to the 26th cause of action is OVERRULED.
