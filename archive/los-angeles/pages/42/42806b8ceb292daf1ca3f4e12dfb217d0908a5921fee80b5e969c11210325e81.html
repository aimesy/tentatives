DEPARTMENT I LAW AND MOTION RULINGS

If the parties wish to submit on the tentative ruling and avoid a court appearance on the matter, the moving party must contact the opposing party and all other parties who have appeared in the action and confirm that each will submit on the tentative ruling. Please call the court no later than 4:30 p.m. on the court day before the hearing, leave a message with the court clerk at (310) 260-3629 advising her that all parties will submit on the tentative ruling and waive hearing, and finally, serve notice of the Court's ruling on all parties entitled to receive service. If any party declines to submit on the tentative ruling, then no telephone call is necessary, and all parties should appear at the hearing.

Fourth, here are the issues that remain.  The exhibit list.  The court encourages the parties to work
together further on this and waive objections where the parties know that the
exhibit is admissible—such as where it is obvious that a foundation can be
laid.  Further, procedural objections—such
as an objection that an exhibit is an improper amalgam of other documents,
ought to be something the parties can work out. 
The same is true where the objection is that it is a duplicate.  The court appreciates that a lot of work went
into this, and the court further appreciates that many of the exhibits are
there to insure that they can be introduced if needed even if the likelihood is
that the exhibit will not in fact be offered. 
The court has one important note, though.  The fact that an exhibit was admitted during
the bench trials does not make it admissible now.  The rules are a bit different.  For example, the court was not as concerned
about a 352 objection at a bench trial as it might be here.  The issues the court had to decide may not be
relevant or pertinent here.  So each
exhibit will have to be re-offered to be admitted.  Jury instructions.  The parties need to work together further on this.  Some of the CACI instructions should be
easier to agree upon, for example. 
Plaintiff has a whopping 34 special instructions.  The court understands, though, that many of
them relate to issues that the court previously decided such that the court
will need to instruct the jury as to the court’s conclusions.  But the parties need to work more closely on
these.  The court will also need to know
if defendants are objecting to the giving of the instruction in full or whether
it is a wording issue.  That can be
further discussed on August 17, assuming the case goes forward (which the court
believes it will).  Lodging of
Depositions.  There are rules about
who needs to lodge.  According to the
general rule (if the court’s memory is correct), the party with the original
transcript needs to lodge it.  That said,
the parties might want to agree that a certified copy can be lodged instead so
long as it has all corrections made by the deponent, and they might want to
agree that the party intending to use the deposition transcripts will take care
of lodging the transcript.  As to trial
transcripts, the parties ought to just split the cost of it.  Short statements of the case.  They are not short.  The court suggests the following:
mini-openings.  The court would suggest
that before voir dire, each side give a mini-opening not to exceed 4 minutes in
length.  That way the parties do not have
to agree on language, and some detail beyond the typical short statement can be
given.  Defendants will decide between
themselves how to allot their four minutes. 
Special Verdicts.  The
court sees that the parties differ.  They
ought to meet and confer further, but if they cannot come to an agreement, the
court will ask that the parties submit a red-lined version with the redlining
be defendants’ version (meaning that strike outs and additions will be in a
color other than black and will reflect what defendants seek to strike or add).  Hopefully, that will clarify the major
differences.  As a general proposition,
the traditional way to deal with verdicts is not to have a preliminary
statement in the verdict form about facts that the court has ordered.  Those will be included in the jury
instructions and the jury instructions will be in the jury room for the jury’s
reference.  Rather, the court prefers
that the verdict form be limited to questions the jury must answer.  The court typically sees each element listed
as a separate question with liability first for each cause of action.  If the element is conceded, sometimes there
is a question, and sometimes not.  After
the liability questions, then would come affirmative defenses as to liability,
again with each defense separately set out and with each disputed element as a
question.  If a defense only relates to a
particular cause of action, it ought to go right after liability as to that
cause of action.  If it is a defense to
liability in total, then it would come after the liability questions.  If a cause of action could lead to punitive
damages, the punitive damage question would be at the end of the questions as
to that cause of action.  Where the
damages essentially overlap (as the court believes they do here), the damages
questions would follow liability and affirmative defenses (other than the
affirmative defense of mitigation). 
Otherwise, if it is done after each cause of action, juries get confused
and the court has seen it mess up.  To
the extent that plaintiff seeks prejudgment interest in tort, that has to be a
question for the jury.  That would be
followed by the damages questions, after which would come questions concerning mitigation—whether
there a failure to mitigate, and, if so, the amount of it.  And that would lead to the final net
verdict.  The parties need not do it that
way, but in the event of a conflict, the court would tend to gravitate to that
methodology.

 

Fifth, the motion to compel. 
Generally, the court views punitive damages subpoenae as proper at the
start of the trial.  The statute limiting
discovery of punitive damage information has no application to a trial
subpoena, so it is proper.  Further,
because punitive damages will begin immediately following the jury’s verdict
finding them appropriate, the court will not make plaintiff wait until then for
the documents to be turned over. 
Plaintiff needs to be ready to go immediately, and having to sort through
a bunch of papers on the fly is not proper. 
The documents will be subject to an automatic protective order and may
not be introduced or used without leave of court until the punitive damages
phase, though.  So the documents will be
turned over on Monday, August 17, 2026, if the case remains on track.  That leaves the question of overbreadth.  And here, the court has concerns.  Normally, it would not be that hard.  But here, in Phase II defendants’ experts
testified that defendants’ books and records were not reliable.  Given that, the court is hard pressed to do
what it usually does, and simply have what are essentially accountants’ records
turned over.  Rather, the court tends to
agree that plaintiff will need underlying documents.  The court is seriously considering ordering
defendants to turn over tax returns for the last two years given the financial
issues here.  Certainly that is
appropriate for NHH.  But the court is
considering it for Mavaddat as well.  The
court is considering it, but is reluctant to do so if there are other
options.  The court tends to agree that
perhaps the four months of most recent statements from any financial
institution associated with Mavaddat or his entities (directly or indirectly)
would be a good way to ascertain his assets other than real estate and
currency.  As to real estate, the court
would be inclined to have defendants turn over the most recent property tax
bills for any real estate relating to Mavaddat or his entities (directly or
indirectly).  And finally, the court
would think that Mavaddat ought to turn over any promissory notes as to him or
his entities (directly or indirectly) whether he is the borrower or the lender
and any financial records within the last 12 months related thereto (like
payments made or received).  That is
relatively specific, not overly burdensome, and ought to give plaintiff enough
information to meet its burden of showing defendants’ net worth.  The court recognizes that there are some
assets that might not be listed—for all the court knows, Mavaddat owns a
yacht—but it ought to be enough to get plaintiff where it needs to go.  Alternatively, defendants can waive this item
of proof by conceding that whatever number the jury determines would not be
improper on the basis that it is too high given defendants’ financial
condition.  That would not be a waiver of
a due process argument—that is, the number is just too high under the fourteenth
amendment.  But it would relieve
plaintiff of the burden of demonstrating defendants’ net worth, and then the calculus
would be different.  If defendants will
not concede that—and the court is not suggesting that they should—then plaintiff
will need this information to meet its burden. 
The court also notes that no one will be able to testify for the defense
concerning financial condition that relate to any document sought but not
turned over.  Can’t use the objection as
a sword and a shield.

TRIAL PROCEDURES

 

The court does not know if the parties have retained a court
reporter.  If not, then the parties are
ORDERED at the conclusion of each day of trial to have lead counsel MEET AND
CONFER to arrive at an agreed settled statement.  The meeting will last until the earlier of:
(1) reaching an agreement; or (2) midnight. 
No later than 9:30 on the following court day, the parties will submit
the agreed statement to the court.  If
the parties cannot agree, they will submit plaintiffs’ proposal red-lined
against defendants’ proposal.  On even
numbered calendar days, plaintiffs will be responsible for the submission.  On odd numbered calendar days, defendants
will be responsible for the submission. 
(That refers to the day of the month for the trial, not the day of
submission.)  Alternatively, the parties
may elect to waive appeal, although the court STRONGLY ADVISES AGAINST IT.  The point of the settled statement is to have
a record for appeal of what occurred during the trial (beyond that reflected in
the Clerk’s Transcript).  If there is no
appeal, there is no need for the statement. 
The reason that the court is ordering daily meetings is because too
often that process begins after the notice of appeal is filed, which could be
months after the trial has concluded.  By
that time, memories have faded—especially the court’s memory.  Yet the parties must either agree or the
court must settle the statement.  In the
court’s experience, the sooner that is done, the better.  If there is a court reporter, of course, then
this is not needed.

 

The court holds trials Monday through Thursday, starting at
10:30.  Fridays are reserved for other
matters, but the jury can deliberate on Fridays.  The court has an ELMO available for the
parties’ use and the court screens can be linked to counsel’s lap tops.  Other technology must be provided by the
parties and the parties need to work with court staff to be sure that it will
be up and running as needed.  The
technology the court has allows a party to put a document on a screen that can
be seen only by counsel and the court before being put on screens visible to
the jury.  That can be used when an
exhibit is being used but has not yet been admitted.  Sadly, though, the witness cannot see the
document, so the witness will need a hard copy.

 

The court does not appreciate speaking objections.  The parties should be very careful not to
divulge the contents of a document or an exhibit to the jury through
questioning until the document or exhibit has been admitted.

 

The court will allow use of the screens for opening
statements (but not the mini-openings). 
Please remember that opening statement is opening statement, not opening
argument.  The slides need not be
exchanged in advance, but the parties should be careful not to abuse the
privilege with slides that go beyond organizational type slides (like a topic
index).  Documents can be shown to the
jury or a transcript read to the jury only if there is no good faith objection
to that testimony or document. 
Otherwise, it can be described to the jury but not read or shown.

 

The parties will lodge on the first day of trial the
following trial notebooks.  Volume 1: (1)
The operative complaint; (2) the operative answer; (3) any substantive rulings
made by the court that are binding at trial; (4) the witness list; (5) the
exhibit list; (6) the short statement of the case (if applicable); and (7)
motions in limine and rulings. 
(Item 7 might have to be its own volume.)  Volume 2: A list of jury instructions as well
as the instructions themselves as they are to be read to the jury (meaning no
brackets or the like) and in the order in which they are to be read.  If there is a disputed instruction, it will
be so noted on the list.  The actual
draft instructions follow.  However,
where there are dueling instructions, they will be placed together.  Volume 3: Exhibit binder.  There must be a binder for the court, the
witness, and the judicial assistant.  The
parties will also lodge the deposition transcripts of any witnesses on the
witness list.  The parties should also
inform the court whether they want to do “mini-openings” to the venire (as the
court has suggested).  If they do, they
will be limited to 4 minutes per side and the court will not read the short
statement of the case. 

 

For voir dire, the court generally gives the venire an
introductory statement and reads the short statement of the case (or allows the
mini-openings).  The court then gives
additional information about the case—such as the list of witnesses and the
estimated length.  The court then deals
with hardships, releasing jurors for the remainder of the day who do not claim
hardship.  The court asks the parties to
meet and confer regarding any jurors to which they will stipulate there is a
hardship or to which they both believe there is no hardship.  The court then conducts the hardship
examination.  While the court encourages
agreement of the parties, the final hardship decision is the court’s
alone.  Note that this is before voir
dire.  The remaining venire typically
convenes the next day and voir dire begins. 
The court generally uses a “six-pack” procedure.  Voir dire begins with the court, then with
plaintiffs, and then goes to defendants. 
Voir dire may be had as to all 18 jurors first selected.  When voir dire is concluded, the court will
typically excuse the jury or conduct a side-bar with counsel to discuss
challenges for cause.  The defense goes
first and makes all challenges for cause the defense wishes to make.  A challenge for cause can be made as to any
juror for whom there was voir dire. 
After the defense, plaintiffs will make their challenges for cause.  Once the parties have passed for cause (or
the court has ruled on the challenges) those jurors cannot be later challenged
for cause unless there is new information. 
When those challenges are resolved, the successfully challenged jurors
are excused, the venire reconvenes, and the “box” of 12 is filled to the extent
of successful cause challenges.  After
that, plaintiffs and defendants may exercise peremptory challenges, alternating
between plaintiffs and defendants with plaintiffs going first.  Peremptory challenges may only be made to the
12 jurors in the box, not to those not yet in the box.  A “pass” by one side does not use a challenge
nor does it establish that the remaining jurors are immune from peremptory
challenge.  If the box cannot be filled
due to challenges, additional names will be called for the six pack.  Voir dire will then go forward as to the
newly-called prospective jurors only, after which the court will go through the
cause procedure (as to the newly called jurors only) and then peremptory
challenges, which can be made against any juror in the box.  If a party makes a Batson/Wheeler
motion, it must do so before the challenged juror is actually excused.  The reason is so that the court can re-seat
the juror if the challenge is successful. 
When the 12 members of the jury are chosen, the court will utilize a
similar procedure regarding alternates, still using the “six pack.”  Peremptory challenges can only be made as to
the jurors who are actually in the alternate seats.  The jurors are usually sworn when the panel
and alternates have been selected.  In
the event that a second venire is needed, the court will discuss procedure with
the parties.  The court reminds the
parties that, while some latitude is allowed during voir dire, the court will
not allow pre-conditioning of the panel. 
The court views there to be multiple parties on the defense side.  Accordingly, each side will have 8 peremptory
challenges to the jury and the defense can allocate between themselves.  The court’s initial thinking is that there
will be four alternates.  Each side will
therefore have four peremptory challenges as to the alternates.

 

The court advises the parties to discuss the following two
stipulation before jury selection begins. 
First, an agreement on fewer peremptory challenges.  For example, 6 per side may be sufficient, or
2 per side for alternates.  The reason is
that the court would like to avoid having to call panel after panel, and the
challenges could cause the court to do that. 
Second, an agreement as to whether a verdict can be returned with
less that 12 jurors.  This would only
occur if we run out of alternates midway through the trial.  If that happens, and if we lose an additional
person, then there are only 11 left, and a mistrial is automatic.  To get around that, the parties might want to
agree that if, and only if, that occurs, a verdict can be returned with fewer
than 12 jurors.  If such an agreement is
reached, the parties will need to agree on how many jurors will be enough.  For example, the parties might want to agree
that as few as 8 jurors can (if necessary) deliver the verdict.  The parties will also need to agree as to the
number of jurors that must agree for a verdict. 
For example, if 11 jurors can deliver a verdict, do 9 of them have to
agree, or will 8 suffice?  The court
urges the parties to discuss this now because once voir dire begins, reaching
an agreement becomes exponentially harder. 
If an agreement is reached on either stipulation, it should be recited
to the court.  If no agreement is
reached, the court does not want to know who was willing or not willing to
agree.  Only that no agreement was reached.

 

At the close of each day, the parties will exchange the
witnesses that each intends to call in the next three court days and the order
in which they will be called.  The court
understands that plans change, but a good faith list will be given.  This allows the parties to best prepare for
cross examination. 

 

The court is very liberal about taking witnesses out of
order, especially third parties.  If the
defense needs to put a witness on during plaintiff’s case in chief, the court
warns the defense that the out of order witness’s testimony cannot form a part
of any nonsuit motion.  The court will
consider allowing witnesses to testify remotely if there is good cause.  However, the party calling the witness
remotely takes all of the technology risk. 
Further, the party calling the witness must ensure that the witness will
be able to review any document, including documents used during cross
examination other than impeachment exhibits not on the exhibit list and not
disclosed ahead of time.  The witness
must be visible to the jury at all times. 
If plaintiff calls a witness adversely during the case in chief, the
court typically gives the defense a choice. 
The defense can either do a short examination at that time and reserve
the right to call the witness during the defense case in chief for a fuller
examination, or the defense may do the full examination then and there, but the
court would not expect the witness to be called again during the defense case
in chief other than for new and unexpected issues.  The goal is to avoid having the same
testimony given twice. 

 

Although the court does not require this, the court has a
suggestion: witness notebooks.  This is a
notebook that is unique to each witness and has the documents that the party
intends to use with that witness.  By
putting these documents in a single notebook, the witness (and the parties and
the court) will not have to keep going between bulky binders.  Such a notebook must be exchanged with the
other side at the start of the examination. 
The court urges the same for cross examination (excluding documents
purely for impeachment).  While that
might give a bit of a heads up to the witness and the other side, not much in
actual practice.  The court has seen this
in use, and it is amazing how well it actually works, although it takes extra
effort.
