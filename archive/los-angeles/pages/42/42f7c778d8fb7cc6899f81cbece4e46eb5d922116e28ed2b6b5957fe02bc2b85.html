DEPARTMENT 224 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 22STCV06081    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: 224

Pitt v. Jolie 

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiffs William B. Pitt and Mondo Bongo, LLC's Motion to Compel Defendant Tenute del Mondo B.V.'s Further Response to Request for Production No. 11 and Production of Responsive Documents is GRANTED in part and DENIED in part as described below.

A further verified response, production, and amended privilege log are due within 20 days of this order.

Defendant Tenute del Mondo B.V.'s request for monetary sanctions is denied.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On February 17, 2022, Plaintiffs William B. Pitt and Mondo Bongo, LLC ("Plaintiffs") filed the Complaint in this action against Defendants Angelina Jolie, Nouvel, LLC, Yuri Shefler, Alexey Oliynik, and Tenute del Mondo B.V. ("Tenute"). Related cross-actions are pending.

Plaintiffs served their Requests for Production of Documents, Set Two, on Tenute, including Request for Production No. 11, which seeks all documents and communications concerning the diligence performed or contemplated to be performed by any of the Stoli Parties in connection with the Transaction. Tenute responded with general and specific objections and a qualified statement that it would produce non-privileged documents located after a reasonable search.

In January 2024, Tenute produced a May 27, 2021 email chain bearing Bates number Oliynik_00002643, in which Tenute CEO Jon Pepper circulated a document titled "Provence - DD risks.docx" to Christopher Caldwell, Alexey Oliynik, Damian McKinney, and Todd Culyba, Stoli Group's General Counsel. Tenute produced the cover email and the executives' responsive comments without redaction but withheld the attachment in its entirety.

On August 3, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an Informal Discovery Conference ("IDC") statement raising Tenute's withholding of the attachment. Tenute filed a response on August 12, 2026. On August 19, 2026, the Court held the IDC and directed Tenute to serve a privilege log identifying the dates, authors, versions, and page counts of the withheld material.

The parties continued to meet and confer on August 20, 2026. On August 22, 2026, Tenute served a supplemental log titled "Provence - DD Risks Privilege Log," which identifies three versions of the document: an original dated May 27, 2021; a May 27, 2021 revision incorporating Mr. Caldwell's comments; and a June 16, 2021 version forwarded by Mr. Culyba to Mr. Oliynik. Tenute asserts attorney-client privilege and work product protection over each version. Mr. Culyba's continued deposition was taken on August 25, 2026, and the parties met and conferred again by videoconference that day.

On August 26, 2026, Plaintiffs filed this Motion.

On September 4, 2026, Tenute filed its Opposition.

Meet and Confer

The Declaration of Adebola Olofin executed on August 26, 2026, sets forth the meet and confer efforts of counsel for the Plaintiffs, and counsel’s attempts to resolve the discovery dispute informally through IDC and videoconference. The declaration satisfies the requirement of Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040.

Legal Standard

“A party may demand that any other party produce . . . a document that is in the possession, custody, or control of the party on whom the demand is made.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.010(b).) The demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to the demand if the demanding party deems that (1) a statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete, (2) a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive, or (3) an objection in the response is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310(a).) “The motion shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand,” and “[t]he motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310(b).)

Unlike with requests to compel further interrogatories, a moving party seeking to compel further responses for requests for production must show good cause along with satisfaction of the meet and confer requirement. (CCP§ 2031.310(b).)

To prevail, a party moving for an order compelling further responses to a document production demand must first offer specific facts demonstrating “good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) This burden “is met simply by a fact-specific showing of relevance.” (TBG Ins. Servs. Corp. v. Superior Court (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.) If “good cause” is shown by the moving party, the burden shifts to the responding party to justify any objections made to document disclosure. (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98.)

“The party to whom a demand for inspection, copying, or testing, or sampling has been directed shall respond separately to each item or category of item by any of the following: (1) A statement that the party will comply with the particular demand... A statement that the party ... will comply with the particular demand shall state that the production will be allowed either in whole or in part, and that all documents or things in the demanded category that are in possession, custody, or control of that party and to which no objection is being made will be included in the production. (2) A representation that the party lacks the ability to comply with the demand... A representation of inability to comply with the particular demand ... shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand. This statement shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in possession, custody, or control of the responding party. The statement shall set forth the name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of items. (3) An objection to the particular demand... If only part of an item or category of item in a demand... is objectionable, the response shall contain a statement of compliance or a representation of inability to comply with respect to the remainder of that item or category. If the responding party objects to the demand... the response shall do both of the following: [a] Identify with particularity any document or tangible thing... falling within any category of item in the demand to which an objection is being made, [b] Set forth clearly the extent of, and the specific ground for the objection.... If an objection is based on a claim of privilege or a claim that the information sought is protected work product, the response shall provide sufficient factual information for other parties to evaluate the merits of that claim, including, if necessary, a privilege log.” (California Code of Civil Procedure §§ 2031.210, 2031.220, 2031.230, and 2031.240)

Good Cause

Plaintiffs contend that the withheld assessment bears directly on the Stoli Parties' knowledge of Mr. Pitt's then-ongoing negotiations with Ms. Jolie and their evaluation of the risks of proceeding with the Transaction notwithstanding those negotiations, which are subjects material to Plaintiffs' breach and tortious interference claims and to Defendants' cross-claims.

Plaintiffs have established good cause for the requests.

The May 27, 2021 Original Assessment and the May 27, 2021 Revision

Tenute has not carried its burden of establishing the preliminary facts supporting an attorney-client privilege claim as to these two versions.

The contemporaneous record, which consists of Tenute's own unredacted production, reflects that the assessment was prepared by Mr. Pepper, a non-attorney, at the request of Mr. McKinney, a non-attorney, and was described by its author as a draft of his own notes for discussion. It was transmitted simultaneously to four recipients, three of whom are business executives. One of those executives, Mr. Caldwell, responded with substantive comments that Tenute produced without redaction, and a revised version incorporating those business comments was circulated the same day. The produced chain reflects no response, direction, or input from Mr. Culyba.

Tenute's showing rests principally on two facts: that Mr. Pepper's cover email invited Mr. Culyba's "builds / questions," and that Mr. Culyba testified in August 2026 that the document "was sent to [him] looking for advice."

Neither carries Tenute's burden.

As to the first, an invitation for comment directed to one of four recipients does not establish that the dominant purpose of the document's preparation and transmittal was to obtain legal advice, particularly where the same transmittal solicited and received commercial input from the other recipients. Tenute is correct that a client need not incant the words "legal advice" for the privilege to attach, and that the presence of commercial subject matter does not defeat a privilege claim. But the question is not whether Mr. Culyba was asked for input but rather whether the record establishes that obtaining his legal assessment was the dominant purpose of the communication. On this record it does not. The employee-to-counsel privilege Chadbourne recognizes turns on the corporation's dominant purpose in directing the transmission, and here the assessment was compiled at a non-attorney's direction to evaluate transaction risks and was circulated for business comment. (Chadbourne, supra, 60 Cal.2d at pp. 734, 737-738.)

As to the second, Mr. Culyba's testimony is materially qualified. He testified that he lacks a specific recollection of reviewing the document and framed his answer in terms of likelihood given that a question had been directed to him. His broader testimony that he "didn't wear more than one hat" during the Transaction speaks to his general role and not to the dominant purpose of this communication. Testimony that an in-house lawyer acted only as a lawyer does not, without more, render privileged every document sent to him.

Tenute's reliance on Costco is misplaced at this stage. Costco holds that where the dominant purpose is established, the privilege protects the communication in its entirety, including its factual content. (Costco, supra, 47 Cal.4th at pp. 739-740.) It does not relieve the proponent of establishing the dominant purpose in the first instance.

Johnson v. Department of Transportation (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 917 is likewise distinguishable. There the record independently established that the communication was made for the purpose of investigating and defending pending litigation. Here, the record supports the opposite inference and Tenute has offered no declaration from Mr. Pepper, Mr. McKinney, or any other participant to supply the missing foundation.

The work product claim fares no worse and no better. Tenute has not shown that either May 27, 2021 version was prepared by an attorney, at an attorney's direction, or that it would not exist but for counsel's initiative, decision, and effort. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.030; Coito v. Superior Court (2012) 54 Cal.4th 480, 494-495.)

Coito confirms that a writing independently prepared by a non-attorney does not become protected work product simply upon its transmission to an attorney. (Id. at p. 494.)

Having found no privilege established, the Court does not reach Plaintiffs' waiver argument or Tenute's response under Evidence Code section 912.

The motion is granted as to the May 27, 2021 original assessment and the May 27, 2021 revision. Tenute shall produce both versions in full.

The June 16, 2021 Version

The analysis differs as to the June 16, 2021 version, which Tenute's log identifies as having been sent by Mr. Culyba himself to Mr. Oliynik and as incorporating Mr. Culyba's input.

To the extent that version contains content authored or supplied by Mr. Culyba in his capacity as counsel and reflecting legal advice, that content is privileged, and the privilege is not defeated by his inability to recall specific edits nearly five years later. To the extent the version consists of the earlier assessment carried forward, Tenute cannot rely on later attorney input to render the earlier content privileged retroactively. Tenute's assertion that "[t]here is no earlier, unprivileged draft" for Plaintiffs' argument to reach depends on the premise that the May 27 versions were privileged from the outset, which the Court has rejected above.

The motion is granted in part as to the June 16, 2021 version. Tenute shall produce that version subject to narrowly tailored redactions limited to content that Mr. Culyba added, and that reflects his legal advice or his impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal research or theories. Content carried forward from the May 27, 2021 versions shall not be redacted.

The motion is otherwise denied as to the June 16, 2021 version to the extent it seeks production of that version without redaction.

Monetary Sanctions

Tenute requests monetary sanctions of $15,000 jointly and severally against Plaintiffs and their counsel under Code of Civil Procedure sections 2031.310(h), 2023.010(h), and 2023.030, on the ground that Plaintiffs' motion was not substantially justified.

Section 2031.310(h) authorizes sanctions against a party who "unsuccessfully makes or opposes" a motion to compel further response, absent substantial justification or other circumstances making the sanction unjust. Plaintiffs have not unsuccessfully made this motion.

The motion is granted in substantial part, and Plaintiffs' position was substantially justified in any event. The dispute presented a genuine question of dominant purpose on a contested record, and Tenute's own supplemental privilege log, which first identified the withheld versions, was not served until August 22, 2026, four days before Plaintiffs' moving papers were due.

The Court further notes that both sides participated meaningfully in the IDC process and narrowed the dispute to a single category of documents. Under these circumstances, the imposition of sanctions would be unjust.

Tenute's request for monetary sanctions is denied.

Conclusion

Plaintiffs' Motion to Compel Further Response to Request for Production No. 11 and Production of Responsive Documents is GRANTED in part and DENIED in part as described above.

Within 20 days of this order, Tenute shall (1) serve a further verified, code-compliant response to RFP No. 11; (2) produce the May 27, 2021 original assessment and the May 27, 2021 revision of "Provence - DD risks.docx" in full; (3) produce the June 16, 2021 version subject only to the narrowly tailored redactions permitted above; and (4) serve an amended privilege log consistent with this order.

Tenute's request for monetary sanctions is denied.

Case Number: 23STCV31795    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: 224

Anonymity Assets, LLC v. Pacific Crest Realty Advisors LLC et al.,

TENTATIVE RULING

The motion for attorney's fees is GRANTED IN PART. The Court awards $88,592.77 in attorney's fees against Plaintiffs Anonymity Assets, LLC and Victoria Green, jointly and severally. The request for $2,946.39 in costs is DENIED.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On December 29, 2023, Plaintiffs filed the Complaint in this action, asserting a sole cause of action for breach of contract (specific performance) against Defendants.

On October 14, 2025, the Court granted Defendants' Motion for Summary Judgment, holding Plaintiffs' claim barred by collateral estoppel.

On November 14, 2025, Defendants filed the instant Motion for Attorney's Fees, seeking $88,592.77 in fees and $2,946.39 in costs.

On September 4, 2026, Anonymity filed its opposition. Green filed no opposition.

Legal Standard

“[A]s a general rule, attorney fees are not recoverable as costs unless they are authorized by statute or agreement.” (People ex rel. Dept. of Corporations v. Speedee Oil Change Systems, Inc. (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 424, 429.)

For contracts actions, “where the contract specifically provides that attorney's fees and costs, which are incurred to enforce the contract, shall be awarded either to one of the parties or to the prevailing party, then the party who is determined to be the party prevailing on the contract, whether he or she is the party specified in the contract or not, shall be entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees in addition to other costs.” (Civ. Code § 1717(a).) “[T]he party prevailing on the contract shall be the party who recovered a greater relief in the action on the contract.” (Id.)

“The determination of what constitutes a reasonable fee generally ‘begins with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate....’” “[T]he lodestar is the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community; it may be adjusted by the court based on factors including, as relevant herein, (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award....” (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.)

“In determining the lodestar amount, a prevailing party generally may not recover for work on causes of action on which the party was unsuccessful” except in some cases where are amounts are so intertwined that it would be impracticable to separate them out. (Mann v. Quality Old Time Service, Inc. (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 328, 342; Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 157.) " ‘Attorneys fees need not be apportioned between distinct causes of action where plaintiff's various claims involve a common core of facts or are based on related legal theories.'"" Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 159.) “[A]ttorney fees need not be reduced for work on unsuccessful claims if the claims ‘are so intertwined that it would be impracticable, if not impossible, to separate the attorney's time into compensable and noncompensable units.’" (Mann v. Quality Old Time Service, Inc. (2006) 139 Cal. App. 4th 328, 342.)

In setting the hourly rate for an attorney fees award, courts are entitled to consider the rate of “‘fees customarily charged by that attorney and others in the community for similar work.’" (Bihun v. AT&T Information Systems, Inc. (1993) 13 Cal. App. 4th 976, 997, overruled on other grounds by Lakin v. Watkins Associated Indus. (1993) 6 Cal. 4th 644, 664.) “‘A fee request that appears unreasonably inflated is a special circumstance permitting the trial court to reduce the award or deny one altogether.” (Chavez v. City of L.A. (2010) 47 Cal.4th 970, 990.)

Motion for Attorney’s Fees

Here, Defendants move the Court for an award of attorney's fees in the amount of $88,592.77 and costs in the amount of $2,946.39, for a total of $91,539.16.

Entitlement to Fees

Section 7.3 of the Agreement provides that where any party to the Agreement, "or any person or entity acting for them," commences an action or proceeding to enforce any provision of the Agreement, or is required to defend any action or proceeding the defense to which is any provision of the Agreement, "the unsuccessful Party or Parties agrees to pay the successful Party or Parties all recoverable attorneys' fees and costs."

Plaintiffs pleaded a single cause of action for breach of contract seeking specific performance of the purchase terms set out in the Agreement. That is an action on a contract within the meaning of Civil Code section 1717. Defendants obtained summary judgment and Plaintiffs recovered nothing. Defendants are the parties prevailing on the contract.

Anonymity argues that it never signed the Agreement and never contracted to assume its fee-shifting provision, and that section 1717 reciprocity does not confer fee rights on every nonsignatory. Relying on Sessions Payroll, it contends that a nonsignatory suing as a third-party beneficiary cannot recover contractual fees absent evidence that the contracting parties intended to include it within the fee provision, and that Defendants have identified no such language.

However, here, Anonymity did not sue as a third-party beneficiary. It sued as Green's assignee. In granting summary judgment, the Court found that "Green, who assigned her rights to Anonymity, is in privity with it as a matter of law," and relied on that finding to supply the privity element of collateral estoppel.

Sessions Payroll provides that reciprocity applies where the nonsignatory would have been entitled to fees had it prevailed. (Sessions Payroll, supra, 84 Cal.App.4th at pp. 677-679.) Anonymity did not defend a contract claim brought against it. Rather, it affirmatively prosecuted this action seeking specific performance. Had it prevailed, it would have sought fees under section 7.3, which by its terms reaches "any person or entity acting for" a party.

Anonymity further argues that Constantinescu is not a party to the Agreement and has cited no basis for fees, and it quotes Defendants' summary judgment brief stating that he acted only as the Receiver's representative and bears no personal liability. Under Reynolds Metals, that cuts the other way. Plaintiffs named Constantinescu as a defendant on their single contract cause of action and sought to hold him answerable for performance of the Agreement. Had they prevailed on that theory, they could have recovered fees against him under section 7.3. A defendant sued on a contract as though bound by it may recover fees when he prevails by showing he was not. (Reynolds Metals, supra, 25 Cal.3d at p. 128.)

Defendants are accordingly entitled to recover their reasonable attorney's fees under section 7.3 of the Agreement and Civil Code section 1717. Green is liable as a signatory to the Agreement. Anonymity is liable because it prosecuted this action on the Agreement as Green's assignee and would have been entitled to fees under section 7.3 had it prevailed. Constantinescu may recover because Plaintiffs sued him on the Agreement as though he were bound by it, and he prevailed.

Lodestar

Defendants request a lodestar in the amount of $88,592.77, comprising $70,028.00 billed by Kunzler Bean & Adamson, PC ("KBA") from the inception of the action through approximately February 2025, and $18,564.77 billed by Buchalter thereafter. (Adamson Decl. ¶¶ 8-10.)

The billing records reflect hourly rates of $665 (2024) and $715 (2025) for Mr. Adamson, a shareholder admitted in 2007 with more than eighteen years of litigation experience; $500 for attorneys S. Richards and R. Calderon; $425 for S. Hughes; $325 for L. Scoresby; $195 to $275 for A. Ahlstrom; $225 for paralegal time; and $72 to $105 for staff research services. (Adamson Decl. ¶ 11 & Exhs. 3-4.)

The Court finds the hourly rates to be reasonable.

Defendants do not state the approximate number of hours for which they seek compensation. The Adamson declaration sets forth only the total fees billed by each firm. (Adamson Decl. ¶¶ 8-10.) The Buchalter invoices include timekeeper recaps reflecting 28.30 hours; the KBA invoices, with one exception, contain no hours summary, and the Court cannot reconstruct a reliable total from the entries as submitted. Counsel shall be prepared to address the issue of the failure to address the total hours claimed at the hearing, as this may be a basis to reduce the requested amount. 

In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence. General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.” (Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc. v. California Ins. Guarantee Assn. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 564.)

Anonymity's opposition challenges the hours as excessive, but does so in general terms. Anonymity fails to point to any specific entries. The lodestar is awarded in the amount of $88,592.77.

Costs

Defendants seek $2,946.39 in costs by way of this motion. Costs are claimed by memorandum of costs under California Rules of Court, rule 3.1700. Section 7.3 refers to costs as well as fees, but a contractual right to costs does not displace the statutory procedure for claiming them.

The request for costs is denied.

Anticipated Fees

Paragraph 20 of the Adamson declaration reserves a request for additional fees, to be quantified in reply, for preparing the reply, preparing for the hearing, and preparing an order. A fee request first made in reply deprives the opposing party of an opportunity to respond, and no evidence supporting such an award is before the Court. The request is denied. The award above includes reasonable compensation for the fee motion.

Conclusion

The motion for attorney's fees is GRANTED IN PART. The Court awards $88,592.77 in attorney's fees against Plaintiffs Anonymity Assets, LLC and Victoria Green, jointly and severally. The request for $2,946.39 in costs is DENIED.

Case Number: 24STCV33658    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: 224

Fabian v. FCA US LLC et al.,

TENTATIVE RULING

The demurrer is SUSTAINED in part, with leave to amend. The Motion to Strike is GRANTED, with leave to amend.

Introduction

On December 19, 2024, Plaintiffs filed a Complaint arising out of Plaintiffs’ warranty contract with Defendant FCA US, LLC (“FCA”) regarding a 2020 Jeep Cherokee, VIN 1C4PJLDB5LD573899 (“Subject Vehicle”).

On November 20, 2025, Plaintiff filed their First Amended Complaint.

On December 22, 2025 Defendants filed the instant Demurrer and Motion to Strike

to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint (“FAC”)

On August 20, 2026 Plaintiff filed an Opposition to the instant motion.

On September 11, 2026 Defendants filed a Reply.

Meet and Confer

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (CCP §§ 430.41)

The Declaration of Armando Lopez, executed on December 22, 2025, sets forth Defendant’s counsel meet and confer efforts and attempts to resolve the disputes informally by telephone. The Court finds that the parties conferred as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41

Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.¿ (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)¿When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context.¿ (Wilson v. Transit Authority of City of Sacramento (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 716, 720-21.)¿In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿“A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not on the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Id.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Fifth Cause of Action: Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment

Duty to Disclose

Defendant argues that Plaintiffs have not pled a transactional relationship between FCA and Plaintiffs which gives rise to a duty to disclose.

The California Supreme Court “has described the necessary relationship giving rise to a duty to disclose as a ‘transaction’ between the plaintiff and defendant.” (BiglerEngler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 311.) Specifically, “[i]n¿transactions¿which do not involve fiduciary or confidential relations, a cause of action for non-disclosure of material facts may arise in at least three instances: (1) the defendant makes representations but does not disclose facts which materially qualify the facts disclosed, or which render his disclosure likely to mislead; (2)¿the facts are known or accessible only to defendant, and defendant knows they are not known to or reasonably discoverable by the plaintiff; (3) the defendant actively conceals discovery from the plaintiff.” (Ibid.)¿For example, “a duty to disclose may arise from the relationship between seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement.” (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336.)¿

Here, the Court is not persuaded that FCA’s written warranty, without more, is sufficient to impose a duty to disclose upon FCA where Plaintiff has not alleged facts indicating any “direct dealings” between the parties. (FAC, ¶ 7.) “Under California law, manufacturer warranties that accompany the sale of a vehicle without regard to the substantive terms of the sale contract between the buyer and the dealer are independent of the sale contract.” (Davis v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2024) 100 Cal.App.5th 825, 837, emphasis added.)

Although Plaintiffs’ Opposition contends that Plaintiffs have pled a buyer-seller relationship, that “Plaintiffs purchased the Vehicle from FCA’s authorized dealership… and FCA’s authorized dealerships act as FCA’s agents for purposes of conveying FCA’s information to purchasers” these facts are not reflected on the face of the First Amended Complaint itself. (Opp., at 11:19-21) For example, Plaintiff never alleges that FCA financed or controlled any sale, or otherwise had a direct interest in the sale of the Vehicle to Plaintiff. Further, while Plaintiff alleges FCA concealed information in its advertising “materials” (FAC, ¶¶ 91-92), Plaintiff has not alleged any factual detail identifying these materials such that it can be inferred that the materials were anything more than communications to the public at large. (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 41 (“Such a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.”))

While courts do consider the existence of an express warranty in evaluating the

duty to disclose, Plaintiffs have not set forth any legal authority to indicate that a written warranty, standing alone, constitutes the type of “direct dealings” necessary for such a duty, particularly where there are no facts alleging Plaintiffs purchased the Vehicle from an FCA-authorized dealership or other FCA sales representatives, or that FCA’s agents directly solicited business from Plaintiff. (Cf. Dhital¿v. Nissan North America, Inc.,¿(2022)¿84 Cal.App.5th 828, 844 (holding a transactional relationship is sufficiently pled for the purposes of a demurrer where “plaintiffs alleged that they bought the car from a Nissan dealership, that Nissan backed the car with an express warranty, and that Nissan’s authorized dealerships are its agents for purposes of the sale of Nissan vehicles to consumers.”))

In other words, although Plaintiffs argue that sales by intermediaries do not necessarily disrupt the “transactional relationship” between a manufacturer and a consumer (citing Bader v. Johnson & Johnson (2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 1094, 1132), Plaintiffs have not pled any factual detail to support an inference that FCA itself was selling through a dealership, as Plaintiffs contend in their Opposition. (Opp., at 11.) At present, the FAC is completely devoid of facts clarifying the circumstances through which Plaintiffs acquired the Vehicle. Plaintiffs must allege additional facts to support the existence of a transactional relationship with FCA.

A duty to disclose has not been pled.

The demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend on this ground.

Specificity

Assuming, arguendo, that Plaintiffs had successfully pled a transactional relationship with FCA, Plaintiffs must still plead the circumstances of fraudulent concealment with particularity. (Rattagan v. Uber Techs., Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 43–44.)

Defendant argues that Plaintiffs have not pled this cause of action with the requisite specificity. Namely, Defendant argues that Plaintiffs (1) failed to identify by name any person who made any alleged omissions when he purchased the Subject Vehicle; (2) failed to allege the general point at which the omitted facts should or could have been been revealed; and (3) failed to allege the materials that Plaintiffs reviewed and relied upon in purchasing the Subject Vehicle.

Defendant relies on the general rule for pleading fraud against a corporation, which requires a plaintiff to allege the identity of the speaker, their authority, to whom they spoke, what they said, and when it was said. (See, e.g., Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157.) However, this rule is inapplicable to a claim of fraudulent concealment. (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Association, Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384 (“How does one show ‘how’ and ‘by what means’ something didn’t happen, or ‘when’ it never happened, or ‘where’ it never happened?”).) Instead, such details, in the context of a fraudulent concealment claim, “are properly the subject of discovery, not demurrer.” (Id. at 1384-85.)

Here, the Court agrees that Plaintiffs do not plead the fraudulent concealment claim with the requisite particularity, though only for reasons two and three. As discussed above, the Tarmann factors FCA contends Plaintiff omitted – the identity of the speaker, their authority, to whom they spoke, and what was said and when – do not govern a claim sounding in concealment rather than affirmative misrepresentation. (Alfaro, supra, 171 Cal.App.4th at 1384.) The claim is instead deficient because the FAC fails to plead the factors required for a concealment claim under Rattagan. Although Plaintiff alleges that FCA had superior knowledge of the defect through pre-production and post-production testing data; early consumer complaints about the Transmission Defect; aggregate warranty data; testing conducted in response to these complaints; as well as warranty repair and part replacements data, (FAC ¶ 89) the pleading does not identify which advertisements, marketing materials, or representations Plaintiffs reviewed, when they reviewed them, or how those materials were prepared or disseminated by FCA as opposed to the dealership. Further, Plaintiffs do not allege the general point at which disclosure should or could have been made.

Further, while Plaintiffs allege that FCA’s concealment induced them to purchase the vehicle and that they would not have done so had they known of the defect (FAC ¶¶ 90-91), the FAC does not allege any facts establishing Defendant’s intent to defraud.

The demurrer is SUSTAINED in part on this ground, with leave to amend.

Sixth Cause of Action: Negligent Repair

Defendants demur to the sixth cause of action in the FAC for Negligent Repair against Los Angeles Chrysler Dodge Jeep Ram (“LA Jeep”) on the grounds that the Economic Loss Rule bars Plaintiffs’ claim.

To plead a claim for negligent repair, a plaintiff must allege the elements of negligence. “Ordinarily, negligence may be pleaded in general terms and the plaintiff need not specify the precise act or omission alleged to constitute the breach of duty.” (Lopez v. S. Cal. Rapid Transit Dist. (1985) 40 Cal.3d 780, 795; Crouse v. Brobeck (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1509, 1532 (a plaintiff need only state what occurred, and generally that the acts were negligently done, but “need not state the specific act or omission constituting negligent conduct.”)) Likewise, causation may be alleged “succinctly and generally, unless the pled facts ‘do not naturally give rise to an inference of causation…’” (Bockrath v. Aldrich Chemical Co., Inc.¿(1999) 21 Cal.4th 71, 78, internal citations omitted.)

In order to plead around the economic loss rule, a party must plead the existence of a duty that arises independent of any contractual duty and independent injury, other than economic loss, that arises from the breach of that duty. (Robinson Helicopter, supra, 34 Cal.4th at 991. 988-91.) “In general, there is no recovery in tort for negligently inflicted ‘purely economic losses,’ meaning financial harm unaccompanied by physical or property damage.” (Sheen v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 905, 922.)

Here, Plaintiff’s negligent repair claim is brought against LA Jeep (not FCA). Plaintiff allegedly delivered the Vehicle to LA Jeep for repairs “on at least one occasion.” (FAC, ¶ 96.) Plaintiff generally alleges that LA Jeep “owed a duty to Plaintiff to use ordinary care and skill in storage, preparation, and repair” of the Vehicle “in accordance with industry standards.” (Id., ¶ 97.) Indeed, “[a]ny individual, partnership, corporation, association, or other legal relationship which engages in the business of providing service or repair to new or used consumer goods has a duty to the purchaser to perform those services in a good and workmanlike manner.” (Civ. Code, § 1796.5.) Thus, Plaintiff has sufficiently pled an independent duty.¿

However, Plaintiff’s negligent repair claim is devoid of factual detail clarifying how LA Jeep acted negligently. Plaintiff alleges LA Jeep breached its duty to Plaintiff “by failing to properly store, prepare and repair [the Vehicle] in accordance with industry standards.” (Id., ¶ 98.) Plaintiff further alleges LA Jeep’s “negligent breach of its duties owed to Plaintiff was a proximate cause of Plaintiff’s damages.” (Id., ¶ 99.) Plaintiffs’ opposition mistakenly alleges that the FAC “need only allege the ultimate facts constituting the cause of action, not the evidentiary facts by which those ultimate facts will be proven.” (Opp., at 12:8-9). Plaintiff must at least include facts indicating what is alleged to have occurred with enough factual detail to put LA Jeep on notice of the claim brought against it. For example, the FAC is vague and uncertain as to the nature or type of storage and/or repairs that LA Jeep was supposedly hired to perform on the Vehicle, and fails to allege how LA Jeep conduct deviated from industry norms. Additionally, the FAC does not allege any facts indicating what type of damages were sustained by Plaintiff. Without more factual detail as to what LA Jeep allegedly did or failed to do, it cannot be inferred that its negligent acts or omissions caused Plaintiff to suffer a particular injury, economic or otherwise. Thus, while the Economic Loss Rule may not necessarily bar Plaintiffs’ Negligent Repair claim, Plaintiffs have not adequately pled their cause of action for negligent repair.¿

The demurrer to the sixth cause of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

Motion to Strike

Defendants move to strike Plaintiffs’ prayer for punitive damages from the Complaint on the grounds the Complaint fails to state facts sufficient to support the recovery of punitive damages.

Civil Code § 3294(a) authorizes the recovery of punitive damages in non-contract cases “where it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice…

(1) “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.

(2) “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.

(3) “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the party of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.

(Civ. Code § 3294(c)(1)-(3).)

As reviewed above, the Court finds that Plaintiffs’ fraud-based claim is not sufficiently pled to survive general demurrer. The Court also found that Plaintiffs’ negligent repair claim is devoid of factual detail clarifying how LA Jeep acted negligently.

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s Motion to Strike Plaintiffs’ punitive damage request based on Plaintiffs’ Fraudulent Concealment claim and Negligent

Repair claim. Because the Court has allowed Plaintiffs leave to amend to address the issues in the fifth and sixth causes of action and punitive damages may be awarded upon properly pled fraud and negligent repair claims (See Civil Code § 3294), the rulings as to Plaintiffs’ fifth and sixth causes of action are without prejudice – Plaintiffs may plead punitive damages upon amendment of their fifth and sixth causes of action.

Defendant’s Motion to Strike Punitive Damages from Plaintiffs’ First Amended Complaint is GRANTED as to the fifth and sixth causes of action for Negligent Repair and Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment, with leave to amend.

Conclusion

The demurrer is SUSTAINED in part, with leave to amend. The Motion to Strike is GRANTED, with leave to amend.

Introduction

On January 5, 2026, Plaintiff Lawrence Lindsey filed a Complaint arising out of his purchase of a used 2021 Audi Q5, vehicle identification number WA1AAAFY2M2048298, and the warranties and service contract accompanying that purchase.

On March 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint ("FAC") alleging eight causes of action.

On April 27, 2026, CarMax filed the instant Demurrer and Motion to Strike to Plaintiff's First Amended Complaint.

On September 4, 2026, Plaintiff filed oppositions to the demurrer and to the motion to strike.

Meet and Confer

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (CCP §§ 430.41)

The Declaration of Adam Zamost, executed on April 27, 2026, sets forth the meet and confer efforts of counsel for the Defendants, and counsel’s attempts to resolve the disputes informally by telephone. The Court finds that the declaration does not satisfy the requirement of Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, and exercises its discretion to proceed on the merits.

Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.¿ (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)¿When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context.¿ (Wilson v. Transit Authority of City of Sacramento (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 716, 720-21.)¿In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿“A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not on the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Id.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Demurrer for Uncertainty

A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) A demurrer for uncertainty will be sustained only where the complaint is so bad that the defendant cannot reasonably respond. (CCP § 430.10(f).)

Defendants contend the FAC is uncertain.

After reviewing the First Amended Complaint, the Court does not find the FAC to be so bad that Defendant cannot reasonably respond. The allegations are sufficiently clear and certain to place CarMax on notice of the nature of the wrongful conduct in which it is alleged to have engaged.

Second and Eighth Causes of Action: Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability

Defendant argues that these claims fail because the FAC does not allege the Vehicle was ever rendered unusable, and does not allege the duration of any express warranty or when the alleged defects arose.

CarMax's first argument overstates the standard. A plaintiff need not plead that the vehicle was wholly inoperable. "[A] merchantable vehicle under the statute requires more than the mere capability of 'just getting from point "A" to point "B."'" (Brand v. Hyundai Motor America (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1538, 1546.) A vehicle is unmerchantable if it is not in safe condition and substantially free of defects, and consumer safety is an important consideration under the implied warranty. (Id. at pp. 1546–1547.) That the implied warranty does not "impose a general requirement that the goods precisely fulfill the expectation of the buyer" and provides only for a minimum level of quality (American Suzuki Motor Corp. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1291, 1295–1296; Mocek v. Alfa Leisure, Inc. (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 402, 405–406) does not convert the standard into one of total inoperability.

Plaintiff alleges the Vehicle contained or developed fuel pump defects, engine stall, vehicle non-start, fuel pump failure, fuel system issues, and other engine issues. (FAC ¶¶ 7, 29-30, 67.) Accepted as true, those allegations describe conditions bearing directly on safe and reliable operation.

CarMax's second argument regarding duration is well taken. Because the Vehicle is used, the implied warranty is coextensive with the express warranty and lasts no more than three months following the August 4, 2024 sale. (Civ. Code § 1795.5, subd. (c).)

The FAC alleges only that the nonconformities arose "[d]uring the warranty period" and "[w]ithin the time frame set forth in Civil Code section 1791.1." (FAC ¶¶ 7, 29, 67.) It does not allege the duration of CarMax's express warranty, the date that warranty issued, when any defect first manifested, or when the Vehicle was presented for repair. Without a stated duration and some temporal anchor for the defects, the FAC does not allege facts showing that the implied warranty was in force when the nonconformities arose.

To the extent the second cause of action also invokes 15 U.S.C. section 2310(d), "failure to state a warranty claim under state law necessarily constitutes a failure to state a claim under Magnuson-Moss." (Daugherty v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 824, 833.) The federal theory therefore fails with the state theory.

Defendant's demurrer to the second and eighth causes of action is SUSTAINED on this ground as to CarMax, with leave to amend.

Third and Seventh Causes of Action: Breach of Express Warranty

An "express warranty" under the Act is "a written statement arising out of a sale to the consumer of a consumer good pursuant to which the manufacturer, distributor, or retailer undertakes to preserve or maintain the utility or performance of the consumer good or provide compensation if there is a failure in utility or performance." (Civ. Code § 1791.2, subd. (a)(1).) A written contract may be pleaded by its terms or by its legal effect; to plead by legal effect, the plaintiff "must allege the substance of its relevant terms." (McKell v. Washington Mutual, Inc. (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 1457, 1489.)

To prevail on a breach of express warranty claim, the plaintiff must prove (1) the seller’s statements constitute an affirmation of fact or promise or a description of the goods; (2) the statement was part of the basis of the bargain; and (3) the warranty was breached. (Weinstat v. Denstply Internat., Inc. (2010) 180 Cal.App.4th 1213, 1227.)

Repair Attempts

Defendant argues that Plaintiff must allege at least two specifically dated repair attempts, relying on Silvio v. Ford Motor Co. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1207-

1208.

Whether a given number of repair attempts is "reasonable" is ordinarily a question of fact, and the ability to prove the allegation is immaterial on demurrer. Plaintiff alleges that he delivered the Vehicle to CarMax's representative for warranty repairs, notified the representative of the characteristics of the defects, and that the representative "failed to repair the Vehicle, breaching the terms of the written warranty on each occasion." (FAC ¶¶ 9, 36, 61.) That pleads more than a single attempt. Plaintiff is not required to plead the mileage and mechanical particulars of each repair visit.

Defendant's demurrer is OVERRULED on this ground as to CarMax.

Terms and Duration of the Express Warranty

Paragraph 46 pleads the legal effect of the warranty, tracking the language of section 1791.2. What it omits is duration.

As with the implied warranty claims, the duration of the express warranty is necessary to show that the warranty covered the Vehicle at the time the alleged nonconformities arose. An express warrantor is not obligated to repair a defect that first arises after the warranty expires. (Daugherty, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at p. 830.) The FAC alleges no term, no mileage limit, no issue date, and no date on which any defect appeared or the Vehicle was presented for repair. On this pleading the Court cannot determine that any repair obligation was ever triggered. Plaintiff is not required to plead the particulars of each repair visit, but he is required to plead facts placing those visits within a warranty period he has identified.

Defendant's demurrer to the third and seventh causes of action is SUSTAINED as to CarMax on this ground, with leave to amend.

Fourth Cause of Action: Violation of Service Contract (Civ. Code § 1794.4)

Defendant argues that Plaintiff must plead the identity of the obligor, the specific terms of the service contract, its duration, the scope of covered repairs, and any exclusions, and that his failure to do so renders the claim both insufficient and uncertain.

Several of these arguments fail. Civil Code section 1794.4, subdivision (b), supplies the scope of the obligation by statute: "Except as otherwise expressly provided in the service contract, every service contract shall obligate the service contractor to provide to the buyer of the product all of the services and functional parts that may be necessary to maintain proper operation of the entire product under normal operation and service for the duration of the service contract and without additional charge." Plaintiff therefore need not plead the scope of coverage. Any exclusion on which CarMax relies rests on a document outside the pleading.

Nor is the obligor's identity uncertain. The FAC alleges that Plaintiff purchased the service contract from CarMax and refers to "CARMAX's Service Contract." (FAC ¶¶ 40–41.) The cause of action is pleaded against CarMax alone.

Section 1794.4, subdivision (b), imposes the obligation only "for the duration of the service contract." A service contract must state "its duration measured by elapsed time or an objective measure of use." (Civ. Code § 1794.4, subd. (c)(2).) A service contract is distinct from an express warranty. (Gavaldon v. DaimlerChrysler Corp. (2004) 32 Cal.4th 1246, 1256–1259.) That distinction does not change the analysis of timing: like an express warranty, a service contract obligates the contractor only for a defined term.

The FAC alleges that CarMax failed to provide the required services and parts "for the duration of the service contract." (FAC ¶ 42.) It does not identify that duration. It also does not allege when the Vehicle was presented for repair under the contract. (FAC ¶ 41.) As with the express warranty claims, the allegation refers to a term the FAC never identifies and cannot be tested.

Defendant's demurrer to the fourth cause of action is SUSTAINED, with leave to amend.

Fifth and Sixth Causes of Action: Civil Code Section 1793.2, Subdivisions (d) and (b)

The fifth cause of action alleges failure to repurchase or replace the Vehicle after a reasonable number of unsuccessful repair attempts. The sixth alleges failure to service or repair the Vehicle to conform to the applicable warranties within 30 days.

Both obligations attach to CarMax, if at all, through Civil Code section 1795.5, and

only in a sale accompanied by CarMax's own express warranty. Both are therefore measured against the duration of that express warranty. For the reasons stated above, the FAC does not allege the duration of the express warranty, when the nonconformities arose, or when the Vehicle was presented for repair, and so does not allege facts showing that either statutory duty was triggered.

The sixth cause of action illustrates the problem. Paragraph 55 alleges that CarMax "failed to commence the service or repairs within a reasonable time and failed to service or repair the Vehicle so as to conform to the applicable warranties within 30 days." The FAC does not allege when the Vehicle was delivered for repair or when it was returned. Those are the facts that would make the 30-day allegation something other than a recitation of the statutory language.

Additionally, and independently, as discussed above the refund-or-replace remedy of section 1793.2, subdivision (d)(2), and the civil penalty under section 1794, subdivision (e), are confined to "new motor vehicles," and the Vehicle is not one. (Rodriguez, supra, 17 Cal.5th at pp. 200-202.) Leave to amend as to the fifth cause of action extends to repleading under section 1793.2, subdivision (d)(1), as applied through section 1795.5. It does not extend to reasserting the subdivision (d)(2) theory or the section 1794, subdivision (e), penalty.

Defendant's demurrer to the fifth and sixth causes of action is SUSTAINED, as to CarMax, with leave to amend.

Evidentiary Objections

Defendants Manoucher Sarbaz and High Desert Solar, LLC submit evidentiary objections, filed August 21, 2026, to Plaintiff's Supplemental Brief and Declaration of Steven L. Sugars re: Damages, filed August 18, 2026.

The objections are overruled.

The Scope of Remand

Sunrise's threshold position is that the remittitur permits this Court to do nothing but vacate the prior judgment and enter judgment in Sunrise's favor. Sunrise contends the Court is without jurisdiction to consider Defendants' anti-deficiency and one-action defenses.

The Court rejects that position.

Sunrise correctly states the governing rule. On remand, the trial court is reinvested only with such jurisdiction as the remittitur defines, and action inconsistent with the reviewing court's directions is void. (Hampton v. Superior Court (1952) 38 Cal.2d 652, 655–656.) Remand language must be read together with the opinion as a whole. (Bach v. County of Butte (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 294, 302; Eldridge v. Burns (1982) 136 Cal.App.3d 907, 917–918.) Read as a whole, however, the opinion here does not direct entry of judgment for Sunrise.

First, the Court of Appeal decided two questions: whether contracts existed between Sunrise and Manoucher, and which limitations period governs them. It held the April and May Notes are written contracts subject to the four-year statute, and it reversed on that basis alone. It did not address section 580d, section 726, waiver, or estoppel, and it had no occasion to, because this Court had not ruled on them. The doctrine of law of the case reaches points actually decided. It does not reach issues the reviewing court never took up. (People v. Barragan (2004) 32 Cal.4th 236, 246.)

Second, Bach and Eldridge are inapposite. Both concern whether a trial court must reopen the evidence on remand. Neither holds that a general reversal extinguishes affirmative defenses that were properly pleaded and preserved but never adjudicated because the trial court disposed of the claim on another ground. Sunrise's own brief concedes that "[t]his Court's Statement of Decision does not address Defendants' arguments concerning the anti-deficiency statutes or the one action rule, under CCP Sections 580a, 580b, 580d, and 726(a)." (Pl.'s Closing Br., p. 6.) That concession is dispositive of the point: the defenses are unadjudicated, not rejected.

Third, the denial of Defendants' petition for rehearing is not an adjudication on the merits and carries no law-of-the-case or preclusive effect. A summary denial of rehearing decides nothing beyond the request for rehearing itself. Sunrise argues that the Court of Appeal "considered" and refused the anti-deficiency defense. That argument reads a merits determination into a one-sentence order that does not contain one.

Fourth, and most simply, reversal of the judgment on the first cause of action restored that cause of action for determination of the issues left undecided. Had the Court of Appeal intended entry of judgment in a sum certain, it would have said so.

The affirmative defenses are properly before the Court.

The Anti-Deficiency Defense

Defendants' First Amended Answer asserts two groups of defenses. The Fourth Affirmative Defense asserts the one-form-of-action and security-first rule and the anti-deficiency bar. The Second and Third Affirmative Defenses assert waiver and estoppel. (First Amended Answer) Defendants contend the April and May Notes were secured by the Deed of Trust under which Sunrise foreclosed on the Lindbrook Property. They argue Sunrise therefore cannot obtain a personal judgment for the balance.

Section 726, subdivision (a), requires a secured creditor to exhaust its security, in a single action, before obtaining a money judgment on the debt. (O'Neil v. General Security Corp. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 587, 597; Walker v. Community Bank (1974) 10 Cal.3d 729, 736.) Sunrise did not sue first; it foreclosed first. The question is therefore not whether Sunrise violated section 726, subdivision (a), but whether, having sold the security under a power of sale, it may recover the balance.

That question is governed by section 580d. Section 580d provides that "no deficiency shall be owed or collected, and no deficiency judgment shall be rendered for a deficiency on a note secured by a deed of trust . . . in any case in which the real property . . . has been sold by the mortgagee or trustee under power of sale contained in the mortgage or deed of trust."

Whether the April and May 2013 Notes were secured by the January 2013 Deed of Trust

The threshold inquiry is whether the April and May Notes are secured obligations. Section 580d applies only to a note "secured by a deed of trust." If the April and May Notes were not secured by the Deed of Trust under which the Lindbrook Property was sold, section 580d does not apply, and Sunrise may sue on the notes as ordinary unsecured obligations. Defendants bear the burden of proof on their affirmative defenses. (Evid. Code, § 500.)

Dragnet clauses are enforceable in California. However, courts construe them according to the parties' actual expectations rather than the literal wording of the boilerplate. (Fischer v. First Internat. Bank (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1433, 1444-1445.) The question is therefore whether the instruments and the parties' conduct show that the April and May advances were understood to be secured by the Lindbrook Property.

Each note is captioned "Promissory Note Secured By Deed of Trust." Each recites that "[t]he payment of this Note and all interest and other charges thereon is secured by those certain instruments entitled Deed of Trust, Security Agreement, Fixture Filing and Assignment of Leases and Rents." The recital identifies the property as 10748 Lindbrook Drive, Los Angeles. (Exs. 2, 3, p. 3.) Sunrise prepared these instruments. (SOD, p. 3.)

The Court of Appeal held that the notes are written contracts between Sunrise and Manoucher. Their essential terms, the amount loaned and the date repayment was due, are set out in the notes, and Manoucher signed them. Sunrise sues on the notes as the written contract. It may not enforce the writing while disclaiming its security recital. (Civ. Code, § 1641.)

The January 8, 2013 Deed of Trust, recorded February 12, 2013, contains a dragnet clause. The clause secures "payment of additional sums and interest thereon which may hereafter be loaned to Trustor, or its successors or assigns . . . when evidenced by a promissory note or notes or other writing signed by Trustor or its successors or assigns, reciting that they are secured by this Deed of Trust." (Ex. 44, p. 4.) The April and May Notes name Lucerne Valley, LLC, the Trustor, as maker. They were prepared for Kamran Sarbaz's signature as Lucerne's managing member, and they recite the required security language. On their face, they are the instruments the dragnet clause contemplates. (See Union Bank v. Wendland (1976) 54 Cal.App.3d 393, 398–400, 405; Fischer, supra, 109 Cal.App.4th at p. 1444.)

Sunrise argues that the April and May Notes were not agreements with Lucerne, the owner of the Lindbrook Property, and that the foreclosure therefore had nothing to do with them. (Pl.'s Closing Br., pp. 8–9.) Applied to the dragnet clause, the argument is that the notes were not "signed by Trustor" and the advances were not "loaned to Trustor," so the clause never attached.

The Court of Appeal's opinion does not resolve this question. The Court of Appeal held that the April and May Notes are written contracts between Sunrise and Manoucher. In doing so, it noted this Court's finding that neither Elyaszadeh nor Manoucher believed Manoucher was acting as Lucerne's agent when he sought either loan. But it expressly declined to go further. Because Sunrise was not seeking to hold Lucerne liable, the court "need not address Manoucher's authority to sign on Lucerne's behalf." (Opn., p. 7, fn. 5.) Whether the notes bind Manoucher personally and whether they fall within the security of the Deed of Trust are separate questions. The Court of Appeal decided the first and did not reach the second. (People v. Barragan, supra, 32 Cal.4th at p. 246.)

The argument is not frivolous, but it fails for two reasons.

First, Sunrise chose the form of the instruments. This Court found that Sunrise knew the April and May obligations were not intended to be Lucerne's.

"Nevertheless, Sunrise prepared loan documents that identified Lucerne as the borrower." (SOD, p. 3.) Those documents recited that the notes were secured by the Deed of Trust on Lucerne's property. The mismatch between form and substance might matter as against Lucerne, which is not a party to this action. (See Opn., p. 2, fn. 2 [Lucerne's wrongful foreclosure action resolved in Sunrise's favor].) It does not permit Sunrise, the drafter, to deny as against the borrower that the notes it wrote were secured. (Civ. Code, § 1654.)

Second, and most importantly, both Sunrise and Manoucher understood the April and May advances to be secured. This Court found that "Yona Samih does not make unsecured loans." It further found that "[b]oth Ely . . . and [Manoucher] knew this about Samih's practices at the time they negotiated April 29, 2013 and May 17, 2013 loans." (SOD, p. 2.) The Court of Appeal did not disturb those findings. The trial record is consistent with them:

· Samih testified that Sunrise made only secured loans. (10/3/18 RT 39:25–40:5.)

· Elyaszadeh testified that he believed the April and May Notes were secured when funded. (10/11/18 RT 72:6-18.)

· No separate deeds of trust were prepared for those notes. (10/11/18 RT 60:24-61:13.)

· Elyaszadeh testified that when his office calculated the amount stated in the July 11, 2014 notice of default, it included the sums due under the April and May Notes. (10/11/18 RT 72:25–73:5, 76:17-27.)

· In its own proposed statement of decision, Sunrise represented that it "foreclosed on the Lindbrook Property believing that the foreclosure eliminated all three loans - the January, April and May loans." (Pl.'s [Proposed] Tentative Statement of Decision, filed Sept. 24, 2021.)

Sunrise points to the Notice of Trustee's Sale recorded March 3, 2015 and the Trustee's Deed Upon Sale recorded April 10, 2015, each reciting $401,022.00. (RJN Exs. 5, 6.) It argues these documents show the sale concerned only the January note. But the record also contains two documents stating $995,000: a notice of default recorded July 11, 2014, and a notice of trustee's sale recorded January 27, 2015. (AA Vol. 1, pp. 31-33.) It also contains Elyaszadeh's testimony that all three notes were included in the calculation. The Court need not reconcile the figures. Section 580d turns on whether the note sued upon is secured by a deed of trust under which the property was sold, not on the amount noticed or bid. The bid amount bears on the size of any deficiency, not on the existence of the security.

The Court finds that the April and May Notes were secured by the January 8, 2013 Deed of Trust.

Section 580d Bars the First Cause of Action

Here, Sunrise elected nonjudicial foreclosure. On March 24, 2015, the Lindbrook Property was sold under the power of sale in the January 8, 2013 Deed of Trust, and Sunrise acquired title by credit bid. (10/11/18 RT 76:28–77:2, 80:24-26.) Sunrise later resold the property for $1,776,000, netting $738,620.40. (10/11/18 RT 85:1–2, 136:1–13.) Having made that election, Sunrise took title free of any right of redemption. Under section 580d, it also forfeited the right to pursue a deficiency on the notes that deed of trust secured. (Roseleaf Corp. v. Chierighino (1963) 59 Cal.2d 35, 43-44.) The present action on the April and May Notes seeks precisely that: a personal money judgment for the unpaid balance of secured debt after the security has been sold under a power of sale. The action is barred.

The April and May Notes each contain a clause by which the maker purports to waive "the provisions of California Code of Civil Procedure Section 580b and

Section 726." (Exs. 2, 3.) The clause does not mention section 580d, and Sunrise does not invoke it. Because the Court rests its ruling on section 580d, it need not decide the clause's effect.

Bauman v. Castle (1971) 15 Cal.App.3d 990, on which Sunrise relies, is distinguishable. Bauman involved a suit against guarantors under a separate written guaranty whose rights were unaffected by the foreclosure. Manoucher is not a guarantor under a separate instrument; he is the principal obligor on notes that by their terms are secured by the very deed of trust foreclosed.

Waiver and Estoppel

Even if Manoucher were regarded as standing outside the secured obligation, like a guarantor, so that section 580d did not apply of its own force, the Second and Third Affirmative Defenses would bar recovery. A creditor who elects a private sale, which affords no equity of redemption, thereby waives any deficiency. (Consolidated Capital Income Trust v. Khaloghli (1986) 183 Cal.App.3d 107, 111.) Where the creditor's election of remedies destroys the debtor's redemption and reimbursement rights, estoppel is raised as a matter of law to bar the deficiency claim. (Union Bank v. Gradsky (1968) 265 Cal.App.2d 40, 47.)

Sunrise chose the remedy that maximized its recovery on resale and eliminated redemption. It cannot now pursue Manoucher personally for the balance.

Damages

Because the claim fails, the Court does not reach the issue of damages.

Conclusion

The first cause of action for breach of contract is barred by Code of Civil Procedure section 580d, subdivision (a), and by Defendants' Second Affirmative Defense.

Judgment shall be entered in favor of Defendant Manoucher Sarbaz and against Plaintiff Sunrise Financial, LLC on the first cause of action, and Plaintiff shall take nothing on that cause of action. The remaining causes of action stand as adjudicated in the September 12, 2022 judgment, which the Court of Appeal affirmed.

TENTATIVE RULING - Motion for Attorney Fees

The complaint alleges that Defendant approached Plaintiff to secure a loan to prevent foreclosure on real property. The Complaint alleges that Defendant breached the terms of promissory notes by failing to pay Plaintiff. Defendant is indebted to Plaintiff in the amount of $190,000 plus interest.

Motion for Attorney Fees

Plaintiff and Appellant Sunrise Financial moves for attorney’s fees and costs in the amount of $51,055.29, which Plaintiff claims represents the reasonable and necessary attorneys’ fees incurred by the Sunrise as the prevailing party on appeal, as well as attorneys’ fees incurred in connection with the preparing this Motion.

Legal Standard

A prevailing party is entitled to recover costs, including attorneys’ fees, as a matter of right. (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1032(a)(4), 1032(b), 1033.5.) In a breach of contract action, attorneys’ fees shall be awarded when a contract provides that one of the parties or the prevailing party shall be awarded attorneys’ fees in an action on that contract. (See Civ. Code, §1717.)

As in the trial court, attorney fees on appeal are recoverable when authorized by statute or contract.¿ (See Code Civ. Proc. § 1021, Civil Code § 1717, Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1702; see also Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621.)¿ Even if a statute does not expressly authorize attorney fees on appeal, a successful party may recover fees incurred on appeal when the statute expressly grants such an award in the trial court, unless the statute specifically provides otherwise.¿ (Morcos v. Board of Retirement (1990) 51 Cal.3d 924.)¿ However, where a statute does not permit recovery of attorney fees as costs in the trial court, they necessarily are not awardable on appeal.¿ (Imperial Bank v. Pim Electric, Inc. (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 540.)

The fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily begins with the “lodestar” method, i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate. A computation of time spent on a case and the reasonable value of that time is fundamental to a determination of an appropriate attorneys’ fee award. The lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49.) Such an approach anchors the trial court’s analysis to an objective determination of the value of the attorney’s services, ensuring that the amount awarded is not arbitrary. (Id. at 48, n.23.) After the trial court has performed the lodestar calculations, it shall consider whether the total award so calculated under all of the circumstances of the case is more than a reasonable amount and, if so, shall reduce the section 1717 award so that it is a reasonable figure. (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095-96.)

The factors considered in determining the modification of the lodestar include the nature and difficulty of the litigation, the amount of money involved, the skill required and employed to handle the case, the attention given, the success or failure, and other circumstances in the case. (EnPalm, LLC v. Teitler Family Trust (2008) 162 Cal. App. 4th 770, 774 (emphasis in original).) A negative modifier was appropriate when duplicative work had been performed. (Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 819.)

Costs on Appeal

To the extent the motion seeks costs on appeal, it is denied. When the Court of Appeal reverses in part, it must specify the award or denial of costs. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.278(a)(3).) Here it did so, directing the parties to bear their own costs, and the remittitur repeats that direction. On remand, this Court's jurisdiction is defined by the remittitur. The Court may not award costs the Court of Appeal declined to award. (See Hampton v. Superior Court (1952) 38 Cal.2d 652.)

Attorney’s Fees on Appeal

The Court of Appeal's costs order does not decide whether Sunrise may recover attorney's fees on appeal. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.278(d)(2); Butler-Rupp v. Lourdeaux (2007) 154 Cal.App.4th 918, 925–928.) Civil Code section 1717 governs that question.

Each note provides that the maker "promises to pay all attorneys' fees and costs . . . incurred by Holder in connection with the interpretation, collection, or enforcement of this Note." Section 1717 makes that provision reciprocal. Fees are therefore available only to the party prevailing on the contract.

Sunrise contends it is the prevailing party on appeal. Section 1717, however, does not recognize a separate prevailing party for each stage of the litigation. There may be only one prevailing party entitled to fees on a given contract in a given lawsuit, and "action on a contract" refers to the entire lawsuit. (de la Carriere v. Greene (2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 270, [pin].) Success on appeal does not make a party the prevailing party when the other side prevails in the action. (Ibid.; Wood v. Santa Monica Escrow Co. (2009) 176 Cal.App.4th 802, [pin].) In de la Carriere, the court also held that an award of costs on a prior appeal did not conclusively establish entitlement to fees under section 1717. (de la Carriere, supra, at p. [pin].)

Sunrise's appeal revived its first cause of action. It did not resolve that claim or award Sunrise any relief on the notes. The claim has now been resolved against Sunrise. Sunrise did not recover greater relief in the action on the contract. (Civ.

Code, § 1717, subd. (b)(1).) It is therefore not the prevailing party and is not entitled to fees, whether incurred on appeal or in bringing this motion. Sunrise asserted no contract claim against High Desert, so there is no basis for contractual fees against it. Because Sunrise is not entitled to fees, the Court does not reach the reasonableness of the amount requested.

The Court does not decide whether defendants are entitled to attorney's fees under section 1717. That question is not before the Court on this motion.

Conclusion

Plaintiff Sunrise Financial, LLC's Motion for Attorney's Fees and Costs on Appeal is DENIED.
