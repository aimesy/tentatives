1.     Background Facts

Plaintiff, Tyler Chambers (“Plaintiff”), filed this action against Defendants, FCA US, LLC and Cerritos Dodge, Inc. dba Cerritos Dodge Chrysler Jeep Ram (collectively, “Defendants”), for violations of the Song-Beverly Act, breach of implied warranty, negligent repair, and fraudulent inducement – concealment. 

On 6/26/26, the Court (Hon. Mark C. Kim) held a Mandatory Settlement Conference (MSC) and a Trial Setting Conference. Plaintiff’s counsel appeared, but Plaintiff did not appear. Therefore, the Court continued the MSC to August 13, 2026, and set: “Hearing on Motion to Dismiss and sanctions for Plaintiff’s failure to appear and Plaintiff’s counsel’s failure to appear to timely request a continuance … for 08/13/26 ….” (6/26/26 Minute Order, p. 1.)

On 7/2/26, Defendants filed the instant motion for sanctions against Plaintiff’s counsel of record (Strategic Legal Practices, APC) in the amount of $8,335 and/or dismissal for Plaintiff’s failure to appear and timely request a continuance of the MSC.

On 7/30/26, the Court conducted a Non-Appearance Case Review and vacated the MSC date because it is not holding Settlement Conferences at this time.

On 8/6/26, Plaintiff’s counsel, Breana Rucker of Strategic Legal Practices, filed a declaration explaining Plaintiff’s reasons for failing to appear at the MSC. No reply has been filed.  

           

2.     Motion for Sanctions and/or Dismissal for Failing to Appear at MSC or Request Continuance of MSC

Under the Court’s local rules: “The court may hold a mandatory settlement conference in each case before trial. The settlement conference shall be scheduled a sufficient time after the exchange of appraisals to allow the parties to conduct expert depositions and engage in settlement discussions. All counsel and all persons with settlement authority must attend the settlement conference in person, unless prior arrangements have been made with the court for that person to appear by telephone.” (Super. Ct. L.A. County, Local Rules, rule 3.245.)

The rules further state: “The court may impose appropriate sanctions for the failure or refusal to comply with the rules in [Chapter Three (Civil Division Rules) of the Local Rules], including the time standards and/or deadlines, and any court order made pursuant to the rules. Counsel are directed to Code of Civil Procedure sections 128, 128.7, 177.5, 575.2, 583.150, 583.430, 2016.010-2036.050, Government Code section 68608, and California Rules of Court, rule 2.30. The sanctions may be imposed on a party and, if appropriate, on counsel for that party.” (Super. Ct. L.A. County, Local Rules, rule 3.10 [underlining added].)

Defendants move for sanctions against Plaintiff’s counsel (instead of Plaintiff).

However, Plaintiff’s counsel appeared at the MSC and testifies that his client’s failure to appear was due to his client’s alleged preexisting travel arrangements. Plaintiff’s counsel testifies: “Plaintiff and his counsel respectfully apologize to the Court for Plaintiff’s absence from the June 26, 2026 MSC and for counsel’s failure to timely notify the Court in advance regarding Plaintiff’s unavailability.” (Rucker Decl., ¶ 3.) “Prior to the June 26, 2026 MSC, Plaintiff advised counsel that he would be out of state from June 20 through June 27, 2026, and therefore would be unavailable to personally attend the MSC on June 26, 2026.” (Id. ¶ 4.) “Plaintiff’s absence was not intended to disregard the Court’s order or the importance of the Mandatory Settlement Conference. Plaintiff had a preexisting travel conflict that prevented his personal attendance on that date.” (Id. ¶ 5.) “Counsel appeared on Plaintiff’s behalf at the June 26, 2026 hearing. The Court thereafter continued the [MSC] and Trial Setting Conference and set a hearing concerning Plaintiff’s nonappearance.” (Id. ¶ 6.) “Counsel recognizes that Plaintiff’s unavailability should have been brought to the Court’s attention in advance and sincerely apologizes to the Court for failing to do so and for any inconvenienced caused to the Court.” (Id. ¶ 7.) “Plaintiff remains actively engaged in this litigation and ready, willing, and available to proceed with this matter. Plaintiff intends to prosecute his claims and will make himself available for all future proceedings at which his personal attendance is required.” (Id. ¶ 8.) Defendants have not presented evidence that would justify sanctions against Plaintiff’s counsel or dismissal.

            For those reasons, the motion is DENIED. However, Plaintiff is admonished for the failure to appear and is warned that future failure to personally appear when required may result in monetary sanctions.

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice.

On March 27, 2025, SSA Pacific, Inc. and City of Los Angeles filed a cross-complaint for apportionment of fault, indemnification, declaratory relief, and breach of contract, against Plaintiff Jose Villa, U.S. Expeditors. Villa Transportation, Inc., Inner Valley Transport, Inc., and IVT Logistics Worldwide, Inc. On September 3, 2025, Cross-Complainants dismissed U.S. Expeditors.

On January 29 and February 10, 2026, City of Los Angeles dismissed the cross-complaint. On March 3, 2026, the court sustained the demurer of Jose Villa to the first, second and third causes of action in the cross-complaint without leave to amend, and continued the hearing on the breach of contract cause of action. On March 25, 2026, the court entered an order dismissing the entire cross-complaint as to Plaintiff Villa. On April 2, 2026, the court sustained the demurrer of IVT Logistics Worldwide, Inc. to the remaining cross-complaint of SSA Pacific of America without leave to amend.  On April 17, 2026, the court entered a judgment of dismissal on the SSA Pacific, Inc. demurrer. On July 16, 2026, SSA Pacific Inc. filed a notice of appeal on the demurrer to the cross-complaint.

 

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

The pleadings frame the issues for motions, “since it is those allegations to which the motion must respond. (Citation.)” (Scolinos v. Kolts (1995) 37 Cal. App. 4th 635, 640-641; FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 382-383; Jordan-Lyon Prods., LTD. v. Cineplex Odeon Corp. (1994) 29 Cal.App.4th 1459, 1472.) The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atl. Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.)  “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) A defendant moving for summary judgment “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) “Once the defendant . . . has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Ibid.)

“A party may move for summary adjudication as to one or more causes of action within an action... or one or more issues of duty if the party contends that the cause of action has no merit, that there is no affirmative defense to the cause of action that there is no merit to an affirmative defense as to any cause of action, that there is no merit to a claim for damages, as specified in Section 3294 of the Civil Code, or that one or more defendants either owed or did not owe a duty to the plaintiff or plaintiffs. A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

“When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inference that may be drawn form that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) “An issue of fact can only be created by a conflict in the evidence.  It is not created by speculation, conjecture, imagination or guesswork.”  (Lyons v. Security Pacific National Bank (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 1001, 1014 (citation omitted).)

 

Analysis

Defendant SSA Pacific Inc. (SSA) moves for summary judgment on the complaint for premises liability and negligence. SSA moves on grounds of denial of any dangerous condition, no evidence SSA created said dangerous condition, lack of actual notice of any dangerous condition, and lack of proximate cause evidence regarding the condition and Plaintiff’s injuries.

Plaintiff in opposition contends triable issues of material fact exist as to the existence of a non-code compliant unsafe condition, whereby SSA remains liable as the permit holder and therefore in control of the premises.

SSA in reply contends Plaintiff lacks any admissible evidence establishing any triable issues of material fact. The position relies on prior use by Plaintiff and lack of any history of prior incidents by other invitees. SSA contends Plaintiff’s position relies on improper, unsupported inference, including the allegedly improper curb constitutes an improper “mechanism” for the fall. The position relies on challenges to the sufficiency of the expert declaration. Finally, SSA maintains that even if causation were established, the record lacks any indication of a failure to exercise reasonable care, and basis of notice. SSA also challenges any evidence of remedial measures.

 

Evidentiary Objections

SSA’s Objections to the Declaration of Brad Avrit, Numbers 1-14 are Overruled.

 

1st Cause of Action: Premises Liability

2nd Cause of Action: Premises Liability

SSA only moves summary judgment. The standard of review for both causes of action presents a singular test. “The elements of a negligence claim and a premises liability claim are the same: a legal duty of care, breach of that duty, and proximate cause resulting in injury.” (Kesner v. Superior Court (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1132, 1158.) “In order to prevail in an action based upon a defendant's alleged negligence, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the defendant owed the plaintiff a legal duty, that the defendant breached the duty, and that the breach was a proximate or legal cause of his or her injuries.” (Morris v. De La Torre (2005) 36 Cal.4th 260, 264.)

 

Trivial Defect and Open and Obvious

SSA first relies on a characterization of the condition as either a trivial defect or open and obvious condition. “A condition is not a dangerous condition within the meaning of this chapter if the trial or appellate court, viewing the evidence most favorably to the plaintiff, determines as a matter of law that the risk created by the condition was of such a minor, trivial or insignificant nature in view of the surrounding circumstances that no reasonable person would conclude that the condition created a substantial risk of injury when such property or adjacent property was used with due care in a manner in which it was reasonably foreseeable that it would be used.” (Gov. Code, § 830.2.) “[T]o constitute a dangerous condition, the height differential, and the area surrounding it, must have posed ‘a substantial (as distinguished from a minor, trivial or insignificant) risk of injury’ when ‘used with due care in a manner in which it is reasonably foreseeable that it will be used.’” (Huckey v. City of Temecula (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 1092, 1110.)

“Generally, if a danger is so obvious that a person could reasonably be expected to see it, the condition itself serves as a warning, and the landowner is under no further duty to remedy or warn of the condition. (Citation.) However, this is not true in all cases. ‘[I]t is foreseeable that even an obvious danger may cause injury, if the practical necessity of encountering the danger, when weighed against the apparent risk involved, is such that under the circumstances, a person might choose to encounter the danger. The foreseeability of injury, in turn, when considered along with various other policy considerations such as the extent of the burden to the defendant and consequences to the community of imposing a duty to remedy such danger [citation] may lead to the legal conclusion that the defendant’ owed a duty of due care to the person injured.” (Krongos v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 387, 393.) “[B]ecause the possessor or operator of a given premises is not an insurer of the safety of invitees onto his premises, he is entitled to assume that any such invitee will perceive that which should be obvious to him in the ordinary use of his [or her] senses.” (Danieley v. Goldmine Ski Associates, Inc. (1990) 218 Cal.App.3d 111, 121.)

SSA appears to rely on this position without any apparent objective support other than Plaintiff previously visiting the area within the course and scope of employment as a truck driver in variously times of day impacting lighting conditions. [Declaration of Heather Mills, Compendium of Evidence, Ex. F: Deposition of Jose Villa, 32:18-33:11, 34:10-35:2, 47:24-48:3, 52:15-53:4, Ex H: Deposition Exhibit.] The position lacks sufficient articulation establishing either an open and obvious or trivial defect defense simply based on prior visits. “A court should decide whether a defect may be dangerous only after considering all of the circumstances surrounding the accident that might make the defect more dangerous than its size alone would suggest.” (Caloroso v. Hathaway (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 922, 927.)

Plaintiff specifically challenges any factual finding of a trivial defect due to the non-compliant code conditions, and height differential. Plaintiff also denies any open and obvious condition, but even if the specific section constituted an open and obvious hazard, Plaintiff asserts no notice of such danger.

SSA fails to shift the burden establishing either a trivial defect or open and obvious position. The position in reply regarding reliance on inference will not support the finding of insufficient evidence of a dangerous condition at the time of the incident. (See Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 573, 590-593.) Even if SSA shifted the burden, Plaintiff submits admissible expert testimony establishing triable issues of material fact as to both a trivial defect and open and obvious condition. [Declaration of Babak Kheiri, Ex. C: Declaration of Brad Avrit, PE.]

 The expert concludes that the slope change constitutes an “unexpected and significant hazard for pedestrians to traverse.” Such types of area often go “unnoticed” in that a pedestrian may not necessarily recognize a danger simply based on the height differential and change, due to the lack of a “salient” visual cue. [Id., ¶¶ 7-10.] The declaration qualitatively establishes a basis for requirement of consideration by the trier of fact for any determination of the applicability of the two defenses. (See Blumberg v. M. & T. Inc. (1949) 34 Cal.2d 226, 229-230 [Spaces in floor mats created a foreseeable and actual dangerous condition to invitees]; Martinez v. Chippewa Enterprises, Inc. (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 1179, 1184 [Wet condition on premises constituted dangerous condition].) Nothing in this evidence constitutes a speculative position. (Lyons v. Security Pacific National Bank, supra, 40 Cal.App.4th at p. 1014.) Again, the standard requires examination of the condition at the time of the accident. Plaintiff establishes a sufficient foundation for the expert to later analyze and present a factually supported report. (San Francisco Print Media Co. v. The Hearst Corp. (2020) 44 Cal.App.5th 952, 962-963; Sanchez v. Kern Emergency Medical Transportation Corp. (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 146, 155-156.) Plaintiff therefore establishes triable issues of material fact based on evidence of a non-code compliant area foreseeably leading to an invitee falling in the area as a result of the outdated standard. Nothing in the opposition otherwise relies on any later remediation. Such evidence remains inadmissible either way. (Evid. Code, § 1151.)

 

Actual or Constructive Notice

            SSA denies any notice of a defective or dangerous condition. SSA represents regular inspections and denies any prior complaints. Plaintiff denies relief from liability based on prior efforts to demarcate the area. Plaintiff also notes the change to the area following the incident.

            Again, the later modifications are not considered as evidence of prior notice of a dangerous condition. (Evid. Code, § 1151.) “The plaintiff need not show actual knowledge where evidence suggests that the dangerous condition was present for a sufficient period of time to charge the owner with constructive knowledge of its existence. Knowledge may be shown by circumstantial evidence ‘which is nothing more than one or more inferences which may be said to arise reasonably from a series of proven facts.’” (Ortega v. Kmart Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1206–1207.)

“When an unsafe condition which causes injury to an invitee has been created by the owner of the property himself or by an employee within the scope of his employment, the invitee need not prove the owner's notice or knowledge of the dangerous condition; the knowledge is imputed to the owner.” (Sanders v. MacFarlane's Candies (1953) 119 Cal.App.2d 497, 501.) Denial of notice without establishing the lack of a non-trivial, dangerous condition will not meet the standard for SSA to shift the burden regarding a lack of actual or constructive notice. Again, SSA presents no address of the condition of the area at the time, and cannot dismiss the danger because of no prior incidents. Any assumption of reasonable care due to a lack of notice also falls short of the required showing to shift the burden. Even if SSA made the showing, triable issues of material fact exist regarding the reasonableness of the lapse of time regarding inspection and rectification of the dangerous condition before the incident. [Avrit Decl.] (Howard v. Omni Hotels Management Corp. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 403, 431 accord Ortega v. Kmart Corp., supra, 26 Cal.4th at pp. 1206-1207.)

 

Causation

            Finally SSA denies any causation based on the denial of any dangerous condition. Again, SSA fails to shift the burden based on the lack of any factually and legally supported position establishing the lack of a dangerous condition. (Saelzler v. Advanced Group 400 (2001) 25 Cal.4th 763, 768.) Further triable issues of material fact exist based on Plaintiff’s establishment of the dangerous condition through the unchallenged expert declaration. (See Chowdhury v. City of Los Angeles (1995) 38 Cal.App.4th 1187, 1196.) The circumstances establish a basis of causation not resolved as a matter of law in the instant motion. (Saaelzler v. Advanced Group 400, supra, 25 Cal.4th. at p. 781.)

 

CONCLUSION

The motion for summary judgment is DENIED due to the failure to shift the burden on any and all defenses and triable issues of material fact on each challenge.

Final Status Conference and Trial remain set for October 16 and 19, 2026, respectively.

Moving party to give notice.

Trivial Defect and Open and Obvious

Because City raises the issue of statutory liability in reply, the court addresses the fundamental basis for government liability. California Government Code section 815 provides that “[a] public entity is not liable for an injury, whether such injury arises out of an act or omission of the public entity or a public employee or any other person” except as provided by statute. (Govt. Code, § 815(a); Eastburn v. Regional Fire Protection Authority (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1175, 1183.) “[D]irect tort liability of public entities must be based on a specific statute declaring them to be liable, or at least creating some specific duty of care, and not on the general tort provisions of Civil Code section 1714. Otherwise, the general rule of immunity for public entities would be largely eroded by the routine application of general tort principles.” (Eastburn v. Regional Fire Protection Authority (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1175, 1183.) “The sole statutory basis for imposing liability on public entities as property owners is Government Code section 835.” (Cerna v. City of Oakland (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 1340, 1347.)

“Except as provided by statute, a public entity is liable for injury caused by a dangerous condition of its property if the plaintiff establishes that the property was in a dangerous condition at the time of the injury, that the injury was proximately caused by the dangerous condition, that the dangerous condition created a reasonably foreseeable risk of the kind of injury which was incurred, and that either: (a) A negligent or wrongful act or omission of an employee of the public entity within the scope of his employment created the dangerous condition; or (b) The public entity had actual or constructive notice of the dangerous condition under Section 835.2 a sufficient time prior to the injury to have taken measures to protect against the dangerous condition.” (Govt. Code, § 835.)

“The limited and statutory nature of governmental liability mandates that claims against public entities be specifically pleaded. (Citation.) Accordingly, a claim alleging a dangerous condition may not rely on generalized allegations [citation] but must specify in what manner the condition constituted a dangerous condition. (Citation.) Although it is the general rule that it is a factual question whether a given set of facts and circumstances creates a dangerous condition, the issue may be resolved as a question of law if reasonable minds can come to but one conclusion.  (Citations.) Accordingly, if the facts pleaded by the plaintiff as a matter of law cannot support the finding of the existence of a dangerous condition within the meaning of the statutory scheme, a court may properly sustain a demurrer to the complaint. (Citations.)” (Brenner v. City of El Cajon (2003) 113 Cal.App.4th 434, 439-440.)

Code, § 830.2.) “[T]o constitute a dangerous condition, the height differential, and the area surrounding it, must have posed ‘a substantial (as distinguished from a minor, trivial or insignificant) risk of injury’ when ‘used with due care in a manner in which it is reasonably foreseeable that it will be used.’” (Huckey v. City of Temecula (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 1092, 1110.)

(Citation.) (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 387, 393.) “[B]ecause the possessor or operator of a given premises is not an insurer of the safety of invitees onto his premises, he is entitled to assume that any such invitee will perceive that which should be obvious to him in the ordinary use of his [or her] senses.” (Danieley v. City maintains Plaintiff lacks sufficient evidence of any defect.

The position lacks sufficient articulation establishing either an open and obvious or trivial defect defense simply based on prior visits and/or preexisting conditions. While City in reply validly presents a position regarding an alleged inability of Plaintiff to present sufficient evidence, the standard still requires consideration of the conditions at the time of the incident. History and prior practice will not relieve a landowner of liablility. “A court should decide whether a defect may be dangerous only after considering all of the circumstances surrounding the accident that might make the defect more dangerous than its size alone would suggest.” (Caloroso v. Hathaway (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 922, 927.)

Plaintiff specifically challenges any factual finding of a trivial defect due to the non-compliant code conditions, and height differential. Plaintiff also denies any open and obvious condition, but even if the specific section constituted an open and obvious hazard, Plaintiff asserts no notice of such danger. The non-code compliant condition and lack of notice of any potential tripping hazard remains undisputed by City.

City fails to shift the burden establishing either a trivial defect or open and obvious position based on lack of evidence. (See Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 573, 590-593.) Even if City shifted the burden, Plaintiff submits undisputed expert testimony establishing triable issues of material fact as to both a trivial defect and open and obvious condition. [Declaration of Babak Kheiri, Ex. C: Declaration of Brad Avrit, PE.] The expert concludes that the slope change constitutes an “unexpected and significant hazard for pedestrians to traverse.” Such types of area often go “unnoticed” in that a pedestrian may not necessarily recognize a danger simply based on the height differential and change, due to the lack of a “salient” visual cue. [Id., ¶¶ 7-10.] The declaration is unchallenged and qualitatively establishes a basis for requirement of consideration by the trier of fact for any determination of the applicability of the two defenses. (See Blumberg v. M. & T. Inc. (1949) 34 Cal.2d 226, 229-230 [Spaces in floor mats created a foreseeable and actual dangerous condition to invitees]; Martinez v. Chippewa Enterprises, Inc. (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 1179, 1184 [Wet condition on premises constituted dangerous condition].)

 

Actual or Constructive Notice

            City also relies on the existence of the Permit to co-defendant SSA Pacific, as delegating all operational control and duty to inspect the premises to SSA Pacific. City denies any notice of prior complaints as well, which is reiterated in the reply.

            Plaintiff challenges any finding of a non-delegable duty, and maintains an ongoing duty to inspect. A lack of notice of prior incidents will not relieve City from liability for a dangerous condition.

            The delegation of duty position again provides no legal support, and instead only relies on the deposition of Seung-Hyun Kevin Kim, a presumed employee of the City, but no specifically identified position. [Mills Decl., Ex. A: Deposition of Seung-Hyun Kevin Kim.] Kim acknowledges the operative permit [25:23-26:11] and maintains the terms of the permit require acceptance in an “as is” condition with the occupant obligated to maintain the premises [58:20-59:23]. Kim interprets section 2.4 of the permit as a basis for the City to deny any liability for injuries occurring due to the condition of the premises. [Id, 89:9-25.]

            The opinion of Kim constitutes nothing more than a statement without address of any legal standard or qualification to make this determination. City therefore fails to shift the burden establishing this defense.

            Even considering the defense, no valid basis of a non-delegable duty finds support.  “Where the law imposes a definite, affirmative duty upon one by reason of his relationship with others, whether as an owner or proprietor of land or chattels or in some other capacity, such persons can not escape liability for a failure to perform the duty thus imposed by entrusting it to an independent contractor. ... It is immaterial whether the duty thus regarded as ‘nondelegable’ be imposed by statute, charter or by common law.” (Van Arsdale v. Hollinger (1968) 68 Cal.2d 245, 251 overruled on other grounds by Privette v. Superior Court (1993) 5 Cal.4th 689.)

            Plaintiff cites to Government Code section 818.6 for purposes of establishing a duty to inspect regardless of any permit terms. “A public entity is not liable for injury caused by its failure to make an inspection, or by reason of making an inadequate or negligent inspection, of any property, other than its property (as defined in subdivision (c) of Section 830), for the purpose of determining whether the property complies with or violates any enactment or contains or constitutes a hazard to health or safety.” (Gov. Code, § 818.6.) The indirectly worded statute imposes a duty of care on the government entity in control of its property. The existence of the permit in no way relieves City from liability on grounds of non-delegable duty. “The essential justification for this rule is one of ensuring accountability of licensees so as to safeguard the public welfare. ... If a licensee were not liable for the actions of his independent contractor, ‘effective regulation would be impossible. He could contract away the daily operations of his business to independent contractors and become immune to disciplinary action by the licensing authority.’ The principle that a licensee will be held liable for the acts of its agents is one that has been applied whether the agent is an independent contractor or an employee.” (California Assn. of Health Facilities v. Department of Health Services (1997) 16 Cal.4th 284, 296.)

On lack of notice, again, City only cites to the deposition of the terminal manager denying any reported claims for 16 years. [Mills Decl., Ex. C: Deposition of Kevin Nielsen, 93:7-15.] The reply reiterates the lack of prior reports. As addressed above, the legally unsupported position fails to shift the burden establishing a valid basis for denial of the existence of any potentially dangerous condition simply based on lack of notice.

“When an unsafe condition which causes injury to an invitee has been created by the owner of the property himself or by an employee within the scope of his employment, the invitee need not prove the owner's notice or knowledge of the dangerous condition; the knowledge is imputed to the owner.” (Sanders v. MacFarlane's Candies (1953) 119 Cal.App.2d 497, 501.) Given the finding of a non-trivial and not open an obvious hazard, triable issue of material fact exist on grounds that “that the hazardous condition existed long enough for the owner to have discovered it, if an owner exercising reasonable care would have learned of it” (Howard v. Omni Hotels Management Corp. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 403, 431 accord Ortega v. Kmart Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1206-1207.)

 

Causation

            Finally City denies any causation based on the denial of any dangerous condition. Again, City fails to shift the burden based on the lack of any factually and legally supported position establishing the lack of a dangerous condition. (Saelzler v. Advanced Group 400 (2001) 25 Cal.4th 763, 768.) Further triable issues of material fact exist based on Plaintiff’s establishment of the dangerous condition through the unchallenged expert declaration. (See Chowdhury v. City of Los Angeles (1995) 38 Cal.App.4th 1187, 1196.) The circumstances establish a basis of causation not resolved as a matter of law in the instant motion. (Saaelzler v. Advanced Group 400, supra, 25 Cal.4th. at p. 781.)

 

CONCLUSION

The motion for summary judgment is DENIED due to the failure to shift the burden on any and all defenses and triable issues of material fact on each challenge.

The motion was originally reserved for January 14, 2027, and specially advanced via ex parte order on July 27, 2026.

 

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

“To the extent authorized by the chapter governing any particular discovery method or any other provision of this title, the court, after notice to any affected party, person, or attorney, and after opportunity for hearing, may impose the following sanctions against anyone engaging in conduct that is a misuse of the discovery process: ... (c) The court may impose an evidence sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from introducing designated matters in evidence. (d) The court may impose a terminating sanction by one of the following orders: (1) An order striking out the pleadings or parts of the pleadings of any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process. (2) An order staying further proceedings by that party until an order for discovery is obeyed. (3) An order dismissing the action, or any part of the action, of that party. (4) An order rendering a judgment by default against that party.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030.)

Evidence or issue sanctions may be imposed only after parties violated discovery orders compelling further responses, except in exceptional circumstances, including where there was sufficiently egregious misconduct regarding a failure to respond to discovery, or a prior discovery order would be futile. (New Albertsons, Inc. v. Sup. Ct. (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1428.) “The discovery statutes thus ‘evince an incremental approach to discovery sanctions, starting with monetary sanctions and ending with the ultimate sanction of termination.’ (Citation.) Although in extreme cases a court has the authority to order a terminating sanction as a first measure (Citations), a terminating sanction should generally not be imposed until the court has attempted less severe alternatives and found them to be unsuccessful and/or the record clearly shows lesser sanctions would be ineffective.” (Lopez v. Watchtower Bible & Tract Society of New York, Inc. (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 566, 604.) To avoid sanctions, the burden of proving that a discovery violation was not willful is on the party on whom the discovery was served. (Cornwall v. Santa Monica Dairy Co. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 250, 252- 253.)

The court hesitantly imposes issue and evidentiary sanctions, where the result leads to a potential windfall for the prevailing party and an effective “avoidance of a decision on the merits.” (Rutledge v. Hewlett-Packard Co. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 1164, 1194 accord McGinty v. Superior Court (1994) 26 Cal.App.4th 204, 214 [“On balance, we conclude that it was excessive and an abuse of discretion for the trial court to impose the preclusive witness and evidence sanction in this case ... where no real harm has been done ..., and where the punishment falls most harshly not on the supposed malefactor but on the petitioners in their ability to have their case decided on the merits”]; Parker v. Wolters Kluwer United States, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 285, 301.)

A prerequisite to the imposition of the dismissal sanction is that the party has willfully failed to comply with a court order. (Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsytem (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 262, 279-280; Laguna Auto Body v. Farmers Ins. Exchange (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 481, 487 overruled on other grounds in Garcia v. McCutchen (1997) 16 Cal.4th 469, 478, fn. 4.); Young v. Rosenthal (1989) 212 Cal.App.3d 96, 114.) Preventing parties from presenting their cases on the merits is a drastic measure; terminating sanctions should only be ordered when there has been previous noncompliance with a rule or order and it appears a less severe sanction would not be effective. (Link v. Cater (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1326; Department of Forestry & Fire Protection v. Howell¿(2017) 18 Cal.App.5th 154, 191 [“Terminating sanctions are to be used sparingly because of the drastic effect of their application”].) “The trial court may order a terminating sanction for discovery abuse ‘after considering the totality of the circumstances: [the] conduct of the party to determine if the actions were willful; the detriment to the propounding party; and the number of formal and informal attempts to obtain the discovery.’” (Los Defensores, Inc. v. Gomez¿(2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 377, 390.)  

 

Analysis

Honda moves for evidentiary or terminating sanctions on grounds that Plaintiff failed to comply with the June 18, 2026, order compelling a vehicle inspection to be completed no later than July 10, 2026. Honda contends noncompliance with the order at the time of the filing of the order.

            Plaintiff in opposition denies any denial of a vehicle inspection, and offered dates from July 27 to July 29, 2026 (all after the July 10, 2026, ordered compliance deadline). Plaintiff also represents the vehicle itself holds a “junk” status title, which Plaintiffs offered to produce to Honda. Plaintiff denies any prejudice in that Honda refused to either coordinate on said dates, or accept a copy of the junk title. An inspection can still be completed before the August 254, 2026, trial date.

            Given the representation of Plaintiff’s willingness to produce the vehicle for inspection, albeit admittedly late compliance with the June 18, 2026, order of the court, nothing in the conduct supports the finding of a willful violation of the order. The imposition of terminating sanctions are not supported under the circumstances and standard of the law.

Imposition of evidentiary sanctions also appears unnecessarily extreme. Honda first fails to articulate the actual sought after evidentiary sanction(s). Assuming the remaining sanction would be to exclude any and all vehicle information, such exclusion would lead to an effective dismissal of the action. Again, the subject delays will not support an effective terminating sanction regardless of the description of the sought after relief.

            The sanctions standard vests a judge with discretion to impose sanctions for the misuse of the discovery process. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030; City of Los Angeles v. PricewaterhouseCoopers, LLP (2024) 17 Cal.5th 46, 71-74.) Sanctions exist to remedy the wrongs caused to the party seeking sanctions, but should not serve as punishment or provision of a windfall. (Kwan Software Engineering, Inc. v. Hennings (2020) 58 Cal.App.5th 57, 74-75.) The purpose of the statute, as any discovery under the Civil Discovery Act is “to enable a party to obtain evidence in the control of his adversary in order to further the efficient, economical disposition of cases according to right and justice on the merits.” (City of Los Angeles v. PricewaterhouseCoopers, LLP, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 73.) Plaintiff in no way appears to seek to withhold any information.

The unexcused delays however support the request for compensatory sanctions as a result of costs incurred. [Declaration of Paul Peterson.] (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030.) With trial less than two weeks before the hearing of this motion, the imposition imposed on counsel supports the imposition of monetary sanctions against Plaintiff and counsel. The motion for monetary sanctions is GRANTED. The court imposes sanctions of $1,500 against counsel for Plaintiff Loesch, payable within 30 days.

 

CONCLUSION

Honda’s Motion for Evidentiary, Issue, or Terminating sanctions is DENIED.  The parties are ordered to complete the vehicle inspection within 5 days of this order. Sanctions payable within 30 days of this order. The motion for Monetary Sanctions is GRANTED in the amount of $1,500 against Plaintiff’s counsel, and payable in 30 days.

Upon review of the memorandum of points and authorities, the Court notes that no reference is made to the operative FAC. (Memo. of Ps and As at pp. 3-11.) Also, it appears that Defendant only challenges the fifth cause of action for fraudulent concealment in the complaint. (Id.)

On July 31, 2026, Plaintiffs filed and served a notice of non-opposition to the motion for judgment on the pleadings.

  1. Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

Although Plaintiffs filed a notice of non-opposition to the motion, the Court cannot grant the motion. “An amended complaint supersedes all prior complaints . . . and the original complaint ceases to have any effect either as a pleading or as a basis for judgment.” (Malear v. State of California (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 213, 221 [citations omitted, internal quotations omitted].)

The Court finds that Defendant moved for judgment on the pleadings as to a complaint which is no longer operative. The notice of motion is directed at the complaint and makes no reference to the operative FAC. (Not. of Mot. at p. 1:22-24.) Also, the memorandum of points and authorities addresses the fraudulent concealment cause of action in the complaint. Critically, the complaint is no longer an operative pleading. Defendant should have sought judgment on the pleadings as to the FAC.

Thus, pursuant to Malear v. State of California, supra, 89 Cal.App.5th 213, 221, the instant motion cannot be granted irrespective of the lack of opposition. 

  1. Conclusion

Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.

c.     Applicable Law

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending a decision on the merits.  (MaJor v. Miraverde Homeowners Ass’n. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 618, 623.)  In deciding whether or not to grant a preliminary injunction, the court looks to two factors, including “(1) the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits, and (2) the relative balance of harms that is likely to result from the granting or denial of interim injunctive relief.”  (White v. Davis (2003) 30 Cal.4th 528, 553.)  The factors are interrelated, with a greater showing on one permitting a lesser showing on the other.  (Dodge, Warren & Peters Ins. Services, Inc. v. Riley (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 1414, 1420.)  However, the party seeking an injunction must demonstrate at least a reasonable probability of success on the merits.  (IT Corp. v. County of Imperial (1983) 35 Cal.3d 63, 73-74.)  The party seeking the injunction bears the burden of demonstrating both a likelihood of success on the merits and the occurrence of irreparable harm.  (Savage v. Trammell Crow Co. (1990) 223 Cal.App.3d 1562, 1571.)  Irreparable harm may exist if the plaintiff can show an inadequate remedy at law.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 526(a).)  

 

“The granting or denying of a preliminary injunction does not constitute an adjudication of the ultimate rights in controversy.”  (Cohen v. Board of Supervisors (1985) 40 Cal.3d 277, 286; see also Yee v. American National Ins. Co. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 453, 457-458 [“a preliminary injunction is not a determination on the merits….”].)

Based on the Joint Statement, Plaintiff contends that the sole issue is “whether the remaining unauthorized copies of the patient records must be securely destroyed . . . .”  (See Joint Statement at p. 3:16-17.)  The Court references its 1/20/26 Minute Order and incorporates such recitation herein.  The Court stated therein:

 

“To the extent Plaintiff is seeking to have Defendants return or destroy the records and/or provide a sworn declaration, this would be a mandatory injunction. 

 

Pursuant to Brown v. Pacific Found., Inc., (2019) 34 Cal.App.5th 915, 925, mandatory injunctions are rarely granted, and should only be granted in extreme cases where the right thereto is firmly established.  The Court finds, at most, it would be inclined to issue a prohibitory injunction (precluding Defendants from further using or disseminating the subject data), as Plaintiff has not made any showing that a mandatory injunction meets this very high standard.  Notably, the Court provided this analysis in its prior tentative ruling, and Plaintiff’s new moving papers do not address the requirements for obtaining a mandatory injunction.”

 

(01/20/26 Minute Order at p. 3.)

 

The Court finds that the same issue exists that was identified in the Court’s minute order issued on 1/20/26.  Neither the supplemental memorandum of Plaintiff nor the supplemental declaration of Plaintiff’s counsel addresses the requirements for obtaining a mandatory injunction.  According to the Joint Statement, Plaintiff seeks to have Defendants destroy the records at issue.  Such request is a mandatory injunction as it would require Defendants “to take affirmative action . . . .”  (Shaw v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (2023) 95 Cal.App.5th 740, 768.) 

 

Further, although not raised in the opposition, the Court finds that Plaintiff has not argued (or provided admissible evidence) that there is no adequate remedy at law.  Plaintiff has not argued or provided evidence via a declaration that it “has no adequate alternative remedy . . . .”  (Davenport v. Blue Cross of California (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 435, 450.)  “A party seeking injunctive relief must show the absence of an adequate remedy at law.”  (Department of Fish & Game v. Anderson-Cottonwood Irrigation Dist. (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 1554, 1564 [emphasis added].)  Thus, Plaintiff has not met its burden to warrant a preliminary injunction.

 

6.     Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES the motion for preliminary injunction.

Case Number: 25PSCV02072    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: S27

On May 20, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion for summary judgment of their Complaint, arguing that there are no triable issues of material fact for the following reasons. It is undisputed that they remain in possession of five original, un-surrendered and non-transferrable Certificates of Deposit (CDs), each of which state that payment is contingent upon “presentation and surrender of this certificate properly endorsed” to Defendant. They contend that since they still retain the CDs, their retention constitutes conclusive evidence that the condition precedent to payment (i.e., the surrender and cancellation of the CDs) was never met and, therefore, Defendant has breached its contractual obligations by failing to pay the deposit.

On July 24, 2026, Defendant filed its opposition, arguing (among other things) that the absence of business records concerning Plaintiffs’ alleged CDs raises a triable issue of material fact regarding its alleged breach of nonpayment of the CDs.

Non-jury trial is set for February 1, 2027.

On August 4, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their reply, arguing that Defendant’s arguments are speculative, conjectural, and not supported by actual knowledge. In addition, Defendant is repeating the same or similar arguments it made in its motion for summary judgment, which the Court denied.

1.     Background Facts

Plaintiffs Jack and Barbara Greenberg filed their Complaint against Defendant on June 5, 2025, asserting one cause of action for breach of contract. The Complaint alleges that Plaintiffs purchased CDs that Defendant refused to pay when the certificates matured on March 1, 2024. Plaintiffs allege that the principal and interest Defendant owes on the five CDs is $510,817.09.

2.     Motion for Summary Judgment

a.     Burdens on Summary Judgment

Summary judgment is proper “if all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Code Civ. Proc. §437c(c).)  The moving party bears the initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.)

“A plaintiff … has met that party’s burden of showing that there is no defense to a cause of action if that party has proved each element of the cause of action entitling the party to judgment on the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).) “Once the plaintiff or cross-complainant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the defendant or cross-defendant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto. The defendant or cross-defendant shall not rely upon the allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists but, instead, shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).)

  1. Evidentiary Objections

Defendant has filed objections to the declaration of Plaintiff Barbara Greenberg. (Objections filed July 24, 2026, p. i-iii [objecting to Paragraphs 4, 12, and 13 of the declaration].)

Objections to Paragraph 4 of the declaration are OVERRULED.

Objections to Paragraphs 12 and 13 are SUSTAINED as to the plaintiff’s testimony regarding her husband (i.e., Plaintiff Jack Greenberg never signed any renewal instructions or renewal documentation, never endorsed any of the CDs for payment, renewal, or transfer, and never received payment) for lack of personal knowledge and foundation. The objections are otherwise OVERRULED.

The Court notes that Plaintiffs did not file evidentiary objections.

  1. Discussion

Because Plaintiffs are moving for summary judgment, they bear the burden of proving each element of their sole cause of action for breach of contract. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).) “[T]he elements of a cause of action for breach of contract are (1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.”  (Thrifty Payless, Inc. v. The Americana at Brand, LLC (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 1230, 1244.) 

To prove their breach of contract claim, Plaintiffs present the following evidence and law. In 1986, Plaintiffs “purchased a total of five Certificates of Deposit [hereafter, “Certificates” or “CDs”] from Queen City Bank, each at or exceeding $100,000 in principal.” (Defendant’s separate statement in response to Plaintiffs’ separate statement of undisputed material facts (“UMF”) No. 1.) “The Certificates were issued on March 14, May 15, June 16, July 16, and August 15, 1986.” (UMF No. 2.) “Each Certificate contains repayment language requiring presentation and surrender of the original Certificate before payment. Each Certificate also states that it is ‘NON-TRANSFERABLE,’ ‘NON-NEGOTIABLE,’ and that there is ‘NO INTEREST AFTER MATURITY.’” (UMF No. 3.) “Each CD constitutes a written obligation by Queen City Bank to repay the deposited funds according to the terms stated on the Certificates.” (UMF No. 5.) “Plaintiffs retained uninterrupted possession of all five original Certificates from the time they were issued through the present. Although the Certificates were misplaced among long-stored records for many years, they were located in 2024 in the same condition in which they were original issued.” (UMF No. 6.) Under the California Commercial Code: “An action to enforce the obligation of a party to a certificate of deposit to pay the instrument shall be commenced within six years after demand for payment is made to the maker ….” (Com. Code, § 3118, subd. (e).) “On March 1, 2024, Plaintiffs made a formal written demand for payment and attempted to present all five original Certificates at First Bank’s Rowland Heights branch, the successor office of issue following First Bank’s merger with Queen City Bank. Plaintiffs had not previously made formal demand for payment on the Certificates.” (UMF No. 8.) Plaintiffs argue that because they filed this lawsuit on June 5, 2025, within six years of making the demand to Defendant on March 1, 2024, their action is timely. Plaintiff Barbara Greenberg testifies that, after the demand, Defendant declined to take custody of the Certificates and refused to accept them for any form of processing, redemption, or payment. (Motion, Greenberg Decl., ¶ 9.) “At no time did the bank assert that the Certificates had ever been surrendered, redeemed, paid, or cancelled, and stated only that it had ‘no records.’ The five Certificates collectively reflect principal deposits totaling $508,169.57. The Certificates further reflect accrued contractual interest totaling $2,780.85 through maturity. [Therefore,] [t]he total amount due and owing under the Certificates as of maturity is $510,950.42 [as alleged in the Complaint], exclusive of any additional prejudgment interest recoverable by law.” (Id. ¶ 9.)

The Court finds that Plaintiffs have met their initial burden of proving each element of their breach of contract claim through the evidence above. Therefore, the burden shifts to Defendant to show that one or more triable issues of material fact exist as to that claim or defense.

In its opposition, Defendant first argues that “recent fabrication can be inferred when it is shown that a witness did not speak about an important matter at a time when it would have been natural for him [ or her] to do so." (People v. Riccardi (2012) 54 Cal.4th 758, 803, citing People v. Manson (1976) 61 Cal.App.3d 102, 143.) Here, Plaintiffs claim that they did not request repayment non-interest bearing deposits totaling over $500,000 between 1986 and 2024. It would have been natural for a customer of Queen City Bank to request repayment of matured CDs in 1986, even if he or she lost the certificates; and even more so for Plaintiffs, who were shareholders and friends of the bank president. Therefore, based solely on the passage of time, a trier of fact could reasonably infer that Barbara Greenberg's statement that Plaintiffs never received repayment is a fabrication intended to advance Plaintiffs' interest in this dispute.” (Opposition, pp. 6:24-7:7.)

The Court finds Defendant’s fabrication argument unpersuasive because “‘[s]peculation … is not evidence’ that can be utilized in opposing a motion for summary judgment. [Citations.]” (Knapp v. Doherty (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 76, 99.)

Defendant also argues that Plaintiffs fail to submit competent evidence of breach and Plaintiff Jack Greenberg, who has not submitted any declaration or affidavit in support of this motion, “could have withdrawn the funds or rolled over the CDs without Barbara Greenberg’s knowledge or consent.” (Opposition, p. 8:25-27.)

The Court also finds the above argument is unpersuasive. Although the Court has sustained Defendant’s objections to Barbara Greenberg’s testimony regarding her spouse, the Court has still found that Plaintiffs have established breach particularly through the undisputed facts that: (1) Plaintiffs obtained the CDs from First Bank’s predecessor Queen City Bank, (2) each CD constitutes a written obligation by Queen City Bank to repay the deposited funds according to the terms stated on the CDs, (3) each CD contains repayment language requiring presentation and surrender of the original CD before payment, (4) each CD states that it is “NON-TRANSFERABLE,” (5) Plaintiffs retained uninterrupted possession of all five original CDs from the time they were issued through the present, (6) Plaintiffs made the first demand for payment in March 2024, and (7) Defendant refused to pay. Those undisputed facts have established each element of the breach of contract claim, including breach. Plaintiffs have also established damages through the testimony of Barbara Greenberg that [t]he total amount due and owing under the Certificates as of maturity is $510,950.42 [as alleged in the Complaint], exclusive of any additional prejudgment interest recoverable by law.” (Motion, Greenberg Decl., ¶ 9.)

Notwithstanding the above, the Court finds that Defendant has met its burden through the declaration of expert witness Glen C. Terry (“Terry”) and Director of Operations, Mandi Willis (“Willis”).

Terry testifies that he is a Partner and Consulting CFO at an advisory group in California, holds a Master of Business Administration with a concentration in Finance and Management, attended the Pacific Coast School of Banking, and has more than 40 years of experience in the banking industry, including serving as President, Chief Executive Officer, and/or Director at multiple banks. (Terry Decl., ¶¶ 1-3.)

Terry determined from the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation’s website that Queen City Bank (hereafter, “QCB”) was established on September 26, 1983, and merged into First Bank & Trust (Santa Ana, California) on April 15, 1996. (Terry Decl., ¶ 9.) At the time of the merger, QCB had total assets of approximately $50.8 million and total deposits of approximately $44.27 million. (Ibid.)

Based on his review of the five CDs, Terry opines that the CDs represent a single initial deposit of $100,000 that was successively rolled over into new short term CDs with accrued interest added to principal at each rollover; the CDs do not represent an aggregate of $500,000 in deposits. (Terry Decl., ¶ 12.) This is because “each subsequent CD’s principal precisely equal[ed] the prior CD’s principal plus its accrued interest … [which] is, in [his] experience, the unmistakable signature of a CD rollover. It is not consistent with five separate, independent deposits. If the Plaintiffs had made five separate deposits, one would expect round-number principal amounts, or amounts unrelated to the compounding of interest from a prior instrument. Instead, the odd-cent principal ($101,236.66, $101,791.38, $101,314.28, and $102,827.25) can only be explained as the product of successive interest compounding on a single original deposit of $100,000. [Terry is] aware of no innocent way for five wholly independent deposits to produce principal balances that dovetail to the penny in this manner.” (id. ¶ 14.)

Willis in turn attests to the following facts. “As the Director of Operations, [she] manage[s] the bank’s daily operations, including the entry of deposits, CDs, withdrawals, checks, automatic clearing house operations, and wire transfers.” (Willis Decl., ¶ 3.)

“First Bank’s deposits (including certificates of deposit) and withdrawals are recorded on a core data system through a central processing system that tracks transactions in real-time. When a deposit or a withdrawal is made, the core system updates the customer’s account balance, posts the transaction to the general ledger, and makes the updated information available across all banking channels, such as the only system used by bank tellers and ATMs. This system is the foundation for First Bank’s internal records, including customer data and balances, and enables the processing of daily banking transactions. It is First Bank’s policy and practice to maintain customer account records for seven years.” (Willis Decl., ¶ 4.) “First Bank acquired [QCB], National Association, including its customer relationships, in or around April 1996.” (Id. ¶ 5.)

“When First Bank acquires another financial institution, the records of deposits and certificates of deposit for the institution that was acquired are merged into First Bank’s core data.” (Willis Decl., ¶ 6.) Willis is informed and believes “that the records of [QCB’s] deposits were merged into First Bank’s core data in or around 1996, because First Bank became liable to [QCB’s] customers for the deposits that they made at [QCB] that remained due and owing as of the date of the acquisition, according to records of the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation.” (Id. ¶ 6.)

Willis “conducted searches of First Bank’s core data to determine if either Jack Greenberg or Barbara individually, or Jack Greenberg and Barbara Greenberg jointly, have an account at First Bank that is still open or a certificate of deposit that has not been repaid …,” and “determined that no such account is open and no certificate of deposit that has not been repaid by First Bank or [QCB] exists. [She is] familiar with the methods used to search First Bank’s core data. If a deposit account or an unpaid certificate of deposit owned by Jack Greenberg and/or Barbara Greenberg existed, it would have been discovered by [her] through the searches that [she] performed.” (Willis Decl., ¶ 7.)

The Court finds that through the testimonies of Terry and Willis, Defendant has met its burden of showing that there is a triable issue of material fact regarding the damages element of the breach of contract claim, and whether there was any breach in the first place (i.e., whether Defendant refused to pay Plaintiffs for CDs that were still outstanding). In their reply, Plaintiffs insist that the possession of the CDs is conclusive evidence supporting their breach of contract claim given that it is undisputed that each CD requires presentation and surrender of the original CD before payment. However, in their moving papers, Plaintiffs did not submit evidence showing that (1) Defendant’s roll-over theory is impossible, and/or (2) QCB could not have both allowed payment of the CDs while allowing Plaintiffs to retain possession. The answers to those questions have not been answered. Therefore, the Court is unable to conclude as a matter of law that Plaintiffs’ possession of the CDs is conclusive evidence of their claim. Even if there are no answers to those questions, the Court cannot determine through this motion whether Plaintiffs’ version of the story is more believable than Defendant’s. Credibility determinations are reserved for trial.

For those reasons, the Court denies the motion.

3.     Conclusion

Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment is DENIED.

2.     Leave to Intervene

Code of Civil Procedure Section 387 states in part: 

(c) A nonparty shall petition the court for leave to intervene by noticed motion or ex parte application. The petition shall include a copy of the proposed complaint in intervention or answer in intervention and set forth the grounds upon which intervention rests. 

(d) (1) The court shall, upon timely application, permit a nonparty to intervene in the action or proceeding if either of the following conditions is satisfied:  

(A) A provision of law confers an unconditional right to intervene. 

(B) The person seeking intervention claims an interest relating to the property or transaction that is the subject of the action and that person is so situated that the disposition of the action may impair or impede that person’s ability to protect that interest, unless that person’s interest is adequately represented by one or more of the existing parties. 

(2) The court may, upon timely application, permit a nonparty to intervene in the action or proceeding if the person has an interest in the matter in litigation, or in the success of either of the parties, or an interest against both. 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 387(c)-(d).)  

“The trial court has discretion to permit a nonparty to intervene where: (1) the proper procedures have been followed, (2) the nonparty has a direct and immediate interest in the action, (3) the intervention will not enlarge the issues in the litigation, and (4) the reasons for the intervention outweigh any opposition by the parties presently in the action….” (Chavez v. Netflix (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 43, 51.) 

“Because the decision whether to allow intervention is best determined based on the particular facts in each case, it is generally left to the sound discretion of the trial court.” (City and County of San Francisco v. State of Cal. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1030, 1036. Accord, Hinton v. Beck (2009) 176 Cal.App.4th 1378, 1382 [“Intervention pursuant to section 387, subdivision (a) is not a matter of right, but is discretionary with the trial court.”].) 

Proposed Intervenor DB Insurance seeks to intervene on the following basis. DB Insurance is the insurer of Defendants Golden Horn Transportation Inc., and Ghaith Ahmed Alhamadani aka Alex Kayak (“Defendants”) (Orlando Decl. ¶ 7.) DB Insurance states it has a direct and immediate interest in this litigation because Defendants may be liable for judgment entered in this action, and DB Insurance’s interests may be affected by the proceeding and outcome. (Id)

Amazon opposes this motion for several reasons. First, Amazon argues that DB Insurance lacks sufficient interest in this litigation because it completely denied coverage (see Declaration of Darrion Jackson-Laws, Exhibit A). Second, Amazon asserts that DB Insurance’s intervention would improperly enlarge the issues. Finally, Amazon contends that DB Insurance cannot disclaim its obligations to its insureds while simultaneously claiming a protectable interest (Opposition p. 5).

On reply, DB Insurance argues that intervention is mandatory because it may be obligated to satisfy a judgment under the MCS-90 endorsement. Second, DB Insurance asserts that it meets all elements required for mandatory intervention.

When an action asserting a claim for bodily injury or property damage is brought against an insured, and the insured tenders the defense to the insurer, there are three possible positions the insurer can take towards the request for coverage and a defense. An insurer can (1) admit coverage and provide a defense; (2) deny coverage and refuse to provide a defense; or (3) provide a defense under a reservation of rights to contest coverage. (Gray v. Begley (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 1509)

If the insurer denies coverage and refuses to provide a defense, the insurer “does not have a direct interest in the litigation between the plaintiff and the insured to warrant intervention. The rationale behind this rule is that by its denial, the insurer has lost its right to control the litigation.” (Hinton v. Beck (2009) 176 Cal.App.4th 1378)

Here, DB Insurance denied coverage and refused to provide a defense. (Opposition, Jackson-Laws Decl., ¶ 4, Exhibit B – 001.) As a result, DB Insurance has lost its right to control the litigation.

.

3.     Conclusion

Based on the forgoing, the motion for leave to intervene is denied.

1.     Background Facts

Plaintiff Hui Chiang filed this action against Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. for violations of the Song-Beverly Act, breach of implied warranty, and fraudulent inducement – Concealment.

2.     Demurrer

a.     Meet and Confer

California Civil Procedure Code section 430.41 requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41(a).) The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (Id., § 430.41(a)(2).) Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (Id., § 430.41(a)(3).) The meet and confer requirement also applies to motions to strike. (CCP § 435.5.)  Even so, failure to meet and confer is insufficient grounds to sustain or overrule a demurrer. (CCP § 430.41(a)(4).) 

Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.¿(Hahn v. Mirda¿(2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context.¿In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿“A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. …. The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.”¿(Hahn¿147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)¿¿¿ 

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true. (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.) Courts also consider exhibits attached to the complaint and incorporated by reference. (See Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94 (Frantz).)¿¿ 

 

b.     Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment (5th Cause of Action)

Defendant argues Plaintiff’s fraudulent concealment allegations lack the required particularity, Plaintiff fails to plead the essential element of duty to disclose with particularity, Plaintiff has not adequately pled Defendant actively concealed the alleged defect, and Plaintiff’s fraudulent concealment claim is barred by the economic loss doctrine.

1.     Specificity

Firstly, the Court notes that the general rule to “allege the names of the persons who made the allegedly fraudulent representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written” (Tarmann v. State Farm Mutual Auto. Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157) is inapplicable to a claim of fraudulent concealment. (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Association, Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384 (“How does one show ‘how’ and ‘by what means’ something didn’t happen, or ‘when’ it never happened, or ‘where’ it never happened?”).) Instead, such details, in the context of a fraudulent concealment claim, “are properly the subject of discovery, not demurrer.” (Id. at pp. 1384-85; Tarmann, supra, 2 Cal.App.4th at p. 158 [“We acknowledge that the requirement of specificity is relaxed when the allegations indicate that ‘the defendant must necessarily possess full information concerning the facts of the controversy’ [Citation.]”].) 

The Supreme Court’s recent decision in Rattagan re-affirms and clarifies this rule; although it held that the same specificity pleading standard for affirmative fraud applies to fraudulent concealment claims, it clarified that “the focus of inquiry shifts to the unique elements of the claim. [Citations.]” (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 43.) Thus, in a concealment claim based on a defendant’s duty to disclose arising from the defendant’s exclusive knowledge, the complaint must specifically allege “(1) the content of the omitted facts, (2) defendant’s awareness of the materiality of those facts, (3) the inaccessibility of the facts to plaintiff, (4) the general point at which the omitted facts should or could have been revealed, and (5) justifiable and actual reliance, either through action or forbearance, based on the defendant’s omission.” (Id. at pp. 43-44.)¿ 

            In support of the Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment claim, Plaintiff alleges the following facts:

(1) “that the 1.5L engine and/or its related components installed in the Subject Vehicle suffer from one or more defects that can result in loss of power, stalling, engine running rough, engine misfire(s), failure or replacement of the engine (the "Engine Defect") (Compl., ¶ 47);

(2) “The Engine Defect causes unsafe conditions in vehicles equipped with the 1.5L

engine, including, but not limited to, the engine losing power while driving. These conditions

present a safety hazard because they severely affect the driver's ability to control the vehicle,

and substantially increase the likelihood that the engine will fail, lose power, and/or cut off

during operation, thereby resulting in accidents involving property damage, personal injury and

even death” (Compl., ¶ 49);

(3) “prior to sale ofthe Subject Vehicle, HONDA knew, or should have known, about the Engine Defect through its exclusive knowledge of non-public, internal data about the Engine Defect, including: pre-releasing testing data; early consumer complaints about the Engine Defect to Defendant Honda’s dealers who are HONDA's agents for vehicle repairs; dealership repair orders; testing conducted in response to those complaints; and other internal sources of information possessed exclusively by Defendant HONDA and its agents. Nevertheless, Defendant HONDA and its agents have actively concealed the Engine Defect and failed to disclose this defect to Plaintiff at the time of purchase of the Subject Vehicle or thereafter” (Compl., ¶ 51);

 (4) “ Reasonable consumers, like Plaintiff, reasonably expect that a vehicle's engine is safe, will function in a manner that will not pose a safety hazard, and is free from defects. Plaintiff further reasonably expects that HONDA will not sell or lease vehicles with known safety defects, such as the Engine Defect, and will disclose any such defects to its consumers when it learns of them. Plaintiff did not expect Defendant HONDA to fail to disclose the Engine Defect to Plaintiff and to continually deny the defect.” (Compl., ¶ 61);

 (5) “The existence of the Engine Defect is a material fact that a reasonable consumer would consider when deciding whether to purchase or lease a vehicle equipped with a 1.5L engine. Had Plaintiff known that the Subject Vehicle was equipped with a defective engine, Plaintiff would not have purchased the Subject Vehicle.” (Compl., ¶ 60.)

The Court finds that Plaintiff alleged the elements of fraud with the requisite particularity. (See Dhital v. Nissan N. Am., Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 844 [“[P]laintiffs alleged the CVT transmissions installed in numerous Nissan vehicles (including the one plaintiffs purchased) were defective; Nissan knew of the defects and the hazards they posed; Nissan had exclusive knowledge of the defects but intentionally concealed and failed to disclose that information; Nissan intended to deceive plaintiffs by concealing known transmission problems; plaintiffs would not have purchased the car if they had known of the defects; and plaintiffs suffered damages in the form of money paid to purchase the car.”].) 

Thus, the demurrer cannot be sustained on this ground.

2.      Duty to Disclose

Additionally, Defendant argues that the Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment cause of action is subject to demurrer because Plaintiff has failed to sufficiently allege a duty to disclose.

Absent a fiduciary relationship between the parties, a duty to disclose can arise in only three circumstances: (1) the defendant had exclusive knowledge of the material fact; (2) the defendant actively concealed the material fact; or (3) the defendant made partial representations while also suppressing the material fact. (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 312; LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336.) The California Supreme Court “has described the necessary relationship giving rise to a duty to disclose as a ‘transaction’ between the plaintiff and defendant ….” (Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at p. 311; Warner Construction Corp. v. City of Los Angeles (1970) 2 Cal.3d 285, 294; Hoffman v. 162 North Wolfe LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 1178, 1187–89 [rejecting concealment claim where plaintiffs “were not involved in a transaction with the parties they claim defrauded them”]; LiMandri, supra, 52 Cal.App.4th at p. 337 [“such a relationship can only come into being as a result of some sort of transaction between the parties”].)¿¿ 

The Bigler-Engler Court explained that a duty to disclose facts “arises only when the parties are in a relationship that gives rise to the duty, such as ‘“seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual arrangement[]”’” and, in the absence of a fiduciary relationship, a fraudulent omission case may be grounded on one party's exclusive knowledge of material facts, active concealment of material facts or suppression of material facts in the context of a partial disclosure, but only where the parties share some sort of relationship in which a duty to disclose may arise.¿(Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at p. 311 [citing to Shin v. Kong (2000) 80 Cal.App.4th 498, 509].)¿ The Bigler-Engler Court goes further to state that “[s]uch a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.”¿(Id. at p. 312.)¿ 

In Dhital, the Court concluded that allegations were sufficient to support the existence of a buyer-seller relationship between the parties as “Plaintiffs alleged that they bought the car from a Nissan dealership, that Nissan backed the car with an express warranty, and that Nissan's authorized dealerships are [the manufacturer’s] agents for purposes of the sale of Nissan vehicles to consumers. (Dhital, supra, 84 Cal.App.5th at p. 844.)

The Court finds that Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged facts supporting a duty to disclose. Here, Plaintiff has alleged a direct transaction between Plaintiff and Defendant. (Compl., ¶ 6.) Plaintiff specifically alleges that “[o]n or about December 29, 2023, Plaintiff entered into a warranty contract with Defendant Honda regarding a 2024 Honda Civic, vehicle identification number 2HGFE1F91RH303063 (“Subject Vehicle”), which was manufactured and or distributed by Defendant HONDA.” (Ibid) This alone is sufficient to impose upon Defendant a duty to disclose. (Dhital, supra, 84 Cal.App.5th at p. 844.) Although Plaintiff did not purchase her vehicle directly from Defendant, she did enter into a direct contractual warranty agreement with Defendant. (Comp., ¶¶ 6-7, Ex. A; see LiMandri, supra, 52 Cal.App.4th at p. 336 (“parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement”) (emphasis added); Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th 1 (same).) The alleged parties to the warranty are Plaintiff and Defendant and the warranty itself is “some sort of transaction” between the two parties giving rise to a duty to disclose. Thus, Plaintiff has pled sufficient facts to establish a duty to disclose. The demurrer to the fifth cause of action cannot be sustained on this ground.

3.     Economic Loss Rule

Defendant lastly argues that the economic loss rule bars Plaintiff’s fraud cause of action.  

The Court disagrees. 

As the Dhital Court explained, “under California law, the economic loss rule does not bar [P]laintiff’s claim here for fraudulent inducement by concealment. Fraudulent inducement claims fall within an exception to the economic loss rule recognized by our Supreme Court (Citation) and [P]laintiff[] allege[s] fraudulent conduct that is independent of [Defendant’s] alleged warranty breaches.” (Dhital, supra, 84 Cal.App.5th at p. 843; see also Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 38 [“A plaintiff may assert a tort claim for fraudulent concealment based on conduct occurring in the course of a contractual relationship …. [T]he independent tort duty to refrain from engaging in fraudulent conduct is well established by statute and common law”].) Because Plaintiff has adequately alleged fraud, the claim is not barred by the economic loss rule.

The Demurrer to the fifth cause of action is overruled.

3.     Motion to Strike

“In order to survive a motion to strike an allegation of punitive damages, the ultimate facts showing an entitlement to such relief must be pled by a plaintiff.” (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255. Accord, Spinks v. Equity Residential Briarwood Apartments (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1004, 1055.)  

Fraudulent acts of concealment may support awards of punitive damages. (E.g., Werschkull v. United Cal. Bank (1978) 85 Cal.App.3d 981, 1004.) 

In Lemon Law cases, as to vehicle sales through dealerships acting as manufacturer agents, a car manufacturer’s presale fraud or concealment is distinct from its subsequent conduct in breaching statutory warranty obligations, such that plaintiffs can recover punitive damages with a penalty under the Song-Beverly Act. (See, e.g., Dhital v. Nissan N. Am., Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 841; Bowser v. Ford Motor Co. (2022) 78 Cal.App.5th 587, 627 [“entitled to compensatory damages (and attorney fees) under the Song-Beverly Act as well as punitive damages for fraud.”]; Anderson v. Ford Motor Co. (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 946, 967-973; Covert v. FCA USA, LLC (2022) 73 Cal.App.5th 821, 828 [“we assume … prayer for punitive damages was based on … cause of action for fraudulent concealment because the Song -Beverly Act provides for civil penalties, but not punitive damages.”]; Santana v. FCA US, LLC (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 334, 350 [“In the case of fraud, the harm stems from a deception. And in the case of the Song-Beverly Act, it stems from the failure to honor the warranty.”].) 

            Here, the Complaint sufficiently alleges ultimate facts regarding concealment, as analyzed with regard to the Demurrer, which also supports the remedy of punitive damages.

Defendant moves to strike Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages. The Court denies Defendant’s request to strike punitive damages because, as determined above, Plaintiff has alleged sufficient facts to plead their Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment cause of action.

            Therefore, Defendant’s motion to strike Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages is denied.

4.     Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action is overruled. Defendant’s motion to strike Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages is denied.
