DEPARTMENT P LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 23NWCV03952    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: P

SARBJIT SINGH v.  KINH TO

CASE NO.: 23NWCV03952 

HEARING: 08/11/2026 @ 10:30 AM

 

#11

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant Kinh To aka Kinh To Quang, individually, and dba Farmer Boys Liquor Market’s motion for summary judgment is GRANTED. Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication is MOOT.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant Kinh To aka Kinh To Quang, individually, and dba Farmer Boys Liquor Market (Defendant) moves for summary judgment or, alternatively, summary adjudication.

 

Background

 

On December 6, 2023, Plaintiff Sarbjit Singh (Plaintiff) filed this breach of contract against Defendant and Does 1 through 25. The complaint alleges that on July 12, 2023, Plaintiff and Defendant entered into a written agreement for the sale of Farmer Boys Liquor Market, located at 7501 Somerset Blvd., Paramount, CA 90723 (Subject Property); however, after escrow opened and after Plaintiff completed various conditions under the agreement, Defendant sought to close escrow, withdrew the transfer of the liquor license, and locked Plaintiff out of the premises. (Complaint, ¶¶ 6-17.) The complaint asserts three causes of action: (1) breach of written contract, (2) specific performance, and (3) declaratory relief with request for temporary, preliminary, and permanent injunctive relief.

 

On June 5, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s former counsel’s motion to be relieved as counsel.

 

Legal Standard

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) In reviewing a motion for summary judgment, courts apply a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent’s claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.)

 

In moving for summary judgment, a defendant has the “burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) A defendant must support the motion “with evidence including ‘affidavits, declarations, admissions, answers to interrogatories, depositions, and matters of… judicial notice.’” (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 855, citing Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (b).)

 

Once a moving party has satisfied the initial burden of proof, “the burden shifts to the plaintiff or cross-complainant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.) “Where…the motion for summary judgment is unopposed, the moving party may still not be granted summary judgment unless his papers clearly establish that there is no triable issue of fact and he is entitled to judgment.” (Harman v. Mono General Hospital (1982) 131 Cal.App.3d 607, 613.)

 

Summary judgment must be granted “if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119, see also Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Yanowitz v. L’Oreal USA, Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1127.)

 

Discussion

 

Defendant moves for summary judgment or, alternatively, summary adjudication on the following grounds: (1) failure of condition precedent; (2) fraud/forged lease/unclean hands; (3) $50,000 outside-escrow payments were pre-existing debts; (4) mandatory mediation/arbitration; (5) lack of standing; (6) Plaintiff’s breaches excuse Defendant’s performance; (7) specific performance is unavailable due to fraud, unclean hands, and the business is not unique; and (8) the declaratory relief cause of action is duplicative. This motion is unopposed.

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s failure of satisfying a condition precedent constitutes a complete defense.

 

To prevail on a breach of contract claim, a plaintiff must be able to establish “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiffs.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.)

 

“Generally, a party’s failure to perform a condition precedent will preclude an action for breach of contract.” (Richman v. Hartley (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1182, 1192.) “[A] condition precedent is either an act of a party that must be performed or an uncertain event that must happen before the contractual right accrues or the contractual duty arises.” (Platt Pacific, Inc. v. Andelson (1993) 6 Cal.4th 307, 313, citing Civ. Code, § 1436.)

 

Condition precedents may be created either expressly, by words such as “subject to” or “conditioned upon” or impliedly. (Estate of Jones (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 948, 953, citation omitted.) “They are generally disfavored and are strictly construed against a party arguing the agreement imposes one.” (Ibid.) “Courts will not interpret a provision as a condition precedent absent clear, unambiguous language requiring that construction.” (Ibid.) “[C]ourts shall not construe a term of the contract so as to establish a condition precedent absent plain and unambiguous contract language to that effect.” (Ibid.)

 

Defendant establishes the following: Defendant is the seller and property owner of the Subject Property. (UMF, ¶ 1.) On July 12, 2023, the parties entered into a written agreement, a Business Purchase Agreement (BPA), for the sale of the Subject Property for $80,000 in which Plaintiff is named individually as the buyer. (UMF, ¶ 7.) The BPA was drafted by Plaintiff’s broker. (UMF, ¶ 7.)

 

Section 12 of the BPA provides:

 

LEASE (Check applicable items): The sale is contingent upon Buyer obtaining, within 21…Days After Acceptance, the assignment, new lease, option to extend, sublease or other lease as indicated below. Buyer shall submit an application for such lease to Seller's landlord or Seller, as applicable, within 15…Days After Acceptance.

 

(UMF, ¶ 8, BPA, § 12.) Additionally, the following options provision is checked: “A new lease with Seller's landlord, on terms acceptable to Buyer, to become effective concurrently with the Close Of Escrow.” (UMF, ¶ 8, BPA, § 12.)

 

Defendant argues that Section 12 of the BPA makes an executed lease with Plaintiff at closing an essential term of the contract for Defendant. Defendant shows that Plaintiff did not obtain new lease within 21 days after acceptance, nor did he submit an application for such lease to Seller’s landlord or Defendant within 15 days after acceptance. (UMF, ¶ 13.) Further, Plaintiff did not obtain a new lease with Seller’s landlord to become effective concurrently with the close of escrow. (UMF, ¶ 14.)

 

Defendant argues that beyond not procuring the lease as a condition precedent, Plaintiff attempted to procure the lease through fraud. Namely, Defendant refers to Plaintiff’s prior attempt to purchase the Subject Property from Defendant in December of 2020, when the parties negotiated a 30-page lease. (UMF, ¶ 3.) The lease named the lessor as Quang D Phu and To Family Trust. (UMF, ¶ 4.)

Quang D Phu is Defendant’s husband who passed away on March 1, 2023. (UMF, ¶ 5.)

 

Escrow was opened on July 13, 2023, and the instructions provided: “NEW LEASE: Buyer is presently negotiating a new lease for the subject business premises directly with the Lessor. A fully executed lease shall be deposited into escrow for final delivery to the buyer at the close of escrow.” (UMF, ¶ 16; Kim Decl., Exh. F.) While Plaintiff and Defendant were negotiating lease terms, Plaintiff introduced a purported second lease, negotiated and executed by Quang D Phu, dated January 11, 2023, while he was terminally ill. (UMF, ¶¶ 17-19.) The document included parts of the former failed lease with changed terms favorable to Plaintiff and Quang D Phu’s signature. (UMF, ¶ 22.) Defendant points to her own declaration stating that while she is involved in the business and all negotiations, she had never seen or discussed the purported lease with her late husband. (UMF, ¶ 20.)

 

Defendant points out inconsistencies and errors in the purported lease to show that it is fraudulent and cannot satisfy the condition precedent. Firstly, the lease is dated January 11, 2023, which was before the BPA was signed in July of 2023. (UMF, ¶ 19.) Defendant argues these dates are illogical given that the BPA, written by Plaintiff’s broker, represented that Plaintiff was currently negotiating a lease with Defendant. (UMF, ¶ 16.) Defendant also shows that the lease provides a twenty-five year term plus three five-year options which is contradicted by the complaint, which alleges a twenty-year lease. (UMF, ¶ 21; Complaint, ¶ 8.) Defendant also establishes through Plaintiff’s deposition testimony that Plaintiff never gave a copy of the purported lease to his broker, escrow company, or to Defendant. (UMF, ¶ 26, Kim Decl., Exh. M, Plaintiff’s Depo., 152:1-25-153:1-13.) Defendant also shows that there are spelling errors, missing paragraph sections, and nonsense terms—including that the lessor would pay the lessee’s operating expenses. (UMF, ¶ 21.)

 

Here, the Court agrees that Section 12 of the BPA unambiguously and plainly creates a condition precedent requiring Plaintiff to obtain the new lease. (See Estate of Jones, supra, 82 Cal.App.5th at p. 953 [terms like “subject to” or “conditioned upon” create express condition precedent].) The terms of the BPA provide that the sale is “contingent upon” the buyer obtaining a “new” lease. (UMF, ¶ 8, BPA, § 12.) Moreover, Defendant establishes that Plaintiff did not satisfy the condition precedent because Plaintiff did not obtain a new lease from Defendant within 21 days after acceptance, July 12, 2023, nor did Plaintiff apply for such a lease from Defendant within 15 days of July 12, 2023. (UMF, ¶ 13.)

 

To the extent that Plaintiff had already obtained a lease, Plaintiff presented it after October of 2023, thus, after the time allotted under the BPA. (UMF, ¶¶ 18-19.) Defendant establishes that the lease was not signed by Defendant, but rather, included a forged signature of her late husband, procured prior to his death and prior to Plaintiff and Defendant entering the BPA. The Court agrees that this lease, even if it was not forged, does not satisfy the condition precedent of obtaining a “new lease” from Defendant or Defendant’s landlord. The lease predates the BPA, thus showing it cannot be a “new” lease from Defendant and contradicting the language of the BPA drafted by Plaintiff’s broker, that Plaintiff was actively negotiating a new lease.

 

The Court therefore finds that Defendant establishes that Plaintiff’s failure to meet the condition precedent is a complete defense to Plaintiff’s breach of contract claim.

 

Plaintiff’s specific performance claim and declaratory relief claim are wholly dependent on Plaintiff’s breach of contract claim. Therefore, these claims do not independently survive. “[S]pecific performance is a remedy for breach of contract, a cause of action which requires proof the contract was breached.” (Golden West Baseball Co. v. City of Anaheim (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 11, 49.) Plaintiff’s declaratory relief claim seeks a “determin[ation] that Plaintiff performed as agreed, and that the purchase Agreement remain in full force and effect and binding upon the parties pending resolution of the failure of the Defendant to act in accordance with the Purchase Agreement in the transfer of the business assets and possession to Plaintiff.” (Complaint, ¶ 41.) Adjudication of the declaratory relief claim would be duplicative of the breach of contract claim.

 

As this motion for summary judgment is not opposed, Plaintiff does not meet his burden of raising any triable issues of material fact.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion for summary judgment is GRANTED. Pursuant to this ruling, the Court does not reach Defendant’s alternative motion for summary adjudication.

(Defendants) now apply ex parte to continue the trial date to August 31, 2026, or a date thereafter. A trial continuance to August 31, 2026 or thereafter is reasonable. The Court will confer with the parties to select new trial and FSC dates. The expert discovery deadline and motion deadline as related to Dr. Swerdlow’s deposition will correspond with the new trial date.

MICHAEL CLAYTON v. XPO LOGISTICS, INC., ET AL.

CASE NO.: 23STCV12335

HEARING: 08/11/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#8

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant and Cross-Complainant, Jong Uk Byun’s motion for a determination of good faith settlement is GRANTED.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant and Cross-Complainant, Jong Uk Byun moves for a determination of good faith settlement.

 

Background

 

On March 31, 2023, Plaintiff Michael Clayton (Plaintiff) initiated this action against Defendants XPO Logistics Freight Inc., Alameda Engle Properties, LLC, Doe Fence Company, Secure Mobile Destruction, Inc., and Does 1 to 50. Plaintiff alleges that, during a delivery on June 17, 2021, at 8440 Alameda Street in the City of Los Angeles (Subject Premises), he was electrocuted by an electrified fence that enclosed the Subject Premises. It is further alleged that, at the time of the incident, the electrified fence discharged over 7,000 volts of electricity, did not have a perimeter fence, and did not have any warning signs visible to Plaintiff. The only warning sign was located on the opposite side of the fence facing the opposite direction of the Plaintiff. The complaint asserts four causes of action: (1) negligence; (2) products liability; (3) premises liability; and (4) strict liability.

 

On August 16, 2024, Plaintiff filed an Amendment to Complaint naming Central Metal, Inc. (CMI) as Doe 4. On February 6, 2025, Plaintiff filed an Amendment to Complaint naming Byun as Doe 6.

 

On May 19, 2025, Byun filed a Cross-Complaint for equitable indemnity and contribution against XPO Logistics Freight Inc., Amarok LLC, Fleet Yards, Inc., and Moes 1 to 50.

 

Legal Standard

 

“Any party to an action in which it is alleged that two or more parties are joint tortfeasors or co-obligors on a contract debt shall be entitled to a hearing on the issue of the good faith of a settlement entered into by the plaintiff or other claimant and one or more alleged tortfeasors or co-obligors.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 877.6, subd. (a).)  A judicial determination of good faith “bar[s] any other joint tortfeasor…from any further claims against the settling tortfeasor…for equitable comparative contribution, or partial or comparative indemnity, based on comparative negligence or comparative fault.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 877.6, subd. (c).) 

 

In evaluating whether a settlement has been made in good faith, courts consider the following factors, as set forth by the California Supreme Court in the landmark case Tech-Bilt, Inc. v. Woodward-Clyde & Associates (1985) 38 Cal.3d 488 [Tech-Bilt]: (1) “a rough approximation of plaintiffs’ total recovery”; (2) “the settlor’s proportionate liability”; (3) “the amount paid in settlement”; (4) “the allocation of the settlement proceeds among plaintiffs”; (5) “a recognition that a settlor should pay less in settlement than he would if he were found liable after a trial”; (6) the settling party's “financial conditions and insurance policy limits”; (7) any evidence of “collusion, fraud, or tortious conduct aimed to injure the interests of nonsettling defendants.” (Id. at 499.) “The issue of the good faith of a settlement may be determined by the court on the basis of affidavits served with the notice of hearing, and any counteraffidavits filed in response, or the court may, in its discretion, receive other evidence at the hearing.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 877.6, subd. (b).) 

 

The “good faith” concept in Code of Civil Procedure section 877.6 is a flexible principle imposing on reviewing courts the obligation to guard against the numerous ways in which the interests of nonsettling defendants may be unfairly prejudiced. (Rankin v. Curtis (1986) 183 Cal. App. 3d 939, 945.)

 

Discussion

 

Byun moves for an order determining that that the settlement entered into between him, Plaintiff, and CMI was entered in good faith. This motion is unopposed.

 

“[O]nly when the good faith nature of a settlement is disputed, it is incumbent upon the trial court to consider and weigh the Tech-Bilt factors. That is to say, when no one objects, the barebones motion which sets forth the ground of good faith, accompanied by a declaration which sets forth a brief background of the case is sufficient.” (City of Grand Terrace v. Superior Court (1987) 192 Cal.App.3d 1251, 1261.)

 

Byun states that this action arises out of an electrocution incident on June 17, 2021, when Plaintiff came into contact with an electrified fence located in a truck fleet parking lot known as the “Huntington Park Yard.” Byun is the owner of CMI which uses the Huntington Park Yard to run a scrap metal business. Byun provides a copy of the Settlement and Release Agreement (Agreement) entered into between Byun, CMI, and Plaintiff in which Byun and CMI agree to pay $325,000, which represents over 42% of Plaintiff’s medical specials identified in discovery. (Daley Decl., ¶ 5.) Byun states that this Agreement was reached after engaging in private mediation.  

 

Upon review of the moving papers and declaration and based on the lack of any opposition, the Court finds that this settlement was entered into in good faith.

 

Byun’s motion for an order determining that the settlement entered into between Byun, Plaintiff, and CMI was made in good faith is GRANTED.

 


Case Number: 24NWCV04924    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: P

JOEL CESSNA, ET AL. v.  EDWARD YOUNG, ET AL.

CASE NO.: 24NWCV04924

HEARING: 08/11/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#9

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant Edward Young’s demurrer to the first amended complaint is SUSTAINED with 30 days LEAVE TO AMEND in part, OVERRULED in part, and MOOT in part. See below for a detailed ruling.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant Edward Young (Defendant or Young) demurs to the first amended complaint (FAC).

 

Background

 

On December 18, 2024, Plaintiffs Joel R. Cessna (Cessna) and The Young and Cessna Partnership (the Partnership), a general partnership (Plaintiffs), filed this breach of contract action against Defendants Edward L Young, Sunny Rose Corporation (SRC), Junk King Enterprises, Inc. (JKE), Junk King Franchise Systems, Inc. (JKFS), and Does 1 through 10. On April 16, 2026, following the Court’s ruling on Young’s demurrer to the complaint sustaining the demurrer with leave to amend, Plaintiffs filed the operative FAC.

 

The FAC alleges the following: Young and Cessna have known each other for over thirty years. (FAC, ¶ 13.) In 2011, Young joined JKE and/or JFKS as a franchise owner and encouraged Cessna to invest in the Junk-King franchise (Franchise). (FAC, ¶¶ 14-15.) On June 17, 2011, Young and Cessna executed a partnership agreement (Partnership Agreement) under which the two would be legal partners in the Franchise and by which “Young agreed to sell a 49% working interest in the Franchise to Mr. Cessna for the sum of $250,000.00. Mr. Cessna’s 49% working interest was to be a fully participating partnership interest in every aspect of the Franchise with no exclusions or exceptions of any kind whatsoever.” (FAC, ¶¶ 16-19.) The FAC alleges that since then, Young breached the Partnership Agreement by paying Cessna less than the amount owed, underperforming under the Partnership Agreement, and misrepresenting the nature of Cessna’s investment. (FAC, ¶¶ 26-46.) Prior to the formation of the Partnership Agreement and without Cessna’s knowledge, Young incorporated SRC. (FAC, ¶ 22, 25.) The FAC alleges an alter ego relationship between Young and SRC. (FAC, ¶¶ 22-25.)

 

The FAC asserts nine causes of action: (1) breach of contract, (2) breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (3) violation of Corporations Code section 25401, (4) breach of fiduciary duty, (5) violation of Penal Code section 496, (6) accounting, (7) declaratory relief re Cessna’s share of the franchise, (8) declaratory relief re JKE, and (9) declaratory relief re JFKS.

 

This is a continuation. On June 30, 2026, this demurrer first came on for hearing. The Court continued this demurrer to today’s date and ordered the parties to meet and confer. On July 16, 2026, the parties filed a Joint Stipulation regarding their meet and confer process.

 

Legal Standard

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) In assessing a demurrer, the court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of law.” (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.” (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at p. 747.) A complaint will be upheld against a demurrer if it pleads facts sufficient to place the defendant on notice of the issues sufficient to enable the defendant to prepare a defense. (Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 549-50.)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (f) provides for a demurrer when a pleading is uncertain. Demurrers for uncertainty are disfavored and are only sustained where a pleading is so incomprehensible a defendant cannot reasonably respond. (A.J. Fistes v. GDL Best Contractors, Inc. (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 677, 695; Khoury v. Maly’s of California (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)  

 

“Generally it is an abuse of discretion to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment.” (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) A “[p]laintiff must show in what manner he can amend his complaint and how that amendment will change the legal effect of his pleading.” (Ibid.) 

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that the demurring party meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference at least five days before the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) The demurring party shall file and serve a declaration stating either: (a) “The means by which the demurring party met and conferred…and that the parties did not reach an agreement resolving the objections raised in the demurrer” or (b) “[t]hat the party who filed the pleading subject to demurrer failed to respond to the meet and confer request…or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).)

 

The parties have adequately met and conferred. (Joint Decl.)

 

Discussion

 

Defendant demurs to the FAC on the grounds of standing, that the claims are barred by the statute of limitations, and that the statutory claims are improper. The Court proceeds to rule on the demurrer in the order the arguments are raised in the demurrer.

 

          Standing

 

Defendant demurs to the FAC on the grounds that Plaintiffs fail to allege facts to support Cessna’s standing to bring claims belonging to the Partnership.

 

Individual partners may not sue for damage to the partnership property or to their individual “beneficial interest” in the property. (Mayer v. C.W. Driver (2002) 98 Cal.App.4th 48, 60.) But a partner may maintain a derivative suit on behalf of the partnership where the partnership itself fails or refuses to sue. (Everest Investors 8 [Everest] v. McNeil Partners (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 411, 425.) “An action is derivative, that is, in the corporate right, “ ‘ “if the gravamen of the complaint is injury to the corporation, or to the whole body of its stock and property without any severance or distribution among individual holders, or it seeks to recover assets for the corporation or to prevent the dissipation of its assets.”’” (Ibid.) “California cases recognize that a stockholder's individual suit “ ‘ “is a suit to enforce a right against the corporation which the stockholder possesses as an individual.”’ ” (Id., at p. 426.)

 

Here, as argued by Defendant, the FAC does not allege that Cessna is proceeding derivatively as it does not allege a demand or excuse. The FAC seeks recovery on behalf of both Plaintiffs. The Court agrees that there are issues of standing as Plaintiffs, Cessna and the Partnership, are simultaneously bringing claims in the same action. Cessna cannot bring claims that belong to the Partnership without pleading derivative standing prerequisites.

 

Plaintiffs do not appear to address standing in their opposition except to argue that they plead claims in the alternative. While pleading claims in the alternative is permitted, this does not excuse the requirements for establishing standing. Plaintiffs also fail to allege or argue regarding the gravamen of their claims for standing purposes.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer is SUSTAINED with 30 days LEAVE TO AMEND as to Cessna’s standing to bring claims.

 

Statute of Limitations

 

Defendant argues that the FAC shows that Plaintiffs’ claims are time-barred under the statute of limitations.

 

“The statute of limitations usually commences when a cause of action ‘accrues,’ and it is generally said that ‘an action accrues on the date of injury.’ [Citation.] Alternatively, it is often stated that the statute commences ‘upon the occurrence of the last element essential to the cause of action.’” (Bernson v. Browning-Ferris Indus. (1994) 7 Cal. 4th 926, 931.) “A plaintiff whose complaint shows on its face that his claim would be barred without the benefit of the discovery rule must specifically plead facts to show (1) the time and manner of discovery and (2) the inability to have made earlier discovery despite reasonable diligence. The burden is on the plaintiff to show diligence, and conclusory allegations will not withstand demurrer.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Services (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1319.) Under the delayed discovery rule, a cause of action accrues at the time when the cause of action is complete with all its elements. (Fox v. Ethicon Endo-Surgery, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 797, 806 807.) The test for when the statute of limitations on a claim starts to run under the delayed discovery rule is “whether the plaintiff has information of circumstances sufficient to put a reasonable person on inquiry, or has the opportunity to obtain knowledge from sources open to his or her investigation.” (McGee v. Weinberg (1979) 97 Cal.App.3d 798, 803.)

 

The FAC alleges Defendant and Cessna entered into the Agreement on June 17, 2011. (FAC, ¶ 16.) The FAC alleges Cessna made two contributions and that “as soon as Mr. Cessna made his initial contributions, Young began breaching the material terms of the Agreement, excusing Mr. Cessna from further performing under the Agreement.” (FAC, ¶¶ 26-30.) The FAC alleges that “[f]rom 2017 through 2020, Young paid Mr. Cessna $202,400.00 in five installments.” (FAC, ¶ 31.) The FAC alleges Defendant issued Cessna a check in September of 2022 which did not clear. (FAC, ¶ 33.) Additionally, “[i]n May 2024, Young finally provided Mr. Cessna with records detailing the Franchise’s finances—after years of stonewalling and refusing to provide Mr. Cessna with any information regarding the Franchise’s finances, profits, or ongoing business.” (FAC, ¶ 37.) The FAC alleges that Cessna did not discover and did not know facts to lead him to “to suspect the degree of harm that Young had caused Mr. Cessna and the Partnership through his continuous breach of the Agreement.” (FAC, ¶ 39.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs again argue that because the breaches are continuing, they have not accrued. Plaintiffs rely on Aryeh v. Canon Business Solutions, Inc. (2013) 55 Cal.4th 1185, 1191 for this proposition. The FAC alleges “Young breached and continues to breach the Agreement by unilaterally making purchases and acquisitions for the Franchise without consulting Mr. Cessna or obtaining his consent”; “Young breached and continues to breach the Agreement by intentionally concealing and withholding the Franchise’s profits from Mr. Cessna”; and “Young also breached and continues to breach the Agreement by stonewalling Mr. Cessna from all the Franchise’s business decisions.” (FAC, ¶¶ 61-63.) Cessna notes that the franchise records he obtained in 2024 showed the stonewalling. (FAC, ¶ 63.)

 

As explained in the prior demurrer, Plaintiffs’ reliance on “continuing violations” does not cure prior any statute of limitations issues for prior breaches. However, the Court finds that on the face of the complaint, Plaintiffs allege facts to put Plaintiffs on notice of the claims in May of 2024, upon discovery of the stonewalling, or possibly September of 2022, upon the check. The FAC alleges Plaintiffs’ inability to discover the relevant facts based on Defendant’s stonewalling prior to May of 2024. (FAC, ¶ 63.) The statute of limitations for actions based upon a contract is four years. (Code Civ. Proc., § 337.) As this action was commenced in December of 2024, the Court finds that on the face of the pleading, using the September of 2022 or May of 2024 dates of discovery, Plaintiff’s contractual claims are timely.

 

Defendants’ demurrer is OVERRULED as to arguments regarding statute of limitations.

 

          Fifth Cause of Action – Penal Code Section 496

 

Defendant’s arguments under Penal Code section 496 are moot. The parties’ joint separate statement provides: “Defendant Young has determined that he no longer requests a ruling on the Fifth Cause of Action for Violation of Penal Code section 496. The parties agree that the Fifth Cause of Action is expressly pled against Defendant Sunny Rose Corporation, and not against Defendant Edward L. Young.” (Joint Decl., ¶ 4.)

 

Defendant’s demurrer to the third cause of action is MOOT.

 

          Third Cause of Action – Violation of Corporations Code Section 25401

 

Pursuant to Corporations Code section 25401: “It is unlawful for any person to offer or sell a security in this state, or to buy or offer to buy a security in this state, by means of any written or oral communication that includes an untrue statement of a material fact or omits to state a material fact necessary to make the statements made, in the light of the circumstances under which the statements were made, not misleading.” Corporations Code section 25019 defines “security.”

 

Defendant demurs to the third cause of action on the grounds that the FAC fails to allege facts to establish that the transaction at issue involved a security.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argues that the FAC alleges Young sold and continues to sell a security by way of a written communication that includes an untrue statement of material fact. (FAC, ¶¶ 90-92.)

 

The Court finds that the FAC adequately alleges that the Agreement is a security. The FAC alleges that “[t]he Agreement is an investment contract. Mr. Cessna’s sole obligation under the Agreement is to provide Young with start-up funds to launch the Franchise. In return for his investment in the Franchise, the Agreement entitles Mr. Cessna to a 49% working interest in the Franchise.” (FAC, ¶ 91.) Moreover, the FAC alleges a “security” through evidence of indebtedness and, alternatively, “the extent that Young never intended to form the Partnership: Young sold a security by way of a written communication that includes an untrue statement of fact because he fraudulently secured a loan for SRC from Mr. Cessna while misrepresenting the loan as a 49% working interest as a partner in the Franchise in the Agreement.” (FAC, ¶¶ 95-96.) Therefore, on the face of the pleading, the FAC adequately alleges a security. (See Corp. Code, § 25019 [“ ‘Security’ means any  note; stock; treasury stock; membership in an incorporated or unincorporated association;…evidence of indebtedness;… investment contract;…”].)

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer to the third cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

          Declaratory Relief Causes of Action

 

Defendant demurs to the seventh, eighth, and ninth causes of action for declaratory relief on the grounds that the claims are improper and duplicative.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1060 provides: “Any person interested under a written instrument…or under a contract, or who desires a declaration of his or her rights or duties with respect to another, or in respect to, in…may, in cases of actual controversy relating to the legal rights and duties of the respective parties, bring an original action or cross-complaint in the superior court for a declaration of his or her rights and duties in the premises, including a determination of any question of construction or validity arising under the instrument or contract.” A cause of action for declaratory relief should not be used as a second cause of action for the determination of identical issues raised in another cause of action. (General of America Insurance Co. v. Lilly (1968) 258 Cal.App.2d 465, 470.)

 

As to the seventh cause of action the FAC seeks a declaration regarding Cessna’s share under the Agreement. (FAC, ¶ 137.) As to the eighth cause of action, the FAC seeks a declaration as to whether Young committed securities fraud or engaged in illicit business practices while operating a franchise license issued by JKE. (FAC, ¶ 141.) For the ninth cause of action, the FAC seeks a declaration as to whether Young committed securities fraud or engaged in illicit business practices while operating a franchise license issued by JKFS. (FAC, ¶ 145.)

 

The Court finds that the declaratory relief causes of action are adequately pled and seek judicial declarations arising out of the Agreement. On the face of the FAC, Plaintiffs allege ongoing damages as the partnership remains undissolved, and therefore, the declaratory relief claims do not only seek determinations of past wrongdoing.

 

Therefore, Defendant’s demurrer as to the declaratory relief causes of action is OVERRULED.

 

          Uncertainty

 

Defendant lastly demurs to the complaint on he grounds that it is uncertain and internally inconsistent because it pleads inconsistent theories of liability. Specifically, Defendant argues that the alternative theories, “that no partnership was ever formed, that a partnership was formed and invested in a corporation, and that Defendants stole the investment altogether” are conflicting such that it renders the FAC uncertain. (Dem., 9:22-23.)

 

The Court does not agree that the alternative pleading here renders the action “so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (A.J. Fistes Corp., supra, 38 Cal.App.5th at p. 695.) The Court does not sustain the demurrer based on uncertainty.

 

In sum, Defendant’s demurrer to the FAC is SUSTAINED with 30 days LEAVE TO AMEND in part, OVERRULED in part, and MOOT in part.

 

The demurrer is SUSTAINED with 30 days LEAVE TO AMEND as to the issue of Cessna’s standing and derivative liability.

 

The demurrer is OVERRULED as to the statute of limitations and the third, seventh, eighth, and ninth causes of action.

 

The demurrer is MOOT as to the fifth cause of action.
