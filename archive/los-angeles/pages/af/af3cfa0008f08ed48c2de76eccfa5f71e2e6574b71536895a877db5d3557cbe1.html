BACKGROUND

This is a negligence action. On July 4, 2021, plaintiff Leticia Garcia (Plaintiff) allegedly sustained an injury at a Whole Foods store when riding down an inclined moving walkway. On July 3, 2023, Plaintiff filed a complaint against defendants Whole Foods Market California Inc., Manager Blanca, and DOE Defendants 1-50 including defendant Gatekeeper Systems, Inc. (Defendant Gatekeeper) alleging general negligence.

On June 24, 2024, Defendant Schindler Elevator Corporation (Defendant Schindler) filed a Cross-Complaint against Defendant Gatekeeper, P&H Casters, and Glide Rite Corporation seeking Equitable Indemnity, Declaratory Relief, and Contribution.

On June 8, 2026, Defendant Gatekeeper filed the instant motion for summary judgment (Res ID: 3606) against Plaintiff as to the initial Complaint and Defendant Schindler as to the Cross-Complaint. On August 28, 2026, Defendant Schindler filed an opposition. On September 2, 2026, Defendant Gatekeeper filed a reply. Plaintiff has not filed an opposition as of September 16, 2026.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

In reviewing a motion for summary judgment or adjudication, courts must apply a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent’s claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.”¿(Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.)

            “[T]he initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facia showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.”¿(Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.)¿When a plaintiff seeks summary judgment, the plaintiff must produce admissible evidence on each element of each cause of action on which judgment is sought. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(p)(1).) The plaintiff is not required to disprove any affirmative defenses to discharge this burden. (Oldcastle Precast, Inc. v. Lumbermens Mutual Casualty Co. (2009) 170 Cal.App.4th 554, 564-65.)¿

If the moving party fails to carry its burden, the inquiry is over, and the motion must be denied. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2); see also Consumer Cause, Inc. v. SmileCare (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 454, 468.)¿Even if the moving party does carry its burden, the non-moving party will still defeat the motion by presenting evidence of a triable issue of material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 849-50.) The supporting evidence can be in the form of affidavits, declarations, admissions, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and matters of which judicial notice may be taken.¿(Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 855.)

            “Once the [moving party] … has met that burden, the burden shifts to the [non-moving party] … to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) The non-moving party may not merely rely on allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists, but instead, “shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action.”¿(Ibid.)¿“If the [non-moving party] cannot do so, summary judgment should be granted.”¿(Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467.)

The court must “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve all doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party,” including “all inferences reasonably drawn therefrom.”¿(Yanowitz v. L’Oreal USA, Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1037; Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at pp. 844-45.) “On a summary judgment motion, the court must therefore consider what inferences favoring the opposing party a factfinder could reasonably draw from the evidence.¿While viewing the evidence in this manner, the court must bear in mind that its primary function is to identify issues rather than to determine issues. [Citation.] Only when the inferences are indisputable may the court decide the issues as a matter of law.¿ If the evidence is in conflict, the factual issues must be resolved by trial.”¿(Binder v. Aetna Life Ins. Co. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 832, 839.)¿Further, “the trial court may not weigh the evidence in the manner of a factfinder to determine whose version is more likely true. [Citation.] Nor may the trial court grant summary judgment based on the court’s evaluation of credibility. [Citation.]” (Id. at p. 840; see also Weiss v. People ex rel. Department of Transportation (2020) 9 Cal.5th 840, 864 [“Courts deciding motions for summary judgment or summary adjudication may not weigh the evidence but must instead view it in the light most favorable to the opposing party and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of that party”].)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Summary Judgment on Complaint

Defendant Gatekeeper seeks summary judgment against Plaintiff on the grounds that Plaintiff cannot establish negligence and cannot show breach or causation. (Mot., pp. 2-3.)

The Court acknowledges that Defendant Gatekeeper references premises liability, however Defendant Gatekeeper is DOE 5, against whom Plaintiff does not assert premises liability. Thus, that argument is moot. (Mot., p. 3.)

            In order to state a claim for negligence, Plaintiff must allege the elements of (1) “the existence of a legal duty of care,” (2) “breach of that duty,” and (3) “proximate cause resulting in an injury.” (McIntyre v. Colonies-Pacific, LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 664, 671.)

Movant’s Burden

            Defendant Gatekeeper argues that they only had limited involvement because they designed and manufactured one wheel on each shopping cart, the wheel locking component. (Mot., p. 10.) Defendant Gatekeeper argues the wheel was pre-installed by The Peggs Company before the carts arrived at the subject Whole Foods location, and carts with the pre-installed wheel were tested after delivery and before opening. (Ibid.) Further, Defendant Gatekeeper alleges that the subject cart was not preserved or turned over to them for inspection after the incident, so Plaintiff cannot establish any breach of causation involving the wheel. (Mot., pp. 10-11.) Additionally, Defendant Gatekeeper argues that Whole Foods stated it is not aware of any written agreement between them regarding the wheel locking mechanism and does not accuse Defendant Gatekeeper of being responsible for inspecting them. (Mot., pp. 11.) Defendant Gatekeeper argues that Plaintiff cannot establish causation through speculation about a wheel on the subject shopping cart. (Mot., p. 20.)

            Defendant Gatekeeper presents discovery responses in support of their motion for summary judgment against Plaintiff. (See Compendium.)

            Here, Defendant Gatekeeper essentially argues that since the subject shopping cart has not been provided, Plaintiff cannot possibly establish Defendant Gatekeeper breached a duty tied to the wheel locking mechanism they admittedly designed and manufactured. (Greenberg Decl., ¶ 8.) The Court is not persuaded by Defendant Gatekeeper’s argument. At this time triable issues of fact remain as to whether Defendant Gatekeeper’s wheel was involved in the subject incident. Defendant Gatekeeper provides the deposition of Plaintiff in which Plaintiff states that the shopping cart caught a bump “at the bottom of the conveyor” to establish their wheel was not responsible. (Feldman Decl., ¶ 14, Ex. 12, Garcia Depo., p. 205.) However, Defendant Gatekeeper also provides Defendant Schindler’s responses to special interrogatories in which they assert that the incident may have been caused by wheels on the shopping cart. (Feldman Decl., ¶ 7, Ex. 5, No. 21.) This contradiction in itself shows that a triable issue of fact remains. Thus, Defendant Gatekeeper has not met their burden.

Accordingly, the motion for summary judgment as to the initial Complaint is DENIED.

Summary Judgment on Cross Complaint

            Defendant Gatekeeper seeks summary judgment as to Defendant Schindler’s entire cross-complaint on the grounds that it is derivative of the alleged tort liability to Plaintiff. (Mot., p. 24.)

“To state a claim for equitable indemnity, a defendant must allege the same harm for which he may be held liable is properly attributable—at least in part—to the cross-defendant.” (Platt v. Coldwell Banker Residential Real Estate Services (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 1439, 1445, fn.7.)

“To qualify for declaratory relief, a party would have to demonstrate its action presented two essential elements: (1) a proper subject of declaratory relief, and (2) an actual controversy involving justiciable questions relating to the party’s rights or obligations.” (Jolley v. Chase Home Finance, LLC (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 872, 909, quotation marks and brackets omitted.)

A claim for contribution requires allegations of (1) “a money judgment,” (2) “rendered jointly against two or more defendants in a tort action,” (3) “in accordance with the principles of equity,” (4) “after one tortfeasor has, by payment, discharged the joint judgment or has paid more than his pro rata share thereof,” (5) without intentional injury by the tortfeasor. (Code Civ. Proc., § 875, subds. (a)-(d).)

Defendant Gatekeeper argues that these claims fail because they require an underlying liability, which they argue is absent. (Mot., p. 24.) Defendant Gatekeeper argues that because they had no involvement in Plaintiff’s injuries and no underling liability, Defendant Schindler’s claims fail as a matter of law. (Mot., p. 26.)

As discussed above, Defendant Gatekeeper has not established that they are completely absolved of liability to Plaintiff in the underlying Complaint. At this stage it would be premature to grant summary judgment because triable issues of fact remain as to liability for Plaintiff’s injuries.

Accordingly, the motion for summary judgment as to the Cross-Complaint is DENIED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant Gatekeeper Systems, Inc.’s motion for summary judgment is DENIED.

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Defendant Schindler seeks summary judgment against Plaintiff as to the cause of action for general negligence on the grounds that Plaintiff cannot establish breach or causation. (Mot., p. 4.)

            In order to state a claim for negligence, Plaintiff must allege the elements of (1) “the existence of a legal duty of care,” (2) “breach of that duty,” and (3) “proximate cause resulting in an injury.” (McIntyre v. Colonies-Pacific, LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 664, 671.)

Movant’s Burden

            Defendant Schindler argues that Plaintiff cannot prove that they breached their duty to maintain the walkway or that such breach caused her injuries. (Mot., p. 4.) Defendant Schindler concedes that its sole duty arises from its contractual obligation to maintain the subject walkway but argues there is no evidence that Defendant Schindler breached this duty. (Mot., p. 5.) Defendant Schindler argues that they performed the required periodic routine maintenance, inspection, and repairs on the subject walkway and it had been deemed code compliant. (Ibid.) Defendant Schindler argues that since they sufficiently maintained the subject walkway, any allegation that failure to do so caused Plaintiff’s injuries is unsupported. (Mot., p. 6.) Further, Defendant Schindler argues that Plaintiff’s claim that they failed to warn Plaintiff of a dangerous condition fails because Defendant Schindler did not control the premises. (Ibid.)  Defendant Schindler argues that it was the responsibility of Whole Foods Market to monitor the equipment and warn customers of potential safety hazards. (Mot., p. 7.)

            Defendant Schindler presents the last work report before the incident to show no repairs were completed because there was no indication of hazards. (Compendium Exh. E.) Further Defendant Schindler presents the deposition of Schindler representative Denis Davis to prove that Whole Foods Market did not reach out via work order and did not request any repairs due to shopping carts locking on the subject walkway. (Compendium, Exh. C.)

            Here, Defendant Schindler essentially argues that a service report reflecting a lack of repairs is definitive proof establishing they did not breach their duty to maintain the subject walkway. The Court has reviewed the service reports provided and finds that there is not sufficient information establishing a maintenance issue did not exist. The report prior to the date of injury, dated May 21, 2021, describes preventative maintenance being done in the form of a routine visual inspection of the equipment. (Compendium, Exh. E, p. 7.)

The Court is not persuaded by Defendant Schindler’s argument that because no repairs were done nearly two months prior to the incident, they could not have possible breached their duty to maintain the subject walkway. At this time triable issues of fact remain as to whether a maintenance issue actually existed within the subject walkway. It is possible that Defendant Schindler’s technician simply did not see it. Moreover, Defendant Schindler states they had an agreement in place with Whole Foods Market, imposing duty, which required them to make monthly maintenance visits. (Compendium, Exh. B, p. 20.) However, service reports provided to the Court do not reflect maintenance being done on a monthly basis. (See Compendium Exh. E.) There is no report provided for April 2021, or more notably, June or July of 2021 just before the incident. (Id.) This inconsistency further calls into question whether Defendant Schindler breached their duty or not. As a result, triable issues of fact remain as to whether a possible lapse in maintenance caused Plaintiff’s injury. Thus, Defendant Schindler has not met their burden.

Accordingly, the motion for summary judgment is DENIED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant Schindler Elevator Corp.’s motion for summary judgment is DENIED.

Ruling: GRANT.

The Court is not requesting oral argument on this matter/motion.  The Court is guided by California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1308(a)(1) whereby notice of intent to appear is requested.  Unless the Court directs argument in the Tentative Ruling, no argument is requested and ANY PARTY SEEKING ARGUMENT SHOULD NOTIFY ALL OTHER PARTIES AND THE COURT BY 3:30 P.M. ON THE COURT DAY BEFORE THE HEARING of the party’s intention to argue as to this matter/motion.  The tentative ruling will become the ruling of the court if no argument is received.   Notice may be given either by email at BurDeptB@LACourt.org or by telephone at (818) 260-8422.

Parties must appear on all other matters scheduled for this case.

BACKGROUND

This is a premises liability action. On May 23, 2025, plaintiffs Timothy Scott Crabaugh, Dawn Crabaugh, and the Estate of Timothy Crabaugh by and through Amy Crabaugh filed a Second Amended Complaint (SAC) against defendants DBAC, Inc., Mike Parks, Custom Iron Corporation, Matt Fox, Mike Knee, David Spring, and Efrain M. Ortiz (collectively, Defendants). Plaintiff Dawn Crabaugh has since dismissed her action against Defendants; the only remaining plaintiff is the Estate of Timothy Crabaugh by and through Amy Crabaugh (Plaintiff).

On June 23, 2026, Defendants DBAC, Inc. and Mike Park filed the instant motion seeking terminating sanctions against Plaintiff. As of September 16, 2026, Plaintiff has not filed an opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure § 2023.030 gives the court the discretion to impose sanctions against anyone engaging in a misuse of the discovery process. Misuse of the discovery process includes failure to respond to an authorized method of discovery or disobeying a court order to provide discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subds. (d), (g).) A court may impose escalating sanctions where a party disobeys a discovery order. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.450(h).) A court may impose terminating sanctions by striking pleadings of the party engaged in misuse of discovery or entering default judgment. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (d).) A violation of a discovery order is sufficient for the imposition of terminating sanctions. (Collison & Kaplan v. Hartunian (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 1611, 1620.) Terminating sanctions are appropriate when a party persists in disobeying the court’s orders. (Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 795-796.)

The court should consider the totality of the circumstances, including conduct of the party to determine if the actions were willful, the detriment to the propounding party, and the number of formal and informal attempts to obtain discovery. (Lang v. Hochman (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1225, 1246.) If a lesser sanction fails to curb abuse, a greater sanction is warranted. (Van Sickle v. Gilbert (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1495, 1516.) However, “the unsuccessful imposition of a lesser sanction is not an absolute prerequisite to the utilization of the ultimate sanction.” (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 787.) Before any sanctions may be imposed the court must make an express finding that there has been a willful failure of the party to serve the required answers. (Fairfield v. Superior Court for Los Angeles County (1966) 246 Cal.App.2d 113, 118.) Lack of diligence may be deemed willful where the party understood its obligation, had the ability to comply, and failed to comply. (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 787; Fred Howland Co. v. Superior Court of Los Angeles County (1966) 244 Cal.App.2d 605, 610-611.) The party who failed to comply with discovery obligations has the burden of showing that the failure was not willful. (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 788; Cornwall v. Santa Monica Dairy Co. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 250; Evid. Code, §§ 500, 605.)

A terminating sanction is a “drastic measure which should be employed with caution.” (Deyo, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 793.) “A decision to order terminating sanctions should not be made lightly. But where a violation is willful, preceded by a history of abuse, and the evidence shows that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with the discovery rules, the trial court is justified in imposing the ultimate sanction.” (Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsystem (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 262, 279-280.) While the court has discretion to impose terminating sanctions, these sanctions “should be appropriate to the dereliction and should not exceed that which is required to protect the interests of the party entitled to but denied discovery.” (Deyo, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 793.) “[A] court is empowered to apply the ultimate sanction against a litigant who persists in the outright refusal to comply with his discovery obligations.” (Ibid.

DISCUSSION

Terminating Sanctions                                                                                  

Defendants seek an order imposing terminating sanctions against Plaintiff for her repeated failure to appear for deposition and pay monetary sanctions. (Mot., p. 2.)

            Defendants argue that Plaintiff has failed to appear for deposition in this action despite three valid deposition notices and extensive meet and confer efforts. (Mot., p. 2.) Defendants also note that Plaintiff has failed to file oppositions and appear at noticed hearings. (Mot., pp. 3-4.) Defendants argue that they are entitled to take the deposition of Plaintiff as it is critical to the claims of the estate. (Mot., p. 5.) Defendants argue that they have met and conferred with Plaintiff on several occasions, and Plaintiff still has failed to appear or engage. (Ibid.) Defendants argue that the refusal to participate without justification is an abuse of the discovery process deserving of terminating sanctions. (Mot., pp. 6-7.) Defendants argue that Plaintiff has clearly displayed no interest in pursuing this litigation. (Mot., p. 8.)

Here, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s violations were willful. Defendants have served notices of depositions on Plaintiff on three separate occasions, August 1, 2025, November 18, 2025, and April 27, 2026, and Plaintiff has failed to appear at all three scheduled depositions. (O’Hara Decl., ¶ 3.) Further, Defense counsel declares they have met and conferred with Plaintiff on several occasions about these depositions and she has responded. (O’Hara Decl., ¶¶ 5-9.) In her correspondence Plaintiff indicated she would respond to Defense counsel at a later date. (O’Hara Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. 6.) However, Defendants subsequently filed a motion to compel deposition, and Plaintiff did not oppose the motion or appear at the noticed hearing. (O’Hara Decl., ¶ 12.) Plaintiff has shown that she is aware of these communications and willfully disregarding Defendants’ communications and the Court’s orders.

Since August of 2025, Plaintiff has refused to appear for Court ordered depositions, noticed hearings, disregarded the discovery process, failed to pay ordered sanctions, and failed to respond to any communications from Defendants’ counsel. (O’Hara Decl., ¶¶ 3, 12, 16.) Thus, the Court finds the repeated violations a willful abandonment of the litigation process.

The burden then shifts to Plaintiff to show the failures were not willful. (Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 788; Cornwall v. Santa Monica Dairy Co. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 250; Evid. Code, §§ 500, 605.) Plaintiff filed no opposition and therefore made no showing that the repeated failures to appear were not willful.

California law favors an incremental approach to sanctions; the Court has imposed sanctions on Plaintiff one time. (O’Hara Decl., ¶ 12.) However, Defendants represent that they have not communicated with Plaintiff since September of 2025. This displays an abandonment of the litigation process.  

Accordingly, the motion for terminating sanctions is GRANTED.

Monetary Sanctions

In addition to terminating sanctions, Defendants request monetary sanctions in the amount of $3,468.20 reflecting attorney’s fees, motion filing fees, and deposition costs in connection with this action. (Mot., p. 7.) Defense counsel declares spending 10.8 hours drafting and preparing the instant motion, an anticipated reply, and attending the hearing at a rate of $305/hour. (O’Hara Decl., ¶ 15.) Defense counsel also cites filing fee of $60 and court reporter costs in the amount of $114.20. (Ibid.)

Code of Civil Procedure § 2023.030 gives the court the discretion to impose sanctions against anyone engaging in a misuse of the discovery process. Misuse of the discovery process includes failure to respond to an authorized method of discovery or disobeying a court order to provide discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subds. (d), (g).)

The Court, in its discretion, will reduce the monetary sanctions as follows: $1,220 reflecting 4 hours at a rate of $305/hour, plus a $60 filing fee for the instant motion, and a court-reporter fee of $750. Defendants do not provide the Court with invoices for the cited court reporter costs. This results in a total of $2,030 in monetary sanctions against Plaintiff.

Accordingly, the request for monetary sanctions is GRANTED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendants DBAC, Inc. and Mike Parks’s motion for terminating sanctions is GRANTED.

Defendants’ request for sanctions is GRANTED. Plaintiff is ordered to pay Defendant in the amount of $2,030 within 30 days from this date.

Responding Party: Defendant FCA US LLC 

Notice: Sufficient

Ruling: GRANT.

 

NOTICE

The Court is not requesting oral argument on this matter/motion.  The Court is guided by California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1308(a)(1) whereby notice of intent to appear is requested.  Unless the Court directs argument in the Tentative Ruling, no argument is requested and ANY PARTY SEEKING ARGUMENT SHOULD NOTIFY ALL OTHER PARTIES AND THE COURT BY 3:30 P.M. ON THE COURT DAY BEFORE THE HEARING of the party’s intention to argue as to this matter/motion.  The tentative ruling will become the ruling of the court if no argument is received.   Notice may be given either by email at BurDeptB@LACourt.org or by telephone at (818) 260-8422.

Parties must appear on all other matters scheduled for this case.

BACKGROUND

This is a lemon law case. On February 21, 2025, plaintiff Meghmik Meg Ghazarian (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against defendant FCA US LLC (Defendant) asserting violations of the Song-Beverly Act arising from the purchase of a 2023 Jeep Wrangler. On August 22, 2026, the matter was settled.

On March 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion seeking payment of attorney’s fees and costs pursuant to the settlement. On September 9, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition. On September 11, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.   

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A prevailing party is entitled to recover costs, including attorneys’ fees, as a matter of right. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1032(a)(4), 1032(b), 1033.5.)¿¿

The fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily begins with the “lodestar” method, i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate. A computation of time spent on a case and the reasonable value of that time is fundamental to a determination of an appropriate attorneys’ fee award. The lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49.)¿ Such an approach anchors the trial court’s analysis to an objective determination of the value of the attorney’s services, ensuring that the amount awarded is not arbitrary. (Id. at 48, n.23.) After the trial court has performed the lodestar calculations, it shall consider whether the total award calculated under all of the circumstances of the case is more than a reasonable amount and, if so, shall reduce the section 1717 award so that it is a reasonable figure. (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095-96.)¿¿

The factors considered in determining the modification of the lodestar include the nature and difficulty of the litigation, the amount of money involved, the skill required and employed to handle the case, the attention given, the success or failure, and other circumstances in the case. (EnPalm, LLC v. Teitler Family Trust (2008) 162 Cal. App. 4th 770, 774 (emphasis in original).) A negative modifier was appropriate when duplicative work had been performed. (Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 819.)¿¿

“The reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work.” (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.) “The experienced trial judge is the best judge of the value of professional services rendered in [her] court.” (Ibid.) “A trial court assessing attorney fees begins with a touchstone or lodestar figure, based on the ‘careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney ... involved in the presentation of the case.” (Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1321.) “The reasonableness of attorney fees is within the discretion of the trial court, to be determined from a consideration of such as the nature of the litigation, the complexity of the issues, the experience and expertise of counsel and the amount of time involved. The court may also consider whether the amount requested is based upon unnecessary or duplicative work.” (Wilkerson v. Sullivan (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Plaintiff seeks to recover attorney fees and reimbursement costs from Defendant pursuant to the Song-Beverly Act and the signed CCP 998 settlement offer. (Mot., p. 2.) Plaintiff seeks a total lodestar figure of $16,677.50 consisting of 37.2 hours. (Kamana Decl., ¶¶ 18-20.) Plaintiff does not seek a multiplier; the Court will not award one. Plaintiff indicates reserving their plans to submit a memorandum of costs but as of September 14, 2026, has not done so. (Reply, p. 2.) Accordingly, the Court will not award a multiplier or costs.

Prevailing Party

Plaintiff argues they are entitled to attorney’s fees because the matter resulted in settlement and the 998 states that Plaintiff is the prevailing party for purposes of the anticipated fee motion. (Mot., pp. 4-5.)

The Court finds—and Defendant does not dispute—that Plaintiff is entitled to recover attorney’s fees and costs from Defendant pursuant to the Song-Beverly Act and the parties’ settlement. (Opp., p. 2.) Under the Song-Beverly Act, “[if] the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” (Civ. Code, § 1794(d).)

Hourly Rates

Plaintiff seeks an hourly rate for the following individuals: (1) $500/hour for attorney Fonseca-Kamana; (2) $425/hour for attorney Gozalez; and (3) $300/hour for attorney Flores. (Kamana Decl., ¶¶ 15-17.)

Plaintiff’s primary support for counsel’s hourly rates is their corresponding years of experience and the firm’s reputation in the area of Lemon Law. (Mot., pp. 7-8.)  

In opposition, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s desired rates are inflated. (Opp., p. 2.) Defendant argues that this matter was not complex, especially for attorneys claiming to be experts in the field and does not justify exorbitant rates. (Ibid.) Defendant argues that the court should apply the logic found in the Real Rate Report, and cap partner rates at $500/hour and associate rates at $200/hour. (Opp., p. 5.) Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s associate counsel does not have the experience to justify a $300/rate. (Ibid.)

In reply, Plaintiff argues that their rates are reasonable in light of skill, experience, and reputation in the community. (Reply, p. 2.) Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s proposed rates are too generalized and does not take these factors into account. (Reply, p. 3.)

The Court has considered the declaration of Michelle Fonseca-Kamana. (Kamana Decl., ¶¶ 15-17.) The Court has also reviewed the invoice provided by counsel. (Kamana Decl., Exh. B.) In light of the experience of Plaintiff’s legal staff, the Court deems the following hourly rates reasonable: (1) $500/hour for attorney and partner Fonseca-Kamana; (2) $300/hour for associate attorney Gozalez; and (3) $200/hour for associate attorney Flores.

Hours Expended

Plaintiff seeks a lodestar figure of $16,677.50 ((26.9 hours x $500 rate = $13,450 for attorney Kamana) + (1.1 hours x $425 rate = $467.50 for attorney Gonzalez) + (9.2 hours x $300 rate = $2,760 for attorney Flores)) for a total of 37.2 hours of work. (Kamana Decl., Exh. B.)

Plaintiff argues that the amount sought should be awarded in full because the settlement provides for recovery, Plaintiff’s counsel diligently worked toward resolving the dispute, and Plaintiff’s counsel took the case on contingency. (Mot., p. 8.)

In opposition, Defendant argues that several entries on counsel’s invoice are duplicative and should be reduced. (Opp., pp. 5-8.) Defendant has identified several duplicative entries credited to both parter and associates. (Ibid.) Defendant argues that excessive time spent drafting and duplicative entries for attending the same hearings and reviewing the same documents must be reduced. (Ibid.) Defendant asks the court to award no more than $8,640 in attorney’s fees. (Opp., p. 10.)

In reply, Plaintiff argues that Defendant fails to identify duplicative entries and does not present competent evidence that Plaintiff has cited time not actually spent. (Reply, pp. 3-4.) Plaintiff argues that this case required so many hours because of Defendant’s failure to prioritize this case resulting in lengthy litigation through mediation. (Reply, p. 4.) Plaintiff argues that all entries correspond to time actually expended. (Reply, pp. 4-7.)

The Court is persuaded by Defendant’s argument surrounding duplicative entries. Most notably, the Court finds Plaintiff seeking $2,480 in fees, reflecting 6.2 hours, for two attorneys attending a singular 3.1-hour long mediation excessive and duplicative. (Kamana Decl., Exh. B, pp. 6-7.)

The Court acknowledges arguments made by both parties and, in its discretion, will reduce the lodestar figure to $9,000. The Court finds that Plaintiff’s legal staff reasonably expended a total number of 22 hours to commence and prosecute this action and to prepare the pending motion. Since the matter was settled early, prior to any discovery, and due to the straightforward nature of action, the Court will reduce requested fees of $16,677.50 (37.2 hours) to $9,000 ((15 hours x $500 rate = $7,500 for attorney Kamana) + (1 hours x $300 rate = $300 for attorney Gonzalez) + (6 hours x $200 rate = $1,200 for attorney Flores)) for a total of 22 hours calculated with reduced hourly rates.

Accordingly, the Court finds a lodestar figure of $9,000 reasonable. Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees and Costs is GRANTED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiff Meghmik Meg Ghazarian’s motion for attorney’s fees is GRANTED

BACKGROUND

This is a disability discrimination case. Plaintiff Perla Mageno (Plaintiff) is a visually impaired individual who brought this action against defendant Dang Good! Ice Cream Inc. (Defendant) for its failure to design, maintain, and operate its website to be accessible and independently usable by Plaintiff and other visually impaired individuals with disability.

On May 21, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Complaint against Defendant for Violations of the Unruh Civil Rights Act due to this purported failure.

On July 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion seeking imposition of sanctions. On September 3, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition. On September 11, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.   

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7 authorizes sanctions where a party or attorney presents a paper that is not warranted by existing law, whose factual contentions lack evidentiary support, or that is presented for an improper purpose such as delay. (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7(b).) Sanctions are proper where the filing is one that no reasonable attorney would consider legally tenable, or that is frivolous or filed in bad faith. (Peake v. Underwood (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 428, 440.)

Section 128.7’s safe-harbor provision requires that the motion be served at least 21 days before filing, during which the offending paper may be withdrawn. (§ 128.7, subd. (c)(1).)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

            Plaintiff seeks imposition of sanctions against Defendant, in an amount sufficient to deter repetition, on the grounds that Defendant’s now denied Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings was made based on contradicting factual assertions. (Mot., p. 2.) Plaintiff argues that the motion for judgment on the pleadings alleges that Defendant operates a purely online business with no physical storefront or bring-and-mortar place of public accommodation. (Mot., p. 9) In contradiction, Plaintiff highlights that Defendant’s responses to requests for Admission (RPDs) obtained via discovery admit that Defendant operates a physical and public place of accommodation. (Ibid.) Plaintiff argues that both statements cannot be true, so Defendant either violated section 128.7(b)(3) by presenting RPD responses without factual support or presenting the motion for judgment on the pleadings based on a lack of evidentiary support. (Mot., p. 10.) Plaintiff argues that a reasonable attorney would have recognized this inconsistency before presenting these statements to the Court. (Ibid.) Further, Plaintiff argues that the motion for judgment on the pleadings was presented for an improper purpose based on frivolous legal theory. (Mot., p. 13.)

            In opposition, Defendant argues that Plaintiff attempts to make every unsuccessful motion sanctionable. (Opp., p. 2.) Defendant argues that it admitted to operating a physical wholesale facility in San Diego but denies that it operates a consumer-retail storefront in Rowland Heights or elsewhere. (Ibid.) Defendant argues that this distinction matters and allows both assertions to be true. (Opp., p. 4.) Further, Defendant argues that the motion for judgment on the pleadings was not made for improper purchase or based on frivolous legal theory because it turned on a factual dispute for what is deemed a place of public accommodation. (Opp., p. 5.) Thus, Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not shown sanctionable conduct. (Opp., p. 8.)

            In reply, Plaintiff argues that Defendant does not address the irreconcilable sworn statements. (Reply, p. 2.) Plaintiff cites the Court’s August 7, 2026, Minute Order which identifies this contradiction. (Ibid.) Further, Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s explanation for the inconsistency confirms the sanctionable violation rather than curing it. (Reply, p. 3.) Plaintiff reiterates that the motion for judgment on the pleadings was frivolous and made for an improper purpose. (Reply, pp. 5-6.)    

Here, the Court does not find that the alleged contradictory statements, or the filing of the motion for judgment on the pleadings, justify such sanctions. Also, the Court will not rehash the motion for judgment on the pleadings. The Court in Marriage of Flaherty found that filings should “be held to be frivolous only when it is prosecuted for an improper motive -- to harass the respondent or delay the effect of an adverse judgment -- or when it indisputably has no merit” (In re Marriage of Flaherty (1982) 31 Cal.3d 637, 650.) Despite any criticism Plaintiff may have of Defendant’s litigation approach or strategy, the Court does not find Defendant’s motion for judgment on the pleadings frivolous or based on improper purpose. Further, the Court is persuaded by Defendant’s explanation of nuance between the responses to RPDs and arguments made in the motion for judgment on the pleadings. Defendant can both admit to operating a physical facility and deny operating a place of public accommodation open to the public.

Accordingly, the motion for sanctions is DENIED.   

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiff Perla Mageno’s motion for sanctions is DENIED.

BACKGROUND

This is a personal injury action arising out of a motor vehicle accident which occurred on January 24, 2025. On August 22, 2025, plaintiff Damaris Macias (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against defendants Sona Grigoryan and Goar Grigoryan (collectively, Defendants).

On August 5, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion to compel deposition of Plaintiff. As of September 14, 2026, no opposition has been filed. On September 4, 2026, Defendants filed a notice of non-opposition.  

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A motion to compel deposition “shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).) “A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.)

The Code of Civil Procedure provides: “If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action . . . without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice, the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent's attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (a).)

A motion under subdivision (a) shall comply with both of the following:

(1) The motion shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.

(2) The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b).)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

            Defendants move the Court for an Order compelling Plaintiff to appear and testify at a noticed deposition. (Mot., p. 2.)   

Meet and Confer

            Defendants’ counsel declares that on July 15, 2026, they reached out to Plaintiff’s counsel for a meet and confer regarding Plaintiff’s non-appearance. (Martinez Decl., ¶ 4.) Defendant’s counsel reached out a second time on August 5, 2026, notifying Plaintiff of the intention to file the instant motion. (Ibid.) Defendant’s counsel declares that Plaintiff has not responded as of the filing of the instant motion. (Ibid.)

            Defendants have made multiple attempts to meet and confer in good faith. Plaintiff is admonished to engage in required meet and confer efforts in the future.   

Motion to Compel Deposition

            Defendants argue on March 12, 2026, they served a deposition notice on Plaintiff for a deposition that was set to take place on June 10, 2026. (Mot., p. 4.) Defendants argue that Plaintiff did not appear to the deposition and at no time prior to June 10, 2026, did Plaintiff serve objections, request a continuance, or notify Defendants that she would not attend. (Mot., p. 5.) Defendants argue that they obtained a certificate of non-appearance and incurred related fees. (Mot., p. 3.)  

            Plaintiff has not filed an opposition to challenge non-appearance. Here, Defendants have provided a notice of deposition to Plaintiff and Plaintiff has failed to appear at the noticed deposition or provide objections. (Martinez Decl., ¶¶ 2-3; Exh. A-B.) This constitutes a non-appearance pursuant to section 2025.450(a). (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450(a).)

Accordingly, the motion to compel deposition of Plaintiff is GRANTED.

Sanctions

“If a motion under subdivision (a) is granted, the court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) in favor of the party who noticed the deposition and against the deponent or the party with whom the deponent is affiliated, unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450(g)(1).) 

Defendants argue that Plaintiff has intentionally and willfully misused the discovery process in the refusal to appear and be deposed. (Mot. pp. 5-6.) Thus, Defendants seeks imposition of sanctions in the amount of $1,623.72. (Mot. p. 6.) This reflects (1) $563.72 fee for the certificate of non-appearance incurred on June 10, 2026; (2) $1,000 in attorney’s fees reflecting 4 hours at a rate of $250/hour; and (3) a filing fee of $60.

            Here, the motion to compel deposition has been granted, thus sanctions are warranted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450(g)(1).) The Court will impose sanctions against Plaintiff in the total amount of $1,123.72. This consists of the filing fee and non-appearance fee for which Defendants have provided the invoice an. BACKGROUND

This is a breach of contract action related to an unlawful detainer action. On February 11, 2021, the parties entered a lease agreement for a commercial property. On October 2, 2025, tenant and plaintiff Pacific Dental Services LLC (Plaintiff), filed an initial Complaint against landlords and defendants Magnolia Vineland OPCO LLC and Westwood Financial LLC (Defendants). On July 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Second Amended Complaint (SAC) seeking declaratory relief regarding rights and obligations under the lease and asserting breach of contract.

On October 24, 2025, Defendants filed an unlawful detainer action against Plaintiff. On November 11, 2025, Defendants attempted to relate the instant action to the unlawful detainer action, but the request was denied.

On July 30, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion for trial preference. On September 4, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On September 10, 2026, Defendants filed a reply.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

            Plaintiffs request judicial notice of Exhibit: (A) The Court’s April 16, 2026, Minute Order. The request is granted. (Evid. Code, § 452 (d).)

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 36, subdivision (e), provides, in pertinent part, that “the court may in its discretion grant a motion for preference that is supported by a showing that satisfies the court that the interests of justice will be served by granting this preference”.¿ (Code Civ. Proc. § 36, subd. (e).)

“Upon the granting of such a motion for preference, the court shall set the matter for trial not more than 120 days from that date and there shall be no continuance beyond 120 days from the granting of the motion for preference except for physical disability of a party or a party’s attorney, or upon a showing of good cause stated in the record. Any continuance shall be for no more than 15 days and no more than one continuance for physical disability may be granted to any party.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 36, subd. (f).)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Defendants argue that the Court is justified in granting preference under Code of Civil Procedure section 1062.3, which authorizes expedited trials in declaratory relief actions. (Mot., p. 6; Code Civ. Proc., § 1062.3(a) “actions brought under this chapter…shall be set for trial at the earliest possible date and shall take precedence over all other cases”.) Further, Defendants argue that since the unlawful detainer action has been stayed, this action is the only avenue for Defendants to adjudicate the claims. (Mot., p. 7.) Defendants argue that they continue to suffer financial and prejudicial harm through Plaintiff’s withholding of rent monies as long as this action is delayed and not adjudicated. (Mot., pp. 8-9.) Thus, Defendants argue the matter must receive trial preference in the interest of justice.  

            In opposition, Plaintiff argues that there are no exigent circumstances warranting preferential trial treatment. (Opp., p. 3.) Plaintiff argues that Code of Civil Procedure section 1062.3(a) does not support Defendant’s position because this action is based in breach of contract with an accompanying declaratory relief cause of action, not a sole declaratory relief action. (Ibid.) Plaintiff concedes that Defendants suffer financial harm as articulated but argue that if parties were able to obtain preferential treatment due to financial harm alone, every case in Southern California would be entitled to trial preference. (Ibid.) Further, Plaintiff argues that the Court already weighed the harm suffered by both parties and decided that Plaintiff’s harm outweighs Defendant’s financial harm in deciding to stay the unlawful detainer action. (Opp., p. 4; RJN, Exh. A.) Thus, Plaintiff argues that Defendants do not show that this action requires a preferential trial schedule. (Opp., pp. 4-5.)

            In reply, Defendants reiterate that Plaintiff has withheld $699,047.80 in rent based on its own interpretation of the lease. (Reply, p. 2.) Defendants reiterate that since the unlawful detainer action was stayed, this action is the only way to obtain relief and possession of the property. (Ibid.) Defendants argue that the April 16, 2026, Minute Order was addressing prejudice that comes with staying the unlawful detainer action, not prejudice associated with obtaining trial preference. (Ibid.) Further, Defendants argue that Plaintiff does not dispute the ongoing financial harm Defendants are suffering. (Reply, p. 4.)

            The Court is afforded discretion to grant or deny a preferential trial date “in the interest of justice”. (Code Civ. Proc. § 36(e).) The Court acknowledges the ongoing financial harm Defendants cite as a result of Plaintiffs withholding rent. However, financial harm arising out of contract alone is not a basis for trial preference. (Code Civ. Proc. § 36(e).) The Court is persuaded by Plaintiff’s argument that if every case citing financial harm received trial preference, Court calendars would become even more impacted. While the April 16, 2026, Minute Order was addressing a different question, the Court is also persuaded by its previous finding that Defendant’s financial prejudice is not substantial enough to prevent staying the unlawful detainer action. (See RJN, Ex. A.) The Court must balance its busy calendar with ensuring cases which receive preference absolutely require it.

Accordingly, Defendants’ motion for trial preference is DENIED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendants Magnolia Vineland OPCO LLC and Westwood Financial LLC’s Motion for Trial Preference is DENIED.

BACKGROUND

This is a fraud action. Pro per plaintiff Allen Shay (Plaintiff) filed this Complaint against defendants City of Pasadena and Victor Gordo (collectively, Defendants) for breach of a promise made by mayoral candidate Victor Gordo on the campaign trail. Defendant Gordo allegedly promised he would “bring justice” to a community member that was shot by police in 2020.

On June 22, 2026, Defendants filed the instant demurrer as to all fifteen causes of action raised in Plaintiff’s complaint. As of September 15, 2026, no opposition has been filed.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

            “[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

Further, the court may, upon motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false, or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

            Plaintiff requests judicial notice of Exhibits: (1) Policy 300 of the Pasadena Police Department’s Policy Manual; (2) Chapter 2.303 of the Pasadena Municipal Code; (3) Notice of a Joint Special Meeting of the Public Safety Committee and City Council on May 15, 2023; (4) Minutes of the Joint Special Meeting of the Public Safety Committee and City Council on May 15, 2023; (5) Agenda of a Public Safety Committee Regular Meeting on July 16, 2025; (6) Minutes of a Public Safety Committee Regular Meeting on July 16, 2025; and (7) Diondra Williams et al. v. Dumaguindin et al., Case No. 2:20-cv09042-VAP-AS. The request as to Exhibits 1 and 2 is granted. (Evid. Code, §452(b).) The request as to Exhibits 3-6 is granted. (Evid. Code, §452(c).) The request as to Exhibit 7 is granted. (Evid. Code, §452(d).)

 

DISCUSSION

Meet and Confer

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that before the filing of a demurrer the moving party “shall meet and confer in person or by telephone” with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a).)  The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(2).)  Thereafter, the moving party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(3).) 

Defendants’ counsel declares that on June 17, 2025, they placed a phone call to pro per Plaintiff for the purpose of meeting and conferring regarding the demurrer and Plaintiff expressed disagreement requesting an email outlining the demurrer. (Aaronian Decl., ¶ 3.) Further, Defendants’ counsel declares that on June 19, 2026, they sent an email outlining the grounds for demurrer and the parties have since been unable to resolve the dispute. (Aaronian Decl., ¶ 4.) Plaintiff does not dispute or address meet and confer efforts since there is no opposition.

Based on declaration of Defendants’ counsel, the parties have complied with meet and confer requirements.

Demurrer       

Defendants demur to all fifteen causes of action on the grounds that each cause of action fails to state sufficient facts. (Mot., p. 2.) Further, Defendants argue that all causes of action are barred by statute of limitations and various governmental immunities. (Mot., p. 11.)

Statute of Limitations

            “[No] suit for money or damages may be brought against a public entity on a cause of action for which a claim is required to be presented in accordance with [section 911.2] until a written claim therefor has been presented to the public entity and has been acted upon by the board, or has been deemed to have been rejected by the board”. (Gov. Code, § 945.4.)

            Pursuant to section 911.2(a), a claim relating to a cause of action for death or for injury to person or to personal property …shall be presented not later than six months after the accrual of the cause of action. A claim relating to any other cause of action shall be presented no later than one year after the accrual of the cause of action. (Gov. Code, § 911.2(a).)

            Pursuant to section 950.2, a cause of action against a public employee or former public employee for injury resulting from an act or omission in the scope of his employment as a public employee is barred if an action against the employing public entity is barred. (Gov. Code, § 950.2.)

            Defendants argue that a cause of action accrue for purposes of claim presentation on the same date a similar cause of action against a non-public entity would be deemed to accrue, accordingly a claim against a public entity accrues when it is complete with all of its elements. (Mot., p. 15.) Therefore, Defendants argue that all causes of action accrued in 2020, when Plaintiff alleges Defendants breached their promise by not ensuring “justice”. (Ibid.) Defendants argue that if Plaintiff alleges the promise was made in October 2020, and breached in November 2020, the claim accrued at that moment. (Mot., pp. 15-16.) Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s presentation of the claim on January 12, 2026, was therefore untimely. (Mot., p. 16.) Further, Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s invocation of the discovery rule is meritless. (Ibid.) Plaintiff contends that they did not discover these claims until July 2025, when the City Attorney’s Office conducted a presentation on how AB 392 should be applied. (Ibid.) However, Defendants argue this is a meritless argument because Plaintiff asserted AB 392 was applied incorrectly in November of 2020 and ignorance of the law does not delay claims. (Mot., pp. 16-17.)

Pursuant to Government Code sections 911.2 and 945.4, Plaintiff had until November 2021 to present these claims and did not do so until January 12, 2026. (Comp., ¶ 18, 33.) Plaintiff’s latent discovery approach is inapplicable here because the basis of Plaintiff’s claim is the allegation that Defendants failed to fulfil their promise to “bring justice to Mr. McClain” and terminate the officers involved. (Comp., ¶ 18.) Plaintiff’s causes of action are all based on reliance on this promise and Defendants’ alleged failure to fulfill this promise which Plaintiff was aware of in November 2020. (Ibid.) The basis of Plaintiff’s claims is not the responsibility of the officers involved in the incident under AB 392, which Plaintiff states was addressed in a separate civil matter. (Comp., ¶ 21.) Thus, all fifteen of Plaintiff’s causes of action are time barred. Since the time to present this complaint pursuant to Government Code section 911.2 has long passed, the Court finds amendment will not cure the deficiencies. The Court will not address subsequent arguments made by Defendants regarding each cause of action’s failure to state a claim because Plaintiff cannot circumvent the time bar.

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendants City of Pasadena and Victor Gordo’s demurrer as to all fifteen causes of action in the Complaint are SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

LEGAL STANDARD

“Service of process, under longstanding tradition in our system of justice, is fundamental to any procedural imposition on a named defendant.” (AO Alfa-Bank v. Yakovlev (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 189, 202.) “To establish personal jurisdiction, compliance with statutory procedures for service of process is essential.” (Kremerman v. White (2021) 71 Cal.App.5th 358, 371.) A defendant’s knowledge of the action does not dispense with the statutory requirements for service of summons. (Kappel v. Bartlett (1988) 200 Cal.App.3d 1457, 1466.)

“A defendant, on or before the last day of his or her time to plead or within any further time that the court may for good cause allow,” may move “to quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over him or her.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10(a)(1).) A defendant has 30 days after the service of the summons to file a responsive pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 412.20(a)(3).)

“When a defendant challenges the court’s personal jurisdiction on the ground of improper service of process, ‘the burden is on the plaintiff to prove the existence of jurisdiction by proving, inter alia, the facts requisite to an effective service.’” (Summers v. McClanahan (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 403, 413.) Filing of a proof of service that complies with the applicable statutory requirements creates a rebuttable presumption of proper service. (Am. Express Centurion Bank v. Zara (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 383, 390.)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Defendants argue that service was not attempted at the identified address despite Plaintiff’s process server’s claims. (Mot., p. 3.) Defendants argue there is no such record of the attempts on their surveillance cameras, which are operable, and argue that the process server committed perjury in claiming those 4 attempts, (Mot., p. 3; Brown Decl., ¶¶ 21-25.) Defendants argue the subsequent post and mail order was obtained by fraud due to Plaintiff lying about effectuating due diligence. (Mot., p. 3; Brown Decl., ¶ 26.) Defendant also indicates they have appealed the Court’s decision on a previous motion to quash. (Mot., p. 4.)   

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that an application for an order to post the summons and complaint was approved by the Court on August 7, 2026. (Opp., p. 3.) Plaintiff argues in light of that order, Defendants were served on August 12, 2026. (Ibid.) Plaintiff argues that Defendants filed the previous motion to quash on August 11, 2026, making that motion premature. (Ibid.) Plaintiff presents the declaration of the process server along with photographic evidence of posting. (Opp., pp. 4-5.) Further, Plaintiff argues that Defendants have refused to accept delivery of certified mail and the documents remain undelivered. (Ibid.) Plaintiff notes that Defendants did not disclose service being completed prior to the previous motion to quash hearing. (Opp., p. 5.)

In reply, Defendants argue that the instant motion to quash is not aimed at the effectuation of service through posting, but rather the order to post itself arguing that it was obtained via perjury. (Reply, p. 2.) Defendants reiterate that Plaintiff obtained the post and mail order by providing the Court with a declaration of diligence is not truthful and Plaintiff did not actually attempt service four times in July of 2026. (Ibid.)

Here, Defendants do not challenge sufficiency of service via post and mail which was authorized by the Court on August 7, 2026. (Reply, p. 2; See Application and Order to Serve Summons by Posting filed 8/7/26.) Defendants concede that the motion is not based on a failure to effectuate that service of process. (Ibid.) In light of the approved order to serve summons by posting, Plaintiff effectuated proper service of process by posting the summons and complaint at the Premises on August 12, 2026. (Rios Decl., ¶ 4; Exh. 1.) Plaintiffs also subsequently mailed the summons and complaint in compliance with that order. (Rios Decl., ¶ 5; Exh. 2.)

Accordingly, the motion to quash service is DENIED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendants Stephanie Brown And Sauskiana Brown’s Motion to Quash Service of Summons is DENIED.

BACKGROUND

This is a breach of contract action. Plaintiff Michael Lawrence Kline (Plaintiff) and defendant Michael Dibacco (Defendant) were partners in a California Limited Liability Company that operated as a restaurant and bar known as Michaels on Riverside and The Red Door in Toluca Lake, California. On September 7, 2018, Plaintiff filed the initial Complaint against Defendant. On May 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Fourth Amended Complaint for breach of fiduciary duty, fraud, and violation of Penal Code § 496 alleging that Defendant had misrepresented the financial status of the business, misappropriated funds, and maintained fraudulent records. (See Fourth Amended Complaint.)

            On October 29, 2020, Defendant filed for Chapter 7 Bankruptcy in United States Bankruptcy Court. On November 10, 2020, Defendant filed a notice of bankruptcy stay with this Court. On February 4, 2021, Plaintiff filed an adversarial action against Defendant with the Bankruptcy Court. In September of 2024, the adversarial Bankruptcy proceedings ended, concluding all bankruptcy proceedings.

On January 15, 2025, this Court ordered the stay on the bankruptcy proceedings lifted in light of notice of Bankruptcy Judgment. 

On July 28, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to stay proceedings. On August 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. As of September 15, 2026, no reply has been filed.  

 

LEGAL STANDARD

“[A] court ordinarily has inherent power, in its discretion, to stay proceedings when such a stay will accommodate the ends of justice.” (OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho¿(2019) 8 Cal.5th 111, 141 [citing¿People v. Bell¿(1984) 159 Cal.App.3d 323, 329.]) “[T]he power to stay proceedings is incidental to the power inherent in every court to control the disposition of the causes on its docket with economy of time and effort for itself, for counsel, and for litigants.” (OTO, L.L.C., supra, 8 Cal.5th at p. 141 [quoting Landis v. North American Co.¿(1936) 299 U.S. 248, 254].) 

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Defendant seeks an order staying the instant action until a pending appeal to a separate bankruptcy proceeding is adjudicated. Defendant argues that the federal bankruptcy appeal concerns the partnership agreement which is the basis of the state claims in this case. (Mot., p. 6.) Defendant argues that Plaintiff relies on a materially different version of the subject agreement and will obtain an advantage if the trial is not stayed until the conclusion of the appeal. (Mot., p. 8.)

In opposition, Plaintiff reminds the Court that Defendant has sought ten continuances and stays in effort to delay litigation up. (Opp., p. 1.) Plaintiff argues that Defendant cites no law entitling him to a stay and attempts to create a correlation between this trial and the bankruptcy proceeding to attempt and force a stay of this proceeding. (Opp., pp. 7-9) Further, Plaintiff argues that there is no basis for an automatic stay due to the pending bankruptcy appeal because this underlying trial court proceeding is not affected by the appeal. (Opp., pp. 10-11.) Plaintiff urges the Court to deny this motion to stay as it has been made up against the start of trial and would egregiously prejudice Plaintiff. (Opp., p. 13.)

Here, the Court has several times stated it would not further delay this action absent good cause. (See 8/7/26 and 8/11/26 Minute Orders.) Trial in the instant matter is set for October 5, 2026. A stay at this juncture would impose great prejudice on Plaintiff less than one month before commencement of trial. (Hamrick Decl., ¶ 24.) Further, Defendant commenced this appeal on April 27, 2026, and has chosen to seek a stay just before commencement of trial. (Hamrick Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. C.) Lastly, Defendant has not shown how the appeal necessitates a stay of the trial court proceedings or how the appeal, which has been dismissed three times before, directly impacts these proceedings. (Kline Decl., ¶ 4; Hamrick Decl., ¶ 16, Exh. N.) Thus, the Court does not find a stay would accommodate the ends of justice.    

Accordingly, the motion to stay proceedings is DENIED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant Michael Dibacco’s Motion to Stay Action is DENIED.
