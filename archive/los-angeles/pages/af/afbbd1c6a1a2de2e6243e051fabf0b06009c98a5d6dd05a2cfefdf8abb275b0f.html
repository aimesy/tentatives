ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On January 21, 2026, Plaintiff Jamie Merchain (“Plaintiff”) filed a Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) against Defendant Los Angeles Dodgers, LLC (“Defendant”) and Does 1-50, alleging causes of action for Premises Liability and Negligence. The underlying action involves Plaintiff tripping and falling over an “uplifted portion of raised asphalt, believed to have been caused by an uplifted tree root… positioned at/near the Centerfield entrance gate located in the Right Pavilion between the parking lot and Dodger Stadium on April 20, 2022… The fall caused Plaintiff to suffer severe bodily injuries.” (SAC, p. 2.)

 

On March 4, 2026, the Court held an Informal Discovery Conference.

 

On June 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion to Quash Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records Served on Third-Party BD&J Law Firm (“BD&J Motion”); Motion to Quash Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records Served on Third-Party Jacob Seropian, Esq. (“Seropian Motion”); Motion to Quash Deposition Subpoena of Business Records Served on Third-Party Martin Kanarek, Esq. (“Kanarek Motion”); and Motion to Quash Deposition Subpoena of Business Records Served on Third-Party the Barnes Law Firm (“Barnes Motion”) (the “Motions”).

 

That same day, Non-Party Kanarek also filed the instant Motion to Quash LA Dodgers’ Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records to Kanarek for Privileged and Protected Records and/or Motion for Protective Order (“Kanarek’s Motion”).

 

On July 23, 2026, Defendants filed an Omnibus Opposition to the Kanarek Motion, Seropian Motion, and BD&J Motion.

 

On July 24, 2026, Defendants filed an Opposition to the Barnes Motion.

 

On July 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed Replies in support of the BD&J Motion, Barnes Motion, Kanarek Motion, and Seropian Motion. Plaintiff also filed a Supplemental Declaration in support of the Kanarek Motion, BD&J Motion, and Seropian Motion.

 

On August 5, 2026, the Court continued the hearing on the Motions to allow a privilege log to be produced regarding certain requests in the BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian subpoenas and to file any supplemental briefing regarding the BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian Motions. (8/5/26 Order.) 

 

On September 1, 2026, the Court continued the hearing on Kanarek’s Motion to allow a privilege log to be produced regarding certain requests in the subpoena and to file any supplemental briefing regarding the motion. (9/1/26 Order.)

 

On September 8, 2026, certain of the instant motions were continued to September 18, 2026. (9/8/26 Order.)

 

To date, no supplemental briefing regarding any of the instant motions has been filed.

 

II.              Procedural Issues

 

Plaintiffs’ Motions

 

As an initial matter, there is no separate statement accompanying any of the Motions. (See Cal Rules of Court, rule 3.1345, subd. (a)(5).) Thus, the Motions are procedurally deficient.

 

Further, the Motions fail to include the electronic service addresses to which the Motions were served. However, given the Oppositions filed addressing all four Motions, the Court exercises its discretion to address the Motions on the merits.

 

Finally, the Court notes that the Court’s reservation system states five motions are on calendar for the instant hearing date; however, the Court’s docket reflects only four motions are substantively set for hearing on the instant hearing date. Plaintiff is cautioned to take care and avoid duplicate entries in making motion reservations in the Court’s system.

 

The Oppositions

 

The Court notes Defendants’ “Omnibus Opposition” is improper given the separate and distinct nature of the Motions. The Court also notes the Omnibus Opposition is just a memorandum of points and authorities and fails to include any evidentiary support.

 

Additionally, the Barnes Opposition is untimely filed and served under Code of Civil Procedure section 1005. However, given the Reply filed, the Court exercises its discretion to address the Barnes Opposition on the merits.

 

The Replies

 

Finally, the Court notes that the Reply to the Seropian Motion includes an argument improperly raised for the first time in the Reply brief – namely, the Seropian Subpoena was never served. (See Seropian Reply, p. 2.)

 

Kanarek’s Motion

 

As an initial matter, the Court notes that the Viselman declaration accompanying the Motion states there are two exhibits attached to the Motion; however, no such exhibits are attached.

 

The Court notes Defendants’ “Omnibus Opposition” is improper given the separate and distinct nature of the Motions. The Court also notes the Omnibus Opposition is just a memorandum of points and authorities and fails to include any evidentiary support.

 

III.            Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.1, subdivision (a) provides, in pertinent part, as follows:

 

If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b), or upon the court’s own motion after giving counsel notice and an opportunity to be heard, may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders. In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)

 

IV.           Discussion

 

A.    Plaintiffs’ Motions

 

Barnes Motion

 

Turning first to the Barnes Motion, the Court notes that Defendants, the moving parties for the subpoena in question, have stated a willingness to withdraw the subpoena as to Barnes. (See Barnes Opp.)

 

Based on the subpoenaing party’s willingness to withdraw the subpoena, the Court GRANTS the Barnes Motion.

 

BD&J Motion, Kanarek Motion, and Seropian Motion

 

Next, the Court addresses the BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian Motions. The Court addresses these motions together given that the subpoenas rendered to each non-party contain nearly identical requests; the Motions are nearly identical; the nature of each non-party is similar in kind in that they are all attorneys/law firms representing the same client in separate litigation – namely, Maria De Los Angeles Perez (see BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian Motions, pp. 1-2, Koppelman Decls.); and Defendants filed an Omnibus Opposition to all three Motions.

 

Plaintiff moves to quash the subpoenas directed at third party BD&J, a law firm; third party Kanarek, an attorney from BD&J; and Seropian, an attorney, on the grounds the subpoenas are overbroad, seek irrelevant material, and seek communications and documents protected by the attorney-client privilege and work product privilege as well as the common interest doctrine. (BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian Motions, pp. 4-8.) Further, Plaintiff contends Defendants have not shown good cause of direct relevance for the records sought in the BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian subpoenas. (BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian Motions, pp. 8-9.) Should the Court be disinclined to grant the Motions, Plaintiff requests the Court issue a protective order modifying and limiting the subpoenas. (BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian Motions, pp. 8-9.) Finally, Plaintiff requests sanctions of $2,500-$4,000 be awarded against Defendants for each motion. (BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian Motions, pp. 9-10.)

 

In the Omnibus Opposition, Defendants state Plaintiff has not met his burden to demonstrate the common interest doctrine applies in this case at all let alone to all the requested documents in the subpoenas. (Omnibus Opp., pp. 3-8.) Defendants further state the subpoenas are not fishing expeditions, and sanctions should not be awarded as the subpoenas were issued in good faith and seek discoverable information directly relevant to the action. (Omnibus Opp., pp. 8-9.)

 

Here, the Court finds a majority of the BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian subpoena requests are overly broad in scope. While the Court acknowledges there is some relevance to some of the subpoenas’ requests as they seek information from witnesses to the underlying incident in this matter – namely, it is undisputed by the parties that Kanarek and Seropian were witnesses to Plaintiff’s injury in the instant action (see Motions, Koppelman Decls.) – the subpoenas effectively ask for all communications and documents in each non-party’s possession relating to the instant litigation or the non-parties’ case with their client Perez. Given the sweeping nature of these requests, the Court finds the majority of the BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian subpoenas are unduly burdensome and thus unenforceable. (See e.g., Calcor Space Facility, Inc. v. Sup. Ct. (Thiem Indus., Inc.) (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 216, 222-223.)

 

Further, the Court finds that to the extent the subpoenas request communications or documents between BD&J, Kanarek, or Seropian and their client, Perez, such requests violate the attorney-client and work product privileges. (See Evid. Code, § 952.)

 

Insofar as the subpoenas request communications between BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian and Plaintiff’s counsel in this matter regarding Plaintiff, Defendants, or this action, the Court proceeds to address the work product privilege and the applicability of the common interest doctrine in an abundance of caution.

 

The ‘joint defense privilege’ and the ‘common interest privilege’ have not been recognized by statute in California. [Citations.] For this reason, we will refer to the joint defense or common interest doctrine, rather than the joint defense or common interest privilege, to avoid suggesting that communications between parties with common interests are protected from disclosure by virtue of a privilege separate from the attorney-client privilege, the work product doctrine, or any other statutorily recognized evidentiary privilege.

…

[T]he party seeking to invoke the doctrine must first establish that the communicated information would otherwise be protected from disclosure by a claim of privilege. For example, the content of the communication may comprise information shared in confidence by a client with his or her attorney, a legal opinion formed and advice given by the lawyer in the course of the attorney-client relationship, or a writing reflecting an attorney's impressions, conclusions, or theories. (See Evid. Code, § 952; Code Civ. Proc., § 2018, subd. (c).) The next step in the analysis is to determine whether disclosing the information to a party outside the attorney-client relationship waived any applicable privileges.

…

‘There is no statutory provision governing waiver of work product protection. [Citation.]’ [Citation.] However, California courts have recognized that the waiver doctrine is applicable to the work product rule as well as the attorney-client privilege. [Citation.] The work product protection may be waived ‘by the attorney's disclosure or consent to disclosure to a person, other than the client, who has no interest in maintaining the confidentiality … of a significant part of the work product.’ [Citation.] Thus, work product protection ‘is not waived except by a disclosure wholly inconsistent with the purpose of the privilege, which is to safeguard the attorney's work product and trial preparation.’

            …

Thus, ‘[f]or the common interest doctrine to attach, most courts seem to insist that the two parties have in common an interest in securing legal advice related to the same matter—and that the communications be made to advance their shared interest in securing legal advice on that common matter.’

 

(OXY Resources California LLC v. Superior Court (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 874, 889-891, as modified.)

 

Applying these principles to the instant circumstances, the Court finds the communications or documents sought by Defendants’ subpoenas between the subject non-parties and Plaintiff’s counsel meet the common interest doctrine requirements.

 

First, the subpoenas’ requested communications or documentations are so broadly phrased that they encapsulate protected attorney work product of both the non-parties and Plaintiff – e.g., writings reflecting an attorney’s impressions, conclusions, opinions, legal research, or theories. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.030.)

 

Indeed, the Court finds the requests here are broader than other requests where appellate courts have found a party’s refusal to produce all requested documents to a nonparty law firm unjustified due to overbreadth. (See Getz v. Superior Court (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 637, 657-658.) For example, in Getz, the Third District Court of Appeal found that the common interest doctrine only applied to some of the requested emails from defendant to a nonparty law firm, not all, and thus defendant’s refusal to “produce the text of the 42,852 indexed emails” on grounds of overbreadth was unjustified. (Id., at p. 657.) By contrast, the subpoena requests here fail to even specify the kind or manner of communications or documents sought from the subject non-parties and instead make sweeping requests that are impossible to meaningfully and narrowly distinguish or differentiate. As such, the Court finds the first requirement of the common interest doctrine is satisfied.

 

Next, both Plaintiff’s counsel and BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian in this matter have in common an interest in securing legal advice related to the same matter of their clients who suffered harm from an identical uplift on Defendants’ property in close temporal proximity, and these communications to each other were made to advance their shared interest in securing legal advice and strategy on the common matter of Defendants’ liability for the uplift. (See id.; BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian Motion, pp. 6-7, Koppelman Decls.)

 

Further, the non-parties and Plaintiff’s counsel did not waive the work product protection as each had an interest in maintaining the confidentiality of a significant part of the work product given that there was “pooled discovery, shared photographs and video, deposition strategy on common witnesses, and analysis of Defendant’s policies” between the non-parties and Plaintiff’s counsel that were exchanged to further “efficient and informed prosecution of overlapping claims.” (BD&J, Kanarek, Seropian Motions, pp. 6-7, Koppelman Decls.; OXY Resources California LLC, supra, 115 Cal.App.4th at pp. 889-991.)

 

Accordingly, the Court finds Plaintiff has satisfactorily shown the common interest doctrine applies to the communications and documents between the subject non-parties and Plaintiff’s counsel sought by Defendants’ subpoenas.

 

To the extent that Defendants state BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian are required to produce at least some requested documents in response to the subpoenas or request an Evidence Code section 915 in-camera hearing with the Court regarding the alleged protected/privileged materials, the Court finds an overly broad and unduly burdensome subpoena does not warrant compliance. Further, the Court may not order disclosure of a communication claimed to be privileged to allow a ruling on the claim of privilege. (See Costco Wholesale Corp. v. Superior Court (2009) 47 Cal.4th 725, 739.)

 

Finally, while a majority of the subpoena requests are overbroad and/or impermissibly infringe on privileges, the Court finds that certain of the subpoena requests warrant a privilege log. Specifically, the Court finds that Request Nos. 1, 8, 13, 14, 17, and 20 in the BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian subpoenas warrant a privilege log to ascertain the validity of the asserted privilege claims.

 

For all these reasons, the Motions are GRANTED IN PART.

 

The Motions are GRANTED as to all items except for the 1st, 8th, 13th, 14th, 17th, and 20th requests in all the subject subpoenas.

 

Plaintiff is to produce a privilege log as to Request Nos. 1, 8, 13, 14, 17, and 20 in the BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian subpoenas.

II.              Legal Standard

 

Probate Code section 9370 states:

 

(a) An action or proceeding pending against the decedent at the time of death may not be continued against the decedent’s personal representative unless all of the following conditions are satisfied:

(1) A claim is first filed as provided in this part.

(2) The claim is rejected in whole or in part.

(3) Within three months after the notice of rejection is given, the plaintiff applies to the court in which the action or proceeding is pending for an order to substitute the personal representative in the action or proceeding. This paragraph applies only if the notice of rejection contains a statement that the plaintiff has three months within which to apply for an order for substitution.

(b) No recovery shall be allowed in the action against property in the decedent’s estate unless proof is made of compliance with this section.

 

 (Prob. Code, § 9370.)

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On May 12, 2026, Petitioner LVNV Assistance, LLC (“Petitioner”) filed a Verified Petition for Approval for Transfer of Structured Settlement Payment Rights Pursuant to California Insurance Code section 10134 et seq. (the “Petition”).

 

On June 24, 2026, Petitioner filed the instant Amended Petition and proof of service on the interested parties to the action.

 

On July 22, 2026, the Court, in pertinent part, set the First Amended Petition for hearing on August 26, 2026. (7/22/26 Order.) The Court ordered Petitioner to give notice of ruling. (7/22/26 Order.)

 

On August 25, 2026, Petitioner filed and served Notice of Hearing as well as the Amended Petition.

 

            On August 26, 2026, the Court held that “[t]he moving party has continued the hearing to September 18, 2026 at 9:00 a.m. in Department 512.” (8/26/26 Order.)

 

No Opposition to the Amended Petition has been filed.

 

II.              Legal Standard

 

“The California Legislature has adopted the Structured Settlement Protection Act (SSPA) (§ 10134 et seq.) to protect structured settlement payees from exploitation by factoring companies.” (RSL Funding, LLC v. Alford (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 741, 745.) 

 

“The court-approval process requires the factoring company to file a petition in the county in which the transferor resides for approval of the transfer, attaching copies of the petition, the transfer agreement, the disclosure form, the annuity contract, any qualified assignment agreement and the structured settlement agreement, a list of the names and ages of the transferor's dependents, notice of the court hearing date, and notice of a right to respond.” (321 Henderson Receivables Origination LLC v. Sioteco (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 1059, 1066 [citing Ins. Code, §10139.5(c)].) 

 

“The California act…governing transfers of structured settlement payment rights defines a ‘“[s]tructured settlement agreement” [as] an arrangement for periodic payment of damages established by settlement or judgment in resolution of a tort claim in which the payment of the judgment or award is paid in whole, or in part, in periodic tax-free payments rather than a lump-sum payment.’” (Matthews v. Liberty Assignment Corp. (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 71, 78.) 

 

“Annuity issuers and structured settlement obligors are defined as ‘interested parties’ under the SSPA (§ 10134, subd. (g)), and as such, are entitled to notice of petitions to authorize transfer of payments under a structured settlement agreement.” (RSL Funding, LLC, supra, 239 Cal.App.4th at p. 745.) 

 

Insurance Code §10137 provides: “A transfer of structured settlement payment rights is void unless a court reviews and approves the transfer and finds the following conditions are met: (a) [t]he transfer of the structured settlement payment rights is fair and reasonable and in the best interest of the payee, taking into account the welfare and support of his or her dependents [; and] (b) [t]he transfer complies with the requirements of this article, will not contravene other applicable law, and the court has reviewed and approved the transfer as provided in Section 10139.5.”  (Ins. Code, § 10137.)  The transfer agreement is effective only upon approval in a final court order.  (Ins. Code, § 10139.5(a).) 

 

Insurance Code §10139.5(a) requires the court to make the following findings: 

 

  1. The transfer is in the best interest of the payee, taking into account the welfare and support of the payee’s dependents. 
  1. The payee has been advised in writing by the transferee to seek independent professional advice regarding the transfer and has either received that advice or knowingly waived, in writing, the opportunity to receive the advice. 
  1. The transferee has complied with the notification requirements pursuant to paragraph (2) of subdivision (f), the transferee has provided the payee with a disclosure form that complies with Section 10136, and the transfer agreement complies with Sections 10136 and 10138. 
  1. The transfer does not contravene any applicable statute or the order of any court or other government authority. 
  1. The payee understands the terms of the transfer agreement, including the terms set forth in the disclosure statement required by Section 10136. 
  1. The payee understands and does not wish to exercise the payee’s right to cancel the transfer agreement. 

 

(Ins. Code, § 10139.5(a).) 

 

In addition, pursuant to Insurance Code section 10139.5(c):  

 

Every petition for approval of a transfer of structured settlement payment rights, except as provided in subdivision (d), shall include, to the extent known after the transferee has made reasonable inquiry with the payee, all of the following: 

 

  1. The payee’s name, address, and age. 
  1. The payee’s marital status, and, if married or separated, the name of the payee’s spouse.       
  1. The names, ages, and place or places of residence of the payee’s minor children or other dependents, if any. 
  1. The amounts and sources of the payee’s monthly income and financial resources and, if presently married, the amounts and sources of the monthly income and financial resources of the payee’s spouse.  
  1.  Whether the payee is currently obligated under any child support or spousal support order, and, if so, the names, addresses, and telephone numbers of any individual, entity, or agency that is receiving child or spousal support from the payee under that order or that has jurisdiction over the order or the payments in question. 
  1. Information regarding previous transfers or attempted transfers, as described in paragraph (11), (12), or (13) of subdivision (b).  The transferee or payee may choose to provide this information by providing copies of pleadings, transaction documents, or orders involving any previous attempted or completed transfer or by providing the court a summary of available information regarding any previous transfer or attempted transfer, such as the date of the transfer or attempted transfer, the payments transferred or attempted to be transferred by the payee in the earlier transaction, the amount of money received by the payee in connection with the previous transaction, and generally the payee’s reasons for pursuing or completing a previous transaction. The transferee’s inability to provide the information required by this paragraph shall not preclude the court from approving the proposed transfer, if the court determines that the information is not available to the transferee after the transferee has made a reasonable effort to secure the information, including making an inquiry with the payee. 

 

(Ins. Code § 10139.5(c).) 

 

III.            Discussion

 

The Payee Salvador Cossio Jr. (“Payee”)’s declaration states Payee agrees to transfer the following payments to Petitioner: 120 monthly payments each in the amount of $1,250.00 starting on 7/4/2026 and continuing through 6/4/2036.  (First Amended Petition, Payee Decl., ¶ 8, Exh. A.) The net amount paid to Payee would be $97,000.00.  (First Amended Petition, Payee Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. A.) If approved, the Payee – who is 30 years old, not married, lives in California, has no dependents, is not currently employed, and has no court-ordered child support obligations – intends to use the money to purchase a vehicle for $30,000.00; pay off a Chase credit card for $30,000; and use $37,000 to remodel the Payee’s home. (First Amended Petition, Payee Decl., ¶¶ 1-9.)

 

Further, the Payee declares that the underlying settlement was regarding a personal injury suit and was settled by Payee’s guardian ad litem in 2018. (First Amended Petition, Payee Decl., ¶ 6.) The settlement provided that Payee would be entitled to 300 monthly payments of $1,250, starting on February 4, 2021, and continuing through January 4, 2046, followed by a series of lump sum payments. (First Amended Petition, Payee Decl., ¶ 6.) The Payee states the “original settlement was intended as compensation for a personal injury; [Payee has] no treatment necessary for any physical injuries and as such [Payee has] no continuing need to provide for future medical expenses. To the best of [Payee’s] knowledge, the future payments were not intended to pay for [Payee’s] future living expenses.” (First Amended Petition, Payee Decl., ¶ 6.)

 

Also, the transferee has provided the payee with a disclosure form that complies with Section 10136, and the transfer agreement complies with Sections 10136 and 10138. (Amended Pet., Exh. 4.)

 

            Finally, Petitioner filed and served the First Amended Petition and amended notice of hearing by FedEx at least 20 days prior to the instant hearing date. (Ins. Code, §§ 1039.5, subd. (f)(2) [“Not less than 20 days prior to the scheduled hearing on any petition for approval of a transfer of structured settlement payment rights under this article, the transferee shall file with the court and serve on all interested parties a notice of the proposed transfer and the petition for its authorization”], 1039.2 [“Any notice required by this article shall be deemed to have been given if addressed to the recipient’s last known address and deposited, first class postage paid, in the United States mail not less than five calendar days prior to the date on which notice is required”].)

 

Thus, the First Amended Petition is GRANTED.

 

IV.           Conclusion & Order

 

Based on the foregoing, Petitioner LVNV Assistance, LLC’s Amended Motion for Order Approving Transfer of Structured Settlement Payment Rights Pursuant to California Insurance Code section 10134 et seq. is GRANTED.

II.              Legal Standard

 

            Code of Civil Procedure section 284 states that “the attorney in an action…may be changed at any time before or after judgment or final determination, as follows: (1) Upon the consent of both client and attorney…; (2) Upon the order of the court, upon the application of either client or attorney, after notice from one to the other.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 284; Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362.)  “The determination whether to grant or deny a motion to withdraw as counsel lies within the sound discretion of the trial court.” (Manfredi & Levine v. Superior Court (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1128, 1133); see Lempert v. Superior Court (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 1161, 1173.)

 

An application to be relieved as counsel must be made on Judicial Counsel Forms MC-051 (Notice of Motion and Motion), MC-052 (Declaration), and MC-053 (Proposed Order). (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subds. (a), (c), (e).) 

 

In addition, California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362 subsection (d) requires that the notice of motion and motion, declaration, and proposed order be served on the client and all other parties who have appeared in the case by personal service, electronic service, or mail. If the notice is served by electronic service, it must be accompanied by a declaration stating that the electronic service address is the client’s current electronic service address. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (d)(2).) As used in this rule, “current” means:

 

“[T]he address was confirmed within 30 days before the filing of the motion to be relieved.

Merely demonstrating that the notice was sent to the client’s last known address and was not returned or no electronic delivery failure message was received is not, by itself, sufficient to demonstrate that the address is current.”

 

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (d).)

 

III.            Discussion

 

On August 12, 2026, Counsel moved the Court to be relieved as attorney of record for Plaintiff. (MC-051.) Counsel filed MC-051, MC-052, and MC-053 Forms. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subds. (a), (c), (e).) 

 

Counsel seeks to be relieved on the following grounds: “Irreconcilable differences have arisen resulting in a breakdown of the attorney-client relationship, and it has now become unreasonably difficult to continue to represent the client. [Counsel] asked [Plaintiff] to secure new counsel but he has yet to do so.” (MC-052, ¶ 2.)

 

The Court is satisfied with Counsel’s reasons for seeking to be relieved. (Cal. Rules of Professional Conduct, rule 1.16.)

 

However, the Court finds notice of hearing on the Motion is defective. There is no proof of service filed that Counsel served Plaintiff with notice of the continued hearing date.

 

Additionally, the Court finds that the MC-053 Form includes inaccurate dates. The MC-053 Form needs to be resubmitted with accurate dates.

 

Accordingly, Counsel’s Motion is CONTINUED to allow Counsel an opportunity to file and serve supplemental papers addressing the deficiencies noted herein.
