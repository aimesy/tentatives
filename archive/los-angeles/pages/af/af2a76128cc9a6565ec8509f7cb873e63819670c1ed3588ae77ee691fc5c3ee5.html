Case Number: 23PSCV00512    Hearing Date: July 20, 2026    Dept: H

Carbajal v. Benihana, Case No. 23PSCV00512

 

ORDER ON MOTION TO ENFORCE SETTLEMENT

Plaintiff Kela Carbajal’s Motion to Enforce Settlement and for Entry of Judgement Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 664.6 is DENIED.

 

Background 

Plaintiff Kela Carbajal (“Plaintiff”) alleges that she sustained injuries and damages in a January 5, 2023 slip and fall on a walkway located at 17877 Gale Avenue in the City of Industry (“premises”). 

On February 23, 2023, Plaintiff filed a complaint, asserting causes of action against Benihana, Inc. (“Defendant”) and Does 1-100 for: (1) Negligence and (2) Premises Liability. 

On February 10, 2026, an “Order Re: Stipulation to Binding Arbitration” was entered.

An Order to Show Cause Re: Dismissal is set for August 31, 2026.


Legal Standard

“If parties to pending litigation stipulate, in a writing signed by the parties outside of the presence of the court or orally before the court, for settlement of the case, or part thereof, the court, upon motion, may enter judgment pursuant to the terms of the settlement . . .” (Code Civ. Proc., 664.6, subd. (a).) 

“For purposes of this section, a writing is signed by a party if it is signed by any of the following:

(1)      The party.

(2) An attorney who represents the party. . .” (Code Civ. Proc., 664.6, subd. (b).)

 

Discussion 

Plaintiff moves the Court for an order enforcing the parties’ settlement and entering judgment in its favor and against Defendant in the amount of the binding arbitration award of $386,785.00, together with prejudgment interest of $7,417.79. 

On or about February 6, 2026, the parties executed and filed a “Stipulation for Binding Arbitration” (“Stipulation”) which provided, in relevant part, as follows: 

“IT IS HEREBY STIPULATED by and between Plaintiff Kela Carbajal and Defendant Benihana, Inc. ("Parties"), through their respective counsel of record, the following:

1.       This above-entitled action be submitted to binding arbitration. . .

5.       The parties stipulate the Hon. Jacqueline Connor (Ret.) shall serve as arbitrator. . . Judge Connor is empowered, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 638 to issue a binding statement of decision and arbitration award. The court will retain jurisdiction to enforce the award;

6. The award of the arbitrator shall not be submitted to the court to be reduced to judgment in this action; . . .

16. . . . The parties further recognize that this agreement to binding arbitration constitutes a settlement agreement and is subject to enforcement pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure § 664.6. . .

18. The court shall retain jurisdiction and may enforce the terms of this stipulation pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 664.6. . .” 

(Ghermezian Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. A). 

On February 10, 2026, an “Order Re: Stipulation to Binding Arbitration” was entered (“Order”), wherein the parties were ordered into binding arbitration pursuant to the Stipulation. The Order also provided that “the Superior Court shall retain jurisdiction to enforce any settlement or arbitration award pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 664.6 or § 1285, et seq., as appropriate.” 

The motion is denied. Plaintiff seeks enforcement of the “parties’ settlement.” Here, the “settlement” was the parties’ agreement to submit the matter to binding arbitration pursuant to certain enumerated conditions. The briefing reflects that the parties, in fact, submitted to binding arbitration and that an award was made. The Court cannot find language in the Stipulation which purportedly provides that “the award constitutes a settlement agreement enforceable under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 . . .” 

That said, in the interest in streamlining the case and avoiding further motion practice regarding the arbitrator’s award, at the time of hearing, the Court will hear from Defendant’s counsel as to when payment will be made.

Case Number: 23PSCV03696    Hearing Date: July 20, 2026    Dept: H

Monfared v. Elliott, et al., Case No. 23PSCV03696

 

ORDER ON DEFAULT JUDGMENT APPLICATION

 

Plaintiff Mo Monfared’s Application for Default Judgment is DENIED without prejudice.

 

Background 

Plaintiff Mo Monfared (“Plaintiff”) alleges as follows:

Plaintiff owns a 1969 Chevrolet Chevelle (“subject vehicle”). On or about April 26, 2022, Plaintiff entered into an oral agreement with Rick Elliott (“Elliott”) and Joe Panzarello (“Panzarello”) (together, “Defendants”) to have Defendants completely restore the Chevelle. On or about November 4, 2022, Plaintiff picked up his subject vehicle in an unfinished condition because Defendants indicated that they would not continue the restoration unless Plaintiff paid more money than agreed upon. Plaintiff subsequently took the subject vehicle to Xtreme Autosports SCV (“Xtreme”) to finish the restoration. Xtreme indicated that all of the work that had been done by Defendants had to be redone because the quality of work was poor. 

On November 19, 2025, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint, asserting causes of action against Defendants and Does 1-50 for: (1) Breach of Contract, (2) Breach of Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing, (3) Fraud, (4) Negligent Misrepresentation, (5) Conversion, (6) Unfair Business Practices [Violation of Business & Professions Code § 17200], and (7) Financial Elder Abuse. 

On January 15, 2026, Defendants’ defaults were entered. On February 2, 2026, Elliott purported to file an answer. On June 29, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion to strike Elliott’s answer. 

An Order to Show Cause Re: Default Judgment is set for July 20, 2026.

 

Discussion

Plaintiff’s Application for Default Judgment is denied without prejudice. The following defects are noted: 

          1.       Plaintiff has failed to dismiss Does 1-50.

2.       Plaintiff has failed to provide the Court with a brief summary of the case.

3.       Plaintiff asserts that he paid Defendants $15,000.00 in cash. The Court requests that Plaintiff provide copies of bank statements or other documentary evidence, to the extent it exists, reflecting withdrawals corresponding with these payments.

4.       Plaintiff has failed to provide the Court with any evidence reflecting that Elliott nor Panzarello possessed a valid license/was not registered with from the Bureau of Automotive Repair (“BAR”) at any point during the time the work was being performed on the subject vehicle.

5.       Plaintiff makes reference to a complaint having been filed with the BAR on his behalf against Defendants, but has not attached a copy of same. The Court requests that Plaintiff furnish a copy, as well as provide any communications received in response from the BAR.

6.       The Court requests that Plaintiff attach a copy of “§ 274.11 Motor Vehicle Repair and Service” referenced in Paragraph 31 of his declaration.

7.       The Court requests more information as to the nature of the work contemplated under the parties’ agreement. It is unclear whether the work contemplated involved upholstery or bodywork versus mechanical work, or both.

8.       The Court requests briefing on the attorney’s fees issue and Plaintiff’s request for same in connection with his financial elder abuse cause of action. 

Accordingly, the application for default judgment is denied without prejudice, and Plaintiff is directed to submit a revised and complete default judgment application that addresses the aforementioned issues.        

Case Number: 24PSCV00660    Hearing Date: July 20, 2026    Dept: H

Melendez v. Ford Motor Company, et al., Case No. 24PSCV00660

 

ORDER ON MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

Defendants Ford Motor Company and WC Performance Ford, Inc.’s Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED. FMC’s Motion for Summary Adjudication is GRANTED as to Issue Nos. 1, 5, and 10, DENIED as MOOT as to Issues Nos. 2-4 and 6, and DENIED as to Issue Nos. 7-9.

 

Background

Plaintiff Rudy Melendez (“Plaintiff”) alleges as follows:

On or about June 22, 2017, Plaintiff entered into a warranty contract with Ford Motor Company (“FMC”) regarding a 2017 Ford F150, VIN # 1FTEW1EG4HKD09633 (“subject vehicle”). Prior to Plaintiff purchasing the subject vehicle, FMC knew that vehicles equipped with the same 10-speed transmission as the subject vehicle suffered from one or more defects that can cause the vehicles and their 10-speed transmissions to experience hesitation and/or delayed acceleration; harsh and/or hard shifting; jerking, shuddering, and/or juddering ("Transmission Defect"). FMC concealed the Transmission Defect. Plaintiff delivered the subject vehicle to WC Performance Ford Inc. (erroneously sued as “Performance Ford”) (“WC Performance”) for substantial repair on at least one occasion. 

On May 8, 2024, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”), asserting causes of action against FMC, Performance and Does 1-10 for: (1) Violation of Subdivision (d) of Civil Code Section 1793.2, (2) Violation of Subdivision (b) of Civil Code Section 1793.2, (3) Violation of Subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code Section 1793.2, (4) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability (Civ. Code §§ 1791.1, 1794 and 1795.5), (5)  Fraudulent Inducement—Concealment, and (6) Negligent Repair. 

The Final Status Conference is set for September 8, 2026. Trial is set for September 22, 2026.

 

Legal Standard 

The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119 [quotations omitted; emphasis theirs].) 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) 

A defendant or cross-defendant moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication “has met that party’s burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) Once that burden has been met, “the burden shifts to the plaintiff or cross-complainant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Id.)

 

Discussion 

FMC and Performance move the Court for summary judgment in their favor and against Plaintiff on Plaintiff’s FAC; in the alternative, they seek summary adjudication as follows:

Issue #1: Plaintiff cannot establish his first through third causes of action of action against FMC for Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivisions (d), (b) or (a)(3) violations, because he cannot establish that there was a nonconformity in the vehicle that FMC or its authorized repair facilities in this state failed to repair under warranty after a reasonable number of repair attempts;

Issue #2: Plaintiff’s first through third causes of action against FMC are time-barred by the four-year statute of limitations;

Issue #3: Plaintiff cannot establish his second cause of action for violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b) against FMC because he cannot establish that any warranty repair lasted longer than 30 days or prove the element of damages;

Issue #4: Plaintiff cannot establish his third cause of action for violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3) against FMC because he cannot establish that FMC failed to make available to authorized service and repair facilities sufficient service literature and replacement parts or prove the element of damages;

Issue #5: Plaintiff cannot establish his fourth cause of action for breach of implied warranty against FMC because there were no warranty repairs during the implied warranty period and the only evidence is that the vehicle drove more than 120,000 miles and was fit for ordinary purposes;

Issue #6: Plaintiff cannot establish his fourth cause of action against FMC because the claim is barred by the statute of limitations and Plaintiff cannot toll same for nearly two years;

Issue #7: Plaintiff cannot establish his fifth cause of action for Fraudulent Concealment against FMC because he cannot establish that FMC had any transactional relationship triggering any duty to disclose;

Issue #8: Plaintiff cannot establish his fifth cause of action against FMC because it is time-barred by the three-year statute of limitations;

Issue #9: Plaintiff cannot establish his fifth cause of action against FMC because he cannot establish that FMC had knowledge of a defect at the time of sale; 

Issue #10: Plaintiff cannot establish his sixth cause of action for Negligent Repair against Performance because he cannot establish damages and the claim is barred by the economic loss rule.

  

Request for Judicial Notice 

The Court rules on Plaintiff’s Request for Judicial Notice (“RJN”) as follows: Denied as to Exhibits Nos. 1-17 (i.e., represented to be copies of various Bates-stamped records in Avila v. Ford Motor Co., No. 22-CV000542-PCP, 2026 WL 508364, at*8 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 24, 2026)) and No. 18 (25 Technical Service Bulletins [“TSBs”]).

Pursuant to Evidence Code section 453, subdivision (b), the party requesting judicial notice must furnish the court with sufficient information to enable it to take judicial notice of the matter. The Law Revision Commission Comments provide, in relevant part, that “[u]nder Section 453, the court is not required to resort to any sources of information not provided by the parties. If the party requesting that judicial notice be taken under Section 453 fails to provide the court with “sufficient information,” the judge may decline to take judicial notice.”

“The court may in its discretion take judicial notice of any court record in the United States. (Evid. Code § 451). This includes any orders, findings of facts and conclusions of law, and judgments within court records.” (Lockley v. Law Office of Cantrell, Green, Pekich, Cruz & McCort (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 875, 882.) “However, while courts are free to take judicial notice of the existence of each document in a court file, including the truth of results reached, they may not take judicial notice of the truth of hearsay statements in decisions and court files. Courts may not take judicial notice of allegations in affidavits, declarations and probation reports in court records because such matters are reasonably subject to dispute and therefore require formal proof.” (Id.) 

Further, as to No. 18, every TSB included therein post-dates Plaintiff’s purchase of the subject vehicle on June 22, 2017 and is thus irrelevant to any purported “pre-sale knowledge.” (Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 544, fn. 4.)

 

Procedural Deficiencies

The Court observes that portions of Plaintiff’s opposing separate statement, including Plaintiff’s response to Fact Nos. 1-3 and 6-20 as to Issue No. 1,  Fact Nos. 1-5 as to Issue No. 2, Fact Nos. 1-3, 8, 9, 18-21, and 23 as to Issue No. 3, Fact Nos. 1, 20, 22 and 23 as to Issue No. 4, Fact Nos. 1, 8, 9, 18, 19, and 20 as to Issue No. 5, Fact Nos. 1, 8, 9, and 18-20 as to Issue No. 6, Fact Nos. 1 and 20 as to Issue Nos. 7 and 8, Fact No. 1, 20, and 25-31 as to Issue No. 9, Fact No. 1, 20, and 33 as to Issue No. 10, reflect non-compliance with California Rules of Court rule 3.1350, subdivision (f)(2) (i.e., “the response must unequivocally state whether that fact is ‘disputed’ or ‘undisputed’. . .”). 

Plaintiff’s opposing separate statement also improperly includes evidentiary objections (see below).

 

Evidentiary Objections

The Court overrules Plaintiff’s evidentiary objections, which are contained solely in his opposing separate statement. “[A] separate statement is not evidence; it refers to evidence submitted in support of or opposition to a summary judgment motion. (Jackson v. County of Los Angeles (1997) 60 Cal.App.4th 171, 178, fn. 4 [emphasis theirs].) California Rules of Court rule 3.1354 requires that evidentiary objections be separately served and filed and states that they “must not be restated or reargued in the separate statement.” (Hodjat v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 1, 9 [upholding the trial court’s refusal to rule on objections contained solely in the separate statement].)  

The Court rules on FMC and Performance’s evidentiary objections as follows:

Overruled in full as to “Objections to Plaintiff’s Separate Statement.”

As to Plaintiff’s Declaration: sustained as to Nos. 1 and 6-11; overruled as to Nos. 2, 4, 12 and 13; sustained in part as to No. 3 (i.e., as to language “a condition Ford knew or should have known about prior to selling me the vehicle”) and otherwise overruled; sustained in part as to No. 5 (i.e., as to language “the Subject Vehicle began experiencing a transmission defect”); and otherwise overruled; 

As to Gregory Yu (“Yu”) Declaration: Sustained as to No. 1.

 

Merits 

On May 8, 2024, Plaintiff filed a FAC against FMC and Performance, asserting causes of action for (1) Violation of Subdivision (d) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (2) Violation of Subdivision (b) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (3) Violation of Subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (4) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability; (5) Fraudulent Inducement—Concealment; (6) Negligent Repair; the first through fifth causes of action were directed to FMC only, while the sixth cause of action was directed against Performance only.

 

First Through Third Causes of Action (i.e., Violations of Subdivision (d), (b) and (a)(3) of Civil Code Section 1793.2, Respectively)

The first cause of action in Plaintiff’s FAC alleges that Plaintiff entered into a warranty contract with FMC regarding a 2017 Ford F150, VIN #1FTEW1EG4HKD09633 (“subject vehicle”); that defects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves in the subject vehicle within the applicable express warranty period; that Plaintiff delivered the subject vehicle to FMC’s authorized repair facility ; that FMC failed to conform the subject vehicle to the terms of the express warranty after a reasonably number of opportunities; and that FMC subsequently failed to repurchase or replace the subject vehicle. (FAC, ¶¶ 7, 13, 17, 43, 44, and 61-63.) 

The second cause of action in Plaintiff’s FAC alleges that FMC and its in-state representatives failed to service or repair the subject vehicle so as to conform to the applicable warranties within 30 days. (Id., ¶ 67.)

The third cause of action in Plaintiff’s FAC alleges that FMC failed to make available to its authorized service and repair facilities sufficient service literature and replacement parts to effect repairs during the express warranty period. (Id., ¶ 72.)

FMC moves for summary adjudication of the first through third causes of action on grounds that Plaintiff cannot establish that there was a non-conformity in the subject vehicle that FMC or its authorized in-state repair facilities failed to repair under warranty after a reasonable number of repair attempts (i.e., Issue No. 1). 

“A plaintiff pursuing an action under the Act has the burden to prove that (1) the vehicle had a nonconformity covered by the express warranty that substantially impaired the use, value or safety of the vehicle (the nonconformity element); (2) the vehicle was presented to an authorized representative of the manufacturer of the vehicle for repair (the presentation element); and (3) the manufacturer or his representative did not repair the nonconformity after a reasonable number of repair attempts (the failure to repair element).” (Donlen v. Ford Motor Co. (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 138, 152; Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (d).) Out-of-state presentations cannot form the basis for a plaintiff’s claim. (Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (c) [requiring the buyer “to deliver the nonconforming goods to the manufacturer's service and repair facility within this state, unless, due to reasons of size and weight, or method of attachment, or method of installation, or nature of the nonconformity, delivery cannot reasonably be accomplished”]; see also subds. (d)(1) and (d)(2) [requiring presentations to in-state facilities].) 

FMC proffers the following evidence:

Plaintiff purchased the subject vehicle on June 22, 2017. (Moawad Decl., ¶¶ 2 and 3, Exhs. 1 and 2.) The bumper-to-bumper warranty, which ran for three years from purchase or 36,000, whichever occurred first, expired sometime prior to September 17, 2019. (Id., ¶¶ 3 and 4, Exh. 2(A), p. 8 and Exh. 4.) The powertrain warranty, which ran for five years from purchase or 60,000 miles, whichever occurred first, expired sometime shortly after December 3, 2020. (Id.) Between September 26, 2017 and February 4, 2020, Plaintiff presented the subject vehicle six times to the dealership Ford of West Covina for routine maintenance. (Id., ¶ 4, Exh. 4.) Plaintiff produced no evidence that he authorized, let alone requested, anything other than routine maintenance during the six maintenance visits at Ford of West Covina. (Id., ¶¶ 4, 14, 16 and 17, Exhs. 4, 14, 16, and 17.) Plaintiff then presented the subject vehicle multiple times to the dealership, Bradley Ford, which is located in Lake Havasu City, Arizona. (Id., ¶¶ 4-11 and 15-17, Exhs. 4-11 and 15-17.) Plaintiff brought the subject vehicle to Performance for repair on February 21, 2023; at that time, the subject vehicle had 97,731 miles on the odometer. (Id., ¶¶ 12 and 15-17, Exhs. 12 and 15-17.) Plaintiff complained during this visit that the subject vehicle had sporadic rough shifting gears. (Id., ¶¶ 12 and 15. Exhs. 12 and 15.) The dealership replaced the transmission, performed a road test to verify the repairs and returned the subject vehicle to Plaintiff by March 10, 2023. (Id., ¶¶ 12 and 15. Exhs. 12 and 15.) On May 9, 2023, Plaintiff presented the subject vehicle to Performance, complaining that the vehicle would shudder when warm. (Id., ¶¶ 12 and 15-17, Exhs. 12 and 15-17.)  The subject vehicle had 99,596 miles on the odometer at the time of this visit. (Id., ¶¶ 12 and 15, Exhs. 12 and 15.) Performance verified the concern, replaced a vacuum check valve and returned the subject vehicle to Plaintiff by May 19, 2023. (Id.) 

Here, FMC meets its initial burden by offering evidence that Plaintiff never presented the subject vehicle for a single warranty repair. The burden shifts to Plaintiff to show a triable issue of fact. Plaintiff, in turn, asserts that he presented the subject vehicle for “at least three qualifying repairs.” (Opp., 9:11-12; Plaintiff’s Decl., ¶ 12.) However, in response to FMC’s interrogatory to identify all dates Plaintiff presented the subject vehicle for repair, Plaintiff identified the subject vehicle’s repair history, complaint and document productions; he did not identify the maintenance visits listed in Plaintiff’s Statement of Facts (“SGD”) 11. (Moawad Dec., ¶ 16, Exh. 16, at No. 2.) “Where a declaration submitted in opposition to a motion for summary judgment motion clearly contradicts the declarant's earlier deposition testimony or discovery responses, the trial court may fairly disregard the declaration and conclude there is no substantial evidence of the existence of a triable issue of fact.” (Whitmire v. Ingersoll-Rand Co. (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 1078, 1087 [internal quotations and citation omitted].) The Court determines that Plaintiff has failed to meet his burden. 

The Court grants FMC’s motion for summary adjudication as to Issue No. 1; as such, the Court denies the motion for summary adjudication as moot with respect to Issues Nos. 2-4.

 

Fourth Cause of Action (i.e., Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability)

The fourth cause of action in Plaintiff’s FAC alleges that the Subject Vehicle was sold with latent defects, such that it “(1) does not pass without objection in the trade under the contract description, (2) is not fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used, (3) is not adequately contained, packaged, and labelled, and (4) does not conform to the promises or affirmations of fact made on the container or label.” (FAC, ¶ 77; see Civ. Code § 1791.1.) 

FMC moves for summary adjudication of the fourth cause of action on grounds that there was no breach during the implied warranty period. 

“The duration of an implied warranty of merchantability is one year if the express warranty is one year or more. (Civ. Code, § 1791.1, subd. (c).)” (Isip v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (2007) 155 Cal.App.4th 19, 22, fn. 1.) 

FMC proffers the following evidence:

Plaintiff purchased the subject vehicle on June 22, 2017. (Moawad Decl., ¶¶ 2 and 3, Exhs. 1 and 2.) Between September 26, 2017 and February 4, 2020, Plaintiff presented the subject vehicle six times to the dealership Ford of West Covina for routine maintenance. (Id., ¶ 4, Exh. 4.) Plaintiff produced no evidence that he authorized, let alone requested, anything other than routine maintenance during the six maintenance visits at Ford of West Covina. (Id., ¶¶ 4, 14, 16 and 17, Exhs. 4, 14, 16, and 17.) Plaintiff brought the subject vehicle to Performance for repair on February 21, 2023. (Id., ¶¶ 12 and 15-17, Exhs. 12 and 15-17.) Here, FMC has shifted its initial burden to Plaintiff to show a triable issue of fact.

Plaintiff, in turn, argues that the Subject Vehicle had “transmission defects” and a “massive coolant leak” without regard for when those issues occurred. As Plaintiff’s SGD 13 and 14 states, the repairs for the transmission and coolant leak occurred years after the implied warranty expired. Nor does Plaintiff provide any evidence or arguments that these were “latent defects”; rather, he asserts only that their mere existence breaches the implied warranty. The Court determines that Plaintiff has failed to meet his burden. 

The Court grants FMC’s motion for summary adjudication as to Issue No. 5; as such, the Court denies the motion for summary adjudication as moot with respect to Issue No. 6.

 

Fifth Cause of Action (i.e., Fraudulent Inducement—Concealment) 

The fifth cause of action in Plaintiff’s FAC alleges that prior to Plaintiff acquiring the Subject Vehicle, FMC knew that the 10-speed transmission installed in the Subject Vehicle was defective but failed to disclose this fact to the Plaintiff at the time of the sale and after. (FAC, ¶¶ 80-94.)

“[T]he elements of an action for fraud and deceit based on concealment are: (1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.” (Boschma v. Home Loan Center, Inc. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 230, 248 [citation and internal quotations omitted].) 

There are “four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.” (Heliotis v. Schuman (1986) 181 Cal.App.3d 646, 651.) The second through fourth circumstances in which nondisclosure may be actionable “presupposes the existence of some other relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise.” (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336-337.) “[A] duty to disclose may arise from the relationship between seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement.” (Id. at 337.)

FMC argues that there is no “transactional relationship” giving rise to a duty to disclose. Plaintiff, however, has alleged the existence of such a relationship created between the parties at the time of the purchase of the subject vehicle via the warranty. (See Scherer v. FCA US, LLC (S.D. Cal. 2021) 565 F.Supp.3d 1184, 1994 [“Plaintiffs do present a contractual relationship with Defendant, because they entered into a warranty agreement. Accordingly, this contractual relationship or transaction gives rise to a duty to disclose.”].) The Court disagrees with FMC’s characterization of Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1 and Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2025) 17 Cal.5th 1122 as standing for the proposition that the existence of a warranty alone does not constitute a transactional relationship. Additionally, “a vendor has a duty to disclose material facts not only to immediate purchasers, but also to subsequent purchasers when the vendor has reason to expect that the item will be resold.” (OCM Principal Opportunities Fund, L.P. v. CIBC World Markets Corp. (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 835, 851.) FMC has failed to negate the existence of a duty of disclosure.

Next, FMC moves for summary adjudication of the fifth cause of action on grounds of lack of scienter. FMC points to Plaintiffs discovery responses and asserts that Plaintiff cannot establish that FMC had knowledge of a transmission defect in his vehicle at the time of sale. “On summary judgment, an alternative method by which a defendant may meet its burden of showing that an essential element of the plaintiff's claim cannot be established is to present evidence that the plaintiff ‘does not possess and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence.’” (Lona v. Citibank, N.A. (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 89, 110.) The defendant must “present evidence, and not simply point out through argument, that the plaintiff does not possess and cannot reasonably obtain the needed evidence.” (Id.) “Circumstantial evidence supporting a defendant’s summary judgment motion can consist of factually devoid discovery responses from which an absence of evidence can be inferred, but the burden should not shift without stringent review of the direct, circumstantial and inferential evidence.” (Andrews v. Foster Wheeler LLC (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 96, 101.) FMC does not establish, through Plaintiff’s discovery responses, that Plaintiff cannot reasonably obtain evidence regarding the element of scienter. FMC does not independently provide evidence refuting scienter. The Court determines that FMC has failed to meet its initial burden. 

Next, FMC moves for summary adjudication of the fifth cause of action on grounds that it is time-barred. FMC’s reasons that “[i]f Plaintiff had ‘been complaining about this transmission [issue] every oil change’ as he claims, that only proves Plaintiff was aware of his claim as early as 2017. (See UF 4.) Thus, Plaintiff’s claim also likely accrued by May 2019 (if not earlier) and expired three years later in May 2022—about two years before suit.” (Motion, 26:4-6.) Under Code of Civil Procedure section 338, subdivision (d), however, a cause of action “is not deemed to have accrued until the discovery, by the aggrieved party, of the facts constituting the fraud or mistake.” Here, FMC has offered no evidence to suggest when Plaintiff learned the facts constituting the fraud. The Court determines that FMC has failed to meet its initial burden.

 

Sixth Cause of Action (i.e., Negligent Repair) 

The sixth cause of action in Plaintiff’s FAC alleges that Plaintiff delivered the Subject Vehicle to Performance for substantial repair on at least one occasion, that Performance owed a duty to use ordinary care and skill in storing, preparing and repairing the Subject Vehicle in accordance with industry standards, which it breached, and that Plaintiff sustained damages. (FAC, ¶¶ 96-99.) 

“The elements of any negligence cause of action are duty, breach of duty, proximate cause, and damages.” (Peredia v. HR Mobile Services, Inc. (2018) 25 Cal.App.5th 680, 687.) 

Performance moves for summary adjudication of the sixth cause of action on the basis that Plaintiff does not allege nor have evidence of damages related to the alleged negligent repair. (Moawad Decl., ¶¶ 16, Exhs. 16-18.) Here, FMC has shifted its initial burden to Plaintiff to show a triable issue of fact. Plaintiff, in turn, merely argues that “[he] complained about transmission issues at multiple repair visits, but Defendant failed to take any action, and the vehicle’s problems continued, damaging Plaintiff. (SGD 11, 15).” (Opp., 19:8-11.) Plaintiff has failed to raise a triable issue of fact. The Court grants Performance’s motion for summary adjudication as to Issue No. 10.

Arellano, et al. v. Food 4 Less of California, Case No. 24PSCV03892

 

ORDER ON MOTIONS TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES

Does 1-60 for: (1) Premises Liability (Negligence), (2) Negligence (against security Does 41-50), (3) Negligent Hiring Training Supervision, and (4) Negligence/Negligent Infliction of Emotional Distress.

A Post-Mediation Status Conference/Case Management Conference is set for September 14, 2026.

 

1. Motion to Compel Furthers Re: Special Interrogatories 

Legal Standard 

“[T]he propounding party may move for an order compelling a further response if the propounding party deems that any of the following apply:

(1) An answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete.

(2) An exercise of the option to produce documents under Section 2030.230 is unwarranted or the required specification of those documents is inadequate.

(3) An objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general.”

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (a).)

The motion must include a meet and confer declaration which “state[s] facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2016.040; 2030.300, subd. (b)(1).) “In lieu of a separate statement required under the California Rules of Court, the court may allow the moving party to submit a concise outline of the discovery request and each response in dispute.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (b)(2).) 

Notice of the motion must be provided “within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the propounding party and the responding party have agreed in writing. . .” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (c).) The responding party has the burden of justifying the objections to the requests. (Coy v. Superior Court (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220-221.) 

“The court shall impose a monetary sanction. . . against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a further response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (d).)


Discussion 

Arellano moves the Court for an order compelling Alpha Beta Company dba Food 4 Less (“Defendant”) to serve further, verified responses to Arellano’s Special Interrogatories, Set No. One (i.e., Nos. 16, 17 and 32). Arellano also seeks sanctions against Defendant and its counsel of record, jointly and/or severally, in the amount of $5,707.20. 

Evidentiary Objections

Merits 

Arellano’s counsel Lindsey Downey (“Downey”) represents, and/or the accompanying exhibits reflect, as follows:

(Id., ¶ 10.) On May 4, 2026, Defendant provided further responses. (Id., ¶¶ 10 and 11, Exh. 5.) Defendant’s counsel agreed to extend any motion filing deadline until June 15, 2025 to permit the court to respond to the IDC request and to hold an IDC. (Id.) On May 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed the IDC request; however, on May 26, 2026, the clerk served a minute order which advised the parties that the Court could not accommodate the request but that motions could be filed if the parties were unable to resolve their dispute. (Id., ¶ 13, Exh. 6.) On June 5, 3036, counsel telephonically met and conferred, during which time the motion filing deadline was extended to June 22, 2026. (Id., ¶ 14.) (See Downey Decl., ¶ 16, Exh. 7.) The parties are requested to provide clarification in this regard at or before the time of the hearing. The Court will proceed to review the sufficiency of Defendant’s June 12, 2026 response to No. 16 under the assumption that it has since been verified. 

Defendant’s substantive response to No. 16 (i.e., that “Responding Party did not have a security guard at the subject store at the time of the subject incident. 2. Motion to Compel Furthers Re: Request for Production of Documents 

Legal Standard 

“[T]he demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to the demand if the demanding party deems that any of the following apply:

(1) A statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete.

(2) A representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive.

(3) An objection in the response is without merit or too general.”

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).) 

The motion must include a meet and confer declaration which “state[s] facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2016.040, 2031.310, subd. (b)(2).) “In lieu of a separate statement required under the California Rules of Court, the court may allow the moving party to submit a concise outline of the discovery request and each response in dispute.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(3).)

A motion to compel further responses to a demand for inspection or production of documents must “set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) If the moving party has shown good cause for the production of documents, the burden is on the objecting party to justify the objections. (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98.) 

Notice of the motion must be provided “within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the demanding party and the responding party have agreed in writing . . .” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (c).)

“[T]he court shall impose a monetary sanction. . . against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further response to a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (h).)


Discussion 

Arellano moves the Court for an order compelling Alpha Beta Company dba Food 4 Less (“Defendant”) to serve further, verified responses to Arellano’s Request for Production of Documents, Set One (i.e., Nos. 3 and 12). Arellano also seeks sanctions against Defendant and its counsel of record, jointly and/or severally, in the amount of $5,707.20. 

Evidentiary Objections 

The Court rules on Defendant’s evidentiary objection as follows: Sustained. 

Merits 

See synopsis of Motion #1. 

The requests in dispute are Nos. 3 and 12. No. 3 asks Defendant to produce all documents evidencing any type of agreement or contract between it and any third-party security service to provide security for the Food 4 Less and/or the shopping center that was in effect on November 13, 2022. No. 12 asks Defendant to produce all documents that reflect any type of contract or agreement between it and any person that it hired to provide any type of security for the Food 4 Less during the 5 years before the incident and including the date of the incident.

“For discovery purposes, information is relevant if it might reasonably assist a party in evaluating the case, preparing for trial, or facilitating settlement.” (Gonzalez v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1539, 1546 [internal quotations and citation omitted].) In this case, Plaintiffs have alleged that Defendant knew, or should have known, of the dangers posed to the public and its customers by the frequency of criminal activity in or around the Food 4 Less and/or shopping center, yet failed to provide adequate security. Defendant asserts that Nos. 3 and 12 seek irrelevant information because it has conceded that it had no security guard at the store at the time of the incident. The Court agrees. No further responses are warranted. 

Under the circumstances presented, the Court declines the parties’ reciprocal sanctions requests.

Case Number: 25PSCV01214    Hearing Date: July 20, 2026    Dept: H

Patel, et al. v. State of California, et al., Case No. 25PSCV01214 (Related to Case No. 25PSCV01549)

 

ORDER ON MOTION TO CONSOLIDATE


Defendant Thomas Flores’ Motion to Consolidate is DENIED.

 

Background 

Case No. 25PSCV01214 

Plaintiffs Mallika Patel (“M. Patel”) and Karan Patel (“K. Patel”) (together, “the Patels”) sustained injuries and damages in a July 18, 2024 motor vehicle accident on the southbound Interstate 605. 

On April 3, 2025, the Patels filed a complaint, asserting causes of action against

The People of the State of California, acting by and through the Department of

Transportation (erroneously sued as “State of California”) (“State of California”),

Thomas Flores (“Flores”) and Does 1-100 for: (1) Dangerous Condition of Public Property, (2) Negligence, and (3) Loss of Consortium. 

On February 4, 2026, Case Nos. 25PSCV01214 and 25PSCV01549 were deemed related; Case No. 25PSCV01214 was designated as the lead case. 

On May 28, 2026, K. Patel dismissed the third cause of action, without prejudice.

The Final Status Conference is set for January 29, 2027. Trial is set for February 9, 2027.

 

Case No. 25PSCV01549

Plaintiff Rogelio Gonzalez (“Gonzalez”) sustained injuries and damages in a July 18, 2024 motor vehicle accident on the southbound Interstate 605. 

On April 30, 2025, Gonzalez filed a complaint, asserting causes of action against The Flores and Does 1-50 for: (1) Motor Vehicle and (2) General Negligence. 

On December 3, 2025, Flores filed a cross-complaint, asserting causes of action against M. Patel and Roes 1-100 for: (1) Indemnity, (2) Apportionment of Fault, (3) Comparative Fault, and (4) Declaratory Relief. 

On February 4, 2026, Case Nos. 25PSCV01214 and 25PSCV01549 were deemed related; Case No. 25PSCV01214 was designated as the lead case. 

The Final Status Conference is set for September 24, 2026. Trial is set for October 12, 2026.

 

Legal Standard 

“When actions involving a common question of law or fact are pending before the court, it may order a joint hearing or trial of any or all the matters in issue in the actions; it may order all the actions consolidated and it may make such orders concerning proceedings therein as may tend to avoid unnecessary costs or delay.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1048, subd. (a).) 

“There are two types of consolidation: a complete consolidation resulting in a single action, and a consolidation of separate actions for trial. Under the former procedure, which may be utilized where the parties are identical and the causes could have been joined, the pleadings are regarded as merged, one set of findings is made, and one judgment is rendered. In a consolidation for trial, the pleadings, verdicts, findings and judgments are kept separate; the actions are simply tried together for the sake of convenience and judicial economy.” (Sanchez v. Superior Court (1988) 203 Cal.App.3d 1391, 1396 [citation omitted].)

“(1) A notice of motion to consolidate must:
(A) List all named parties in each case, the names of those who have appeared, and the names of their respective attorneys of record;
(B) Contain the captions of all the cases sought to be consolidated, with the lowest numbered case shown first; and
(C) Be filed in each case sought to be consolidated.” (Cal. Rules Court, rule 3.350(a)(1).) 

“(2) The motion to consolidate:
(A) Is deemed a single motion for the purpose of determining the appropriate filing fee, but memorandums, declarations, and other supporting papers must be filed only in the lowest numbered case;
(B) Must be served on all attorneys of record and all nonrepresented parties in all of the cases sought to be consolidated; and
(C) Must have a proof of service filed as part of the motion.” (Cal. Rules Court, rule 3.350(b).) 

Discussion 

Flores moves the Court for an order consolidating Case Nos. 25PSCV01214 (the “Patel Case”) and 25PSCV01549 (the “Gonzalez Case”) for all purposes and designating the Patel Case as the lead case.

 

Procedural Deficiencies 

At the outset, the Court notes that Flores’ motion fails to comply with California Rules of Court Rule 3.350(a)(1)(C) [see above], in that the notice of motion to consolidate was not filed in Case No. 25PSCV01549. Additionally, the notice of motion fails to comply with subdivision (1)(B). Flores’ counsel is admonished in this regard.

 

Merits 

Flores seeks consolidation of the aforesaid cases on the basis that they both arise from the same July 18, 2024 motor vehicle accident. (Korff Decl., ¶ 3, Exhs. 1 and 2). He asserts that both cases involve him, have similar or the same witnesses and have overlapping issues of liability, causation and damages. 

The Patels and the State of California separately oppose consolidation. The Patels describe the Patel Case as “a complex action against the State of California for a dangerous condition of public property, brought by a plaintiff who suffered a permanent spinal cord injury” and describe the Gonzalez Case as a “straightforward motor-vehicle negligence action against Flores.” (Patel Opp., 1:25-28). 

The Court determines that the aforesaid cases are not so similar as to warrant consolidation, for the reasons articulated by the Patels and the State of California. The Court also agrees with the State of California’s position that consolidation at trial could create a conflict between The Patels and Gonzalez, inasmuch as the Patels, in seeking to place at least some fault with the State of California, could undermine Gonzalez’s case against Flores. The motion is denied.
