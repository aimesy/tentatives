MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER DISCOVERY RESPONSES

(Requests for Production of Documents, Set One)

Los Angeles Superior Court Case # 24CHCV04669

 

Motion filed: 4/27/26

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiffs Matthew Mathews; and Lisa Mathews (collectively, “Plaintiffs”)

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendant Ford Motor Company (“Defendant”)

NOTICE: OK

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: An order compelling Defendant’s further responses to Plaintiffs’ Requests for Production of Documents (“RFPs”), Set One, Nos. 16–18, 20–29, and 30. Plaintiffs also request monetary sanctions against Defendant and its counsel in the amount of $2,195.00.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: The motions are granted in part. The Court orders Defendant to provide further code-compliant responses to Plaintiff’s Requests for Production of Documents (“RFPs”), Set One, Nos. 16–18, 20–29, and 30, as limited below, within 30 days. The Court imposes $410.00 in monetary sanctions against Defendant and its counsel, payable within 45 days.

 

Plaintiffs are reminded to review the 5/3/19 First Amended General Order Re Mandatory Electronic Filing for Civil. When e-filing documents, parties must comply with the “TECHNICAL REQUIREMENTS” which are set forth at page 4, line 4 through page 5, line 12 of the Court’s 5/3/19 First Amended General Order Re Mandatory Electronic Filing for Civil (particularly bookmarking declarations and exhibits). (CRC 3.1110(f)(4).) Failure to comply with these requirements in the future may result in papers being rejected, matters being placed off calendar, matters being continued so documents can be resubmitted in compliance with these requirements, documents not being considered and/or the imposition of sanctions.

 

BACKGROUND

 

Plaintiffs bring this action under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (Civil Code § 1790 et seq.) for a vehicle they purchased on 6/25/22, for which Defendant issued the manufacturer’s express warranty. (Compl. ¶¶ 9–10.)

 

On 12/18/24, Plaintiffs filed their complaint, alleging against Defendants the following causes of action: (1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Express Warranty; (2) Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment; and (3) Negligent Repair. On 2/5/25, Defendant filed its answer.

 

On 3/13/25, Plaintiffs served their first set of RFPs on Defendant. (Decl. of Matias Leite ¶ 3.) On 4/18/25, Defendant served objection-only responses thereto. (Id. at ¶ 4.) On 2/11/26, Defendant served responses to the subject discovery requests. (Id. at ¶ 8.)

 

On 4/27/26, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion to compel further responses to the subject RFPs. On 7/31/26, Defendant filed its opposition. On 8/5/26, Plaintiffs filed their reply.

 

ANALYSIS

 

California law requires a responding party to respond to each request for production of documents with either a statement of compliance, a representation that the party lacks the ability to comply, or an objection to the demand. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.210, subd. (a).) If the response includes an objection to the demand in part, it must also include a statement of compliance or noncompliance. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.240, subd. (a).) Additionally, the response must (1) identify the particular document that falls within the category of the request to which the objection is being made, and (2) expressly set forth the extent of, and specific ground for, the objection. (Id. at subd. (b).)¿A propounding party may move for an order compelling further response to a discovery request if it decides that “an objection in the response is without merit or too general.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.310, subd. (a).)

 

  • Meet and Confer

 

A motion to compel further discovery responses must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration stating “facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 2031.310, subd. (b)(2); 2016.040.) 

 

Here, Plaintiffs’ counsel declares that beginning on 3/27/26, he and Defendant’s counsel began the meet and confer process regarding the issues raised in the instant motion. (Leite Decl. ¶¶ 9–11.) While Defendant disputes the good faith nature of Plaintiff’s meet and confer efforts, the Court finds that counsel has satisfied the preliminary meet and confer requirements under Code of Civil Procedure sections 2030.300, subdivision (b)(1) and 2031.310, subdivision (b)(2).

 

  • RFPs

 

Here, Plaintiffs seek to compel Defendant’s further responses to their RFP Nos. 16–18, 20–29, and 30.

//

//

//

//

o   RFP Nos. 16–18 and 20–24

 

Plaintiffs’ RFP Nos. 16–18 and 20–24 seek the production of Defendant’s warranty claims policies and procedures manuals and employee training materials from 2022 to the present. (Ex. A to Leite Decl.) In response, Defendant agreed to produce its Warranty Policy and Procedure Manual and training materials applicable to the year 2024 only, subject to a protective order, and refused to produce any further materials.

 

Plaintiffs argue that further responses are warranted because “Courts have long recognized that a manufacturer’s policies—or lack of policies—related to how it deals with consumer complaints and its decision on whether to replace or repurchase are important in determining whether a manufacturer has committed a willful violation of the Act.” (Pls.’ Mot. 5:2–6.)

 

In opposition, Defendant argues that its responses agreeing to produce documents partially responsive to RFP Nos. 16–18 and 20–24 are sufficient because Defendant “properly objected to these Requests as overly broad and seeking irrelevant documents because they are not limited to a reasonable or relevant timeframe, to any particular warranty claim at issue, to Plaintiffs, or to the subject vehicle. Further, there are no allegations in this case regarding the warranty policy and procedure manuals.” (Def.’s Opp. 8:13–16.)

 

In reply, Plaintiffs argue that “policies and procedures for additional years while the vehicle is still under warranty are relevant, at a minimum, to show changes in Ford’s practices.” (Pls.’ Reply 2:8–9.) Plaintiffs therefore maintain that “because Ford limits the scope of document that it is willing to produce solely to the policies from 2024, further responses are warranted.” (Id. at 2:15–16.) The Court agrees.

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have provided sufficient facts to establish the requisite good cause for the further production of documents relating to Defendant’s warranty claims policies and procedures from the date of purchase (2022) to present. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) Accordingly, the motion is granted as to Plaintiffs’ RFPs, Set One, Nos. 16–18 and 20–24.

 

o   RFP Nos. 25–29

 

Plaintiffs’ RFP Nos. 25–29 seek the production of “all documents from 2022 to present evidencing or discussing” Defendant’s goals relating to vehicle replacements/repurchases, and statistics related to other consumer complaints. In response, Defendant asserted objections based on overbreadth, undue burden, relevance, and privilege.

 

Plaintiffs argue that further responses are warranted because “these requests are relevant … because it would tend to show whether a defect or nonconformity exists in these types of vehicles and when this defect or nonconformity first arose. Information about other similar vehicle complaints may also refute Ford’s affirmative defenses claiming that Plaintiff and/or others misused or abused the vehicle or engaged in unauthorized or unreasonable use.” (Pls.’ Mot. 5:28–6:8.)

 

In opposition, Defendant argues that “Plaintiffs’ requests for ‘all documents’ were not limited to the claims made by Plaintiffs in this case or to their 2022 Ford F-150 vehicle, and are therefore overly broad. Further, Plaintiffs’ request for the production of ‘all documents’ does not identify with reasonable particularity which documents Plaintiffs want Ford to produce.” (Def.’s Opp. 9:12–16.)

 

The Court finds that information about similar defects in other vehicles is relevant to proving Defendant’s willful failure to comply with the Song-Beverly Act. Documents responsive to the subject discovery requests may reasonably lead to the discovery of information as to the nature and duration of the defects, Defendant’s knowledge of the defects, and Defendant’s inability to repair the defects.

 

However, the Court finds that these RFPs remain overbroad in scope. The Court therefore limits the scope of the requests to the extent that they require Defendant to search its internal databases for emails or other electronic correspondence. The Court further limits the scope of RFPs Nos. 28 and 29 specifically to 2022 Ford F-150 vehicles purchased in the State of California, experiencing the same defects alleged in Plaintiffs’ complaint.

 

As to Defendant’s privilege arguments, the Court agrees with Plaintiffs that “Ford failed to provide a declaration supporting these claims of privilege or a privilege log, [therefore] Ford’s objections are insufficient.” (Pls.’ Reply 2:23–25.) Accordingly, the motion is granted as to Plaintiffs’ RFPs, Set One, Nos. 25–29, subject to the limitations set forth above.

 

o   RFP No. 30

 

Plaintiffs’ RFP No. 30 seeks the production of “all DOCUMENTS which evidence or describe the numbers of owners of 2022 Ford F150 vehicles who have complained of any of the conditions, defects, or nonconformities for which the SUBJECT VEHICLE was presented.” (Pls.’ Sep. Stmt. 89:23–26.)

 

Here, Defendant argues that it “agreed to supplement its Responses to these Requests after the parties met and conferred regarding the repair presentations to be included within the scope of Ford’s searches but, to date, Plaintiffs have not been willing to engage in these discussions. Otherwise, Ford properly objected to this Request because it is overly broad, unduly burdensome, and seek irrelevant documents, as it is not limited to a reasonable or relevant timeframe or to the subject vehicle.” (Def.’s Opp. 10:26–11:5.)

 

To the extent that Defendant argues that Plaintiffs failed to meet and confer regarding RFP No. 30, it appears to the Court that on 4/27/26, Plaintiffs’ counsel asked Defendant’s counsel for their availability to meet and confer, but received no response. (Ex. G to Leite Decl.) Therefore, the Court finds this argument unavailing.

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that further responses to RFP No. 30 are warranted, with the limitation that Defendant is not required to to search its internal databases for emails or other electronic correspondence. The Court further limits the scope of RFP No. 30 specifically to 2022 Ford F-150 vehicles purchased in the State of California, experiencing the same defects alleged in Plaintiff’s complaint. (Compl. ¶ 14.) Accordingly, the motion is granted as to Plaintiff’s RFP No. 30, subject to the limitations set forth above.

 

  • Sanctions

 

“The court shall impose a monetary sanction … against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.310, subd. (h).) Additionally, “the court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2023.030, subd. (a).)

 

Here, Plaintiffs seek to recover $2,195.00 in monetary sanctions against Defendant and its counsel, accounting for: (1) 2.1 hours of Plaintiffs’ counsel’s time spent drafting the instant motion and accompanying papers; (2) an additional anticipated 2 hours spent reviewing Defendant’s opposition and drafting a reply; and (3) an additional anticipated 2 hours spent preparing for and attending the instant hearing, at counsel’s hourly billing rate of $350.00 per hour. (Leite Decl. ¶ 12.) Plaintiffs also seek to recover $60.00 in filing fees. (Id. at ¶ 13.)

 

Defendant argues in opposition that “even if the Court were to grant Plaintiffs’ Motion, monetary sanctions would not be warranted under California Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030 because Ford served proper and appropriate responses, supplemental responses, and objections.” (Def.’s Opp. 15:2–4.)

 

In granting the instant motion, the Court finds it reasonable to impose $410.00 in monetary sanctions against Defendant and its counsel.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The motions are granted in part. The Court orders Defendant to provide further code-compliant responses to Plaintiff’s Requests for Production of Documents (“RFPs”), Set One, Nos. 16–18, 20–29, and 30, as limited above, within 30 days. The Court imposes $410.00 in monetary sanctions against Defendant and its counsel, payable within 45 days.

DEMURRER WITH MOTION TO STRIKE

Los Angeles Superior Court Case # 25CHCV04577

¿ 

Demurrer with Motion to Strike Filed: 4/8/26

¿ 

MOVING PARTY: Defendants Alexis Antonio Quezada; and Quezada Realty, Inc. (“Moving Defendants”)

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff AHL Funding, LLC (“Plaintiff”)

NOTICE: OK

¿ 

RELIEF REQUESTED: Moving Defendants demur against the first, second, third, fourth, and sixth causes of action in Plaintiff’s complaint. Moving Defendants further move to strike Plaintiff’s references to punitive damages and attorney fees from the complaint.

¿ 

TENTATIVE RULING: The unopposed demurrer is overruled as to Plaintiff’s sixth cause of action and sustained as to Plaintiff’s first through fourth causes of action. The unopposed motion to strike is granted. Plaintiff is granted 30 days leave to amend.

 

BACKGROUND¿ 

 

This is a fraud action in which Plaintiff, a residential mortgage lender, alleges that while employed by real estate brokers including Moving Defendants, defendant Ricardo De La Torre submitted to Plaintiff multiple fraudulent residential mortgage loan applications. (Compl. ¶¶ 16–18.)

 

On 12/22/25, Plaintiff filed its complaint, alleging against five named defendants the following causes of action: (1) Fraudulent Misrepresentation; (2) Negligent Misrepresentation; (3) Fraudulent Concealment; (4) Constructive Fraud; (5) Violation of Business & Professions Code § 17200; and (6) Violation of Business & Professions Code § 10232.5.

 

On 4/8/26, Moving Defendants filed the instant demurrer and motion to strike. No opposition has been filed to date. On 8/5/26, Moving Defendants filed a notice of non-opposition.

//

//

//

ANALYSIS

 

As a general matter, a party may respond to a pleading against it by demurrer on the basis of any single or combination of eight enumerated grounds, including that “the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿

 

“A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) 

 

Here, Moving Defendants demur against the first, second, third, fourth, and sixth causes of action in Plaintiff’s complaint, arguing that Plaintiff fails to allege facts sufficient to constitute those causes of action.

 

        I.            Meet-and-Confer 

 

Before filing its demurrer, “the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41, subd. (a).) The demurring party must file and serve a meet and confer declaration stating either: “(A) The means by which the demurring party met and conferred with the party who filed the pleading subject to demurrer, and that the parties did not reach an agreement resolving the objections raised in the demurrer;” or “(B) That the party who filed the pleading subject to demurrer failed to respond to the meet and confer request of the demurring party or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith.” (Id. at subd. (a)(3).)

 

Here, Moving Defendants’ counsel declares that on 3/30/26, she sent Plaintiff’s counsel a meet and confer letter regarding the issues raised in the instant demurrer and motion to strike. (Decl. of Pamela Albanese ¶ 2.) On 4/3/26, Plaintiff’s counsel responded, but the parties were unable to informally resolve the issues. (Id. at ¶ 3.) Therefore, the Court finds that counsel has satisfied the preliminary meet and confer requirements of Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a).

 

    II.            Fraud Causes of Action

 

Plaintiff’s first through fourth causes of action respectively allege against Defendants: (1) Fraudulent Misrepresentation; (2) Negligent Misrepresentation; (3) Fraudulent Concealment; and (4) Constructive Fraud. The elements that must be pleaded in a cause of action for fraud are: (1) a misrepresentation (false representation, concealment or nondisclosure); (2) knowledge of its falsity (or “scienter”); (3) intent to defraud (i.e., to induce reliance); (4) justifiable reliance; and (5) resulting damage. (Philipson & Simon v. Gulsvig (2007) 154 Cal.App.4th 347, 363.) Fairness requires that allegations of fraud be pled “with particularity” so that the court can weed out nonmeritorious actions before a defendant is required to answer. (Small v. Fritz Companies, Inc. (2003) 30 Cal.4th 167, 184.) The particularity requirement necessitates pleading facts that “show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.” (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.)

 

Here, Moving Defendants argue that “Plaintiff’s allegations lack the necessary detail for a fraudulent misrepresentation claim. First, Plaintiff does not specify details about the Subject Loans, such as when the loan applications were submitted, who submitted them, what documentation was provided to Plaintiff and by whom, who the borrowers are, or which properties are involved. Additionally, Plaintiff groups all Defendants together and fails to specify what misrepresentations Quezada supposedly made, how they were made, when they occurred, or to whom. There are three other named defendants in the Complaint, each with distinct roles related to the issues at different times. As a result, these vague allegations hinder Quezada’s ability to mount a proper defense—either by asserting necessary affirmative defenses or understanding how to respond to the claims.” (Dem. 8:6–14.)

 

The Court agrees, and notes that Plaintiff has failed to file any opposition to the instant demurrer. Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that Plaintiff has failed to allege facts sufficient to constitute its fraud causes of action with sufficient particularity. Accordingly, the demurrer is sustained against Plaintiff’s first through fourth causes of action.

 

 III.            Violation of Business & Professions Code section 10232.5

 

Plaintiff’s sixth cause of action alleges against Defendants a Violation of Business & Professions Code section 10232.5, which requires a real estate broker who negotiates a loan secured by real property to provide a written disclosure statement to the prospective lender containing truthful, complete, and accurate information, including but not limited to: a. The identity and financial information of the borrower; b. Borrower income and credit data as represented to the broker; c. Information concerning encumbrances and liens on the property; and d. Other material facts necessary for the lender to evaluate the loan. (Bus. & Prof. Code § 10232.5.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that “Defendants violated Business and Professions Code section 10232.5 by failing to provide AHL with truthful, complete, and accurate disclosure statements in connection with the Subject Loans.” (Compl. ¶ 98.) Moving Defendants argue that this statute does not apply because “based on the four corners of the Complaint, Quezada was merely an intermediary who transmitted the alleged loan information to Plaintiff—there is not one allegation that Quezada ‘negotiated’ the Subject Loans.” (Dem. 3–5, citing Compl. ¶ 20.) However, the Court finds that Moving Defendants overlook Plaintiff’s allegation that “Defendants were real estate agents and/or brokers performing acts described in Business and Professions Code section 10131(d), including negotiating and arranging loans to be secured by liens on real property.” (Compl. ¶ 95.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that Plaintiff has alleged facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for Violation of Business & Professions Code section 10232.5. Accordingly, the demurrer is overruled against Plaintiff’s sixth cause of action.

 

MOTION TO STRIKE

 

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Id., § 437.)

 

Here, Moving Defendants move to strike Plaintiff’s references to punitive damages and attorney fees.

 

    II.            Punitive Damages

 

Punitive damages may be recovered in non-contract actions upon a proper showing of malice, fraud, or oppression by clear and convincing evidence. (Civ. Code § 3294, subd. (a).) “Malice” is defined as conduct intended to cause injury to a person or despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard for the rights or safety of others. (Ibid.; Turman v. Turning Point of Cent. Cal., Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) “Oppression” means despicable conduct subjecting a person to cruel and unjust hardship, in conscious disregard of the person’s rights. (Ibid.) “Fraud” is an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known by defendant, with intent to deprive a person of property, rights or otherwise cause injury. (Ibid.) Punitive damages must be supported by factual allegations. Conclusory allegations, devoid of any factual assertions, are insufficient to support a conclusion that parties acted with oppression, fraud or malice. (Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1042; Anschutz Entertainment Group, Inc. v. Snepp (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 598, 643.)

 

Here, Moving Defendants argue that Plaintiff has not alleged a basis to recover punitive damages because “the complaint fails to plead specific facts establishing that Quezada’s actions were intentional. … Although the word ‘intentional’ is used, these allegations do not plead with any particularity that Quezada acted with an intent to deceive Plaintiff.” (MTS 5:28–6:7.) The Court agrees and again notes that Plaintiff has failed to file any opposition to the instant motion to strike. Accordingly, the motion is granted as to Plaintiff’s references to punitive damages.

 

 III.            Attorney Fees

 

An award of attorney fees is proper when authorized by contract, statute, or law. (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 1032, subd. (b); 1033.5, subd. (a)(10).) Here, Defendant argues that “Plaintiff requests attorneys fees in its prayer for relief, … [but] there is no applicable contract or statute that entitles Plaintiff to attorneys’ fees against Quezada, nor has Plaintiff cited to any relevant ones in its Complaint.” (MTS 6:24–7:1.)

 

The Court agrees. Based on the foregoing, the motion is granted as to Plaintiff’s prayer for attorney fees.

 

LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

 

Here, the Court notes that Plaintiff has failed to oppose the instant demurrer and therefore has failed to meet its burden to show that it can successfully amend its complaint. Nevertheless, the Court notes that this is the first demurrer and motion to strike brought against Plaintiff’s original complaint. Accordingly, under the Court’s liberal policy of granting leave to amend, Plaintiff is granted 30 days leave to amend the complaint to cure the defects set forth above.

 

CONCLUSION¿ 

 

The unopposed demurrer is overruled as to Plaintiff’s sixth cause of action and sustained as to Plaintiff’s first through fourth causes of action. The unopposed motion to strike is granted. Plaintiff is granted 30 days leave to amend.
