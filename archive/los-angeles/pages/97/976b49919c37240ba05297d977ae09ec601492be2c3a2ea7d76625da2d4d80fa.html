TENTATIVE RULING

 

Plaintiffs’ motion to vacate dismissal is denied.

 

BACKGROUND

 

This is a product-defect case.  The alleged product is a penis-enlargement device called Penuma.  The complaint alleges that Plaintiffs underwent surgeries to implant the device.  Plaintiffs claim the device did not perform as marketed and ended up disfiguring them. 

 

On April 14, 2025, Plaintiff voluntarily dismissed Implantech Associates, Inc. (“Implantech”) from the case with prejudice.

 

Here, Plaintiffs move to vacate the dismissal. 

 

DISCUSSION

 

Arguments

 

Plaintiffs

 

In deciding to dismiss Implantech with prejudice, Plaintiffs assert that their prior counsel relied on two 2025 declarations – the declaration of Defendant Dr. James Elist, the inventor of Penuma, and the declaration of Edward Leicht, Implantech’s Chief Operating Officer and General Manager. Plaintiffs claim the 2025 declarations stated that Implantech played no role in the development, design, or design changes of Penuma.  (See Motion, pp. 4-5; see also id. at pp. 3, 6-8.) 

 

Allegedly, Plaintiffs’ new counsel recently discovered statements that Leicht made in a federal case in 2020 that contradict the 2025 declarations.  Plaintiffs insist that the 2020 statements indicate that Implantech discussed design concepts and design changes with Dr. Elist and participated in creating prototypes.  (See id. at pp. 4-5; see also id. at pp. 3, 6-8.)

 

Plaintiffs contend relief should be granted because:

 

* “California courts . . . possess[] inherent equitable authority to vacate [] judgment[s] or dismissal[s] procured through extrinsic fraud or extrinsic mistake.”  (Id. at p. 8 [citing Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975 (Rappleyea) and Olivera v. Grace (1942) 19 Cal.2d 570].)

 

* The “inherent equitable authority extends fully to voluntary dismissals executed with prejudice.”  (Ibid. [citing Zamora v. Clayborn Contracting Group, Inc. (2002) 28 Cal.4th 249 and Whittier Union High School District v. Superior Court (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 504 (“Whittier Union”)]; see also id. at p. 9.)

 

* The misrepresentations in the 2025 declarations constitute extrinsic fraud.  (See id. at pp. 9-12.)

 

* “Plaintiffs satisfy the equitable requirements” (id. at p. 12, bolding and capitalizing deleted):

 

– “Plaintiffs possess meritorious claims against Implantech[.]”  (Ibid., bolding and capitalizing deleted.)

 

– “Plaintiffs possess a satisfactory excuse for not challenging the dismissal earlier[.]”  (Id. at p. 13, bolding and capitalizing deleted; see also id. at p. 14.)

 

– “Plaintiffs acted diligently upon discovering the basis for relief[.]”  (Id. at p. 14.)

 

Defendants

 

Defendants claim:

 

* Plaintiffs failed to meet and confer.  (See Opposition, pp. 6-7.)

 

* Statutory relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473(b) is time-barred.  (See id. at pp. 11-12.)

 

* Equitable relief is unavailable for voluntary dismissals.  (See id. at pp. 12-13.)

 

* Rappleyea, supra, 8 Cal.4th 975 is distinguishable.  (See id. at pp. 13-14.)

 

* “Plaintiffs misstate the three-prong test” for equitable relief.  (Id. at p. 14, bolding and capitalizing deleted.)

 

* The prototypes mentioned in the 2020 statements were research-and-development concepts, “not actionable design defects.”  (Ibid., bolding and capitalizing deleted.)

 

* “Plaintiffs’ tactical decision to forgo discovery precludes any claim of extrinsic fraud.”  (Id. at p. 15, bolding and capitalizing deleted; see also id. at p. 16.)

 

* “Plaintiffs’ strategic decision to dismiss was a calculated tactical choice[.]”  (Id. at p. 16, bolding and capitalizing deleted.)

 

* New “counsel’s belated review of public dockets cannot unwind prior counsel’s strategic decisions.”  (Id. at p. 17, bolding and capitalizing deleted; see also id. at p. 18.)

 

Reply

 

In reply, Plaintiffs state:

 

* Even after a voluntary dismissal, the Court retains inherent equitable authority “to grant relief from a judgment or order obtained through extrinsic fraud or mistake.”  (Reply, p. 3.)

 

* “Defendants’ ‘commercial device’ qualification does not resolve the material conflict[.]”  (Id. at p. 4 [“The Opposition attempts to rewrite the declarations by inserting a limitation their authors did not disclose when seeking dismissal. According to Defendants, ‘no role’ meant no role in designing only the final commercial device implanted in Plaintiffs, not no role in Penuma-related design discussions, engineering, molds, or prototypes. [¶] But the dismissal request was made against claims that expressly placed Implantech’s design-modification and prototype activities at issue. In that context, stating that Implantech played ‘no role’ in development, design changes, or prototype creation conveyed that no such activity existed.”], bolding and capitalizing deleted; see also id. at p. 5.)

 

* “Defendant’s misrepresentations . . . prevented adjudication of Plaintiffs’ claims[.]”  (Id. at p. 5 [“The fraudulent declarations were transmitted for the specific purpose of inducing Plaintiffs to dismiss Implantech before the factual representations were subjected to discovery, deposition, or judicial determination. Plaintiffs relinquished their claims because they accepted those representations. The deception therefore did not occur during a trial in which Plaintiffs had a fair opportunity to expose it; it prevented any adjudication from occurring.”], bolding and capitalizing deleted; see also id. at pp. 6-7.)

 

* The three equitable requirements are met.  (See id. at pp. 7-9 [arguing that (1) “Plaintiffs have shown a meritorious case[,]” (2) “Plaintiffs have a satisfactory excuse for not presenting their claims[,]” and (3) “Plaintiffs acted diligently after discovery”], bolding and capitalizing deleted.

 

* Plaintiffs met and conferred.  (See id. at p. 9 [“Defendants contend that Plaintiffs failed to meet and confer in good faith, but the parties did meet and confer. Defendants requested that Plaintiffs withdraw the motion, Plaintiffs declined to do so, and the parties did not reach a resolution.”].)

 

* Defendants’ attorney-turnover argument is unavailing.  (See ibid. [“The changes in Plaintiffs’ counsel likewise do not resolve the issue before the Court. Current counsel took over these cases on May 14, 2026 and subsequently discovered Mr. Leicht’s 2020 declaration and its conflict with the misrepresentations previously supplied to obtain Implantech’s dismissal. Plaintiffs promptly sought relief. The fact that different attorneys previously handled the cases does not change what the declarations said, what Defendants now acknowledge about Implantech’s prototype work, or the stated reason Plaintiffs agreed to dismiss Implantech.”].)

 

Analysis

 

Subject to certain exceptions, a “plaintiff has the absolute right to dismiss the action ‘any time before the actual commencement of trial.’”  (Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civ. Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶ 11:2; see also id. at ¶ 11:6.)

 

The “[p]laintiff may dismiss the entire complaint or just some part of it (e.g., one of several causes of action).  Or, [the] plaintiff can dismiss as against some, but not all, defendants named in the complaint.”  (Id. at ¶ 11:3.)

 

Moreover, the “[p]laintiff may dismiss with or without prejudice to the right to file another action on the same claim.”  (Id. at ¶ 11:4.)

 

“[A] voluntary dismissal of an entire action deprives the court of both subject matter and personal jurisdiction.”  (Id. at ¶ 11:34.)

 

“Even so, the court has power under [Code of Civil Procedure section] 473(b) to vacate a voluntary dismissal entered as a result of ‘mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect[.]’”  (Id. at ¶ 11:34.11 [citing Zamora, supra, 28 Cal.4th at 254-255]; see also id. at ¶ 11:41.)

 

“A voluntary dismissal may also be set aside if procured by fraud.”  (Id. at ¶ 11:34.15.)  “If the fraud is discovered within 6 months, relief may be sought in the dismissed action under” section 473(b).”  (Ibid.)[1]  “Thereafter, equitable relief may be sought in an independent action but is available only for ‘extrinsic,’ not ‘intrinsic,’ fraud[.]”  (Ibid. [citing Heyman v. Franchise Mortgage Acceptance Corp. (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 921, 926], emphasis in original.)

 

“Extrinsic fraud occurs when a party is deprived of his opportunity to present his claim or defense to the court, where he was kept in ignorance or in some other manner fraudulently prevented from fully participating in the proceeding.”  (Heyman, supra, 107 Cal.App.4th at 926.)  “Examples of extrinsic fraud are: concealment of the existence of a community property asset, failure to give notice of the action to the other party, convincing the other party not to obtain counsel because the matter will not proceed (and it does proceed).”  (Ibid.; see also Weil & Brown, supra, at ¶ 5:441 [listing examples].)

 

In contrast,

 

[f]raud is intrinsic and not a valid ground for setting aside a judgment when the party has been given notice of the action and has had an opportunity to present his case and to protect himself from any mistake or fraud of his adversary, but has unreasonably neglected to do so.  [Citation.]  Such a claim of fraud goes to the merits of the prior proceeding which the moving party should have guarded against at the time.  Where the defrauded party failed to take advantage of liberal discovery policies to fully investigate his or her claim, any fraud is intrinsic fraud.

 

(Heyman, supra, 107 Cal.App.4th at 926, emphasis in original.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion to vacate their dismissal of Implantech is denied. Equitable relief is unwarranted for two reasons. 

 

First, when Plaintiffs filed their voluntary dismissal with prejudice, they were represented by counsel and could have conducted discovery to verify the 2025 declarations.  (See Weil & Brown, supra, at ¶ 5:440 [“The usual case in which ‘extrinsic’ fraud is found is where a party was not represented by counsel.  If the [party] had a lawyer and the opportunity to litigate, any fraud or mistake will usually be classified as ‘intrinsic’ (hence, not ground for equitable relief).”], emphasis in original.)  In other words, even if there had been fraud, it was not extrinsic fraud

 

Second, Defendants establish that the products addressed in the 2020 statements were different from the product at issue in Plaintiffs’ lawsuit, meaning the 2025 declarations were not fraudulent.  (See Opposition, pp. 8-10, 14-15; see also Supp. Leicht Decl., ¶¶ 4-18; Grimaldi Decl., ¶¶ 6-8, Exs. A-C; cf. Poindexter Decl., Exs. B-D.)

 

Plaintiffs’ authorities do not change the result.  The cases they cite involved unique and distinguishable facts. In Rappleyea, supra, 8 Cal.4th 975, the defendants were out-of-state residents who represented themselves, snf the court clerk’s office misinformed the defendants’ informal counsel about the correct amount for the filing fee for the defendants’ answer. Further, the plaintiff’s counsel “misdescribed California law” to the defendants. On these facts, denial of a motion to vacate default was reversed. In Olivera, supra, 19 Cal.2d 570, he plaintiff obtained a default judgment against an incompetent defendant by concealing the defendant’s incompetency from the court. Accordingly, the administrator of the incompetent defendant stated a claim for relief from the default judgment. In Zamora, supra, 28 Cal.4th 249, the court granted a motion to vacate judgment granted under section 473(b)’s discretionary-relief provision because the settlement offer contained a typo. In Whittier Union, supra, 66 Cal.App.3d 504, a voluntary dismissal was set aside because the plaintiffs’ attorney settled the case without plaintiffs’ knowledge and consent. Nothing like these circumstances apply to Plaintiffs’ dismissal here.

 

Plaintiffs’ motion to vacate the dismissal  is denied.

 

Consequently, the motion to vacate is denied.

PRELIMINARY APPROVAL OF CLASS ACTION SETTLEMENT

            As a “fiduciary” of the absent class members, the trial court’s duty is to have before it sufficient information to determine if the settlement is fair, adequate, and reasonable.  (7-Eleven Owners for Fair Franchising v. The Southland Corp. (2000) 85 Cal.App.4th 1135, 1151 [citing Dunk v. Ford Motor Co. (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 1794, 1801, 1802 (“Dunk”)].)

            California Rules of Court, rule 3.769 governs settlements of class actions.  Any party to a settlement agreement may submit a written notice for preliminary approval of the settlement.  The settlement agreement and proposed notice to class members must be filed with the motion, and the proposed order must be lodged with the motion. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.769(c).)

            In determining whether to approve a class settlement, the court’s responsibility is to “prevent fraud, collusion or unfairness to the class” through settlement and dismissal of the class action because the rights of the class members, and even named plaintiffs, “may not have been given due regard by the negotiating parties.”  (Consumer Advocacy Group, Inc. v. Kintetsu Enterprises of America (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 46, 60.)

 

FAIRNESS OF THE SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT

            In an effort to aid the Court in the determination of the fairness of the settlement, Wershba v. Apple Computer, Inc. (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 224, 244-245 (“Wershba”), discusses factors the Court should consider when testing the reasonableness of the settlement.

            A presumption of fairness exists where: (1) the settlement is reached through arm’s length bargaining; (2) investigation and discovery are sufficient to allow counsel and the Court to act intelligently; (3) counsel is experienced in similar litigation; and (4) the percentage of objectors is small.  (Wershba, supra at p. 245 [citing Dunk, supra at p. 1802].)  The test is not the maximum amount plaintiff might have obtained at trial on the complaint but, rather, whether the settlement is reasonable under all of the circumstances.  (Wershba, supra at p. 250.)

            In making this determination, the Court considers all relevant factors including “the strength of [the] plaintiffs' case, the risk, expense, complexity and likely duration of further litigation, the risk of maintaining class action status through trial, the amount offered in settlement, the extent of discovery completed and the stage of the proceedings, the experience and views of counsel, the presence of a governmental participant, and the reaction of the class members to the proposed settlement.”  (Kullar v. Foot Locker Retail, Inc. (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 116, 128 (“Kullar”) [citing Dunk, supra at p. 1801].)

            “The fact that a proposed settlement may only amount to a fraction of the potential recovery does not, in and of itself, mean that the proposed settlement is grossly inadequate and should be disapproved.”  (City of Detroit v. Grinnell Corporation (2d Cir. 1974) 495 F.2d 448, 455; see also Linney v. Cellular Alaska Partnership (9th Cir. 1998) 151 F.3d 1234, 1242 [“[I]t is the very uncertainty of outcome in litigation and avoidance of wasteful and expensive litigation that induce consensual settlements.  The proposed settlement is not to be judged against a hypothetical or speculative measure of what might have been achieved by the negotiators.”].)

ANALYSIS OF SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT

A. Does a Presumption of Fairness Exist?

1. Was the Settlement reached through arm’s-length bargaining? Yes. Counsel represents that “after months of negotiations, the parties reached a fair resolution of this case that will allow both sides to avoid any further delay or uncertainty regarding the scope of the case and amount of damages.” (Declaration of Shannon Liss-Riordan (“Liss-Riordan Decl.”), ¶4.)

2. Were investigation and discovery sufficient to allow counsel and the Court to act intelligently? Yes. Counsel represents that “prior to the settlement in this case, the parties exchanged ‘extensive’ information, allowing [her] to determine the risks and potential recovery in this case.” (Ibid.) In particular, Defendant produced sample banquet orders, invoices, and bills of events during the Class Period, as well as a spreadsheet of a breakdown of the events during the Class Period which included additional data regarding total food and beverage revenue, total service charge, and the portion of the service charge retained by Defendant. In addition, Defendant provided Plaintiffs with the number of employees that worked banquet events during the Class Period. (Settlement Agreement, ¶2.3.)

3.  Is counsel experienced in similar litigation?  Yes. Class Counsel is experienced in complex litigation, including wage and hour class action cases. (Liss-Riordan Decl., ¶¶3,6.)

4. What percentage of class has objected? This cannot be determined until the fairness hearing.  (See Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2011) ¶ 14:139.18 [“Should the court receive objections to the proposed settlement, it will consider and either sustain or overrule them at the fairness hearing.”].)

 

B. Is the settlement fair, adequate and reasonable?

            1. Strength of Plaintiffs’ case.  “The most important factor is the strength of the case for plaintiffs on the merits, balanced against the amount offered in settlement.”  (Kullar at 130.)

            Counsel represents based on the data provided by Defendants, Plaintiffs’ counsel was able to estimate that the maximum realistic recovery of each claim asserted in the operative complaint comes to $246,000. Specifically, Plaintiffs brought this action because they believe that Defendants’ retention of service charges violates Labor Code, section 351, which they sought to enforce through the California Unfair Competition Law, Business & Professions Code, sections 17200, et seq. (“UCL”). Counsel contends that restitution under the UCL is to restore to any person any money or property, real or personal, which may have been acquired by means of such unfair competition; and in this case, the issue is whether the full amount of the service charge added to Defendants’ banquet bills would look to reasonable customers as gratuities, or whether, as Defendant contends the amount would be less. (Revised Motion ISO Preliminary Approval (“Supp. Brief”), 11:18-12:22.)

2. Risk, expense, complexity and likely duration of further litigation.  Further litigation carries the possibility of non-certification and unfavorable rulings on the merits of the above legal issues. 

            3. Risk of maintaining class action status through trial.  It would have been Plaintiffs’ burden to maintain the class action status through trial.

4. Amount offered in settlement.  Based on the calculations above, the Settlement amount of $135,000 therefore represents approximately 56% of the estimated recovery in this matter. This percentage is within the “ballpark of reasonableness.”

5. Extent of discovery completed and the stage of the proceedings. As stated above, it appears that Plaintiffs have completed sufficient discovery in order to make an informed decision.

            6. Experience and views of counsel. As indicated above, Class Counsel is experienced in class actions, including wage and hour class action cases. Class Counsel is of the opinion that the settlement is fair, reasonable, and adequate and is in the best interest of the Class in light of all known facts and circumstances.

            7.  Presence of a governmental participant. This factor is not applicable.

8. Reaction of the class members to the proposed settlement. The class members’ reactions will not be known until they receive notice and are afforded an opportunity to object or opt-out.  This factor becomes relevant during the fairness hearing.

CONDITIONAL CLASS CERTIFICATION

A. Standards

            A detailed analysis of the elements required for class certification is not required, but it is advisable to review each element when a class is being conditionally certified.  (Amchem Products, Inc. v. Winsor (1997) 521 U.S. 620, 622-627.) The trial court can appropriately utilize a different standard to determine the propriety of a settlement class as opposed to a litigation class certification.  Specifically, a lesser standard of scrutiny is used for settlement cases.  (Dunk at 1807, fn. 19.)  Because a settlement eliminates the need for a trial, when considering whether to certify a settlement class, the court is not faced with the case management issues present in certification of a litigation class.  (Global Minerals & Metals Corp. v. Superior Court (2003) 113 Cal.App.4th 836, 859.)  Finally, the Court is under no “ironclad requirement” to conduct an evidentiary hearing to consider whether the prerequisites for class certification have been satisfied.  (Wershba, supra at p. 240.)

 

B. Analysis

1. Numerosity.  This action involves a class of approximately 37 members. (Settlement Agreement, ¶1.8.)

2. Ascertainability.  This class definition “is precise, objective and presently ascertainable.” (Sevidal v. Target Corp. (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 905, 919.) Class Members are identifiable from Defendant’s records. (Settlement Agreement, ¶1.8.)

3. Community of interest.  “The community of interest requirement involves three factors: ‘(1) predominant common questions of law or fact; (2) class representatives with claims or defenses typical of the class; and (3) class representatives who can adequately represent the class.’”  (Linder v. Thrifty Oil Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 429, 435.)

Counsel contends that commonality is met because Plaintiffs allege that they and other service staff were entitled to the total proceeds of the charge imposed on banquet food and beverage service charges it charged its customers at banquet events held at its hotel, because reasonable customers would have understood it to be a gratuity, and Defendants did not make sufficiently clear to customers that the charge was not being distributed in its entirety to the service employees as gratuity income. Counsel contends that this uniform policy gives rise to a common claim by all class members that Defendants wrongfully withheld a portion of the service charge proceeds. (Supp. Brief, 7:14-21.)

Further, counsel contends commonality is met because Plaintiffs predicate their claims entirely upon the Defendants’ uniform practices of: (1) charge imposed on banquet food and beverage service charges it charged its customers at banquet events held at its hotel; and (2) failing to remit the total proceeds of that charge to the service staff. (Supp. Brief, 8:18-21.)

Finally counsel contend that Plaintiffs are adequate representative because they do not have conflicts with the class and are represented by adequate counsel. (Declaration of Plaintiff Sullivan, passim; Declaration of Plaintiff James, passim.)

Hyatt and named plaintiff Michael Sullivan have separately agreed to a settlement of Mr. Sullivan’s separate and independent claims before the Division of Labor Standards Enforcement, captioned as Michael Sullivan v. Hyatt Corporation, Charge Nos. WC-CM-930722 and WC-CM-960515 (the “Charges”). Per that agreement Hyatt agrees to pay the gross sum of $15,000. (MPA, pg. 1, FN.1.) This settlement was negotiated contemporaneously with the class settlement by the same counsel. This settlement (unlike the class settlement) contains a general release of claims. Nothing in the agreement makes it contingent on approval of the class settlement (or vice versa). (Liss-Riordan Supp. Decl., ¶14 and Exhibit E thereto.) The Court requires further briefing as to this individual settlement agreement as explained further above.

            4. Adequacy of class counsel.  As indicated above, Class Counsel is experienced in class actions. 

5. Superiority.  Given the size of the potential individual recovery, it would be impracticable to bring each Class Member's claim as an individual claim.

Because the elements of class certification have been met, the class may be conditionally certified at this time.

ATTORNEY FEES AND COSTS

            California Rules of Court, rule 3.769(b) states: “Any agreement, express or implied, that has been entered into with respect to the payment of attorney fees or the submission of an application for the approval of attorney fees must be set forth in full in any application for approval of the dismissal or settlement of an action that has been certified as a class action.”

            Ultimately, the award of attorney fees is made by the Court at the fairness hearing, using the lodestar method with a multiplier, if appropriate. (PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095-1096; Ramos v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc. (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 615, 625-626; Ketchum III v. Moses (2000) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132-1136.) In common fund cases, the Court may utilize the percentage method, cross-checked by the lodestar. (Laffitte v. Robert Half Int’l, Inc. (2016) 1 Cal.5th 480, 503.) Despite any agreement by the parties to the contrary, “the court has an independent right and responsibility to review the attorney fee provision of the settlement agreement and award only so much as it determined reasonable.” (Garabedian v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Company (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 123, 128.)

             The question of whether class counsel is entitled to $35,000 (<33%) in attorneys’ fees and costs will be addressed at the fairness hearing when class counsel brings a noticed motion for attorney fees. Counsel should also be prepared to justify any costs sought by detailing how such costs were incurred.

BACKGROUND

This is a wage and hour class action.  On September 4, 2024, Plaintiff filed a wage-and-hour class action lawsuit in the Superior Court of California, County of Los Angeles. The lawsuit alleged violations of: (a) Labor Code sections 510 and 1198 (unpaid overtime); (b) Labor Code sections 226.7 and 512(a) (unpaid meal period premiums); (c) Labor Code section 226.7 (unpaid rest period premiums); (d) Labor Code sections 1194 and 1197 (unpaid minimum wages); (e) Labor Code sections 201 and 202 (final wages not timely paid); (f) Labor Code section 226(a) (non-compliant wage statements); (g) Labor Code sections 2800 and 2802 (unreimbursed business expenses); and (h) Business & Professions Code section 17200, et seq.

On October 29, 2024, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint that removed the seven (7) causes of action, leaving only a cause of action for violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200, et seq.

On June 25, 2025, in line with the Settlement, Plaintiff filed the Operative Second Amended Complaint adjusting the “class” definition and reasserting the seven (7) causes of action that were removed by the First Amended Complaint.

Counsel represents that the parties engaged in investigation and discovery wherein: 1) Defendant produced documents relating to the policies, practices, and procedures regarding reimbursement of business expenses, paying nonexempt employees for all hours worked, and meal and rest breaks along with payroll, timekeeping, and operational policies; 2) Plaintiff reviewed time records, pay records, and information relating to the size and scope of the Class, as well as data enabling Plaintiff to understand the number of Workweeks; and 3) Plaintiff located and interviewed Class Members who worked for Defendant throughout the Class Period.

On June 17, 2025, the Parties attended mediation with the mediator Lisa Klerman that resulted in a settlement via a mediator’s proposal. A fully executed Settlement Agreement was filed with the Court on July 23, 2025 attached to the Declaration of Douglas Han (“Han Decl.”) ISO Preliminary Approval as Exhibit 2.

Preliminary Approval was granted on February 18, 2026.  Notice was given to the Class Members as ordered. (See Declaration of Taylor Mitzner (“Mitzner Decl.”).)

Now before the Court is the motion for final approval of the settlement agreement.

C.     Attorney Fees and Costs

Class Counsel, Justice Law Corporation, requests $145,000  (33%) in fees and litigation costs and expenses in the amount of $23,969.31 to Class Counsel. (Han Decl. ISO Final Approval, ¶¶8, 22.)

 The Settlement provides for attorney's fees up to $145,000 (33%) and costs of $25,000 (Settlement Agreement, ¶C.2.a). The class was provided notice of a maximum requested award amount of $145,000 (1/3) attorney's fees up and $25,000 for costs as requested awards and none objected. (Mitzner Decl., ¶11 and Exhibit A thereto.)

 “Courts recognize two methods for calculating attorney fees in civil class actions: the lodestar/multiplier method and the percentage of recovery method.”  (Wershba at 254.)  Here, class counsel requests attorney fees using the percentage method, as cross checked by lodestar.

 The fee request represents 33% of the gross settlement amount which is the average generally awarded in class actions.  See In re Consumer Privacy Cases (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 545, 558, fn. 13 (“Empirical studies show that, regardless whether the percentage method or the lodestar method is used, fee awards in class actions average around one-third of the recovery.”).
