Case Number: 23SMCV04699    Hearing Date: July 6, 2026    Dept: I

This is a motion to tax costs.  The court notes that some aspects of the motion are so trivial the court wonders why the issue is even raised, such as the request for certain filing fees or service of process.  The court has looked at those items, and they are legitimate.  The motion is DENIED in those respects.

 

There are two other areas, though, and they are more significant.  One is the request for expert fees.  The fees requested are $7030.90.  Initially, it appeared that this was to be based on a 998 offer, but plaintiff is not so claiming.  Rather, plaintiff claims that expert fees are recoverable under the Song-Beverly Act as a matter of course irrespective of whether a 998 offer was rejected by the defense.  The court tends to agree that under the Act, expert fees are recoverable under Jensen v. BMW of North America, Inc. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112, whether or not there is a 998.  The problem that the court has is that plaintiff retained two different experts to do an examination of the vehicle.  The second expert’s examination was not done on notice and was long before the scheduled trial.  In scope, it appears to be the same as the initial inspection.  Plaintiff’s explanation is that the expert’s inspection was needed because the lease was almost up and plaintiff was going to have to surrender the vehicle.  The point of the examination was to document the vehicle’s condition at the time it was surrendered.  The fear was that once the vehicle was in defendant’s possession, it might be changed in some way and evidence would be lost.  The court’s concern is that there was no effort made to coordinate.  There was no mutual exchange of experts at the time or offer to do so.  There was no suggestion that the original inspection, done early in the case, could not have been sufficient.  There is just no showing that the second inspection was either reasonable or necessary, and the court is inclined to GRANT the motion as to the second expert but DENY as to the first.

 

The next aspect is mediation.  Defendant contends that the service fee for the mediator is not recoverable as of right.  Plaintiff says that it is reasonable and mediations are favored.  In reply, defendant suggests that this was a two case mediation, but plaintiff is seeking the full recovery in both cases—in essence a double recovery.  The court will inquire, but that is plainly improper if true.  At a minimum, the court would GRANT the motion in the amount of $520, which is half the mediation cost.  The court notes, though, that if this is true, the issue almost certainly could have been avoided with a phone call.

 

Finally, there is an “appearance” fee for counsel to make a special appearance at a CMC.  That is not a cost, so the motion is GRANTED as to that $190.  (That said, it might be recoverable as a fee.)

 

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED IN PART AND DENIED IN PART.

 

The court notes that this issue ought to have been resolved by discussion, not motion.


Case Number: 23SMCV06010    Hearing Date: July 6, 2026    Dept: I

The court has now read even the supplemental document Hart-Flaherty has filed.  For the reasons stated earlier, the court does not agree with Hart-Flaherty.  In brief, Hart-Flaherty claims that the property from which plaintiff is trying to evict her was fraudulently taken.  She asserts that in fact she owns the property, or at least has an ownership interest in it.  That interest stems from a marriage settlement between her and her former spouse, Flaherty.  The agreement, which purports to give her a 50% interest in the property, was (to the best of this court’s knowledge) never formally recorded and title never formally transferred; title remained in Flaherty’s name alone.  According to plaintiff, Flaherty later signed a deed of trust in support of a loan.  However, Hart-Flaherty contends that the documents surrounding that transaction were forged or otherwise falsified, even though they were recorded.  Later, the trustee foreclosed on the loan and the property was sold.  Plaintiff now holds written title and seeks to evict Hart-Flaherty, who resides there.  Because Hart-Flaherty claimed actual ownership of the property, the court refused plaintiff’s attempts to pursue this case along a regular UD timeline.  Instead, this case, which was filed at the very end of 2023, has proceeded along a normal litigation timeline.  During the interim, there have been a number of other cases involving plaintiff, Flaherty, and Hart-Flaherty.  The cases largely concerned a nearby property in which Hart-Flaherty is not claiming ownership.  But the issue in that case involved an easement held in favor of the property here at issue.  Hart-Flaherty attempted to intervene in that case on the ground that she owned the property holding the easement rights.  That request was rejected on the merits because the court found that in fact she did not hold such ownership rights; her claim was found to be insufficient to overcome the very strong presumption favoring written title.  There is another case that originated in bankruptcy that was also adjudicated against Hart-Flaherty that is now pending before the Supreme Court in that Hart-Flaherty has filed a petition for a writ of certiorari upon which the Court has not yet ruled.  In the meantime, the court has set the UD case for trial.  Plaintiff contends that the issue of ownership has been resolved and is not a part of the instant case.

 

The court must divide two questions.  The first is whether Hart-Flaherty in fact owns the property.  To that end, the court believes that prior courts have adjudicated the question and it has been resolved against her, leading to collateral estoppel.  The court will, however, review and hear whatever evidence Hart-Flaherty has of her actual ownership and consider it given that those resolutions appear to have been on motion rather than full trial.  At the end of the day, if there is truly clear and convincing evidence (which is the standard to challenge written title) that she holds title, the court will not dismiss it out of hand.  But that is different from evidence that Flaherty was defrauded out of his ownership or things of that ilk.  This first issue goes to Hart-Flaherty’s personal ownership in the property.  She has had well over two years to litigate the case on that question, and the court will not delay further, but it will hear the evidence if she has it. 

 

The second issue is whether there was fraud concerning the transactions with Flaherty.  As the court has previously explained, were Flaherty attempting to litigate the question, he would have certain rights to attempt to establish that ownership to the extent not already adjudicated elsewhere.  And indeed, he was named as a defendant in this case but elected to default.  The court knows he was aware of this case because he was present on the phone during various hearings involving Hart-Flaherty.  Because he is in default, he cannot assert his rights; that was an election that he made, and Hart-Flaherty has no right to challenge it.  Under the law, Hart-Flaherty’s rights are very circumscribed when the argument is that although she might not own the property, there are flaws in plaintiff’s title.  Generally speaking, in unlawful detainer actions the defendant will not be heard to challenge the plaintiff’s title on the theory that another party really is the true owner if the title appears regular on its face.  Hart-Flaherty will not be able to make that argument either unless she is able to produce as a live witness the notary who Hart-Flaherty claims perjured herself in notarizing a document that in fact was never signed and that plaintiff was aware of the fraud and participated in it.  If the notary actually appears in court and so states, and if there is evidence that plaintiff here was a part of that fraud, the court will not reject that out of hand either.  If Hart-Flaherty cannot do that, though, then that is not going to be before the court.  

 

The bottom line is that Hart-Flaherty’s real problem is Flaherty, not the court, and not even plaintiff.  Flaherty should have changed title on the property after the marriage settlement, but he did not do so.  Flaherty should have brought the issue to the attention of the potential lenders at the time, but he did not do so.  Flaherty should have challenged plaintiff’s ownership in the instant case if he felt he was defrauded out of the property and that the deed was forged, but he did not do so.  At this point, the court has done all it will do.  The UD case will go to trial.  Because the court is presently engaged in another trial, this case will begin at 2:30 today.  (The jury ought to begin deliberating in that case by lunch.)

 


Case Number: 24SMCV02107    Hearing Date: July 6, 2026    Dept: I

This is part of the motion for reconsideration.  Plaintiff sued defendant but would not respond to discovery requests.  Even after the court issued orders compelling responses, plaintiff ignored the orders.  That led the court to grant defendant’s motion for terminating sanctions.  The court stayed its ruling, though, because it was based on the notion that plaintiff had abandoned the case, which is why plaintiff had not provided discovery or even opposed the motion for terminating sanctions.  Shortly thereafter, plaintiff moved for reconsideration.  Her counsel said it was counsel’s fault due to a medical condition.  At the prior hearing, the court agreed that by falling on the proverbial sword, counsel had established that a terminating sanction was too harsh.  The court invited the defense, though, to suggest more limited sanctions that would mitigate any prejudice caused by the delay.  The defense filed a supplemental opposition.  In it, the defense suggests issue and evidentiary sanctions that are the functional equivalent of terminating sanctions.  For example, the defense seeks issue sanctions deeming the following issues to be resolved: defendant did not breach the standard of care, defendant was not negligent, anything defendant did caused no harm, defendant’s conduct led to no special damages, defendant’s conduct did not cause pain and suffering, defendant did not cause emotional distress, defendant did not contribute to bodily injury, and it goes on.  There is no effort made to link the sanction to the prejudice the defense suffered.  The court can only conclude that defendant has not been prejudiced other than the fact that the trial is upon us.  Oddly, plaintiff did not respond to defendant’s filing.  The court will inquire whether in fact plaintiff has abandoned the case.

 

Therefore, the court will award no evidentiary sanctions nor issue sanctions.  The court will award monetary sanctions for the original motion and the opposition to the motion for reconsideration, but not for the supplemental brief, in an amount to be determined by further motion. 

 

There is obvious prejudice to the defense given the upcoming trial.  Therefore, the FSC and trial dates are VACATED.  The court will hold a TSC on August 24, 2026, at 8:30 am.  The court makes no promises as to when the matter will be restored to the trial calendar.  Right now, the end of the trial line is April 2028.  The discovery cut off for the moving defendant will be tied to the new trial date.  In all other respects, fact discovery is tied to the original trial date, but expert discovery will be tied to the new trial date.  If plaintiff can establish good cause to re-open discovery generally, this order is without prejudice to a motion to obtain such an order.  To the extent plaintiff is or might be entitled to prejudgment interest, such interest will not accrue between now and the time of trial.

Case Number: 25SMCV06086    Hearing Date: July 6, 2026    Dept: I

This is a motion to tax costs.  Defendant filed an untimely opposition.  Plaintiff filed a reply.  The court will consider the opposition, but the late filing is indicative of sloppy practice by the defendant, which is mirrored in the lack of any justification for the aspect of costs under attack.

 

The issue here is attorneys’ fees.  There is also an aspect of electronic filing fees.  As to the latter, it appears that there is a typo on the cost memo, and the court is inclined to DENY that aspect of the motion.  That is a $439.50 issue.

 

As to attorneys’ fees, the court is inclined to GRANT.  The court sees no basis for a fee award.  Defendant argues that somehow CCP section 1033 allows for such a recovery, but the defense is wrong.  There is no statutory basis for fees that the court can see.  There might in theory be a contractual basis for one, although it has not been pointed out to the court.  But even if there were, plaintiff’s decision to dismiss the case would mean that such fees cannot be recovered under Civil Code section 1717.  Accordingly, as to the fees, the motion is GRANTED.  Defendant never should have sought the cost and never should have opposed the motion.

Case Number: 26SMCV01214    Hearing Date: July 6, 2026    Dept: I

The court will inquire.  The court had been informed that the parties had settled their differences on a global level.  If that is the case, then this case would be resolved as well and there is no reason for the court to rule.  If the settlement has fallen apart, then the court will ask the status of the DV case.  The court’s understanding is that the DV hearing had been taken off calendar due to the mediation and potential settlement, but that until the settlement becomes final, the DV is pending although not calendared. One would think that if the settlement fell apart, the DV would be speedily restored to the calendar.

 

The court notes that the DV case and the instant case have a lot of similarities.  Many of the instances of abuse that underlie the DV case are at the heart of the instant case.  But even so, the cases are not the same.  First, a DV case typically is forward looking.  It does not award damages for the injury; it results (if successful) in an injunction to stop any further acts.  While one supposes that this court could also issue an injunction, the court agrees with the defense that the better place for that is the DV court—which can and will hear the case very speedily and issue appropriate relief if warranted.  Where the DV case is going to be tried very soon, it makes sense to abate this case because the timing will be so short and the court would not want the issues or procedures in this case to interfere with the DV case.  But “abate” means something different to this court than it does to defendant.  The court understands that if there is going to be a DV trial in a few weeks, it probably makes some sense to let that trial go forward and pause this case to so allow.  Otherwise, this court runs the risk of interfering with the DV matter through discovery issues or the like.  But if the timing is not like that, then there is no reason in “abatement” for this court to stay its hand.  Trust the court on this.   The trial in this case will not occur until long after the DV case has been tried, and the court has various tools available to it to ensure that there are not inconsistent judgments.  Further, while the issues in the DV case and this case are very similar (as to the facts giving rise to the actions), they are not identical. 

 

There is no “concurrent jurisdiction” problem.  That is another way of saying abatement, as a practical matter.  The Family Law action in no way would trigger the doctrine.  The DV matter might, but the court discussed that above.

 

And finally, there is no reason to abate the defamation cause of action.  Or at least the court cannot see it.

 

So the court will inquire as to the status of the settlement.  If the settlement has fallen apart, the court will inquire as to the DV matter and the timing of that case.  The court, as a general matter, does view that as the far better avenue for injunctive relief.  Accordingly, as a practical matter, the court will STAY this case for a short time to allow the DV case to be tried if a trial is on the horizon other than as to the defamation issue.  If not, the court will inquire as to why not.
