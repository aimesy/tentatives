DEPARTMENT 400 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 20STCV31478    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO COMPEL

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Progress Place v. Eva Cepeida, Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 20STCV31478, set for hearing on July 27, 2026. Jose Rivera, et al. (collectively, Plaintiffs) move for an order compelling Eva Cepeida (Defendant) to comply with the statements of compliance she served but did not honor. Plaintiffs also seek monetary, evidence, and issue sanctions. Defendant opposes the motion. The motion is granted in part.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

“If a party filing a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling . . .  thereafter fails to permit the inspection, copying, testing, or sampling in accordance with that party’s statement of compliance, the demanding party may move for an order compelling compliance.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.320, subd. (a).) The Court shall impose a monetary sanction against “any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel compliance with a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Id., § 2031.320, subd. (b).) If a party then fails to obey an order compelling compliance, issue, evidence, or terminating sanctions may be imposed. (Id., § 2031.320, subd. (c).)

 

“For discovery purposes, information is relevant if it ‘might reasonably assist a party in evaluating the case, preparing for trial, or facilitating settlement.’ [Citation]. Admissibility is not the test and information, unless privileged, is discoverable if it might reasonably lead to admissible evidence. [Citation] These rules are applied liberally in favor of discovery.” (Gonzales v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1539, 1546.) “[A] party seeking to compel production of records from a nonparty must articulate specific facts justifying the discovery sought; it may not rely on mere generalities.” (County of Los Angeles v. Superior Court (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 621, 639 (Los Angeles).) There is a “more limited scope of discovery available from nonparties.” (Ibid.)

 

B.     Discussion

 

            Plaintiffs contend that Defendant has disregarded this Court’s order in failing to provide certain discovery. On October 28, 2025, this Court compelled Defendant to provide responses to Plaintiffs’ requests for production, set one (RFPs) and imposed monetary sanctions in the amount of $660. On June 1, 2026, the Court imposed further monetary sanctions on Defendant in the amount of $5,660 because Defendant failed to provide responses as required or pay the previous sanctions. In making this order, the Court found that Defendant still possessed the ability to comply with certain discovery demands by seeking out documents from third-parties.

 

            Plaintiffs argue that Defendant has still failed to produce the required documents. Moreover, they assert that Defendant’s opposition is untimely and that her declaration is unsigned and inadmissible. The Court previously denied Defendant’s ex-parte application to continue this hearing, stating that it would consider a late opposition upon a showing of good cause. Defendant’s opposition is indeed late by 5 court days. And Defendant’s declarations are unsigned and fail to explain the untimeliness or her briefing on this matter. Thus, the Court declines to consider Defendant’s late opposition or unsigned declarations. In any event, Plaintiff has established a basis to conclude that prior representations are false. For example, the Cruz Group payments over 14 months were explained as a payment for a one month tenancy, which does not appear to be correct.  There are numerous other examples offered by Plaintiff in his Reply Brief.  Indeed, it appears that Defendant used Artificial Intelligence to draft the opposition as there are passages in the Opposition which contain guidance to counsel on how to frame the issues.  The reliance on A.I. may explain the absence of satisfactory explanations for the errors and inconsistencies in the discovery responses.  Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ motion is granted.

 

            With respect to the specific sanctions to be imposed, the Court notes that evidence, issue, and terminating sanctions are only available when an order pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.320 is violated. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.320, subd. (c).) Thus, the Court is only empowered to impose further monetary sanctions at this time, as Defendant has not disobeyed a previous order compelling compliance and has only failed to comply with an order to compel initial discovery. The Court credits counsel’s hourly rate of $800. (Biggins Decl. ¶ 13.) However, given counsel’s claimed experience, the Court finds the 28 hours claimed with respect to the present motion and reply to be excessive. (Ibid.) Instead, the Court credits counsel with 5 hours for drafting the present motion, 2 hours for reviewing Defendant’s opposition and drafting the reply, and 1 hour for attending the hearing on this matter, along with an additional $60 filing fee. Accordingly, $6,460 in sanctions are imposed on Defendant’s counsel ([$800 per hour] x [8 hours] + [$60 filing fee] = $6,460).

 

Conclusion:


            The Court grants Plaintiffs’ motion to compel compliance and imposes $6,460 on Defendant’s counsel.  Sanctions to be paid within 30 days.


Case Number: 23STCV09114    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO ENFORCE SETTLEMENT

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Benjamin Moreno v. Pacific Reproductive Medical Center Inc., et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 23STCV09114, set for hearing on July 27, 2026. Pacific Reproductive Medical Center, Inc. and Rifaat Salem (collectively, Defendants) move for an order enforcing a PAGA settlement agreement between them and Benjamin Moreno (Plaintiff) pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6. The motion is denied.


A.    Procedural History


Plaintiff filed the present action against Defendants on April 24, 2023. On April 11, 2025, the parties signed a settlement agreement. The Court denied Plaintiff’s motion for approval of the settlement agreement on July 17, 2025. Plaintiff did not cooperate with Defendants in attempting to reach an alternative settlement between then and the present hearing. (Davila Decl. ¶¶ 6–7.) Defendants filed the present motion to enforce the PAGA agreement on June 3, 2026. On June 30, 2026, the Court ordered the parties “to submit a declaration by July 17, 2026 describing (1) the efforts made to fulfill this cooperation obligation and (2) their respective positions on how the agreement may be amended to address the Court’s concerns.” Specifically, the Court had previously taken issue with the following: “(1) the definition of ‘aggrieved employees’ was used inconsistently in the settlement documentation and (2) plaintiff asked for an additional distribution from the settlement fund to be paid to lawyers who were not involved in the case, and the Court needed to understand why that was justified.”

 

Defendants represent that the parties conferred after the June 30 hearing. (Davila Decl. ¶¶ 4–5.) After those discussions, Defendants contend that the proposed notice to aggrieved employees can be altered to match the definition within the PAGA settlement agreement, resolving the Court’s first concern with the agreement language. (Id. ¶¶ 7–9.) Defendants additionally take no position on whether the fee request for Plaintiff’s counsel within the PAGA settlement should be approved but notes that counsel could submit a declaration substantiating its work on this matter for the sake of obtaining approval. (Id. ¶¶ 10–12.) However, Plaintiff’s counsel has refused to do so. (Id. ¶ 13.)

 

Plaintiff simply maintains that he has no obligation to cooperate with Defendants under Code of Civil Procedure 664.6 because no judgment was ever entered by the Court based on an approved settlement. (Gutierrez Decl. ¶ 4–7.) Additionally, Plaintiff argues that circumstances have changed since the parties entered the settlement agreement because this Court imposed issue and evidence sanctions against Defendants on August 21, 2024. (Id. ¶ 22.) Plaintiff has a currently pending motion for summary judgment relying on these sanctions.


B.     Legal Standard

 

“If parties to pending litigation stipulate, in a writing signed by the parties outside of the presence of the court or orally before the court, for settlement of the case, or part thereof, the court, upon motion, may enter judgment pursuant to the terms of the settlement. If requested by the parties, the court may retain jurisdiction over the parties to enforce the settlement until performance in full of the terms of the settlement.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 664.6; see Mesa RHF Partners, L.P. v. City of Los Angeles (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 913, 917.)

 

C.     Discussion 

  

 Defendants argue that although the Court refused to approve the PAGA settlement, the parties still entered into a binding, enforceable agreement. Specifically, Defendants contend that the settlement agreement required the parties to negotiate a final agreement that would be approved by the Court, while Plaintiff maintains that the agreement is not binding because the Court did not approve it. All parties had signed the agreement, along with the PAGA addendum, by May 8, 2025. The Court denied Plaintiff’s motion to approve the agreement on July 17, 2025.

 

The agreement provides that after entry of judgment, the Court would retain jurisdiction over the parties to enforce the agreement. However, judgment was never entered in this matter, as the Court declined to enforce the agreement. Defendants point to section 6.3 of the agreement, which provided that Plaintiff and Defendants would expeditiously work together to modify the agreement should the Court not grant approval. But section 664.6 does not grant this Court authority to compel parties to reach certain settlement terms. (Weddington Products, Inc. v.  Flick (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 793, 797.) This Court’s role is limited to enforcing binding settlement agreements. The Court did not approve the relevant agreement here, and Defendants have not set forth a basis for further intervention. Accordingly, Defendants’ motion is denied.

 

D.    Conclusion

 

The Court denies the motion.  The Court will reschedule the other pending motions.

Case Number: 24STCV33648    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO COMPEL

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Ramon Rincon v. General Motors, LLC, Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 24STCV33648, set for hearing on July 27, 2026. Ramon Rincon (Plaintiff) moves for an order compelling General Motors, LLC (Defendant) to produce its person most knowledgeable (PMK) for deposition and produce the records identified in the deposition notice. Defendant opposes the motion. Plaintiff’s motion is denied.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

An oral deposition must be scheduled for at least 10 days after the service of the deposition notice. (Id., § 2025.270, subd. (a).) “If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action . . . without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document . . . the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document . . . described in the deposition notice.” (Id., 2025.450, subd. (a).) The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration or declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about their nonappearance. (Id., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Plaintiff initially served a notice of deposition on Defendant’s PMK along with a request for production on March 7, 2025, setting a deposition date of March 19 and providing an invitation to accommodate Defendant regarding the deposition date. (Barry Decl. ¶ 4.) On March 12, Defendant served objections to the deposition notice. (Id. ¶ 6.) Throughout April and May 2025, the parties met and conferred regarding Defendant’s objections but failed to secure a different date for deposition. (Id. ¶¶ 7–11.)

 

A motion to compel deposition pursuant to section 2025.450 “shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).) A meet and confer declaration “shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Id., § 2016.040.) Here, Plaintiff has not demonstrated sufficient meet and confer efforts. The last communication cited between the parties was from over a year ago in May 2025 and did not mention the possibility of a motion to compel being filed. The Court does not find that over a year of silence between the parties constitutes a good faith attempt at informal resolution. Thus, Plaintiff’s motion is improper. Accordingly, the motion is denied.

 

C.     Conclusion


The Court denies Plaintiff’s motion.

Case Number: 25STCV17509    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO COMPEL

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Cornell Fontaine McTier v. Robert Aguirre Casanova, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 25STCV17509, set for hearing on July 27, 2026. Spectrum Pacific West LLC (Defendant) moves for an order compelling Cornell Fontaine McTier (Plaintiff) to present for deposition within 15 days. Plaintiff opposes the motion. Defendant’s motion is granted.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

An oral deposition must be scheduled for at least 10 days after the service of the deposition notice. (Id., § 2025.270, subd. (a).) “If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action . . . without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document . . . the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document . . . described in the deposition notice.” (Id., 2025.450, subd. (a).) The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration or declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about their nonappearance. (Id., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).)

 

B.     Discussion

 

As a threshold matter, Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s motion is procedurally defective for multiple reasons. First, Plaintiff asserts that Defendant’s motion is premised on the incorrect statute; however, Defendant’s notice of motion properly references Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.450, not solely section 2032.310 like Plaintiff suggests. Thus, the notice properly appraises Plaintiff of the contents of the motion as required under section 1010.

 

Second, Plaintiff argues that section 2025.450 is inapplicable because Plaintiff never failed to appear for a deposition. Indeed, motions to compel pursuant to that section apply if a party fails to appear for a deposition without having served a valid objection. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (a).) Here, Defendant noticed Plaintiff’s deposition unilaterally on April 16, 2026. (Gezukarayan Decl. ¶ 2.) An amended notice set the deposition for May 28, 2026. (Ibid.) Plaintiff canceled this deposition 2 days before it was set to occur, and it was taken off calendar. This did not constitute a proper objection under section 2025.410 as the objection was lodged less than three days before the date of deposition. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.410, subd. (a).) Thus, Plaintiff failed to appear for deposition without lodging a proper objection and section 2025.450 is the proper section to invoke.

 

Third, Plaintiff argues that Defendant failed to meet and confer in good faith. However, Defendant properly requested on June 4, 2026, that Plaintiff provide alternative dates for deposition within two weeks. (Morris Decl. ¶ 4.) Plaintiff never responded to this communication. (Gezukarayan Decl. ¶ 6.) Thus, Defendant’s meet and confer efforts were sufficient.

 

Fourth, Plaintiff argues that the motion is moot because Plaintiff offered Defendant potential deposition dates preceding the hearing. However, those dates — July 14, 20, 22, 23, and 24 — were offered on June 30, 2026, twelve days after the present motion was filed. No deposition has occurred in advance of this hearing.

 

Plaintiff offers no further substantive argument in opposition. Plaintiff failed to appear for deposition without serving a valid objection and did not timely respond to Defendant’s efforts at informal resolution. Thus, the present motion to compel deposition attendance is proper. Accordingly, Defendant’s motion is granted.

 

If a motion pursuant to section 2025.450 is granted “the court shall impose a monetary sanction . . . in favor of the party who noticed the deposition and against the deponent . . . unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (g)(1).) Plaintiff’s arguments were meritless and the Court does not find that he has acted with substantial justification. Thus, sanctions are appropriate. The Court credits Defendant’s counsel’s hourly rate of $350. (Morris Decl. ¶ 7.) Given the present motion’s simplicity and the lack of any reply brief from Defendant, the Court only credits counsel with 2 hours for drafting the motion and declaration and 1 hour for attending the hearing on this matter. (Ibid.) Additionally, the Court credits the $60 filing fee for the present motion. (Ibid.) Accordingly, $1,110 are imposed on Plaintiff’s counsel ([$350 per hour] x [3 hours] + [$60 filing fee] = $1,110).

 

C.     Conclusion


The Court grants Defendant’s motion and imposes $1,110 in monetary sanctions on Plaintiff’s counsel.

Case Number: 25STCV29844    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 400

DEMURRER WITHOUT MOTION TO STRIKE

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Mamak Eatesam v. Dignity Health, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 25STCV29844, set for hearing on March 17, 2026. SoCal Hospitalists (SoCal) demurs to the second, third, and fifth through ninth causes of action asserted against it in Mamak Eatesam’s (Plaintiff) first amended complaint (FAC). Separately, Babak Gachpaz demurs to Plaintiff’s tenth cause of action. Plaintiff opposes both demurrers. SoCal’s demurrer is sustained and Gachpaz’s is overruled. Plaintiff is granted leave to amend.


A.    Legal Standard


A demurrer is sustained where “[t]he pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., 430.10, subd. (e).) “A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations in a complaint.” (Yalung v. State (2023) 98 Cal.App.5th 71, 80.) In reviewing a complaint’s legal sufficiency, a court will treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of law. (Esparza v. Kaweah Delta Dist. Hospital (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 547, 552.) It is well settled that a “demurrer lies only for defects appearing on the face of the complaint[.]” (Stevens v. Superior Court (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 594, 601.) “We not only treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but also give the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Guclimane Co. v. Stewart Tit. Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, the complaint must be construed liberally by drawing reasonable inferences from the facts pleaded. (Wilner v. Sunset Life Ins. Co. (2000) 78 Cal.App.4th 952, 958.)

 

When ruling on a demurrer, a court may only consider the complaint’s allegations or matters which may be judicially noticed. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The Court may not consider any other extrinsic evidence or judge the credibility of the allegations pleaded or the difficulty a plaintiff may have in proving his allegations. (Ion Equipment Corporation v. Nelson (1980) 110 Cal.App.3d 868, 881.) A demurrer is properly sustained only when the complaint, liberally construed, fails to state facts sufficient to constitute any cause of action. (Kramer v. Intuit Inc. (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 574, 578.)

 

When a demurrer is sustained or motion to strike is granted, the Court determines whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318).  When a plaintiff “has pleaded the general set of facts upon which his cause of action is based,” the court should give the plaintiff an opportunity to amend his complaint, since plaintiff should not “be deprived of his right to maintain his action on the ground that his pleadings were defective for lack of particulars.” (Reed v. Norman (1957) 152 Cal.App.2d 892, 900.)

 

B.     Discussion

 

SoCal and Gachpaz demur to the following causes of action alleged against it in Plaintiff’s complaint on the grounds that Plaintiff has not pled sufficient facts to constitute any cause of action: (1) second cause of action for harassment on the basis of gender in violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA); (2) third cause of action for discrimination on the basis of gender in violation of the FEHA; (3) fifth cause of action for aiding and abetting harassment, discrimination, and retaliation in violation of the FEHA; (4) sixth cause of action for retaliation in violation of the FEHA; (5) seventh cause of action for whistleblower retaliation in violation of Labor Code section 1102.5; (6) eighth cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of the FEHA; and (7) ninth cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).) Meanwhile, Gachpaz demurs to Plaintiff’s tenth cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress on the grounds that Plaintiff has not pled sufficient facts. (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff has not alleged an employer relationship with SoCal.

 

Specifically, SoCal argues that each cause of action alleged against it requires allegations that it was Plaintiff’s employer, allegations that Plaintiff has failed to assert. Indeed, Plaintiff’s second, third, fifth, sixth, and eighth causes of action, brought pursuant to the FEHA, require a showing that SoCal was Plaintiff’s employer. (See Vernon v. State of California (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 114, 123 (Vernon); Gov. Code, § 12940.) Likewise, Labor Code section 1102.5 prohibits an employer from retaliating against an employee. (Lab. Code, § 1102.5, subd. (b).) And Plaintiff’s claim for wrongful termination must arise out of an employer-employee relationship as well. (Koehrer v. Superior Court (1986) 181 Cal.App.3d 1155, 1166.) Thus, the Court must determine whether Plaintiff’s FAC sufficiently alleges that SoCal was her employer to determine whether demurrer is appropriate to all causes of action asserted against it.

 

In determining whether a sufficient employer-employee relationship exists for liability under the FEHA, Courts “consider the ‘totality of circumstances’ that reflect upon the nature of the work relationship of the parties, with emphasis upon the extent to which the defendant controls the plaintiff’s performance of employment duties.” (Vernon, supra, 116 Cal.App.4th at p. 124.) “Factors to be taken into account in assessing the relationship of the parties include payment of salary or other employment benefits and Social Security taxes, the ownership of the equipment necessary to performance of the job, the location where the work is performed, the obligation of the defendant to train the employee, the authority of the defendant to hire, transfer, promote, discipline or discharge the employee, the authority to establish work schedules and assignments, the defendant’s discretion to determine the amount of compensation earned by the employee, the skill required of the work performed and the extent to which it is done under the direction of a supervisor, whether the work is part of the defendant’s regular business operations, the skill required in the particular occupation, the duration of the relationship of the parties, and the duration of the plaintiff’s employment.” (Id. at p. 125.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that the conduct underlying her complaint occurred at SoCal’s place of business. (FAC ¶ 2.) Plaintiff alleges that all named defendants, including Defendant, directly and indirectly employed her, compelled, coerced, aided, and abetted the alleged harassment, and acted as agents of one another. (Id. ¶¶ 5–7.) She alleges that she was hired in 2019 by Defendant ProHealth Partners (ProHealth). (Id. ¶ 12(a).) Plaintiff further alleges that though she was hired by ProHealth, Dignity Health (Dignity) and its CEO Carolyn Caldwell maintained direct operational control over her employment. (Id. ¶ 12(b).) Plaintiff does not make similar allegations regarding SoCal. Rather, she alleges that on April 14, 2025, she received notice that Defendant would be taking over ProHealth’s duties and that this change effectively terminated her employment. (Id. ¶ 12(s).) She did not submit her CV to Defendant to be considered for a position. (Id. ¶ 12(u).)

 

Plaintiff has not alleged that SoCal was her employer under the standard set forth in Vernon. Plaintiff does not allege that SoCal ever paid her salary, conducted any training, established her work schedule or assignments, or even hired her. Plaintiff specifically alleges that SoCal’s engagement with the hospital at which she worked ended her employment. (Id. ¶ 12(s).) She specifically alleges that her employment with ProHealth was terminated when the new contract with SoCal officially took effect on July 20, 2025. (Id. ¶ 12(x).) Based on these allegations, there was no point in time at which SoCal employed Plaintiff. Thus, Plaintiff cannot state causes of action under the FEHA against SoCal. Similarly, Plaintiff’s other causes of action for whistleblower retaliation under the Labor Code and for wrongful termination fail because of the lack of any employment relationship between Plaintiff and SoCal.

 

Plaintiff argues that she was still entitled to FEHA protections as an applicant. (See Gov. Code, § 12940.) However, Plaintiff specifically alleges that she refused to apply to a job with SoCal. (FAC ¶ 12(u).) Thus, Plaintiff cannot claim liability under the FEHA as either an employee or applicant to SoCal. Accordingly, SoCal’s demurrer is sustained as to Plaintiff’s second, third, and fifth through ninth causes of action.

 

Gachpaz’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s tenth cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress is overruled.


Gachpaz argues that Plaintiff has not pled sufficient facts to constitute her tenth cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).) To prevail on such a claim, a plaintiff must demonstrate: “(1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant's outrageous conduct.” (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1050.) Here, Plaintiff alleges that Gachpaz engaged in extreme and outrageous conduct. (FAC ¶ 116.) Specifically, Plaintiff alleges that he invited her to a supposedly professional dinner but proceeded to make unwanted sexual advances, that he and his wife forced her to stay at their house, that he subjected her to months of harassment afterwards, and that after she told him to stop contacting her he sought to intimidate her in her office. (Id. ¶¶ 12(c)–(f).) This conduct could be considered outrageous given its intentionality and persistence. Plaintiff additionally alleges that he intended to cause emotional distress and that she suffered severe emotional distress and damages. (Id. ¶¶ 117–118.) Accordingly, Gachpaz’s demurrer is overruled as to Plaintiff’s tenth cause of action.

 

C.     Conclusion

 

The Court sustains SoCal’s demurrer. Gachpaz’s demurrer is overruled. Plaintiff is granted 30 days leave to amend.





DEMURRER WITHOUT MOTION TO STRIKE

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Mamak Eatesam v. Dignity Health, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 25STCV29844, set for hearing on July 27, 2026. Carolyn Caldwell (Defendant) demurs to Mamak Eatesam’s (Plaintiff) tenth cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress contained within her first amended complaint (FAC). Plaintiff opposes demurrer. Defendant’s demurrer is sustained. Plaintiff is granted leave to amend.


A.    Legal Standard


A demurrer is sustained where “[t]he pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., 430.10, subd. (e).) “A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations in a complaint.” (Yalung v. State (2023) 98 Cal.App.5th 71, 80.) In reviewing a complaint’s legal sufficiency, a court will treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of law. (Esparza v. Kaweah Delta Dist. Hospital (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 547, 552.) It is well settled that a “demurrer lies only for defects appearing on the face of the complaint[.]” (Stevens v. Superior Court (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 594, 601.) “We not only treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but also give the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Guclimane Co. v. Stewart Tit. Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, the complaint must be construed liberally by drawing reasonable inferences from the facts pleaded. (Wilner v. Sunset Life Ins. Co. (2000) 78 Cal.App.4th 952, 958.)

 

When ruling on a demurrer, a court may only consider the complaint’s allegations or matters which may be judicially noticed. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The Court may not consider any other extrinsic evidence or judge the credibility of the allegations pleaded or the difficulty a plaintiff may have in proving his allegations. (Ion Equipment Corporation v. Nelson (1980) 110 Cal.App.3d 868, 881.) A demurrer is properly sustained only when the complaint, liberally construed, fails to state facts sufficient to constitute any cause of action. (Kramer v. Intuit Inc. (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 574, 578.)

 

When a demurrer is sustained or motion to strike is granted, the Court determines whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318).  When a plaintiff “has pleaded the general set of facts upon which his cause of action is based,” the court should give the plaintiff an opportunity to amend his complaint, since plaintiff should not “be deprived of his right to maintain his action on the ground that his pleadings were defective for lack of particulars.” (Reed v. Norman (1957) 152 Cal.App.2d 892, 900.)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Defendant once again demurs to Plaintiff’s tenth cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress on the grounds that she has not pled sufficient facts after the Court previously sustained a demurrer on this basis. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).) To prevail on such a claim, a plaintiff must prove: “(1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant's outrageous conduct.” (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1050.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant engaged in extreme and outrageous conduct. (FAC ¶ 116.) Specifically, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant instructed her to not discuss the harassment by Gachpaz with her colleagues after being told that Plaintiff did not feel safe. (Id. ¶¶ 12(j), (k).) She also allegedly decided to have Plaintiff work alongside Gachpaz despite being told of the psychological impact it would have on her. (Id. ¶¶ 12(n), (o).) Additionally, Defendant took no action to stop harassment by Gachpaz’s wife. (Id. ¶ 12(p).) Finally, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant awarded a hospital contract to SoCal, headed by Gachpaz. (Id. ¶¶ 12(s), (t).) But such conduct alleged by Plaintiff — siding with an alleged harasser and awarding a contract — does not rise to the level of outrageous. Such management decisions are entitled to deference. (Lewin v. St. Joseph Hospital of Orange (1978) 82 Cal. App. 3d 368, 384.) Indeed, “a simple pleading of personnel management activity is insufficient to support a claim of intentional infliction of emotional distress, even if improper motivation is alleged.” (Janken v. GM Hughes Electronics (1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 55, 80) The Court finds that Plaintiff has not pled sufficient facts to allege outrageous conduct. Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer is sustained as to Plaintiff’s tenth cause of action.

 

C.     Conclusion

 

The Court sustains Defendant’s demurrer. Plaintiff is granted 30 days leave to amend.


Case Number: 26STCV16707    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO SEAL

 

The Court tenders the following tentative ruling in the matter Mohammed Shaikh v. Quinn Emanuel Urquhart & Sullivan, LLP, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 26STCV16707, set for hearing on July 27, 2026. Mohammad Shaikh (Plaintiff) moves for an order to file his opposition to Quinn Emanuel Urquhart & Sullivan, et al.’s (collectively, Defendants) motion to compel arbitration and certain supporting documents under seal. The motion is unopposed. The motion is granted.

 

A.    Legal Standard


Pursuant to California Rule of Court, rule 2.550(d), the court may order that a record be sealed if it finds facts that establish the following: (1) There exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) The overriding interest supports sealing the record; (3) A substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and (5) No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest.

A “reasoned decision about sealing or unsealing records cannot be made without . . . (1) identifying the specific information claimed to be entitled to such treatment; (2) identifying the nature of the harm threatened by disclosure; and (3) identifying and accounting for countervailing considerations. The burden of presenting information sufficient to accomplish the first two steps is logically placed on the party seeking the sealing of the documents, who is presumptively in the best position to know what disclosures will harm and how.” (H.B. Fuller Co. v. Doe (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 879, 894.) The burden of showing there are less restrictive means of achieving the overriding interest lies with one who is seeking disclosure of the allegedly confidential information.  (NBC Subsidiary (KNBC-TV), Inc. v. Superior Court (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1178, 1224.) 

 

B.     Discussion


Plaintiff has met all the requirements for sealing under rule 2.550(d). First, Plaintiff demonstrates that there is an overriding interest in sealing the relevant records because of the existence of a confidentiality agreement between the parties. Second, he argues that this interest supports sealing the records because Defendants would suffer financial hardship if they were released. Third, Plaintiff argues that Defendant will suffer prejudice if the opposition brief remains unsealed. Fourth, Plaintiff contends that the proposed sealing is narrowly tailored, and Defendants have not offered any alternative means of achieving the same result. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion is granted.

 

Conclusion:


            The Court grants the motion.
