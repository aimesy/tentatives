I.               BACKGROUND 

Plaintiff Pierce P. Shaad (“Plaintiff”) brings this action against La Casa Royale (JLP Enterprises) (“JLP”), Paula Dresser (“Dresser”), and Leonard Levine and Lisa Levine as trustees of the Levine Family Trust dated April 2, 2019. Plaintiff alleges he entered into a lease agreement with JLP wherein they agreed to specific parking spaces to accommodate Plaintiff’s physical limitations. Plaintiff alleges JLP, through Dresser, retaliated against Plaintiff for his delay in rent payments during the Covid-19 pandemic by changing his parking spaces to significantly farther spots.

Plaintiff moves for leave to amend the Complaint to add a cause of action.

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On February 1, 2024, Plaintiff filed a Complaint alleging (1) Breach of Lease, (2) Breach of Quiet Enjoyment, (3) Retaliation, and (4) Retaliation (Civil Code § 1942.5(d).)

On March 25, 2024, Defendants filed an Answer and Cross-Complaint.

On July 3, 2025, Plaintiff designated Joel Rotham as Doe 1.

On July 18, 2025, Plaintiff corrected the name of La Casa Royale (JLP Enterprises), a business entity of unknown form to JLP Enterprises, a general partnership.

On September 18, 2025, Defendants filed their Answer.

On July 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion for Leave.

On August 11, 2026, Defendants filed an Opposition.

On August 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Reply.

III.         LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure § 473(a) [1] provides: “The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 473(a)(1); see also Code Civ. Proc. § 576.)¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

The court has broad discretion to permit amendments to pleadings, and “the court’s discretion will usually be exercised liberally to permit amendment of the pleadings.” (Howard v. County of San Diego (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 1422, 1428.)¿“The policy favoring amendment is so strong that it is a rare case in which denial of leave to amend can be justified.”¿(Id.)¿“If the motion to amend is timely made and the granting of the motion will not prejudice the opposing party, it is error to refuse permission to amend.”¿(Morgan v. Superior Court (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 527, 530.) Prejudice includes “delay in trial, loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation.” (Solit v. Tokai Bank, Ltd. New York Branch (1999) 68 Cal.App.4th 1435, 1448.)¿¿¿¿ 

A motion for leave to amend a pleading must comply with the procedural requirements of Rules of Court, rule 3.1324, under which the motion must include (1) a copy of the proposed and numbered amendment, (2) specifications by reference to pages and lines the allegations that would be deleted and added, and (3) a declaration specifying the effect, necessity and propriety of the amendments, date of discovery and reasons for delay. (See Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a), (b).)

IV.          ANALYSIS

Plaintiff seeks leave to add a fifth cause of action for defendants’ violations of Los Angeles City Ordinance 187109 (article 5.3).[2] (See Notice at 1:26-27.) Plaintiff contends he first learned Dresser did not double the security deposit for any other tenant besides Plaintiff during Dresser’s deposition on April 10, 2025. (See Mot. at 3:13; Declaration of Jeffrey A. Cohen filed July 16, 2026 (“Cohen Decl.”), ¶ 3.) Plaintiff explains Jessica Dresser confirmed in her deposition on June 9, 2026, that Dresser swapped her parking spaces with Plaintiff’s. (See Mot. at 3:19-22.) Plaintiff also attests he learned after the filing of this action his rent was raised in a greater amount than it was for other tenants. (See Declaration of Pierce P. Shaad filed July 16, 2026, ¶ 4.)

On April 27, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel met and conferred with Defendants’ counsel about stipulating to amending the Complaint, but Defendants declined the request. (See Mot. at 3:26-4:9.) Plaintiff attaches a redlined copy of the proposed First Amended Complaint. (See Cohen Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. 1.)

In opposition, Defendants argue Plaintiff does not satisfy Rule 3.1324(b)(4) because he does not explain why he delayed in seeking leave so close to the discovery cutoff date when he knew since April 10, 2025, that Dresser doubled Plaintiff’s security deposit. (See Opp. at 6:11-28.) Defendant argues Plaintiff did not need to wait for Jessica Dresser’s deposition to seek leave as the proposed fifth claim rests on the security deposit testimony obtained in Dresser’s deposition. (See id., at 7:7-16.) Finally, Defendants argue they will be prejudiced if the Motion is granted. Defendants argue percipient discovery is essentially complete and discovery closes November 7, 2026. (See Opp. at 9:1-4.) If the Motion is granted, Defendants will need to conduct additional discovery, including a second deposition of Plaintiff and the depositions of Plaintiff’s parents, to address the elements of the new claim. (See id., at 9:6-11, 9:16-20; Declaration of Gary D. Fidler filed August 11, 2026, ¶¶ 5-6.) Defendants contend such discovery would require at least six months to conduct and would require trial be continued. (See id.)

In reply, Plaintiff argues his deposition has not been completed and Defendants will have time to depose Plaintiff’s parents. (See Reply at 2:22-23.) Plaintiff argues he waited until completing Defendants’ depositions and Jessica Dresser’s deposition before seeking leave to amend as additional facts might have been and were, in fact discovered that support the ordinance claim. (See id., at 3:3-6.)

The Court will grant the Motion. The Court finds Plaintiff substantially complies with Rule 3.1324(a), as a redlined copy of the proposed FAC is attached to the motion. (See Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a).) The Court finds Plaintiff also substantially complies with Rule 3.1324(b)(4), which requires the party explain the reasons why the request for the amendment was not made earlier. Plaintiff’s proposed fifth cause of action is based upon facts discovered at Dresser’s deposition, which occurred on April 10, 2025. Defendant is correct that Plaintiff’s counsel’s initial declaration does not explain why he waited until April 27, 2026, to first raise the issue with Defendants’ counsel about amending the Complaint. However, Plaintiff’s counsel explains in reply he waited to seek leave because he believed he could discover more facts at Jessica Dresser’s deposition that would support the proposed fifth claim. (See Reply at 3:3-6.) Given the totality of the record, the Court finds Plaintiff has adequately complied Rule 3.1324(b)(4).

“Where no prejudice is shown to the adverse party, the liberal rule of allowance prevails.” The Court notes trial is scheduled for December 7, 2026, with the discovery cutoff on November 7, 2026. While Defendants would incur an increased burden in discovery, given they have concluded most depositions and written discovery, having to engage in discovery practice is a basic feature of litigation, not proof of actual prejudice.¿Additionally, this Motion is not brought on the eve of trial, trial has been continued once before, and although the Court sees no need to continue trial now, it can continue the trial date to allow the parties to conduct necessary discovery if appropriate.  

Based on the foregoing and the liberal policy favoring amendment, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to Amend.

V.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to Amend.

Plaintiff is ordered to file its proposed amended complaint by no later than August 28, 2026.

     CASE NO.: 25VECV02850

 

ORDER SUSTAINING DEFENDANT MARK NABIL GOBRIAL, M.D.S’ DEMURRER WITH LEAVE TO AMEND

 

ORDER DENYING DEFENDANT MARK NABIL GOBRIAL, M.D.’S MOTION TO STRIKE

 

I.               BACKGROUND 

Plaintiff Sharon Asher (“Plaintiff”) brings this elder abuse action against Normandie/Wilshire Retirement Hotel, LLC, dba California Healthcare and Rehabilitation Center (“Normandie”), Longwood Management, LLC (“Longwood”), and Mark Nabil Gobrial, M.D. (“Dr. Gobrial”) (collectively, “Defendants”). Plaintiff is 84 years old and alleges while in Normandie’s care, she slipped and fell and was denied physical therapy following her fall despite repeated requests to facility staff for physical therapy.

Dr. Gobrial demurrers to and moves to strike portions of the Complaint.

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On May 21, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Complaint alleging (1) Elder Abuse & Neglect (Welf. & Inst. Code § 15610.57)[1]; (2) Premises Liability (Negligence); and (3) Negligence & Medical Malpractice.

ON July 30, 2025, Normandie and Longwood filed an Answer.

On September 9, 2025, the Court granted the parties’ stipulation to strike a demand for attorneys’ fees contained in Normandie and Longwood’s Answer.

On July 27, 2026, Dr. Gobrial filed the instant Demurrer with Motion to Strike.

As of August 24, 2026, no opposition has been filed.

III.         MEET AND CONFER

Before filing a demurrer or motion to strike, the moving party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer or motion to strike for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer or motion to strike. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a), §435.5(a).) A declaration must be filed with a demurrer regarding the results of the meet and confer process. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(3).) 

Dr. Gobrial’s counsel attests he mailed a meet and confer letter to Plaintiff requesting a telephonic meet and confer. (See Declaration of RENEI A. CABALLES filed July 27, 2026, ¶¶ 3-4.) Dr. Gobrial’s counsel attests Plaintiff, who is now unrepresented, did not respond to counsel’s meet and confer letter or request for a telephonic meet and confer. (See id.)  

The Court finds Dr. Gobrial did not satisfy his obligation to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference. The Court expects the moving party to reach out by any of these methods to meet and confer in good faith. In the interest of judicial efficiency, the Court will consider the merits of the demurrer.

IV.          DEMURRER

A.             Legal Standard

A demurrer may be brought if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 430.10(e).) “[T]he court gives the complaint a reasonable interpretation, and treats the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded.”¿(Id.) Allegations are to be liberally construed. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 452.) In testing the sufficiency of the complaint, the court must assume the truth of (1) the properly pleaded factual allegations; (2) facts that can be reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded; and (3) judicially noticed matters. (See Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (See SKF Farms v. Superior Court¿(1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) A demurrer can only be sustained when it disposes of an entire cause of action.¿(See Poizner v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redev. Agency (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1046.)

¿Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court a pleading can be amended successfully. (See id.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.)

B.             Analysis

1.               Elder Abuse & Neglect (Welf. & Inst. Code § 15610.57)

The Elder Abuse and Dependent Adult Civil Protection Act (Welf. & Inst. Code § 15600 et seq.) requires, among other things, a caretaking or custodial relationship in which the defendant has assumed significant responsibility for attending to one or more of the dependent adult’s basic needs that an able-bodied and fully competent adult would ordinarily be capable of managing without assistance. (See Winn v. Pioneer Medical Group, Inc. (2016) 63 Cal.4th 148, 158-159.) The Act distinguishes between professional negligence and statutory neglect and cautions against blurring the two. (Id. at 159-160.)

The necessary elements for a dependent adult abuse cause of action are: (1) defendant had responsibility for meeting the basic needs of the dependent adult, such as nutrition, hydration, hygiene or medical care; (2) defendant knew of conditions that made the dependent adult unable to provide for their own basic needs; (3) defendant denied or withheld goods or services necessary to meet the dependent adult’s basic needs, either (a) with knowledge that injury was substantially certain to befall the dependent adult (if the plaintiff alleges oppression, fraud or malice) or (b) with conscious disregard of the high probability of such injury (if the plaintiff alleges recklessness); and (4) defendant’s neglect caused the dependent adult to suffer physical harm, pain or mental suffering.¿(See Carter v. Prime Healthcare Paradise Valley LLC (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 396, 407.) “[T]he facts constituting the neglect and establishing the causal link between the neglect and the injury ‘must be pleaded with particularity,’ in accordance with the pleading rules governing statutory claims.” (Id.)

The Act “does not apply unless the defendant health care provider had a substantial caretaking or custodial relationship, involving ongoing responsibility for one or more basic needs, with the elder patient.” (Winn v. Pioneer Med. Group, Inc. (2016) 63 Cal.4th 148, 152.) “It is the nature of the elder or dependent adult’s relationship with the defendant—not the defendant’s professional standing—that makes the defendant potentially liable for neglect.” (Id.) Admittance to an acute care facility is sufficient to make [a Plaintiff] a “dependent adult” who would be entitled to the [Elder Abuse Act's] protections. (See Stewart v. Superior Court (2017) 16 Cal.App.5th 87, 102 (citing Welf. & Inst. Code § 15610.23(b) and Health & Safety Code § 1250(a).) The Elder Abuse Act still requires, however, “a separate analysis to determine whether such a [custodial] relationship exists.” (Winn, 63 Cal.4th at 164.)

          Dr. Gobrial argues the first cause of action fails to allege conduct constituting elder abuse. Dr. Gobrial argues paragraph 9 alleges negligence arising from substandard care, not wholesale failure to provide medical care. (See Dem. at 6:21-23.) Dr. Gobrial argues Plaintiff fails to plead with particularity a substantial caretaking or custodial relationship with him existed, and thus, he would at most be liable for professional negligence. (See id., at 7:14-17 citing Covenant Care, Inc. v. Superior Ct. (2004) 32 Cal.4th 771, 785 [“claims under the Elder Abuse Act are not brought against health care providers in their capacity as providers but, rather, against custodians and caregivers that abuse elders and that may or may not, incidentally, also be health care providers.”] and Frankland v. Etehad (2025) 113 Cal.App.5th 503, 508, 516.)

Dr. Gobrial also argues Plaintiff fails to allege facts to support the allegations he intentionally or recklessly delayed the provision of medical care, and at most alleges negligence. (See Dem. at 9:14-16.) Dr. Gobrial argues there are no factual allegations, as opposed to conclusory allegations, that he (1) knew Plaintiff’s injury was substantially certain to occur or (2) acted with a conscious disregard of the high probability of such injury. (See id., at 9:26-28, citing Carter v. Prime Healthcare Paradise Valley, LLC (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 396, 406-407 [“plaintiffs’ ‘[u]se of such terminology [as fraudulently and recklessly] cannot cure [the] failure to point out exactly how or in what manner the [Hospital has] transgressed.’”].)

          The Court finds the first cause of action for elder abuse is insufficiently alleged against Dr. Gobrial because Plaintiff insufficiently alleges Dr. Gobrial had the requisite “robust caretaking or custodial relationship” with Plaintiff. “[A] physician’s conduct in providing negligent medical services to an elder residing at a skilled nursing facility does not—without more—constitute ‘neglect’ because that physician lacks the requisite ‘robust caretaking or custodial relationship’ with the elder [citation] and is accordingly being sued for no more than his professional negligence (which, as noted above, falls outside the Act).” (Frankland v. Etehad (2025) 113 Cal.App.5th at 509 citing Winn v. Pioneer Medical Group, Inc. (2016) 63 Cal.4th 148, 158, citation omitted and emphasis added.) In Frankland, the Court found plaintiff failed to state a custodial relationship because plaintiff alleged conclusory statements—including that the physician defendant “engage[d] in a residential in-facility robust medical caretaking and controlling relationship with [plaintiff]” and “expressly and impliedly agreed to provide for [plaintiff’s] professional and custodial medical needs”—“and because they are unmoored from any supporting facts as against [defendant] [Citations.]” (Id., at 515.) the Frankland Court further found the factual “allegations establish[ed] that [defendant’s] relationship with [plaintiff] was episodic and limited.” (Id.)

Here, the Complaint alleges Dr. Gobrial “served as Plaintiff Asher’s admitting and attending physician…and was responsible for overseeing her medical care.” (Compl., ¶ 9.) His negligence includes failure to implement fall prevention measures, failure to modify her care plan, and failure to ensure proper physical therapy. (See id., ¶ 9.) Plaintiff alleges “Defendants, and each of them, had custodial responsibility to attend to the essential health and safety needs of Plaintiff Asher.” (Id., ¶¶ 19, 21.) The Court agrees the Complaint’s conclusory allegations Dr. Gobrial had a custodial relationship with Plaintiff are insufficient to survive demurrer. There are no specific factual allegations “substantiating the necessary substance of the relationship” between Dr. Gobrial and Plaintiff to support the conclusory allegation Dr. Gobrial had a custodial relationship with Plaintiff. (See Frankland, 113 Cal.App.5th at 516.)

The Complaint also insufficiently alleges the third element. Plaintiff makes the conclusory allegation Defendants’ “failures reflect a reckless and deliberate disregard for Plaintiff Asher’s health” and “Defendants’ acts and omissions were done recklessly, fraudulently, and with malice and oppression…and were callous and indifferent to the care and safety of Plaintiff…” (Compl., ¶¶ 20, 25.) Plaintiff alleges she was denied physical therapy following her fall which “prolonged her recovery, increased her pain, and reduced her overall mobility.” (Id., ¶ 8.) The Court disregards the conclusory allegation Dr. Gobrial’s actions were done recklessly or with malice. Further, although the Complaint alleges Dr. Gobrial failed to act on Plaintiff’s medical plan, it fails to allege Dr. Gobrial withheld physical therapy either with knowledge injury was substantially certain to befall Plaintiff or with conscious disregard of such injury. (See id., ¶¶ 8-9, 19.)

          Thus, the Court SUSTAINS Dr. Gobrial’s Demurrer to the First Cause of Action for Elder Abuse WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

V.             MOTION TO STRIKE

A.             Legal Standard

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 436(a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (See id. at § 436(b).) This provision is for “the striking of a pleading due to improprieties in its form¿or in the procedures¿pursuant to which it was filed.”¿ (Ferraro v. Camarlinghi¿(2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 509, 528 [emphasis in original].) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (See Code Civ. Proc. at § 437.)

B.             Analysis

The Court denies the Motion as moot. A plaintiff in a dependent adult abuse case brought under Welfare and Institutions Code § 15657 may be awarded attorney’s fees and may, on appropriate proof, recover punitive damages.¿(See Wel. Inst. Code § 15657(a), (c).) The paragraphs and relief prayed for that Dr. Gobrial moves to strike are encompassed or supported by the first cause of action, which did not survive demurrer. Thus, the Court DENIES as moot Dr. Gobrial’s Motion to Strike.

VI.          CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court SUSTAINS Dr. Gobrial’s Demurrer to the First Cause of Action for Elder Abuse WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

The Court DENIES as moot Dr. Gobrial’s Motion to Strike.

Plaintiff has leave to amend her Complaint until September 11, 2026.

The Court continues the Trial Setting Conference to January 21, 2027, at 8:30 a.m. The parties are ordered to file a Joint Status Update by no later than January 14, 2027.

          IT IS SO ORDERED.

ORDER DENYING DEFENDANTS’ MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANTS’ MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION AS TO VOLKSWAGEN GROUP OF AMERICA, INC. BUT DENYING IT AS TO SHERMAN OAKS-A, INC.

 

I.               BACKGROUND 

Plaintiff Bella Xinwei Dong (“Plaintiff”) brings this Song Beverly action against Volkswagen Group of America, Inc. (“Volkswagen”), the manufacturer, and Sherman Oaks-A, Inc. dba Audi Van Nuys (“Dealer”), the selling dealership (jointly, “Defendants”), arising out of her purchase of a 2025 Audi SQ8.

Defendants move for summary judgment, or alternatively, summary adjudication.

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On October 1, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Complaint alleging (1) Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability under the Song Beverly Warranty Act; (2) Breach of Express Warranty under the Song-Beverly Warranty Act; (3) Breach of Express Warranty under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act; and (4) Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act.

On November 21, 2025, Defendants separately filed an Answer.

On May 6, 2026, Defendants filed the instant Motion.

On August 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

As of August 24, 2026, no reply has been filed.

III.         LEGAL STANDARD

The function of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (See Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) In analyzing such motions, courts must apply a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent’s claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) Thus, summary judgment is granted when, after the Court’s consideration of the evidence set forth in the papers and all reasonable inferences accordingly, no triable issues of fact exist, and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(c);[1] Villa v. McFarren (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 733, 741.)¿ 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) As to each claim as framed by the complaint, a defendant moving for summary judgment must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate or establish an essential element. (See Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc.¿(2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.) A motion for summary judgment must be denied where the moving party’s evidence does not prove all material facts, even in the absence of any opposition. (See Leyva v. Superior Court (1985) 164 Cal.App.3d 462, 475; Salesguevara v. Wyeth Labs., Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 379, 384.)

Once the moving party has met its burden, the burden shifts to the opposing party to show via specific facts that a triable issue of material fact exists to a cause of action or a defense thereto. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(o)(2).)

IV.          ANALYSIS

A.             Moving Defendants’ Undisputed Material Facts

On June 14, 2025, Plaintiff leased a new 2025 Audi SQ8 (the “Vehicle”) from Dealer. (See Def.’s Sep. Stmt. Undisputed Material Facts (“UMF”), No. 1, 35.) On July 5, 2025, Plaintiff purchased the Vehicle used from Dealer. (See id., Nos. 2, 13, 19, 26, 36.) The Vehicle was not “Certified PreOwned.” (See id., Nos. 3, 14, 20.) Volkswagen did not sell the Vehicle to Plaintiff. (See id., Nos. 4, 15, 21, 27, 37.) Dealer sold the Vehicle to Plaintiff. (See id., Nos. 5, 17, 23, 29, 39.)

Neither Volkswagen nor Dealer issued or extended any new warranties on the Vehicle as part of the sale of that vehicle to Plaintiff. (See id., Nos. 6-7, 16, 18, 22, 24-25, 28, 30, 38, 40.) Volkswagen is not – and never has been – a distributor or retailer of used motor vehicles. (See id., Nos. 8, 31, 41.) The warranties Volkswagen provided in the original new vehicle sale were conspicuously labeled as “limited.” (See id., Nos. 9, 32, 42.)

Volkswagen maintains a compliant third-party informal dispute resolution process administered by the Better Business Bureau (“BBB Auto Line”). (See id., Nos. 10, 43.) Volkswagen advises its consumers of the existence of the BBB Auto Line program on the face of Volkswagen’s written warranty. (See id., Nos. 11, 33, 44.) Plaintiff did not participate in BBB Auto Line prior to filing this lawsuit. (See id., Nos. 12, 34, 45.)

B.             Moving Defendants’ Motions for Summary Judgment, Alternatively Summary Adjudication, is Granted as to Volkswagen and Denied as to Dealer.

The Complaint alleges Plaintiff leased the Subject Vehicle on June 14, 2025, and purchased it on July 5, 2025. (See Compl., ¶¶ 7-9.) Plaintiff alleges two Song-Beverly claims and two Magnuson-Moss claims against Defendants.

1.               Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability under the Song-Beverly Warranty Act & Breach of Express Warranty under the Song-Beverly Warranty Act

The elements for a breach of express warranty claim are: (1) plaintiff bought or leased a new motor vehicle from defendant; (2) defendant gave plaintiff a written warranty; (3) the vehicle had a nonconformity that was covered by the warranty that substantially impaired its use, value, or safety; (4) the vehicle was presented to an authorized representative of the manufacturer of the vehicle for repair; (5) the manufacturer or his representative did not repair the nonconformity after a reasonable number of repair attempts; and (6) defendant did not replace or buy back the vehicle. (See Civ. Code § 1790 et seq.; see also Oregel v. American Isuzu Motors, Inc. (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 1094, 1101; Donlen v. Ford Motor Co. (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 138, 152; Rodriguez v. FCA US LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189, 206.)

The elements of a breach of the implied warranty of merchantability are: (1) plaintiff bought a consumer good from or manufactured by defendant; (2) at the time of purchase, defendant was in the business of selling or manufacturing the consumer good; (3) the consumer good was not of the same quality as those generally acceptable in the trade, not fit for the ordinary purposes for which the goods or used, not adequately contained, packed and labeled, or does not conform to the promises or affirmations of fact made on the container or label; (4) plaintiff was harmed; and (5) defendant’s breach of the implied warranty was a substantial factor in causing plaintiff’s harm. (See Civ. Code § 1791.1; see Isip v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (2007) 155 Cal.App.4th 19, 26; see Gutierrez v. Carmax Auto Superstores Cal. (2018) 19 Cal.App.5th 1234, 1247.)

“‘As is the case with liability for breach of express warranties, “in the sale of used consumer goods, liability for breach of implied warranty lies with distributors and retailers, not the manufacturer,’ unless the manufacturer issues a new warranty along with the sale of the used good.” (Rodriguez v. FCA US, LLC (2022) 77 Cal.App.5th 209, 218 [emphasis in original] [quoting Ruiz Nunez v. FCA US LLC (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 385, 398].) “[T]he Song-Beverly Act generally binds only distributors and retail sellers in the sale of used goods” (Kiluk v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 334, 337; see Civ. Code § 1795.5(a).)

Defendants argue Plaintiff’s Song-Beverly claims fail because Plaintiff purchased a used vehicle and thus, she may not assert statutory remedies available to buyers of new cars. (See Mot. at 6:11-21, citing Rodriguez v. FCA US, LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189, 196-205, 19:16-23, 20:12-20.) Defendants submit the Retail Installment Sales Contract establishing Plaintiff purchased the Vehicle used, non-certified preowned, from Dealer on July 5, 2025. (See UMF. Nos. 2-3, 5.) Defendants submit evidence showing the Vehicle, at the time of purchase, came with the remaining balance of the New Vehicle Limited Warranty, which was initially issued on June 2025. (See id.) Defendants also submit the undisputed facts that neither Volkswagen nor Dealer issued or extended any new warranties on the Vehicle as part of the sale of Vehicle. (See UMF Nos. 6-7, 16, 18.)

Defendants meet their burden in showing Volkswagen is entitled to summary adjudication on the first and second causes of action, as the evidence demonstrates Plaintiff purchased the vehicle used. However, Defendants do not meet their burden in showing Dealer is entitled to summary adjudication on the first and second claims based on the same evidence. Under the Song Beverly Act, liability for breach of express and implied warranties for a used vehicle would still lie with the selling dealership. (See Rodriguez, 77 Cal.App.5th at 218; Kiluk, 43 Cal.App.5th at 336.)

Thus, the burden only shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue as to Volkswagen’s liability under the Song-Beverly Act for the used Vehicle.

Plaintiff fails to raise a triable issue and fails to submit a separate statement. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(b)(3).) Plaintiff argues because she purchased the Vehicle after she initially leased it, she is still entitled to her rights under the Song-Beverly Act. (See Opp. at 4:9-17; Declaration of Jeeho H. Lim filed August 12, 2026, ¶ 6.) The Court finds Plaintiff fails to meet her burden.

Thus, the Court GRANTS Defendants’ Motion for Summary Adjudication on the First and Second Causes of Action for Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability under the Song Beverly Warranty Act and Breach of Express Warranty under the Song-Beverly Warranty Act as to Volkswagen.

The Court DENIES the Motion for Summary Adjudication on the First and Second Causes of Action as to Dealer.

2.               Breaches of Written & Implied Warranty under Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act

Under the Magnuson-Moss Act, “… a¿consumer¿who is damaged by the failure of a¿supplier,¿warrantor, or service contractor to comply with any obligation under this chapter, or under a¿written warranty,¿implied warranty, or¿service contract, may bring suit for damages and other legal and equitable relief…” (15 U.S.C. § 2310(d)(1).)[2] “No supplier may disclaim or modify (except as provided in subsection (b)) any implied warranty to a consumer with respect to such consumer product if (1) such supplier makes any written warranty to the consumer with respect to such consumer Product, or (2) at the time of sale, or within 90 days thereafter, such supplier enters into a service contract with the consumer which applies to such consumer product.” (15 U.S.C. § 2308(a).)

The Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act “‘calls for the application of state written and implied warranty law, not the creation of additional federal law,’ except in specific instances in which it expressly prescribes a regulating rule.” (Daugherty v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 824, 833, internal citation omitted; see also Clemens v. DaimlerChrysler Corp. (9th Cir. 2008) 534 F.3d 1017, 1022 n.3.) Thus, the failure to state a warranty claim under state law necessarily defeats a Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act claim. (See Daugherty, 144 Cal.App.4th at 833 [rejecting plaintiff’s argument that she properly pled a Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act cause of action in the absence of a state law warranty claim].)

However, courts have held even if relief is not available under the Song-Beverly Act, a cause of action under the Magnuson-Moss Act may be based on other state laws, such as the warranty provisions under California Commercial Code, sections 2313 and 2714. (See Orichian v. BMW of N. Am., LLC (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1322, 1332; see also Dagher v. Ford Motor Co. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 905, 928 [purchaser’s express warranty claims under the California Commercial Code were viable despite the lack of a viable cause of action under the Song-Beverly Act].) In Dagher, during the pendency of the defendant’s motion for summary judgment, plaintiff moved to amend his complaint to assert claims under Magnuson-Moss based on Ford’s breach of the express warranty and implied warranty provisions of the UCC. (See Dagher, 238 Cal.App.4th at 913.) Accordingly, the Dagher Court found that the plaintiff’s Magnuson-Moss claim survived summary judgment because the plaintiff had valid claims under the UCC by seeking repairs for apparent defects during the unexpired transferred warranty period. (See id., at 928.)

Defendants rely on the evidence above to argue Plaintiff’s Magnuson-Moss claims fail because her Song-Beverly Act claims fail against them. (See Mot. at 16:25-17:23, citing Daugherty, 144 Cal.App.4th at 833.) Defendants further argue Plaintiff’s Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act claims fail as to Volkswagen because she failed to use Volkswagen’s informal dispute resolution procedures mandated by the manufacturer’s warranty. (See Mot. at 17:24-26, citing 15 U.S.C. § 2310(a)(3).) Defendants submit the undisputed facts Volkswagen maintains a compliant third-party informal dispute resolution process administered by the Better Business Bureau (“BBB Auto Line”). (See UMF Nos. 10-11.) Defendants submit the undisputed material fact Plaintiff didn’t  participate in the BBB Auto Line prior to commencing her action. (See id., No. 12.)

The Court finds Defendants meet their burden in showing Volkswagen is entitled to summary adjudication on the third and fourth Magnuson-Moss claims because they were entitled to summary adjudication on the underlying Song-Beverly Act claims above.[3] However, Defendants did not meet their burden in showing Dealer is entitled to summary adjudication on the first and second claims based. Thus, Defendant’s argument the Magnuson-Moss claims against Dealer necessarily fail because Plaintiff’s Song-Beverly claims as to Dealer fail is unavailing.

The burden only shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue as to Volkswagen’s liability for the used Vehicle. For the same reasons above, the Court finds Plaintiff fails to raise a triable issue.

Thus, the Court GRANTS Defendants’ Motion for Summary Adjudication on the Third and Fourth Causes of Action for Breach of Express Warranty under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act and Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act as to Volkswagen.

The Court DENIES the Motion for Summary Adjudication on the Third and Fourth Causes of Action as to Dealer.

C.             Plaintiff’s Request for Leave to Amend is Denied.

“[L]eave to amend is liberally granted, even when a summary judgment motion is pending.” (Willemsen v. Mitrosilis (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 622, 633.) However, leave to amend may be denied due to unwarranted delay or lack of diligence in seeking leave to amend. (See Falcon v. Long Beach Genetics, Inc. (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1263, 1280 [finding no abuse of discretion where trial court denied plaintiff’s request for leave to amend at oral argument during hearing on motion for summary judgment based on finding that any amendment was inconsistent with plaintiffs’ theory of the case].) 

“Thus, when a plaintiff seeks leave to amend his or her complaint only after the defendant has mounted a summary judgment motion directed at the allegations of the unamended complaint, even though the plaintiff has been aware of the facts upon which the amendment is based, “[i]t would be patently unfair to allow plaintiffs to defeat [the] summary judgment motion by allowing them to present a ‘moving target’ unbounded by the pleadings.” (Id., quoting Melican v. Regents of Univ. of Cal. (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 168, 176; see Champlin/GEI Wind Holdings, LLC v. Avery (2023) 92 Cal.App.5th 218, 225 [trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying leave to amend where no motion to amend or proposed amendment was filed despite ability to do so before motion for summary judgment filed]; but see Laabs v. City of Victorville (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1257, fn. 6 [“plaintiff must seek leave to amend the complaint at or prior to the hearing on the motion for summary judgment”; the court may and should permit him to amend].)

In the body of Plaintiff’s opposition, Plaintiff requests leave to amend the Complaint so she can file a Motion for Leave to Amend to remove the Breach of Song-Beverly and Breach of Implied Warranty under Magnuson-Moss claims and add (1) Breach of Express/Written Warranty under Commercial Code §§ 2102, 2103, and 2105, (2) Breach of Contract, and (3) Breach of Written Warranty under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act. (See Opp. at 6:21-24.)

The Court denies the request for leave to amend. (See Dagher, 238 Cal.App.4th at 914 [trial court denied pending motion to amend, filed after timely opposition to motion for summary judgment, without prejudice to plaintiff filing a new action].) Plaintiff was served with the instant Motion on May 6, 2026, but has unreasonably delayed in seeking leave to amend the Complaint. Plaintiff could have filed a noticed motion for leave before Defendants filed the instant Motion during the two months prior to the hearing on this Motion. Thus, the Court denies Plaintiff’s request for leave to amend. This decision is without prejudice to Plaintiff filing a separately noticed motion for leave to amend.

V.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment as to Volkswagen. However, the Court GRANTS Defendants’ Motion for Summary Adjudication as to Volkswagen but DENIES it as to Dealer.
