Motion for Order for Publication of Summons



Hearing Date: 9/11/26



CASE NO.: 23BBCV01209



CASE NAME: Unite Welding Enterprises, Inc. v. E Roane
Construction Management, Inc., et al.



Moving Party: Plaintiff Unite Welding Enterprises, Inc.



Responding Party: Unopposed



Notice: Sufficient



Ruling:
GRANT



 



NOTICE



The Court is not requesting oral argument on this matter/motion.  The Court is guided by California Rules of
Court, Rule 3.1308(a)(1) whereby notice of intent to appear is requested.  Unless the Court directs argument in the
Tentative Ruling, no argument is requested and ANY PARTY SEEKING ARGUMENT
SHOULD NOTIFY ALL OTHER PARTIES AND THE COURT BY 4:00 P.M. ON THE COURT DAY

become the ruling of the court if no argument is received.   Notice may be given either by email at BurDeptB@LACourt.org or by telephone at (818) 260-8422.



Parties must appear on all other matters scheduled for this case.



BACKGROUND



This is a breach of contract action. On
May 17, 2024, plaintiff Unite Welding Enterprises, Inc. (Plaintiff) filed a
Second Amended Complaint (SAC) alleging breach of contract and common counts
against individual defendants Agassi Halajyan and Michael Keheian (Defendants)
as well as E Roane Construction Management, Inc., Edward Dean Roane, Dillon
Construction, Inc., Christopher David Dillon, Dillon Roane, a Joint Venture,
Inc., and Canplan Industries, LLC.



On July 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed the
instant motion for authorization to serve defendants Agassi Halajyan and
Michael Keheia (collectively Defendants) by publication. No opposition has been
filed as of September 8, 2026.



 



LEGAL
STANDARD



Code of Civil Procedure Section 415.50(a) states that a
summons may be served by publication if upon affidavit it appears to the
satisfaction of the court in which the action is pending that the party to be
served cannot with reasonable diligence be served in another manner specified
in this article and that either: (1) a cause of action exists against the
party upon whom service is to be made, or he or she is a necessary or
proper party to the action; or (2) the party to be served has or claims an
interest in real or personal property in this state that is subject to the
jurisdiction of the court or the relief demanded in the action consists
wholly or in part in excluding the party from any interest in the
property. (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.50(a).)



“An application for an order of publication must be accompanied by an
affidavit stating facts from which the trial court can draw the conclusion that
the plaintiff has a cause of action against the defendant.” (Rios v.
Singh (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 871, 884.) “This is a jurisdictional
prerequisite to the issuance of an order of publication.” (Id.) 



To obtain service by publication, the supporting affidavit must
meet two requirements. First, a competent affiant with personal knowledge must
declare probative facts that the party used reasonable diligence to serve the
defendant in a statutorily authorized manner. (Olvera v. Olvera (1991)
232 Cal.App.3d 32, 42.) “The term ‘reasonable diligence’ ... denotes a
thorough, systematic investigation and inquiry conducted in good faith by the
party or his agent or attorney.” (Watts v. Crawford (1995) 10
Cal.4th 743, 749 n.5; see also Rios, supra, 65 Cal.App.5th at
880.) (“The question is whether the plaintiff took the steps a reasonable
person who truly desired to give notice of the action would have taken under
the circumstances.”) Second, the affiant must declare that Defendant is a
necessary party to the cause of action or has an interest in real property
subject to the jurisdiction of the Court. (See Olvera, supra,
232 Cal.App.3d at 42 n.9.)



If the Court finds that service by publication is authorized, “[t]he
court shall order the summons to be published in a named newspaper, published
in this state, that is most likely to give actual notice to the party to be
served.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.50(b).)



 



DISCUSSION                                                                                              



Plaintiff moves
the court for an order authorizing service of summons by publication to
Defendants. (Mot., p. 2.)



Plaintiffs state
that they have met with Defendant Michael Keheia and the cousin of Defendant
Agassi Halajyan and that Defendants are aware of the claims against them.
(Mot., p. 4.) Plaintiff argues that at this meeting, Defendant Michael and the
cousin of Defendant Agassi alluded to it not being economically worth defending
the action. (Ibid.) Plaintiff argues that after an unsuccessful petition
for entry of default, they attempted to serve Defendants on May 2, 6, and 9, 2025
at the addresses of their respective businesses. (Mot., pp. 5-6.) Further,
Plaintiffs argue they have attempted to serve Defendants on June 15, 17, 20,
and 24, 2025, at residential addresses associated with Defendants according to
“SmartLinx Person Report”. (Mot., pp. 6-7.) Plaintiff states that after a
previous motion for publication of summons was denied, they hired a private
investigator to attempt to locate Defendants. (Mot., p. 7.) Plaintiff argues
subsequent attempts to serve Defendants at additional residential addresses identified
via skip trace on March 5, 9, and 12, 2026 were unsuccessful. (Ibid.)



Moreover, Plaintiff argues that a cause of
action exists against Defendants because their entities received the benefit of
construction on their respective properties without providing payment to
Plaintiff who performed the construction. (Mot., p. 8.)



Here,
Plaintiff has been diligent and made reasonable efforts in trying
to locate and serve Defendants in a statutorily authorized manner. The
claims against Defendants arise out of non-payment for construction
services rendered by Plaintiff. (SAC, ¶¶ 19-36.) Plaintiff made a total of ten
renewed service attempts at several addresses associated with Defendants, evidenced by
several non-service reports. (Arvizu Decl., ¶¶ 10, 12, 14, 16, Exh.
10-11, 13-16.) Following those unsuccessful attempts, Plaintiff hired a private
investigator to conduct a skip trace, which revealed different Los Angeles
address’ for Defendants which also resulted in unsuccessful service
attempts. (Arvizu Decl., ¶ 17.)



Further,
Defendants are necessary to this action as all causes of action are asserted
against both Defendants and their associated entities. (See SAC.)



Defendants are
necessary parties to this action, and Plaintiff has engaged in diligent efforts
to serve them. The Court finds that the Los Angeles Times is a
newspaper most likely to give actual notice to the party to be served. 



 



CONCLUSION
AND ORDER



Plaintiff Unite
Welding Enterprises, Inc.’s motion for order for publication of summons is GRANTED.



MOVING PARTY
shall
provide notice and provide a proposed order consistent with this ruling within
10 days from this date.

Petition to Approve Minor’s Compromises

Hearing Date: 9/11/26

CASE NO.: 24NNCP00481

CASE NAME: Asaduriyan, et al. v. Metromile Insurance Co

Petitioner: Araz Asaduriyan

Minor Claimants: Vahagn Asaduriyan and Nairi Asaduriyan

Ruling: GRANT.

 

BACKGROUND

This is a personal injury action arising out of a motor vehicle accident which occurred on October 19, 2021. Minors Vahagn Asaduriyan and Nairi Asaduriyan (collectively Minor Claimants) were in the vehicle being operated by their parent and petitioner Araz Asaduriyan (Petitioner). Because the negligent driver was uninsured, Petitioner filed a petition on August 21, 2024, requesting that the Court open an uninsured motorist case.

On July 31, 2026, Petitioner filed the instant petitions to confirm minor’s compromise for both Minor Claimants.

On September 4, 2026, the matter was called for hearing, and the matter was continued to September 11, 2026, in order to provide Petitioner with the opportunity to produce updated information as to the proposed annuity.

On September 9, 2026, Petitioner filed supplemental declarations as to concerns previously identified by the Court.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Court approval is required for all settlements of a minor’s claim or that of a person lacking the capacity to make decisions.  (Prob. Code, §§ 2504, 3500, 3600 et seq.; Code Civ. Proc., § 372; see Pearson v. Superior Court (2012) 202 Cal.App.4th 1333, 1337.)

“[T]he protective role the court generally assumes in cases involving minors, [is] a role to assure that whatever is done is in the minor’s best interests . . . .  [I]ts primary concern is whether the compromise is sufficient to provide for the minor’s injuries, care and treatment.”  (Goldberg v. Superior Court (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1378, 1382.) 

A petition for court approval of a compromise or covenant not to sue under Code of Civil Procedure section 372 must comply with California Rules of Court Rules 7.950, 7.951, and 7.952.  The petition must be verified by the petitioner and contain a full disclosure of all information that has “any bearing upon the reasonableness” of the compromise or the covenant.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950.)  The person compromising the claim on behalf of the minor or person who lacks capacity, and the represented person, must attend the hearing on compromise of the claim unless the court for good cause dispenses with their personal appearance.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.952(a).)

An order for deposit of funds of a minor or person lacking decision-making capacity and a petition for the withdrawal of such funds must comply with California Rules of Court Rules 7.953 and 7.954.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1384; see also Super. Ct. L.A. County, Local Rules, rules 4.115-4.118.)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                 

Procedural Requirements

Petitioner has filed Proposed Orders with the petitions. (Scruton v. Korean Air Lines Co., Ltd. (1995) 39 Cal.App.4th 1596, 1603-1605 [stating that a petition for court approval must comply with Cal. Rules of Court, rules 7.950, 7.950.5, 7.591 and 7.952].) Petitioner uses Judicial Council form MC-350 for the petitions and presents the Court with proposed orders approving compromise of the disputed claim.

The Petition is thus procedurally proper.

Substantive Requirements

The petitions show that Minor Claimants Vahagn Asaduriyan and Nairi Asaduriyan settled with Defendant for total amount of $31,000 each.

Vahagn Asaduriyan 

Of the $31,000 allocated to Minor Claimant Vahagn, $600 will be used to pay medical expenses, $7,750 will be used to pay attorney’s fees, and $156.76 will be used to pay non-medical expenses, leaving a balance of $22,493.24.  Petitioners propose the net proceeds be invested in a single-premium deferred annuity, subject to withdrawal only on authorization of the Court. The Court notes the terms and conditions of the annuity submitted with the petition expired, at latest, March 2, 2026. (Vahagn Pet., Attachment 18(b)(3).)

The Court finds the settlements are fair, reasonable and in the best interests of Minor Claimant Vahagn.  Further, the Court finds that the declaration of Marine Davtyan is sufficient to support the request for attorney’s fees in the amount of $7,750 (representing 25 percent of the gross $31,000 settlement).  (Vahagn Pet., Attachment 13a, Davtyan Decl., ¶¶ 15-17.)

While the initial terms and conditions of the annuity were expired, the supplemental declaration provided by Petitioner provides the up-to-date terms and conditions of the annuity. (See Supp. Decl.) Further, Petitioner’s counsel declares that the annuities have been purchased for Minor Claimants’ and the terms solidified. (Davtyan Supp. Decl., ¶¶ 5-7, 17.) Thus, Petitioner has provided sufficient information regarding the disposition of balances for Minor Claimant Vahagn.

Nairi Asaduriyan 

Of the $31,000 allocated to Minor Claimant Nairi, $1,311.75 will be used to pay medical expenses, $7,750 will be used to pay attorney’s fees, and $156.76will be used to pay non-medical expenses, leaving a balance of $21,781.49.  Petitioners propose the net proceeds be invested in a single-premium deferred annuity, subject to withdrawal only on authorization of the Court. The Court notes the terms and conditions of the annuity submitted with the petition expired, at latest, March 2, 2026. (Nairi Pet., Attachment 18(b)(3).)

The Court finds the settlements are fair, reasonable and in the best interests of Minor Claimant.  Further, the Court finds that the declaration of Marine Davtyan is sufficient to support the request for attorney’s fees in the amount of $7,750 (representing 25 percent of the gross $31,000 settlement). (Nairi Pet., Attachment 13a, Davtyan Decl., ¶¶ 15-17.)

While the initial terms and conditions of the annuity were expired, the supplemental declaration provided by Petitioner provides the up-to-date terms and conditions of the annuity. (See Supp. Decl.) Further, Petitioner’s counsel declares that the annuities have been purchased for Minor Claimants’ and the terms solidified. (Davtyan Supp. Decl., ¶¶ 5-7, 17.) Thus, Petitioner has provided sufficient information regarding the disposition of balances for Minor Claimant Nairi.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiff Araz Asaduriyan’s petitions to approve compromise of pending action on behalf of Minor Claimants Vahagn Asaduriyan and Nairi Asaduriyan are GRANTED.

MOVING PARTY shall submit a proposed order consistent with this ruling within 10 days from the date of this hearing and provide notice.

Motion for Summary Judgment

Hearing Date: 9/11/26

CASE NO.: 24NNCV01897

CASE NAME: Zanjani v. Harry Balian, M.D.

Moving Party: Defendant Harry Balian, M.D.

Responding Party: Plaintiff Kobra Soorati Zanjani

Notice: Sufficient

Ruling: DENY

 

BACKGROUND

This is medical malpractice case. On May 29, 2024, plaintiff Kobra Soorati Zanjani (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against Harry Balian, M.D. (Defendant) arising from an order to pause medication that occurred on March 23, 2023. Plaintiff alleges that this order to pause his Brillinta prescription put him at severe health risk.

On June 17, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion for summary judgement. On August 21, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On August 31, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD 

In reviewing a motion for summary judgment or adjudication, courts must apply a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent’s claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.”¿(Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) 

            “[T]he initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facia showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.”¿(Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.)¿When a plaintiff seeks summary judgment, the plaintiff must produce admissible evidence on each element of each cause of action on which judgment is sought. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(p)(1).) The plaintiff is not required to disprove any affirmative defenses to discharge this burden. (Oldcastle Precast, Inc. v. Lumbermens Mutual Casualty Co. (2009) 170 Cal.App.4th 554, 564-65.)¿

If the moving party fails to carry its burden, the inquiry is over, and the motion must be denied. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2); see also Consumer Cause, Inc. v. SmileCare (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 454, 468.)¿Even if the moving party does carry its burden, the non-moving party will still defeat the motion by presenting evidence of a triable issue of material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 849-50.) The supporting evidence can be in the form of affidavits, declarations, admissions, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and matters of which judicial notice may be taken.¿(Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 855.) 

            “Once the [moving party] … has met that burden, the burden shifts to the [non-moving party] … to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) The non-moving party may not merely rely on allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists, but instead, “shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action.”¿(Ibid.)¿“If the [non-moving party] cannot do so, summary judgment should be granted.”¿(Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467.) 

The court must “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve all doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party,” including “all inferences reasonably drawn therefrom.”¿(Yanowitz v. L’Oreal USA, Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1037; Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at pp. 844-45.) “On a summary judgment motion, the court must therefore consider what inferences favoring the opposing party a factfinder could reasonably draw from the evidence.¿While viewing the evidence in this manner, the court must bear in mind that its primary function is to identify issues rather than to determine issues. [Citation.] Only when the inferences are indisputable may the court decide the issues as a matter of law.¿ If the evidence is in conflict, the factual issues must be resolved by trial.”¿(Binder v. Aetna Life Ins. Co. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 832, 839.)¿Further, “the trial court may not weigh the evidence in the manner of a factfinder to determine whose version is more likely true. [Citation.] Nor may the trial court grant summary judgment based on the court’s evaluation of credibility. [Citation.]” (Id. at p. 840; see also Weiss v. People ex rel. Department of Transportation (2020) 9 Cal.5th 840, 864 [“Courts deciding motions for summary judgment or summary adjudication may not weigh the evidence but must instead view it in the light most favorable to the opposing party and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of that party”].) 

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Defendant moves for summary judgment against Plaintiff’s cause of action for medical negligence on the grounds that Plaintiff cannot establish that (1) Defendant did not comply with the applicable standard of care, and (2) the conduct of Defendant was not a substantial factor in causing any injury to Plaintiff.

Medical Malpractice/Negligence

            “The elements of a cause of action for medical malpractice are: (1) a duty to use such skill, prudence, and diligence as other members of the profession commonly possess and exercise; (2) a breach of the duty; (3) a proximate causal connection between the negligent conduct and the injury; and (4) resulting loss or damage.” (Chakalis v. Elevator Solutions, Inc. (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 1557, 1571.)

            Moving Party’s Burden

Defendant argues that Plaintiff cannot show that Defendant fell below the applicable standard of care. (Mot., p. 9.) In a medical malpractice action, the requisite standard of practice is determined by the applicable standard of practice then existing in the particular professional community. (Landeros v. Flood (1976) 17 Cal.3d 399.) Defendant argues that the declaration of expert John S. Macgregor, M.D., confirms that at all times Defendant complied with the applicable standard of care. (Mot., p. 9.) Defendant argues that cardiac clearance for a screening colonoscopy warrants the decision to approve/order a five-day discontinuance of the patient’s antiplatelet blood thinner medication and this decision was appropriate for a cardiologist under the same or similar circumstances. (Ibid.) Further, Defendant argues that the declaration of this same expert proves that to a reasonable medical probability, the discontinuance of the Brillinta medication, did not cause or significantly contribute to the occurrence of Plaintiff’s acute MI on March 27, 2023. (Mot., p. 10.)

Here, Defendant’s expert, Dr. John Macgregor, declares that it is his opinion that the care and treatment provided by Defendant at all times complied with the standard of care. (Macgregor Decl., ¶ 14.) Further, Dr. Macgregor declares that patients with stents such as the one Plaintiff has, can have their medication stopped five days prior to a procedure such as the one Plaintiff was preparing for. (Macgregor Decl., ¶¶ 15-19.) Dr. Macgregor also declares that it is his opinion that no negligent act or omission on the part of Defendant caused or contributed to Plaintiff’s claimed injuries. (Macgregor Decl., ¶ 22.) Further, Dr. Macgregor declares that elevates stress, dehydration, elevated blood pressure, and the small size of the stent could have caused the “acute MI” Plaintiff suffered. (Macgregor Decl., ¶¶ 24-25.)

Dr. Macgregor’s opinion is in direct conflict with Plaintiff’s expert, Dr. Zern, who declares and opines to a reasonable degree of medical probability that the same conduct fellow below the standard of care and caused an angiographically confirmed thrombosis of the stent in the proximal left anterior descending artery.  (Zern Decl., ¶¶ 12-21.)

The Court is not persuaded to hold there are absolutely no triable issues of material fact in light of one expert declaration as to standard of care and causation. While Defendant’s evidence certainly aids in their position, Defendant has not met their burden in showing that Plaintiff cannot possibly show a breach in standard of care, causation, or ultimately prove medical malpractice/negligence. Triable issues of fact remain.

Accordingly, the motion for summary judgment is DENIED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant Harry Balian, M.D.’s Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED.

MOVING PARTY is to give notice.

 

 

Motion to Compel Initial Responses; Request for Sanctions

Hearing Date: 9/11/26

CASE NO.: 24NNCV01897

CASE NAME: Zanjani v. Harry Balian, M.D., et al.

Moving Party: Defendant Harry Balian, M.D.

Responding Party: Plaintiff Kobra Soorati Zanjani

Notice: Sufficient 

Ruling: DENY motion to compel as moot; GRANT request for sanctions.

 

BACKGROUND

This is medical malpractice case. On May 29, 2024, plaintiff Kobra Soorati Zanjani (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against Harry Balian, M.D. (Defendant) arising from an order to pause medication that occurred on March 23, 2023. Plaintiff alleges that this order to pause their Brillinta prescription put them at severe health risk.

On August 20, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to compel initial responses to Requests for Production of Documents (RPDs) served on Plaintiff. On August 24, 2026, the Court advanced and continued the hearing to the present date in light of an Ex Parte motion filed by Defendant.

On August 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On September 8, 2026, Defendant filed an untimely reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A party must respond to requests for production of documents within 30 days after service. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.260, subd. (a).) If a party to whom requests for production of documents are directed does not provide timely responses, the requesting party may move for an order compelling responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (b).) The party also waives the right to make any objections, including one based on privilege or work-product protection. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).) There is no time limit for a motion to compel responses to production of documents other than the cut-off on hearing discovery motions 15 days before trial. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.020, subd. (a), 2031.300.) No meet and confer efforts are required before filing a motion to compel responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300; Sinaiko, supra, 148 Cal.App.4th at 411.)

 

DISCUSSION           

Motion to Compel Initial Responses

Defendant contends that Plaintiff failed to timely respond to RPDs on or by the statutory deadline. (Mot., p. 5.)

Here, Defendant’s counsel served Plaintiff with RPDs by electronic service on May 4, 2026. (Looney Decl., ¶ 3; Exh. A.) Defendant contends that initial responses were due June 8, 2026. (Ibid.) Defendant’s counsel declares that although no formal extensions were given, Defendant made good faith efforts to resolve the issue by meeting and conferring with Plaintiff’s counsel after the statutory deadline. (Mot., pp. 4-5, Looney Decl., ¶¶ 4-5.) On August 6, 2026, Defendant’s counsel sent Plaintiff a meet and confer letter requesting response within 5 days to no avail. (Looney Decl., ¶ 6.) As of August 20, 2026, Defendant declares that Plaintiff has not provided verified responses. (Mot., p. 6, Koletsky Decl., ¶ 8.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that they did in fact request an extension to June 19, 2026, via email. (Opp., p. 2.) Nonetheless, Plaintiff argues that they the propounded responses to RPDs on August 21, 2026, via electronic mail. (Opp., p. 3.) Plaintiff argues that Defendant knew this and still went ahead with the instant motion. (Ibid.) Further, Plaintiff argues that Defendant did not attempt to resolve this matter informally in good faith. (Opp., pp. 7-8.)

The Court notes that Defendant’s reply is untimely pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1005(b), but the Court will address its argument. In reply, Defendant argues that Plaintiff did not actually issue replies until August 24, 2026. (Reply, p. 3.) Further, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s responses are insufficient because they state paragraphs of objections and pile on medical records that do not correspond to the documents actually requested. (Reply, pp. 4-7.)

Since the filing of the instant motion, Plaintiff has issued verified responses to Defendant’s RPDs. (Henriks Decl., ¶ 10, Exh. C.) A motion to compel initial responses seeks to compel a response that has not been issued. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300(b).) Here, a response has been issued. Defendant’s reply seeks to compel better quality responses from Plaintiff but a motion to compel initial responses is not the appropriate vehicle to achieve that.

Accordingly, the motion to compel initial responses to RPDs is DENIED as moot.

Sanctions

The Court shall impose sanction on the party, the person, or the attorney whose failure to serve a timely response to the request necessitated the motion to compel responses to interrogatories or RPD. (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.300(c), 2030.290(c); see also Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1348(a) [the court can award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party seeking to compel discovery even though no opposition was filed, the opposition was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed].)

“A request for a sanction shall, in the notice of motion, identify every person, party, and attorney against whom the sanction is sought, and specify the type of sanction sought. The notice of motion shall be supported by a memorandum of points and authorities and accompanied by a declaration setting forth facts supporting the amount of any monetary sanction sought.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.040.)

Although Plaintiff has since provided verified responses to RPDs, Plaintiff was properly served and failed to respond to discovery requests by the statutory deadline. Plaintiff’s failure to comply necessitated the motion, and the conduct is subject to sanctions. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(c); see also Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1348(a).) Moreover, The Court finds Defendant’s Notices of Motion comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.040.

Defendant’s counsel requests sanctions in the amount of $1,200 reflecting 3.0 hours spent researching, preparing, and drafting this motion and 1.0 hours anticipated attending the hearing at a rate of $300 per hour. (Looney Decl., ¶ 10.)

The Court acknowledged Plaintiff’s request for sanctions but will not address the request in this order.

The Court, in its discretion, will only award $600 to reflect 2.0 hours at a rate of $300 due to the straightforward nature of the instant motion.

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s request for imposition of sanctions against Plaintiff in the amount of $600.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant Harry Balian, M.D.’s motion to compel initial responses to RPDs is DENIED.

Defendant’s request for sanctions is GRANTED in the amount of $600, to be paid by Plaintiff to Defendant within 30 days from this ruling.

Motion for Protective Order

Hearing Date: 9/11/26

CASE NO.: 24NNCV01897

CASE NAME: Zanjani v. Harry Balian, M.D.

Moving Party: Defendant Harry Balian, M.D.

Responding Party: Plaintiff Kobra Soorati Zanjani

Notice: Sufficient

Ruling: DENY

 

BACKGROUND

This is medical malpractice case. On May 29, 2024, plaintiff Kobra Soorati Zanjani (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against Harry Balian, M.D. (Defendant) arising from an order to pause medication that occurred on March 23, 2023. Plaintiff alleges that this order to pause his Brillinta prescription put him at severe health risk.

On August 28, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion for protective order. On August 31, 2026, the Court granted Defendant’s Ex Parte motion to schedule to set hearing for the instant motion.

On August 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On September 8, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure §2025.420(b), the court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party… from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense. This protective order may include, but is not limited to, one or more of the following directions: including (1) that the deposition not be taken at all; (2) that the deposition be taken at a different time… and (5) that the deposition be taken only on certain specified terms and conditions. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.420(b).)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Meet and Confer

A¿motion for protective order must be accompanied by a¿meet¿and confer declaration under¿meet¿and confer declaration under Section 2016.040. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.420(a).)

Here, the parties do not stipulate to any meet and confer efforts. Defendant does not reference meet and confer efforts in the instant motion. Thus, meet and confer efforts have not been satisfied. However, the Court will nonetheless address the issue on the merits, for completeness.  

Protective Order

Defendant argues that good cause exists for this protective order requiring Plaintiff to produce their son for deposition or excluding the declaration of Plaintiff’s son from, being admitted come trial. (Mot., pp. 5-6.) Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s son helped them manage medical affairs and Plaintiff’s counsel agreed to produce Plaintiff’s son for this deposition before cooperation dissipated. (Mot., pp. 5-6.) Defendant argues that going to trial without the testimony of this key witness would be unfair to Defendant after the defense relied on the representations of Plaintiff’s counsel. (Mot., p. 6.) Further, Defendant maintains that Plaintiff has secured a declaration from Plaintiff’s son which they intend to use at trial, without giving them the opportunity to cross-examine the witness. (Ibid.)

            In opposition, Plaintiff argues that nearly four months have passed since Defendant has learned of Plaintiff’s son’s involvement in managing Plaintiff’s medical affairs. (Opp., p. 1.) Plaintiff argues that Defendant had several opportunities to seek this deposition and did not do so. (Ibid.) Plaintiff argues that this is a manufactured “emergency” which Defendant created themselves by not following up on this deposition. (Mot., p. 2.) Further, Plaintiff argues that Defendant has had direct access to Plaintiff’s son, knows he is in North Carolina, and has not issued a deposition subpoena which is the process by which as non-party can be required to provide testimony. (Opp., pp. 3-4.) Lastly, Plaintiff’s counsel reiterates that they do not represent Plaintiff’s son and cannot be made to produce him or provide him notice of a proposed deposition. (Mot., p. 5.)

            In reply, Defendant argues that this motion does not intend to force Plaintiff to produce their son, accept service of deposition on behalf of Plaintiff’s son, or force Plaintiff’s counsel to undertake representing Plaintiff’s son. (Reply, p. 1.) Defendant argues that instead this motion seeks to hold Plaintiff to their word and many representations in arranging for the deposition of Plaintiff’s son. Reply, p. 2.)

            Here, Plaintiff fails to state the applicable legal authority and similarly mischaracterizes the objective of the instant motion. As Plaintiff correctly states, Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.420 authorizes protective orders limiting or conditioning a deposition on a showing of good cause, for the protection of a party, a deponent, or another affected natural person. (Opp., p. 5.) It does not authorize an order directing opposing counsel to arrange a non-party's deposition. (Ibid.) The process by which a non-party is required to provide a deposition is via subpoena, the same is true for non-party deponents who are out of state. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2020.010(b); Code Civ. Proc., § 2026.010(c).) Defendant seeks to require Plaintiff to produce a non-party, which is not achieved through a protective order.

            Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion is DENIED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant Harry Balian, M.D.’s Motion for Protective Order is DENIED.

BACKGROUND

This is a worker’s compensation action. On June 3, 2024, plaintiff Rafael Gomez Wilson (Plaintiff) was employed and working within the scope of his employment with Next Level Burger Company, Inc., dba Veggie Grill (Employer). Plaintiff was outside cleaning tables when a car crashed into the building, causing Plaintiff to hit a table and umbrella and sustain injuries (the Incident). On March 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Complaint against defendants Edward Olshansky, Galina Makovoz, 4300 Burbank, LLC, and DOES 1-100 (collectively, Defendants) alleging negligence.  

Technology Insurance Company, Inc. (Technology) is the workers compensation provider of Employer and has been paying out worker’s compensation insurance benefits to Plaintiff as a result of the Incident.

On July 24, 2026, Technology’s previous motion to intervene was denied for insufficient service.

On July 29, 2026, Technology filed a renewed motion for leave to intervene in the action. On September 2, 2026, Technology filed a declaration addressing service. No opposition has been filed as of September 8, 2026.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

An intervention takes place when a nonparty, an intervenor, seeks to become a party to an action or proceeding between other persons by joining a plaintiff in claiming what is sought by the complaint, joining a defendant in resisting the plaintiff’s claims, or demanding anything adverse to both a plaintiff and a defendant. (Code Civ. Proc., § 387, subd. (b).)

“A nonparty shall petition the court for leave to intervene by noticed motion or ex parte application. The petition shall include a copy of the proposed complaint in intervention or answer in intervention and set forth the grounds upon which intervention rests.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 387(c).) 

If the nonparty timely files its application, then the court shall permit the nonparty to intervene in the action as long as either of the following conditions is satisfied: (A) A provision of law confers an unconditional right to intervene; (B) The person seeking intervention claims an interest relating to the property or transaction that is the subject of the action and that person is so situated that the disposition of the action may impair or impede that person's ability to protect that interest, unless that person's interest is adequately represented by one or more of the existing parties. (Code Civ. Proc., § 387, subd. (d)(1).) 

Otherwise, the court may permit the nonparty to intervene in the action, as long as the motion is timely, if the person has an interest in the matter in litigation, or in the success of either of the parties, or an interest against both. (Code Civ. Proc., § 387(d)(2).) This section is construed liberally “in favor of intervention.” (Simpson Redwood Co. v. State of California (1987) 196 Cal.App.3d 1192, 1200.) “For permissive intervention three factors are paramount: the intervenor must have a direct interest in the lawsuit, the intervenor must not enlarge the issues raised by the original parties[,] and the intervenor must not tread on the rights of the original parties to conduct their own lawsuit.” (Kuperstein v. Superior Court (1988) 204 Cal.App.3d 598, 600.)

There is no statutory time limit for filing a motion to intervene. (Noya v. A.W. Coulter Trucking (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 838, 842.) “[T]he timeliness of a motion to intervene under section 387 should be determined based on the date the proposed interveners knew or should have known their interests in the litigation were not being adequately represented.” (Ziani Homeowners Assn. v. Brookfield Ziani LLC (2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 274, 282.) Intervention is possible at any time, even after judgment, if otherwise appropriate.  (Mallick v. Superior Court (1979) 89 Cal.App.3d 434, 437.)  

If intervention is granted, the intervenor must (1) separately file (a) a complaint-in-intervention, (b) answer-in-intervention, or (c) both and (2) serve all parties. (Code Civ. Proc., § 387, subd. (e).)   

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

As of June 3, 2026, Technology has allegedly paid out a total of $13,945.25 to Plaintiff. (Kirsch Decl., ¶ 5.) Technology intervenes in this action on the grounds that it has an interest in the matter in litigation, and in the outcome of the litigation, consisting of (a) a worker’s compensation subrogation lien over and against any settlement or judgment award to Plaintiff and (b) a credit against future worker’s compensation liability in the amount of Plaintiff’s recovery from Defendants.

Procedural Requirements

            The Court notes that Technology has complied with the statutory requirements of the Code of Civil Procedure, section 387. Technology has petitioned the Court with a noticed motion including a copy of the proposed Complaint in Intervention setting forth the ground upon which intervention rests. (Kirsch Decl., Exh. A.)

Right to Intervene

            Technology argues that they have an absolute right to intervene at any time before trial pursuant to California Labor Code section 3850. (Mot., p. 4.) Alternatively, Technology argues they have the permissive right to intervene pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 387(d)(1)(B). (Ibid.) Technology argues that the worker’s compensation subrogation lien interests arise from the Incident and the Incident is the subject of this litigation. (Ibid.) Moreover, Technology argues that no other party is representing their interests, thus the court should permit Technology to intervene in the action. (Mot., p. 5.)

            Here, Technology claims an interest in the form of a lien relating to the Incident that is the subject of the action and the disposition of the action may impair or impede Technology’s ability to protect that interest since no one currently involved in the action is representing their interests. (Kirsch Decl., ¶ 5.) Thus, Technology should be permitted to intervene pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 378(d)(1)(B).

Sufficient Service

            In civil cases involving both represented and self-represented parties, each self-represented party or other person is to file, serve, and be served with documents by non-electronic means unless the self-represented party or other person affirmatively agrees otherwise. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.253(b)(3).) A self-represented party may express consent to electronic service by either (1) serving a notice on all parties and filing the notice with the court, or (2) manifesting affirmative through electronic means with the court and concurrently providing the party’s electronic address. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1010.6(c)(3).) The act of electronic filing shall not be construed as express consent. (Ibid.) Here, the Court finds no record of Plaintiff, who is a pro per litigant, consenting to electronic service.

            Here, Plaintiff has still not consented to electronic service, however, Technology has mailed physical copies of the noticed motion and accompanying documents to Plaintiff’s address (See Proof of Service filed 7/29/26 and 9/2/26.) Further, Technology declares having spoken to Plaintiff in order to confirm the provided address is current and accurate. (Kirsch Decl., ¶¶ 3-5.) Thus, Technology has cured defective service by providing service to pro per Plaintiff via non-electronic means.

Accordingly, the motion for leave to intervene is GRANTED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Technology Insurance Company, Inc.’s Motion for Leave to Intervene is GRANTED.
