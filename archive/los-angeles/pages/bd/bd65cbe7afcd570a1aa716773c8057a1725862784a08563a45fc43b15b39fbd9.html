IMRAN KHAN,

Plaintiff,

vs.

MICHAEL R. “HARRY-O” HARRIS, et al.

Defendants.

Case No.:

23STCV18020

Hearing Date:

August 3, 2026

ORDER GRANTING MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE CROSS COMPLAINT

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Defendants secured funding from Plaintiff Imran Khan (“Khan”) to produce an episodic television program focused on changes in incarcerated individuals, including Defendant Michael Harris (“Harris”), to sell to a major network. Defendant Omar Shakur (“Shakur”) allegedly assured Khan he would either receive a return via sale of the show, or from Harris. Khan has yet to receive any financial return. Khan sued Defendants for four causes of action, including breach of contract.

On August 25, 2025, Harris filed the instant motion for leave to file a cross-complaint. No party filed an opposition.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 426.50[1] provides as follows:

 “A party who fails to plead a cause of action subject to the requirements of this article, whether through oversight, inadvertence, mistake, neglect, or other cause, may apply to the court for leave to amend his pleading, or to file a cross-complaint, to assert such cause at any time during the course of the action. The court, after notice to the adverse party, shall grant, upon such terms as may be just to the parties, leave to amend the pleading, or to file the cross-complaint, to assert such cause if the party who failed to plead the cause acted in good faith. This subdivision shall be liberally construed to avoid forfeiture of causes of action.”

Section 428.50 provides “(a) A party shall file a cross-complaint against any of the parties who filed the complaint or cross-complaint against him or her before or at the same time as the answer to the complaint or cross-complaint. (b) Any other cross-complaint may be filed at any time before the court has set a date for trial. (c) A party shall obtain leave of court to file any cross-complaint except one filed within the time specified in subdivision (a) or (b). Leave may be granted in the interest of justice at any time during the course of the action.”

“The legislative mandate is clear. A policy of liberal construction of section 426.50 to avoid forfeiture of causes of action is imposed on the trial court. A motion to file a cross-complaint at any time during the course of the action must be granted unless bad faith of the moving party is demonstrated where forfeiture would otherwise result. Factors such as oversight, inadvertence, neglect, mistake or other cause, are insufficient grounds to deny the motion unless accompanied by bad faith.” (Silver Organizations Ltd. v. Frank (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 94, 98-99.) 

 

III.       DISCUSSION

Harris requests leave to file an attached compulsory cross-complaint against Defendants Paul Nutall (“Paul”) and Tiffany Nutall (“Tiffany”) for implied contractual indemnification, equitable indemnification, and declaratory relief. In general, the Court must grant a motion to file a cross-complaint absent a showing of bad faith on the moving party’s behalf.

Harris requests leave to file the cross-complaint following information uncovered during discovery. During the November 2024 deposition of Shakur, Shakur produced documents purported to be partnership agreements with Harris and the Nutalls. Counsel reviewed these documents and found them to provide a basis for a cross-complaint. In June 2025, Harris issued subpoenas for the deposition and production of documents to the Nutalls to conduct further discovery into this issue. The Nutalls objected and the depositions did not move forward. At that point, Harris filed this motion for leave to file a cross-complaint against Paul and Tiffany.

Harris filed this motion after attempting to conduct diligent discovery into the Nutalls involvement, only filing upon reviewing documents and being unable to otherwise pursue investigation. There is no currently scheduled trial date, leaving Tiffany and Paul sufficient time to respond and conduct their own discovery. Additionally, the Court notes Tiffany and Paul have yet to appear in this action in any capacity—therefore, they have likely not advanced a trial strategy in any capacity that would be prejudiced by the filing of a cross-complaint.

The Court grants the motion.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Defendant Michael Harris’s motion for leave to file the cross-complaint is GRANTED.

Harris is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 24STCV07942    Hearing Date: August 3, 2026    Dept: 730

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

Department 730

 

CITIBANK N.A.,

Plaintiff,

vs.

JAMIE INZUNZA,

Defendant.

Case No.:

24STCV07942

Hearing Date:

August 3, 2026

ORDER GRANTING MOTION TO VACATE DISMISSAL AND ENTER JUDGMENT PURSUANT TO STIPULATED SETTLEMENT

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Citibank N.A. (“Citibank”) sued Defendant Hamie Inzunza’s (“Inzunza”) for common counts to collect Inzunza’s overdue credit card payments, totaling $36,461.86.

On April 28, 2025, Citibank filed a Stipulation and Order for Settlement Agreement, which the Court granted. Pursuant to stipulation, Inzunza was dismissed and the Court retained jurisdiction pursuant to Code of Civ. Proc. section 664.6.

On November 4, 2025, Citibank filed the instant motion to vacate dismissal and enter judgment. No party filed an opposition. 

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6, subdivision (a)[1], “If parties to pending litigation stipulate, in a writing signed by the parties outside the presence of the court or orally before the court, for settlement of the case, or part thereof, the court, upon motion, may enter judgment pursuant to the terms of the settlement. If requested by the parties, the court may retain jurisdiction over the parties to enforce the settlement until performance in full of the terms of the settlement.” A writing is signed by a party if it is signed by a party, an attorney who represents the party, or if the party is an insurer, an agent who is authorized in writing by the insurer to sign on the insurer's behalf.  (§ 664.6, subd. (b).)

Strict compliance with the statutory requirements is necessary before a court can enforce a settlement agreement under this statute. (Sully-Miller Contracting Co. v. Gledson/Cashman Construction, Inc. (2002) 103 Cal.App.4th 30, 37.) Thus, to enforce a written settlement agreement under section 664.6, the following three elements must be met: (1) the parties must have come to a meeting of the minds on all material points; (2) there must be a writing that contains the material terms of the agreement; and (3) the writing must be signed by the parties. (Weddington Productions, Inc. v. Flick (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 793, 797-98.) 

           

III.       DISCUSSION

Citibank requests the Court vacate dismissal and enter judgment of $35,516.47, consisting of $34,462.86 in unpaid principal and $1,053.61 in costs, pursuant to the Court’s authority to enter judgment under section 664.6. Under section 664.6, subd. (a), the Court may vacate dismissal and enter judgment on a pursuant to the terms of a stipulated settlement.

Here, the settlement stipulation (“the stipulation”) provides in pertinent as follows:

“Jaime Inzunza ("Defendant") stipulates to the entry of judgment in favor of CITIBANK, N.A. ("Plaintift") in the principal sum of $36,461.86 plus court costs pursuant to a memorandum of costs (which will be limited to Plaintiff's fee for filing the complaint; Plaintiff's fee for service of process; fees (including any reporter fee that the court may require at the time a motion or application is filed) for any motion, application, and/or order that has been granted, including the motion or application to enforce this Agreement and any order fee required to file the attached proposed order; and Defendant's first appearance fee if Plaintiff advances that fee in order to file this Agreement); less credit for payments made in good funds before the time of default.

Plaintiff and Defendant (together, the "Parties") agree that $36,461.86 is the accurate liquidated amount owed by Defendant for the account number ending in 1098 (the "Account").

Notwithstanding Defendant's agreement to have judgment entered against him/her, Plaintiff will not request that judgment be entered so long as Defendant is not in default with the payment plan set forth herein.

Defendant agrees to pay to Plaintiff a one-time payment of $499.67 on or before December 31, 2024; followed by a one-time payment of $497.69 on or before January 20, 2025; followed by a minimum of $499.50 on or before the 20th day of each and every month commencing in February, 2025, until the Defendant has paid the Judgment Amount. These consecutive monthly installments, totaling $36.461.86. timely received, will constitute full satisfaction of the Judgment Amount. Time is of the essence with respect to all payments. If Defendant fails to make full and timely payment of any installment or if any payment is reversed, then the full remaining balance will be due, and Plaintiff shall be entitled to enter judgment for the Judgment Amount plus court costs... less credit for payments made in good funds before the time of default.

The Parties jointly request that this court retain jurisdiction under Code of Civil Procedure § 664.6. The Parties further request that this court vacate all future hearings and stay all further action on this case pending completion of the Parties' respective obligations under this Stipulation. If and only if local rules so require, the Parties, by their signatures below, authorize the court to dismiss this case without prejudice. To the extent that the court dismisses the case without prejudice and pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 664.6, the court shall retain jurisdiction. The Parties further agree that in the event of a default in payments and upon Plaintiff's motion served and filed in accordance with Code of Civil Procedure § 1005(b), the court shall reopen the case, vacate any dismissal, and enter judgment for the Judgment Amount plus any motion and/or order fee(s) required by the court, less credit for payments made.”

(Mot., Request for Judicial Notice, Ex. B.)

The stipulation submitted by Citibank was signed by Inzunza and by Citibank’s representative, Ruonan Wang. (Ibid.) The stipulation was approved by the Court, which retained jurisdiction to enforce settlement pursuant to section 664.6.

Citibank asserts Inzunza has not made a settlement payment since April 24, 2025. (Mot., DiPiero Decl. ¶ 5.) Inzunza has paid a total of $1,999.00 pursuant to the stipulation, leaving an unpaid balance of $34,462.86. (Id. at ¶ 7.) Following the failure to render payment, Citibank issued a 10-day written notice on Inzunza of failure to make payment and Citibank’s intention to request entry of judgment. (Id. at ¶ 6, Ex. A.)

The Court finds that the statutory requirements of section 664.6 have been met. Additionally, Inzunza did not oppose the motion. A failure to oppose a motion may be deemed consent to the granting of the motion. (Cal. Rules of Court rule 8.54(c).) The motion to enforce the settlement agreement is therefore granted.

Citibank seeks the unpaid principal and costs, both of which are specifically outlined as recoverable under the settlement agreement. (Mot., Request for Judicial Notice, Ex. B.) Citibank specifically requests costs of $980.00 for filing and motion fees and $73.61 for service of process fees. These costs are prima facie reasonable and outlined as recoverable within the settlement agreement. Therefore, the Court finds the requested judgment both reasonable and requested pursuant to stipulated terms.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

Plaintiff Citibank’s motion to enforce the settlement agreement is GRANTED.

Judgment is entered against Inzunza in the total amount of $35,516.47.

Moving Party is ordered to give notice.

Superior Court of California

SIMONE V. MOORE,

Plaintiff,

vs.

SCH RESTAURANT ASSOCIATES, et al.,

Defendant.

Case No.:

24STCV22751

Hearing Date:

August 3, 2026

ORDER DENYING MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Simone V. Moore (“Moore”) was allegedly physically intimidated and sexually harassed by her co-worker, Defendant Dakota Berman (“Berman”), while working for Defendant SBH Restaurant Associates, LLC (“SBH”). Moore reported this behavior to SBH. SBH allegedly failed to correct Berman’s behavior and retaliated against Moore. Moore sued Defendants for seven causes of action, including unlawful retaliation.

On April 17, 2026, SBH filed the instant motion for summary judgment, or, in the alternative, summary adjudication. Moore filed an opposition.

 

II.         OBJECTIONS

The Court does not rule on the issue of objections, as the Court need only rule on objections to evidence deemed material to its disposition of the motion. (Code of Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (q).)

 

III.       LEGAL STANDARD

A party may move for summary judgment “if it is contended that the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding.”  (Code Civ. Proc.[1] § 437c, subd. (a).) “[I]f all the evidence submitted, and all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law,” the moving party will be entitled to summary judgment. (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

The moving party bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact, and if he does so, the burden shifts to the opposing party to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850, citing to § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence.  (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

Neither a moving nor responding party may rely on the mere allegations or denials of its pleadings. A moving party must submit specific admissible evidence showing that the responding party cannot establish at least one element of his, her or its cause of action or defense. The responding party, to defeat the motion, must submit specific admissible evidence showing that a triable issue of material fact does exist as to that element of the cause of action or defense. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

The “Golden Rule” on a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication is that “if [a fact] is not set forth in the separate statement, it does not exist.” (Zimmerman, Rosenfeld, Gersh & Leeds LLP v. Larson (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 1466, 1477, citing United Community Church v. Garcin (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 327, 337.).

 

IV.        DISCUSSION

SBH requests the Court enter summary judgment as Moore cannot make a prima facie case against SBH, SBH had legitimate, non-retaliatory reasons for alleged adverse employment actions, and Moore’s claims are preempted by the California Workers’ Compensation Act.

A.         Statement of Facts

On or about March 21, 2022, SBH hired Moore as a part-time host at Spago, SBH’s restaurant. (UMF 10.) As part of Moore’s host duties, she was required to welcome and seat guests, provide customer service, process reservations, maintain the cleanliness of the host station and menu, and have knowledge of relevant restaurant information, such as hours, specials, and events. (UMF 11.) Moore also occasionally worked as a reservationist. (UMF 12.) As a reservationist, Moore was required to field all reservation inquiries, manage online reservations, adjust/confirm reservations as needed, and have knowledge of relevant restaurant information. (UMF 14.)

In October 2022, SBH hired Berman as a busser at Spago. (UMF 15.) As a busser, Berman was required to reset and clear tables, refill water glasses, assist servers, and clean service stations. (UMF 16.)

According to Moore, in 2023, Berman began leaning his body into Moore and hugging Moore. (SF 171, 177.) Moore would attempt to avoid Berman, but Berman would come and learn at the host stand, despite the fact the bussing station is across the restaurant. (SF 173.) Moore also attested Berman attempted to hug and ask out another host on the date, who rejected Berman. (SF 175-178.)

On August 6, 2023, while Moore was leaving work, Berman jumped out in front of her and pretended to punch Moore in the fact. (UMF 20-21.) Moore reported the incident that same day to Walter Canas (“Canas”), the former officer manager, via text. (UMF 24.) Canas thanked Moore for the information and copied other Spago managers to advise them of the complaint. (UMF 25.) Berman apologized to Moore the next time they worked together. (UMF 26.) On August 9, 2023, Berman apologized to Moore for the incident via Instagram direct messaging; Moore responded, stating she accepted the apology, two days later. (UMF 27-28.) Moore found this interaction inappropriate; when Moore informed Ian Curtis, front of house manager, he told her this was good enough. (SF 98, 210-211.) Canas emailed Moore that same day, confirming Berman had directly apologized to Moore. (UMF 29.)

On September 19, 2023, SBH issued Moore and three other employees a written warning for improper timekeeping practices; Moore’s was a first and final warning. (UMF 36, SF 212.) Moore signed the warning on September 20, 2023. (UMF 37.) Moore alleges that as early as October 25, 2022, she had problems with Tobie Cancino (“Cancino”), manager of special events, allowing her to clock in for work, of which she informed Gina Borowitz (“Borowitz”), Spago’s director of human resources (“HR”). (SF 110, 168.) She also complained to Borowitz about Cancino making her wait in the restaurant alley for up to 45 minutes. (SF 191.)

The next day, Moore e-mailed Borowitz about the August 6th incident and Berman’s additional inappropriate work behavior. (UMF 38.) Moore alleged Berman stood too close to her at the host stand, was oddly aggressive “like saying hello to me repeatedly and loudly,” snatched chits out of her hands, mocked her, and uncomfortably stared at Moore. (UMF 38.) That same day, Spago’s HR generalist, Natasha Hartwell (“Hartwell”), called Moore and asked Moore to provide additional details to allow SBH to further investigate. (UMF 40.) Moore did not send a responsive statement. (UMF 41.)

On September 29, 2023, Moore emailed Hartwell about a new incident concerning Berman. Berman allegedly went to the host stand to air his grievances with the hosts, to which Moore told Berman to stay away from her. (UMF 43.) Moore claimed she was told by Curtis to not escalate Berman’s harassment. (Mot., Barrick Decl. ¶ 6, Ex. D, Ex. 14.) Moore also noted she felt “extremely harassed and unsafe and uncomfortable by this person who will not stop harassing [her] at work.” (Ibid.) She also submitted an incident report, detailing the incident, and informed multiple supervisors she felt unsafe. (SF 250, 257.) Moore also contacted the owner of Spago to inform her of Berman’s harassment; she was told to report it to HR and ask the person bothering her to leave her alone. (SF 329-330.) SBH suspended Berman from work from October 1, 2023, to October 9, 2023. (UMF 44.) On October 2, 2023, Hartwell and Moore spoke on the phone; Moore reported Berman spent unnecessary time at the host stand and was invading her personal space. (UMF 46-47.)

Hartwell continued to investigate Moore’s complaint by, in part, reviewing documents, watching surveillance video, and interviewing eight witnesses. (UMF 48.) SBH determined Berman’s behavior violated company policy. (UMF 50.) SBH issued Berman a written warning stating he had violated SBH’s policy for the August 6, 2023, and further unprofessional conduct would result in further disciplinary action, up to and including termination. (UMF 51.) Hartwell recommended Berman for termination due to a violation of the workplace violence policy, which Borowitz denied. (SF 271–273.)

On October 11, 2023, Moore e-mailed Hartwell to inform her Berman was “at the top of her [Instagram Home] page,” and he was looking at her Instagram account. (SF 284.) She also informed Hartwell managers told her she was required to be walked to her car. (SF 283.) Hartwell asked for clarification on the managers and suggested Moore utilize tools to block/restrict access to Berman. (SF 284, Opp., Ex. 31.) There is no indication Moore responded.

Moore alleges that, following her complaint, she was shunned. (SF 321.) Berman and Moore were scheduled together for every single shift until Moore left in August 2024. (SF 319.) Berman would block Moore’s movement and pretended to walk into Moore, which Moore reported. (SF 322-323.) Steve Scott Springer, the general manager, stopped being as friendly and scrutinized Moore more. (SF 316, 349.) Moore was uncomfortable and reported all of this to Hartwell. (SF 317.) Moore never worked a Spago private event again; she was snubbed from introductions. (SF 318, 106.)

On or around September 1, 2024, Moore began a California Family Rights Act (“CFRA”) leave of absence, scheduled through November 1, 2024. (UMF 54.) On October 24, 2024, Moore submitted a doctor’s note to extend her medical leave until December 15, 2024; CRFA would have been exhausted on November 23, 2024, so on November 22, 2024, SBH invited Moore to discuss reasonable accommodations of her disability. (UMF 56.) Moore did not respond. (UMF 56.) On December 1, 2024, Moore notified SBH she resigned, effective December 15, 2024. (UMF 57.)

At all relevant times, Spago was only open Tuesday through Sunday. (UMF 4.) Spago was only open to customers for dinner and evening bar service. (UMF 2.) It is usually open from 5 p.m. to approximately 11 p.m. (UMF 4.)

SBH requests all Spago applicants submit a resume and attend a hiring interview prior to SBH making an employment offer. (UMF 8.) SBH also provides training on prevention of workplace harassment, discrimination, and retaliation. (UMF 9.) Berman attended this training on June 14, 2023, while Moore attended on June 15, 2023. (UMF 9.)

B.          Unlawful Harassment

SBH first requests the Court grant summary judgment as to the cause of action for unlawful harassment under the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (“FEHA”) as no actionable harassment occurred and SBH took immediate and corrective action.

1.          Immediate Corrective Action

First, SBH argues it took appropriate and immediate action, and therefore cannot be liable. “An employer is strictly liable for harassment committed by its agents or supervisors, but is liable for harassment committed by its other employees only if it fails to take immediate and appropriate corrective action when reasonably made aware of the situation.” (Jones v. Department of Corrections & Rehabilitation (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 1367, 1377.) It is undisputed Berman was not Moore’s supervisor at the time of the incident. Therefore, SBH may only be liable if SBH did not take immediate and appropriate corrective action.

SBH argues that SBH acted appropriately because, after Moore complained to HR about Berman, HR suspended Berman, investigated, and issued Berman a written warning. Moore never complained about Berman again, indicating SBH’s action appropriately addressed the situation.

The Court disagrees. First, the Court notes SBH offers no case law or analysis that supports SBH’s assertion that the suspension, investigation, and written warning is appropriate corrective action. SBH merely asserts as such. The Court also notes the evidence does not show SBH took timely action in response to Moore's initial complaint to Canas, which may be classified as harassment. Although Berman apologized, there is no indication that apology was elicited in response to any action by SBH. Finally, the Court notes Berman has provided evidence she did make further reported about Berman, indicating the action taken may have been insufficient. Therefore, the Court does not find a basis to grant summary judgment on these grounds.

2.          Prima Facie Case

SBH next argues Moore cannot demonstrate a prima facie case for harassment against SBH. “To establish a prima facie case of a hostile work environment, [the plaintiff] must show that (1) [plaintiff] is a member of a protected class; (2) [plaintiff] was subjected to unwelcome harassment; (3) the harassment was based on [plaintiff’s] protected status; (4) the harassment unreasonably interfered with [plaintiff’s] work performance by creating an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5) defendants are liable for the harassment.” (Ortiz v. Dameron Hospital Assn. (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 568, 581.)

SBH claims Moore cannot show the alleged conduct was based on a protected characteristic. When asked at her deposition why Berman pretended to punch her, Moore stated she was unaware—not that it was based upon her status as woman. (UMF 31.) SBH alleges that any further ‘harassing’ behavior, was not actually harassing, and instead within the scope of Berman’s duties.

The Court finds there is dispute of material facts as to this issue. Moore alleged Berman routinely invaded her personal space by standing close, touching the small of her back, and leaning in at the host stand, because she was a woman. All of this behavior would be outside the scope of Berman’s employment. Moore notes Berman acted similarly towards another female host, but never did anything similar to male coworkers. As such, the Court finds there is dispute as to whether Moore can demonstrate harassment occurred based upon a protected status.

SBH next argues Moore cannot show the alleged conduct was severe or pervasive enough to create an abusive working environment. To determine if conduct is severe or pervasive, a court may review frequency and severity of the conduct, if it is physically threatening or humiliating, or if the conduct unreasonably interfered with the plaintiff’s work performance, based upon the impact to a reasonable person. (Miller v. Dept. of Corrections (2005) 36 Cal.4th 446, 462.) However, there is also dispute of material fact as to this issue.

The evidence shows at least once instance of physically threatening conduct (the ‘air-punch’) and multiple reports of further invasive behavior, such as unnecessarily approaching the hosting stand to touch, crowd, and talk to Moore, all after Moore indicating she did not want to interact with Berman. She provides evidence of reports made indicating she was unable to work properly due to these repetitious behaviors. The allegations and evidence support harassing conduct beyond the unpublished cases cited by SBH, which merely indicate one off incidents of rude speech. The Court finds there is dispute of material fact.

Therefore, the Court denies summary judgment as to this cause of action.

C.          Unlawful Retaliation

SBH requests the Court grant summary judgment as to the cause of action for unlawful retaliation under FEHA, as Moore cannot establish a prima facie case and all alleged ‘retaliation’ was done for legitimate purpose. “[I]n order to establish a prima facie case of retaliation under the FEHA, a plaintiff must show (1) he or she engaged in a ‘protected activity,’ (2) the employer subjected the employee to an adverse employment action, and (3) a causal link existed between the protected activity and the employer’s action. Once an employee establishes a prima facie case, the employer is required to offer a legitimate, nonretaliatory reason for the adverse employment action. If the employer produces a legitimate reason for the adverse employment action, the presumption of retaliation ‘ “ ‘drops out of the picture,’ ” ’ and the burden shifts back to the employee to prove intentional retaliation.” (Yanowitz v. L’Oreal USA, Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1042.)

SBH argues SBH did not take any adverse employment action against Moore, as SBH did not limit Moore’s shifts, terminate Moore’s employment, and scheduled Berman and Moore pursuant to the limited shifts available given Spago’s schedule. Moore argues SBH did take adverse employment action against Moore, by failing to move either Spago or Moore to another local SBH location—eventually resulting in Moore quitting--issuing a pretextual final write up against Moore, and by HR representatives claiming Moore’s e-mails were stressful, indicating she should send less.

Neither party offered any case law or analysis to support their assertions that these are or are not indicative of adverse employment actions. Absent analysis, the Court cannot find SBH has met its initial burden of proving SBH did not take adverse employment action against Moore.

The Court also finds there is dispute as to causal link between protected activity and the employer’s action. For example, if SBH continued to schedule Breman with Moore for every shift, as alleged, despite being aware of Moore’s ongoing complaints against Breman, that made constitute a causal link to Moore eventually leaving due to constructive discharge. As SBH offers no case law or analysis in support of this assertion, the Court also cannot find SBH met their burden of proving there is no causal link.

SBH finally argues all decisions were made for legitimate nonretaliatory reasons. SBH specifically focuses upon Breman and Moore’s scheduling, which remained consistent from before Moore’s reports until Moore’s last day of work, due to Spago’s limited dinner service. SBH does not address issues such as failure to respond Moore’s alleged ongoing complaints regarding Breman, following the original investigation. As there remain unaddressed facts and issues without explanation as to legitimate nonretaliatory purpose, the Court does not find SBH met its burden.

D.         Failure to Take Reasonable Steps to Prevent/Correct Harassment

SBH solely argues the Court should grant judgment as to this cause of action, as Moore cannot prove SBH is liable for harassment or retaliation. However, SBH failed to prove, as a matter of law, Moore cannot bring a viable cause of action for harassment or retaliation under FEHA. Therefore, the Court will not grant judgment as to this cause of action.

E.          Labor Code Retaliation

SBH next argues Moore cannot bring a cause of action for retaliation under Labor Code section 1102.5, as Moore’s alleged ‘whistleblowing’ activity was a contributing factor in SBH’s ‘retaliatory’ decisions. (Lawson v. PPG Architectural Finishes, Inc. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 703, 712.)

Labor Code section 1102.5 provides no employer, or any person acting on behalf of an employer, shall retaliate against an employee for disclosing information to a government or law enforcement agency, to a person with authority over the employee or another employee who has the authority to investigate and correct the violation, or for providing information to a public body for an investigation, if the employee has reasonable cause to believe the information discloses a violation of a statute or rule. (LC § 1102.5, subd. (b).)

Again, SBH argues there is no evidence of retaliatory activity or causal connection. However, SBH again provides no case law or analysis in support of this assertion, merely alleging the consisting scheduling indicates a lack of retaliatory behavior. As noted previously, this is insufficient to meet SBH’s burden of demonstrating a lack of dispute of material fact, as a matter of law.

F.          Negligent Hiring, Supervision, and Retention

SBH requests the Court enter summary judgment as to the cause of action for negligence hiring, supervision, and retention, as Moore has not met her burden of demonstrating Berman created a particular risk or hazard to Moore or other employees. “An employer may be liable to a third person for the employer’s negligence in hiring or retaining an employee who is incompetent or unfit.” (Roman Catholic Bishop v. Superior Court (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 1556, 1564–1565.) Liability for negligent supervision of an employee is one of direct liability for negligence. (Delfino v. Agilent Technologies, Inc. (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 790, 815.) A plaintiff must show that the defendant had actual or constructive notice that a particular employee posed a specific risk of the type of harm that occurred. (Doe v. Capital Cities (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 1038, 1054.)

SBH specifically only focuses upon knowledge leading up the ‘air punch’ event, asserting after the fact Moore never complained Berman was unfit to work as a busser and that this unfitness created a particular risk to others. However, again, there is dispute as to material fact as to whether Moore lodged additional complaints. In the additional complaints, there is evidence of Moore identifying how Berman harassed Moore and other employees by refusing to properly do his job. Therefore, this is still at issue and proper for review at trial.

SBH also argues the workers’ compensation exclusivity provisions of the Workers’ Compensation Act apply such that workers’ compensation is the sole and exclusive remedy for any injuries Moore may have sustained arising from negligent supervision. However, the Workers’ Compensation Act does not apply to allegations of discrimination, harassment, or retaliation. It solely is focused upon the injury or death of an employee. (Labor Code § 3602.) “So long as the basic conditions of compensation are otherwise satisfied [citation], and the employer’s conduct neither contravenes fundamental public policy [citation] nor exceeds the risks inherent in the employment relationship [citation], an employee’s emotional distress injuries are subsumed under the exclusive remedy provisions of workers’ compensation.” (Livitsanos v. Superior Court (1992) 2 Cal.4th 744, 754.) As Moore is alleging her injuries stem from harassment, retaliation, and discrimination, Workers’ Compensation is not the exclusive remedy to Moore’s claims.   

G.         Constructive Discharge

SBH next argues Moore cannot meet her prima facie burden to establish a cause of action for constructive discharge. “[C]onstructive discharge occurs only when an employer terminates employment by forcing the employee to resign. A constructive discharge is equivalent to a dismissal, although it is accomplished indirectly. Constructive discharge occurs only when the employer coerces the employee’s resignation, either by creating working conditions that are intolerable under an objective standard, or by failing to remedy objectively intolerable working conditions that actually are known to the employer. We have said ‘a constructive discharge is legally regarded as a firing rather than a resignation.’ ” (Mullins v. Rockwell Internat. Corp. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 731, 737, internal citations omitted.)

As addressed previously, there is dispute as to whether SBH ignored further reports from Moore as to Berman’s alleged harassment. If SBH was aware of this behavior, failed to investigate, and continued to schedule Moore and Berman together, without taking any further action to discipline or disincentivize Berman, this may be considered a basis for constructive discharge. The mere fact Moore was on medical leave prior to her actually filing a notice of resignation or that she moved at a certain point, does not mean the events leading up to her leave or move felt she had no other means to escape harassment than to leave her job.

The Court denies summary judgment on this basis.

H.         Punitive Damages / Conclusion

The Court denies the request to grant summary judgment as to punitive damages as there remains dispute of material fact as to whether any defendant acted with malice or otherwise oppressively.

In total, SBH has failed to meet their burden of presenting case law and analysis demonstrating the undisputed facts mandate summary judgment as to any cause of action. Therefore, the Court denies the motion in full.

 

V.         CONCLUSION

Defendant SBH Restaurant Associates, LLC’s motion for summary judgment is DENIED.

Moore is to give notice.

ORDER DEEMING MOTIONS TO COMPEL RESPONSES TO DISCOVERY MOOT.

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Chris Baggett (“Baggett”) was allegedly employed by Defendants Allied Health Solutions, Inc. (“AHS”), Allied Health Solutions Medical Group (“AHSMG”), and Allied Health Solutions IPA (“IPA”), when he developed and/or discovered physical and mental disabilities. Defendants allegedly denied Baggett various reasonable accommodations and a good faith interactive process, instead subjecting Baggett to discrimination, a hostile work environment, and eventual wrongful termination. Defendants also allegedly failed to properly pay Baggett. Baggett sued Defendants for 16 causes of action, including disability discrimination under FEHA.

On March 30, 2026, Baggett filed the instant motions to compel IPA and AHSMG’s responses to Request for Production of Documents, Set One. Baggett also requests sanctions of $3,360.00 against each defendant. IPA and AHSMG filed oppositions. Baggett filed replies.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.290,[1] “If a party to whom interrogatories are directed fails to serve a timely response, the following rules apply: (b) The party propounding the interrogatories may move for an order compelling response to the interrogatories.” According to section 2030.260, for a response to interrogatories to be timely, it must be served within 30 days of service.

Section 2030.290, subdivision (c), states “the court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”

Under section 2031.300, “If a party to whom a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling is directed fails to serve a timely response to it, the following rules shall apply: (b) The party making the demand may move for an order compelling response to the demand.” According to section 2031.260, for a response to request for production of documents to be timely, it must be served within 30 days of service.

Section 2023.030, subdivision (a), provides that “[t]he court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.” According to section 2023.010, subdivision (d), misuse of the discovery process includes “failing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery.”

           

III.       DISCUSSION

The Court first notes the electronic record does not include any moving papers in regard to the motion against IPA. Both the document labeled “Order Proposes Order” and “Motion to Compel” concerning IPA only contain a proposed order. As the record does not contain any moving papers for the Court to review, the Court DENIES the motion against IPA, on both the merits and the request for sanctions.

The Court addresses the motion to compel AHSMG.

A.         Motion to Compel

Baggett requests the Court order AHSMG to serve verified, code-complaint responses to Baggett’s Requests for Production, Set One. Pursuant to section 2031.300, subdivision (b), a party may move to compel responses to a request for production if the responding party fails to serve a timely response to the discovery request. A timely response is either within 30 days after service of the demand or within the extended timeframe agreed upon by parties. (§ 2031.260, subd. (a).)

Baggett served discovery on October 24, 2025. (Mot., Butzen Decl. ¶ 4, Ex. 2.) Parties agreed to multiple extensions, with the final extension date being January 26, 2026. (Id. at ¶¶ 5-6, Ex. 3-4.) As of the time of filing this motion, AHSMG had not served responses.

AHSMG served verified, code-compliant responses on July 22, 2026. (Opp., Oldham Decl. ¶ 3.) Therefore, this motion is MOOT. Issues regarding the substance of the motion are issues for motions to compel further responses.

B.          Sanctions

Baggett is entitled to sanctions pursuant to section 2023.030 and section 2023,010, subdivision (d), as AHSMG failed to timely respond to discovery absent Baggett seeking a court order.

Baggett requests $3,360.00 in sanctions, based upon 6 hours of attorney’s work at a rate of $550.00 per hour (Mot., Butzen Decl. ¶ 16), with the attorney’s work consisting of 3 hours drafting the motion, 2 hours reviewing and replying to any opposition, and 1 hour appearing for the hearing on the motions. The Court¿relies on its knowledge and experience in determining the reasonable¿value of the legal services provided under the circumstances in this case, e.g., the nature of this routine discovery motion and the fact responses were served. (In re Marriage of Huntington (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1513, 1524 [“When the trial court is informed of the extent and nature of the legal services rendered, it may rely on its own experience and knowledge in determining their reasonable value.”].) The Court has discretion to fix the amount of reasonable monetary sanctions.¿(Cornerstone Realty Advisors, LLC v. Summit¿Heatlthcare¿Reit, Inc.¿(2020) 56 Cal. App. 5th¿771, 791.)¿The Court finds¿a¿reasonable award is $1180.00, based on four hours of attorney’s work at a rate of $250.00 per hour. Therefore, the Court imposes sanctions of $1000.00 on AHSMG and AHSMG’s counsel.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Plaintiff Chris Baggett’s motion to compel Defendant IPA’s responses to Request for Production, Set One, is DENIED.

Plaintiff Chris Baggett’s motion to compel Defendant AHSMG’s responses to Request for Production, Set One, is MOOT.

Plaintiff Chris Baggett’s request for sanctions is GRANTED, in part. AHSMG and AHSMG’s counsel are ordered to pay $1,000.00 in sanctions to Baggett within 30 days of the hearing on the motion.

The request for sanctions is denied as to IPA.

Baggett is ordered to give notice.
