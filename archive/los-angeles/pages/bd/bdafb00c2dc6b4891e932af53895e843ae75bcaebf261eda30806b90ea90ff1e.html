II.          LEGAL STANDARD

            Demurrer

            “The primary function of a pleading is to give the other party notice so that it may prepare its case [citation], and a defect in a pleading that otherwise properly notifies a party cannot be said to affect substantial rights.” (Harris v. City of Santa Monica (2013) 56 Cal.4th 203, 240.)¿

            “A¿demurrer¿tests the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations in a complaint.” (Ivanoff v. Bank of America, N.A.¿(2017) 9 Cal.App.5th 719, 725.) The Court looks to whether “the complaint alleges facts sufficient to state a cause of action or discloses a complete defense.” (Id.) The Court does not “read passages from a complaint in isolation; in reviewing a ruling on a demurrer, we read the complaint ‘as a whole and its parts in their context.’ [Citation.]” (West v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 780, 804.) The Court “assume[s] the truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations, facts that reasonably can be inferred from those expressly pleaded and matters of which judicial notice has been taken.” (Harris, supra, 56 Cal.4th p. 240.) “The court does not, however, assume the truth of contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. [Citation.]” (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1358.)

            A general demurrer may be brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e) if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted or under section 430.10, subdivision (a), where the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading. All other grounds listed in Section 430.10, including uncertainty under subdivision (f), are special demurrers. Special demurrers are not allowed in limited jurisdiction courts. (Code Civ. Proc., § 92, subd. (c).)

            A demurrer for uncertainty means the pleading is ambiguous or unintelligible. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) “A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly's of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) ““‘[D]emurrers for uncertainty are disfavored and are granted only if the pleading is so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.’” (A.J. Fistes Corp. v. GDL Best Contractors, Inc. (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 677, 695.)

            Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.)

            Motion to Strike

            “Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof, but this time limitation shall not apply to motions specified in subdivision (e).” (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1).)¿¿  

            “The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: (a) Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a); see also Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”.].)¿

            The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with California law, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).) An immaterial or irrelevant allegation is one that is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense; is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense; or a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint. (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subd. (b).) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.)¿¿ 

III.       DISCUSSION

            Meet and Confer

           Prior to filing a demurrer and motion to strike, the demurring/moving party is required to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by videoconference with the party who filed the pleading for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer and motion to strike. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41; Code Civ. Proc., § 435.5.)¿¿

            Cross-Defendants’ counsel of record, Craig J. Mariam, attests to the following meet and confer efforts: “I had a telephonic meet-and-confer with opposing counsel regarding the substance and nature of the Demurrer and Motion to Strike the afternoon of July 6, 2026. Opposing counsel indicated he intended to oppose the motions. I subsequently forwarded drafts of the aforementioned motions to opposing counsel in advance of the filing deadline as well. We did not hear anything further from opposing counsel in advance of filing.” (Mariam Decl., ¶2.) Thus, the Court finds that Cross-Defendants have fulfilled their meet and confer obligation.  

 

            Demurrer

            Cross-Defendants demur to the all four causes of action alleged in the Cross-Complaint on the grounds that Cross-Complainants’ fail to state sufficient facts to constitute causes of action against them.

 

            First Cause of Action for Intentional Interference with Prospective Economic Advantage

            The elements of Intentional Interference With Prospective Economic Relations claim are: “ ‘(1) an economic relationship between the plaintiff and some third party, with the probability of future economic benefit to the plaintiff; (2) the defendant's knowledge of the relationship; (3) intentional acts on the part of the defendant designed to disrupt the relationship; (4) actual disruption of the relationship; and (5) economic harm to the plaintiff proximately caused by the acts of the defendant.’ [Citations.]” (Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1134, 1153.) Specifically, “intentionally interfering with prospective economic advantage requires pleading that the defendant committed an independently wrongful act.” (Ixchel Pharma, LLC v. Biogen, Inc. (2020) 9 Cal.5th 1130, 1142.) “[A]n act is independently wrongful if it is unlawful, that is, if it is proscribed by some constitutional, statutory, regulatory, common law, or other determinable legal standard.” (Id.) Furthermore, the third party must be identifiable for purposes of determining whether the probability of future economic benefit to plaintiff existed. (See generally, Westside Center Associates v. Safeway Stores 23, Inc. (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 507, 527; Roth v. Rhodes (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 530, 546.) Essentially, “[a]n actual economic relationship with a third party is required, and liability cannot be premised on the more speculative expectation that a potentially beneficial relationship will arise in the future.” (Muddy Waters, LLC v. Superior Court (2021) 62 Cal.App.5th 905, 926 [internal quotations omitted].) However, the third party does not need to be identified by name. (Westside Center Associates, supra, 42 Cal.App.4th 527.)

            The Cross-Complaint alleges, inter alia, that Cross-Defendants engaged in a coordinated course of conduct directed at Homi’s existing and prospective customers in order to interfere with Homi’s economic relationships in Southern California and obtain competitive advantage through improper means. (Cross-Compl., ¶¶2, 5-8, 14, 23, 25-29, 36-37.) Homi and the competing hardwood flooring business operated, managed, and controlled by Cross-Defendants compete in the same market for hardwood flooring products. (Id., ¶13.) Beginning in or about early 2026, Aguilar, Zaldivar, and Brito, acting at the direction and under the authority of Hilaski, engaged in communications with customers and prospective customers of Homi during commercial sale visits. (Id., ¶17.)

            For example, Aguilar made statements to customers that “HOMI was involved in a customer dispute involving a container shipment, that HOMI had improperly copied competitor products, that Hugo Perez had stolen money from OMP and was terminated for that reason, and that HOMI and its representatives were unreliable in fulfilling customer orders and were untrustworthy.” (Cross-Compl., ¶¶18, 20-21, 24.) Specifically, in or about March 2026, during a sales visit to a shared customer, Aguilar observed Homi samples displayed alongside competing products and stated to the customer, “Wow, you deal with them? They burned someone with a container.” (Id., ¶19.) These statements were false and misleading, and made without factual basis. (Id., ¶22.) Similarly, Zaldivar falsely stated to a customer that Homi had committed theft, its owners were dishonest, and Homi was operating unlawfully and improperly. (Id., ¶21.)

            Cross-Defendants knew of Homi’s existing prospective economic relationships. (Cross-Compl., ¶39.) Despite that knowledge, Cross-Defendants intentionally engaged in wrongful conduct designed to disrupt those relationships. (Id., ¶40.) Cross-Defendants knew disruption of Homi’s economic relationships was certain or substantially certain to occur as a result of their conduct. (Id., ¶42.) Homi was actually and proximately harmed by Cross-defendants’ conduct. (Id., ¶45.)

            Taking these allegations as true, the Cross-Complaint does plead sufficient facts to support the first cause of action as to all Cross-Defendants. The Cross-Complaint alleges specific statements that Aguilar and Zaldivar made to Homi’s customers and prospective customers during commercial sale visits regarding their business practices and product quality. Although the Cross-Complaint does not allege any specific statements made by Brito to any of Homi’s customers and/or prospective customers, the Cross-Complaint does allege that Brito “acted in concert with and/or as the agent, employee, or representative of HILASKI and the other Cross-Defendants” to disrupt Homi’s prospective economic relationships. (Cross-Compl., ¶8.) Moreover, the Cross-Complaint alleges that Aguilar and Zaldivar were acting within the course and scope of agency, employment, and/or representative capacity for Hilaski and other Cross-Defendants when they participated in the scheme to harm Homi’s prospective economic relationships. (Cross-Compl., ¶¶6-7.) Such allegations are sufficient under California law. (See Garton v. Title Ins. & Trust Co. (1980) 106 Cal.App.3d 365, 376 [“Plaintiffs alleged that each of the defendants were the agents and employees of each other and were acting in the course and scope of their agency, employment and authority and with the permission and consent of their codefendants in committing the acts alleged. This is a sufficient allegation of an agency relationship.”].)

            Accordingly, the demurrer to the first cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

Second Cause of Action for Intentional Interference with Contractual Relations

            “To prevail on a cause of action for intentional interference with contractual relations, a plaintiff must plead and prove (1) the existence of a valid contract between the plaintiff and a third party; (2) the defendant’s knowledge of that contract; (3) the defendant’s intentional acts designed to induce a breach or disruption of the contractual relationship; (4) actual breach or disruption of the contractual relationship; and (5) resulting damage.” (Reeves v. Hanlon (2004) 33 Cal.4th 1140, 1148.) “To establish the claim, the plaintiff need not prove that a defendant acted with the primary purpose of disrupting the contract, but must show the defendant’s knowledge that the interference was certain or substantially certain to occur as a result of his or her action.” (Id.)

            The Cross-Complaint alleges, inter alia, that Homi had valid and enforceable contracts with certain customers for the purchase and delivery of hardwood flooring products. (Cross-Compl., ¶¶49-50.) Cross-Defendants knew of Homi’s contractual relationships with its customers. (Id., ¶51.) Despite that knowledge, Cross-Defendants intentionally engaged in conduct designed to induce disruption or breach of those contracts including making false and misleading statements concerning Homi’s reliability and business practices. (Id., ¶52.) As a result of Cross-Defendants’ conduct, one or more customers delayed, canceled, reduced, or otherwise disrupted their contractual relations to Homi. (Id., ¶53.) Consequently, Homi suffered damages in an amount known to exceed $750,000.00. (Id., ¶55.)

            Taking these allegations as true, the Cross-Complaint does plead sufficient facts to support the second cause of action as to all Cross-Defendants. The Cross-Complaint alleges that Homi had valid and enforceable purchase agreements with certain customers for hardwood flooring products; Cross-Defendants knew about these contracts; Cross-Defendants intentionally engaged in conduct, i.e., making purportedly false statements to Homi’s customers and prospective customers about Homi’s business practices and product quality; and Homi was harmed by such conduct because their customers delayed, canceled, and reduced their contractual relations with Homi. Cross-Defendants cite to no legal authority that requires Cross-Complainant to plead the specific name of the third party to the contracts and/or the contractual terms. On the other hand, it is true that “to state a claim for interference with an at-will contract by a third party, the plaintiff must allege that the defendant engaged in an independently wrongful act.” (Ixchel Pharma, LLC v. Biogen, Inc. (2020) 9 Cal.5th 1130, 1148.) The Cross-Complaint does just that. As discussed above, the Cross-Complaint alleges that Aguilar and Zaldivar at the direction of Hilaski made false statements about Homi’s business practices and product quality to Homi’s customers, who had valid and enforceable contractual agreements to purchase hardwood flooring products from Homi.

            Accordingly, the demurrer to the second cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

Third Cause of Action for Violation of Business & Professions Code Section 17200

            “To bring a UCL claim, a plaintiff must show either an (1) ‘unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business act or practice,’ or (2) ‘unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising.’ ” (Adhav v. Midway Rent A Car, Inc. (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 954, 970.) “ ‘Unlawful’ conduct includes any business practice or act forbidden by local, state or federal statutes or by regulations or case law. [Citation].” (Id.) “ ‘a UCL fraud plaintiff must allege he or she was motivated to act or refrain from action based on the truth or falsity of a defendant’s statement, not merely on the fact it was made.’ [Citation].” (Id.) Generally, a plaintiff alleging unfair business practices under these statutes must state with reasonable particularity the facts supporting the statutory elements of the violation.” (Khoury v. Maly's of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 619.) However, “the conduct needed to maintain a statutory or common law unfair competition cause of action may consist of a tortious interference with business relations.” (Angelica Textile Services, Inc. v. Park (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 495, 510.) Further, “[w]hether a practice is deceptive, fraudulent, or unfair is generally a question of fact which requires ‘consideration and weighing of evidence from both sides’ and which usually cannot be made on demurrer.” (Linear Technology Corp. v. Applied Materials, Inc. (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 115, 135.)

            The Cross-Complaint alleges, inter alia, that Cross-Defendants engaged in unlawful and unfair business acts and practices by (1) engaging in wrongful interference with prospective economic advantage and (2) using false and misleading factual representations in the marketplace to divert customers from a direct competitor. (Cross-Compl., ¶¶58-60.) Homi suffered injury in fact, and lost money or property as a result of Cross-Defendants’ unfair competition. (Id., ¶61.)

            Taking these allegations as true, the Cross-Complaint sufficiently alleges facts to support the third cause of action. In fact, the Cross-Complaint alleges that Cross-Defendants engaged in tortious interference with Homi’s business relations. To the extent that Cross-Defendants argue that the Cross-Complaint seeks remedies not available under a UCL claim, the Cross-Complaint alleges that Homi seeks restitution and injunctive relief. (Cross-Compl., ¶¶62-65.) With respect to the request for disgorgement of monies and economic benefits wrongfully obtained by Cross-Defendants as a result of the alleged tortious interference conduct (Cross-Compl., ¶¶62, 65), restitutionary disgorgement is an available remedy for an UCL claim. (See Madrid v. Perot Systems Corp. (2005) 130 Cal.App.4th 440, 460 [“The California Supreme Court has held that, while restitutionary disgorgement may be an available remedy under the UCL, nonrestitutionary disgorgement is not available in a UCL individual action or in a UCL representative action (i.e., an action by an individual plaintiff on behalf of others without being certified as a class action).”].)

            Accordingly, the demurrer to the third cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

Fourth Cause of Action for False Advertising under Business & Professions Code Section 17500

            “California's FAL prohibits any ‘unfair, deceptive, untrue, or misleading advertising.’ ” (Ketayi v. Health Enrollment Group (S.D. Cal. 2021) 516 F.Supp.3d 1092, 1129.) “California laws prohibit not only advertising which is false, but also advertising which[,] although true, is either actually misleading or which has a capacity, likelihood or tendency to deceive or confuse the public.” (Id.) 

             “To state a claim under the UCL, FAL, and CLRA, [Plaintiff] must allege facts satisfying the ‘reasonable consumer’ standard, i.e., that members of the public are likely to be deceived.” (LeGrand v. Abbott Laboratories (N.D. Cal. 2023) 655 F.Supp.3d 871, 890.) “ ‘Likely to deceive’ implies more than a mere possibility that the advertisement might conceivably be misunderstood by some few consumers viewing it in an unreasonable manner.” (Id.) “Rather, the phrase indicates that the ad is such that it is probable that a significant portion of the general consuming public or of targeted consumers, acting reasonably in the circumstances, could be misled.” (Id.) “In determining whether a statement is misleading under the statute, ‘the primary evidence in a false advertising case is the advertising itself.’ ” (Id.) “Generalized, vague, and unspecified assertions constitute ‘mere puffery’ upon which a reasonable consumer could not rely, and hence are not actionable.” (Oestreicher v. Alienware Corp. (N.D. Cal. 2008) 544 F.Supp.2d 964, 973.) 

            The Cross-Complaint alleges, inter alia, that Cross-Defendants in the course promoting and selling hardwood flooring products in competition with Homi, made false and misleading factual statements to customers and prospective customers concerning Homi’s business practices, reliability, and integrity. (Cross-Compl., ¶68.) These statements were made for the purpose of inducing customers not to transact with Homi and instead transact with the competing business operated and controlled by Cross-Defendants. (Id., ¶69.) Cross-Defendants knew or should have known that these statements were false or misleading. (Id., ¶70.) As a direct and proximate result of these false and misleading statements, Homi suffered the loss of sales to prospective and existing customers. (Id., ¶71.)

            Taking these allegations as true, the Cross-Complaint is devoid of any facts to support the fourth cause of action. There are no allegations that Cross-Defendants made false or misleading advertisements about their own products to induce the public, in this case, Homi’s existing and prospective customers to purchase its products. Cross-Complainants does not cite to any legal authority showing that California Business & Professions Code Section 17500 applies to false or misleading statements made as to a competitor’s services and/or products. To the extent that Cross-Complainants intended to plead an FAL claim under California Business & Professions Code Section 17508, subdivision (a), the Cross-Complaint fails in that regard as well.

            Pursuant to California Business & Professions Code Section 17508, subdivision (a), “It shall be unlawful for any person doing business in California and advertising to consumers in California to make any false or misleading advertising claim, including claims that (1) purport to be based on factual, objective, or clinical evidence, (2) compare the product’s effectiveness or safety to that of other brands or products, or (3) purport to be based on any fact.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17508, subd. (a) [emphasis added].) The Cross-Complaint does not allege that Cross-Defendants made false or misleading statements about its own products in comparison to the effectiveness or safety of Homi’s products and/or services. The Cross-Complaint does not expressly invoke California Business & Professions Code Section 17508 as it only alleges California Business & Professions Code Section 17500. (Cross-Compl., ¶67.)

            Accordingly, the demurrer to the fourth cause of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

 

            Motion to Strike

            Cross-Defendants moves for an order striking references to punitive damages; attorney’s fees, and request for $750,000.00 in damages in the Cross-Complaint on the grounds that: (1) Cross-Complainants fail to plead facts to supporting a request for punitive damages; (2) the request for $750,000.00 in damages in irrelevant and improper; and (3) there is no contractual, statutory, or other basis for attorney’s fees.

            As to punitive damages, “[i]n order to survive a motion to strike an allegation of punitive damages, the ultimate facts showing an entitlement to such relief must be pled by a plaintiff.” (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.) The California Civil Code Section 3294, subdivision (a) authorizes punitive damages upon a showing of malice, fraud, or oppression. Malice is defined as either “conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff”, or “despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1).) “Despicable conduct is conduct which is so vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or loathsome that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people.” (Mock v. Michigan Millers Mutual Ins. Co. (1992) 4 Cal. App. 4th 306, 331.) Fraud under California Civil Code Section 3294, subdivision (c)(3) “means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(3).) The California Civil Code Section 3294, subdivision (2) defines oppression as “despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (2).) Punitive damages thus require more than the mere commission of a tort. (Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890, 894-95.)

            While the Cross-Complaint sufficiently alleges tortious interference with prospective economic advantage and existing contractual relations, it does not allege anything more. As held in Taylor,  the mere commission of a tort including business torts will not support a claim for punitive damages.

            As for the allegations that the damages amount are known to exceed $750,000.00 and the exact amount will be proven at trial (Cross-Compl., ¶¶31, 34, 45, 55; Prayer for Relief at ¶1), these facts when read together are not contradictory as argued by Cross-Defendants. Both allegations indicate that the exact amount in damages suffered by Cross-Complainants is unknown but merely exceeds $750,000.00. Therefore, Cross-Defendants fail to demonstrate that such allegations are irrelevant and/or improper.

            Lastly, attorneys’ fees are not available unless authorized by statute or an agreement between the parties. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1021.) Here, Cross-Complainants’ concedes that California Business & Professions Code Sections 17200 and 17500 do not provide recover of attorney’s fees. (See Opp. re Mot. to Strike at 14:24-28.) Furthermore, Cross-Complainants’ do not address whether there is any contractual basis for attorney’s fees for its underlying claims, thus concedes on this issue. (See California Rules of Court Rule 3.1113(a); see also Rule 3.1320(f); Herzberg v. County of Plumas (2005) 133 Cal.App.4th 1, 20 [the party who fails to argue an issue abandons that issue].)

            Accordingly, the motion to strike is GRANTED with leave to amend as to the punitive damages and without leave to amend as to the request for attorney’s fees. The motion is denied as to the amount of stated damages.

IV.       CONCLUSION

            Based on the foregoing, Cross-Defendants  Jim Hilaski, Andy Aguilar, Jose Zaldivar and Marciela Brito’s Demurrer to Cross-Complainants Homi Hardwood Floors, Inc. and Nathalie  Dina’s Cross-Complaint is SUSTAINED with 30 days leave to amend as to the fourth cause of action only and OVERRULED as to the remaining causes of action.

            Cross-Defendants  Jim Hilaski, Andy Aguilar, Jose Zaldivar and Marciela Brito’s Motion to Strike Cross-Complainants Homi Hardwood Floors, Inc. and Nathalie  Dina’s Cross-Complaint is GRANTED with 30 days leave to amend as to the punitive damages claim and without leave to amend as to the request for attorney’s fees.  The motion is denied as to the stated amount of damages.

            Cross-Defendants Jim Hilaski, Andy Aguilar, Jose Zaldivar and Marciela Brito to give notice.

I.       BACKGROUND

On August 20, 2025, Defendants Surrinder Kumar Malhi and Paramjit Kumar Malhi (hereinafter, “Malhi Defendants”) filed their joint Answer to the Complaint.

On January 30, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Motion for Leave to File First Amended Complaint. The matter came for hearing on February 27, 2026; the motion was granted. On March 3, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants, alleging causes of action for: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Fraudulent Concealment; (3) Violation of California Civ. On March 4, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an Amendment to the FAC identifying SSW Holding LLC as Doe 1.

On April 1, 2026, SSW Holdings, LLC (“SSW”), erroneously sued as SSW Holding LLC, filed its Answer to the FAC. On April 3, 2026, the Malhi Defendants filed their Answer to the FAC.

On June 23, 2026, Plaintiffs Alireza Ghoorchian and Ayda Nostrati filed a notice of Lis Pendens stating that Plaintiff claims title to the Property and thus the object of Plaintiffs’ action is to seek to quiet title to the real property with the Assessor’s Parcel Number 2327-025-016 and a Legal Description as follows: PARCELS C AND D AS SHOWN BY PARCEL MAP NO. 2009-3238, IN THE CITY OF LOS ANGELES, COUNTY OF LOS ANGELES, STATE OF CALIFORNIA, ON FILE IN BOOK 397, PAGE(S) 32 OF PARCEL MAPS, IN THE OFFICE OF THE COUNTY RECORDER OF SAID COUNTY.

On September 14, 2026, the Malhi Defendants and SSW Holdings LLC filed the instant Ex Parte Motion to Expunge Lis Pendens and Plaintiffs filed an opposition on the same day. On September 15, 2026, the Court set a hearing for September 22, 2026, and set a briefing schedule.

On September 16, 2026, Defendant SSW Holdings filed a supplemental brief and evidentiary objections. On September 18, 2026, Plaintiffs/Cross-Defendants filed an opposition.

II.          LEGAL STANDARDS

            “A party to an action who asserts a real property claim may record a notice of pendency of action in which that real property claim is alleged. The notice may be recorded in the office of the recorder of each county in which all or part of the real property is situated. The notice shall contain the names of all parties to the action and a description of the property affected by the action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.20.)

            Absent certain circumstances, “the claimant shall, prior to recordation of the notice, cause a copy of the notice to be mailed, by registered or certified mail, return receipt requested, to all known addresses of the parties to whom the real property claim is adverse and to all owners of record of the real property affected by the real property claim as shown by the latest county assessment roll…. Immediately following recordation, a copy of the notice shall also be filed with the court in which the action is pending.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.22.) 

            “Any notice of pendency of action shall be void and invalid as to any adverse party or owner of record unless the requirements of Section 405.22 are met for that party or owner and a proof of service in the form and content specified in Section 1013a has been recorded with the notice of pendency of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.23.) 

 

 

 

            “A party contending that a lis pendens is ‘void and invalid’ for purposes of [Code of Civil Procedure] section 405.23 due to defective service may raise this ground in a motion for expungement filed pursuant to [Code of Civil Procedure] section 405.30.” (J&A Mash & Barrel, LLC v. S. Ct. of Fresno County (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 1, 24-25.)            “At any time after a notice of pendency of action has been recorded, any party . . . with an interest in the real property affected thereby, may apply to the court in which the action is pending to expunge the notice.” (Code Civ. Proc., §405.30.)

            A lis pendens may be expunged either (1) if the pleadings do not contain a real property claim, or (2) if the court finds that the party claiming the lis pendens has not established by a preponderance of the evidence the probable validity of the real property claim. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 405.31, 405.32.)

            The party asserting the lis pendens has the burden of proof under Section 405.31 and Section 405.32.¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 405.30) The burden is to demonstrate that their pleadings contain a real property claim and that the probable validity of their real property claim can be established by a preponderance of the evidence. (Code Civ. Proc., §405.31; see also McKnight v. Superior Court (1985) 170 Cal. App. 3d 291, 298 [“the burden is upon the recording party to demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence that the action was commenced and prosecuted for a proper purpose and in good faith”] .) “Probable validity” exists when “it is more likely than not that the claimant will obtain a judgment on the claim.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.3.)

            Any time after a notice of pendency of action has been recorded the court may also “upon motion by any person with an interest in the property, require the claimant to give the moving party an undertaking as a condition of maintain the notice in the record title.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.34.)¿ 

III.       DISCUSSION

The Malhi Defendants move for an order expunging the lis pendens, arguing that the Lis Pendens was not properly served on defendants as statutorily required. There was no service of the Lis Pendens by Certified Mail on Defendants nor was a Proof of Service recorded as further required by Statute (Code of Civ. Proc., §§ 405.22, 405.23; Malhi Decl., Exh. A). This deficiency mandates expungement.

In opposition, Plaintiffs/Cross-Defendants note that they have attempted to informally resolve this matter, and that the matter was referred by Court to the Court’s mediation panel and the parties have agreed to a mediation. The mediation is likely to happen in October 2026.  After filing of the lis pendens on or about June 23, 2026 a notice of Lis Pendens was filed with the Court. Plaintiff’s counsel acknowledged the filing of the lis pendens.

Notice

“Any notice of pendency of action shall be void and invalid as to any adverse party or owner of record unless the requirements of Section 405.22 are met for that party or owner and a proof of service in the form and content specified in Section 1013a has been recorded with the notice of pendency of action.”  Code Civ. Proc., § 405.23.  “[A] claimant shall, prior to recordation of the notice, cause a copy of the notice to be mailed, by registered or certified mail, return receipt requested, to all known addresses of the parties to whom the real property claim is adverse and to all owners of record of the real property affected by the real property claim as shown by the latest county assessment roll.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.22; see also Carr v. Rosien (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 845, 853 (Carr) (“the mailing requirement is intended to make sure that all owners of the property, and all adverse parties claiming an interest in the property, are aware of the lis pendens”).)

In the Declaration of Alan Abrams in Opposition to Motion to Expunge Lis Pendens, Abrams states, “[p]rior to the filing of this lis pendens, a copy was mailed to Plaintiff’s counsel who occupies the same building as my office. After filing of the lis pendens on or about June 23, 2026 a notice of Lis Pendens was filed with Court. Plaintiff’s counsel acknowledged the filing of the lis pendens.” (Abrams Decl., ¶ 20.)

Plaintiffs failed to comply with the statutory requirement that prior to recordation of the notice, Plaintiff must cause a copy of the notice to be mailed, by registered or certified mail, return receipt requested, to all known addresses of the parties to whom the real property claim is adverse and to all owners of record of the real property affected by the real property claim as shown by the latest county assessment roll. Here, Plaintiff’s counsel does not state whether the mailing was registered or certified and only that it was sent to counsel, not all known addresses to whom the real property claim is adverse. 

Because Plaintiffs failed to comply with the service requirement as specified in Code of Civil Procedure section 405.22, the notice of lis pendens filed by Plaintiff on June 23, 2026, is void and invalid as to Defendants/Cross-Complainants.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.23; see Carr, supra, 238 Cal.App.4th at p. 853.) 

The motion is granted.

Attorneys’ Fees & Costs 

Code of Civil Procedure section 405.38 provides that, “[t]he court shall direct that the party prevailing on [a motion to expunge lis pendens] be awarded the reasonable attorney’s fees and costs of making or opposing the motion unless the court finds that the other party acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of attorney’s fees and costs unjust.”  
            Here, Defendants/Cross-Complainants is the prevailing party and is thus entitled to reasonable attorney fees and costs. (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.38.) Attorney Green states that his rate is $595 an hour based on fifty years of experience. (Green Decl., ¶¶ 4, 15.) Attorney Green states that he worked for 8 hours on this motion and anticipates two more hours in preparing the reply and at the court appearance(s). (Green Decl., ¶ 15.)

The Court finds that $595 is a reasonable rate based on Attorney Green’s experience, but that 8 hours for this motion is an unreasonable amount of time for such an experienced attorney.  The Court grants two hours for the motion and one hour for the appearance at the hearing, for a total of 3 hours at $595, or $1,785.

IV.    CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, Defendants’ Surrinder Kumar Malhi and Paramjit Kumar Malhi Motion to Expunge Lis Pendens is GRANTED. The request for attorneys fees is granted in the reduced amount of $1,785.00.

            Defendants Surrinder Kumar Malhi, Paramjit Kumar Malhi, and SSW Holdings LLC to give notice.

I.        Background

 

This is a lemon law action arising out of Plaintiff Hernan Hernandez’s (“Plaintiff”) purchase of a 2022 Jaguar F-Pace, VIN No.: SADCZ2EE0NA698196 (the “Subject Vehicle”) manufactured by Defendant Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC (“JLRNA”) and repaired by Defendant Galpin Jaguar Lincoln-Mercury Inc. dba Land Rover Van Nuys. On December 22, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative Complaint against Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC, Land Rover Van Nuys, and DOES 1 through 10 (collectively, “Defendants”), asserting causes of action for: (1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Express Warranty; (2) Violation of Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Implied Warranty; (3) Violation of the Song-Beverly Act Section 1793.2; and (4) Negligent Repair.

 

On February 23, 2026, Defendants filed their Answer to the Complaint.

 

On August 4, 2026, Defendant JLRNA (“Defendant”) filed the instant Motion to Compel Deposition of Plaintiff. On September 9, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition. On September 16, 2026, Defendant filed a Reply.

 

II.     Discussion

On May 27, 2026, Defendant served a Notice of Deposition of Plaintiff and Request for Production of Documents for a deposition date of June 30, 2026. (Declaration of Rosario Stoliker (“Stoliker Decl.”), ¶ 3; Ex. A.) Defendant’s counsel states that after Plaintiff served an objection due to the date being unilaterally set, Defendant sent a meet and confer email on June 23, 2026. (Stoliker Decl., ¶¶ 4-5; Exs. B-C.) On July 8, 2026, Defendant sent a follow up email requesting alternative dates, but Plaintiff did not respond. (Stoliker Decl., ¶ 6; Ex. C.)

A motion to compel deposition  “shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).) Specifically, “[a] meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040, subd. (a).) “[A]ttempting informal resolution means more than the mere attempt by the discovery proponent ‘to persuade the objector of the error of his ways” and “a reasonable and good faith attempt at informal resolution entails something more than bickering with [opposing]counsel.... Rather, the law requires that counsel attempt to talk the matter over, compare their views, consult, and deliberate.” (Clement v. Alegre (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1277, 1294.)

As shown above, the parties did not meet and confer in-person, by telephone, or by videoconference as required under Code of Civil Procedure Section 2025.450, subdivision (b). Though the email communications demonstrate that Defendant made an effort to meet and confer.

 

However, in his opposition, Plaintiff now requests that the deposition take place on October 29, 2026. (Declaration of Camilo Fernadnez, ¶ 7.) In its reply, Defendant agrees to the October 29,2026, deposition date. Because Plaintiff only provided his availability after the instant motion was filed, the Court grants the motion to compel the deposition of Plaintiff and orders Plaintiff to appear on October 29, 2026, for his deposition.

 

In its reply, Defendant requests a short continuance of the trial. Trial is set for July 7, 2027. Thus, the Court finds that a continuance is not warranted as trial is set for over nine months away.

 

III.  Conclusion

 

Accordingly, the hearing on Defendant Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC’s Motion to Compel Deposition of Plaintiff is GRANTED. Plaintiff Hernan Hernandez is ordered to appear for deposition on October 29, 2026.

I.       BACKGROUND  

            Plaintiff was being transported in an ambulance for severe injuries when the ambulance was struck by vehicles owned and operated by Defendants, including a Metropolitan Transportation Authority bus.  On March 13, 2026, Plaintiff filed a complaint against Defendants Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority, erroneously sued as LACounty Metropolitan Transportation Authority (“LACMTA”), Marquisua Ann McQuay and Michael Linggadjaja alleging (1) motor vehicle and (2) general negligence.

            On June 4, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion for order for relief from claim statute.  On June 18, 2026, Defendant LACMTA filed an opposition.  On June 25, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.  On September 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Errata attaching the exhibits referenced in the motion.

II.          LEGAL STANDARDS

            “Except as provided in Sections 946.4 and 946.6, no suit for money or damages may be brought against a public entity on a cause of action for which a claim is required to be presented in accordance with Chapter 1 (commencing with Section 900) and Chapter 2 (commencing with Section 910) of Part 3 of this division until a written claim therefor has been presented to the public entity and has been acted upon by the board, or has been deemed to have been rejected by the board, in accordance with Chapters 1 and 2 of Part 3 of this division.”  (Gov. C.,  § 945.4.)

            “The board shall act on a claim in the manner provided in Section 912.6, 912.7, or 912.8 within 45 days after the claim has been presented. If a claim is amended, the board shall act on the amended claim within 45 days after the amended claim is presented.”  (Gov. C., §912.4, subd. (a).)

            “Timely claim presentation is not merely a procedural requirement, but is a condition precedent to plaintiff's maintaining an action against defendant.  Only after the public entity's board has acted upon or is deemed to have rejected the claim may the injured person bring a lawsuit alleging a cause of action in tort against the public entity.”  (Lowry v. Port San Luis Harbor District (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 211, 219 (plaintiff’s action was barred due to premature filing of lawsuit before District took action on his application to file late claim).)

            If an application for leave to present a claim is denied or deemed to be denied pursuant to Section 911.6, a petition may be made to the court for an order relieving the petitioner from the government claims requirement under Government Code section 945.4.  (Gov. C., §946.6.)  The Court shall grant the petition for relief if (1) a request to file late claim was submitted to the public entity per GC 911.4 within a reasonable time; (2) that request to file late claim was rejected per 911.6; and (3) one of the four conditions set forth in Government Code section 946.6 subdivision (c) is met:

“(1) The failure to present the claim was through mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect unless the public entity establishes that it would be prejudiced in the defense of the claim if the court relieves the petitioner from the requirements of Section 945.4.

(2) The person who sustained the alleged injury, damage or loss was a minor during all of the time specified in Section 911.2 for the presentation of the claim.

(3) The person who sustained the alleged injury, damage or loss was physically or mentally incapacitated during all of the time specified in Section 911.2 for the presentation of the claim and by reason of that disability failed to present a claim during that time.

(4) The person who sustained the alleged injury, damage or loss died before the expiration of the time specified in Section 911.2 for the presentation of the claim.” (Gov. C., §946.6, subd. (c).)

            The court “must” grant the petition under section 946.6, subdivision (c)(1), if the claimant demonstrates by a preponderance of the evidence that (1) the application to the public entity under section 911.4 was made within a reasonable time not exceeding one year after the accrual of the cause of action; (2) the failure to timely present the claim was through mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect; and (3) the public entity would not be prejudiced by the granting of the petition.  (Ebersol v. Cowan (1983) 35 Cal.3d 427, 431-432.) 

            “Section 946.6 is a remedial statute intended to provide relief from technical rules which otherwise provide a trap for the unwary claimant.  The remedial policies underlying the statute are that wherever possible cases be heard on their merits and any doubts which may exist should be resolved in favor of the application.”  (Id. at 435.)

            The showing required for relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 946.6 based on mistake, inadvertence, surprise or excusable neglect is the same as required for Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) relief from a default judgment.  (Ebersol, supra, 35 Cal.3d at 435.)  “Excusable neglect is neglect that might have been the act or omission of a reasonably prudent person under the same or similar circumstances.”  (Id. (petitioner’s failure to file timely government claim was excusable neglect where she was unaware of the tort claim requirement and diligently sought counsel during the period which a claim had to be filed).)

            As a “remedial statute,” section 946.6 is to be liberally construed so as to permit claimants with a recognized excuse to have their cases heard on the merits; i.e., doubts are to be resolved in favor of granting the section 946.6 petition.  (Draper v. City of Los Angeles (1990) 52 Cal.3d 502, 507.)  Where uncontradicted evidence shows adequate ground for relief, the trial court's denial is reversible as an abuse of discretion.  (Cole v. City of Los Angeles (1986) 187 Cal.App.3d 1369, 1375-1376.)  By the same token, a trial court's denial of relief will not be disturbed on appeal where there is clearly no competent evidence upon which it could exercise its discretion to grant relief.  (Id.)

III.       DISCUSSION

            A.  Parties’ Positions

            Plaintiff moves for an order granting her relief from the claims filing requirement of Government Code section 945.6.  Plaintiff’s accident occurred on August 22, 2025.  Plaintiff mistakenly served County of Los Angeles with a government tort claim on September 9, 2025, which County rejected on October 21, 2025.  Plaintiff should have filed a government claim with LA MTA but did not do so.  Plaintiff filed an application to file a late claim with LA MTA on June 5, 2026.  Plaintiff filed the instant lawsuit on March 13, 2026 naming “LA County Metropolitan Tranportion [sic] Authority.”   Plaintiff argues LA MTA will not suffer prejudice if leave to file the late claim is granted. 

            Defendant opposes the motion for leave to file late claim on grounds that an attorney’s mistake is not a basis for granting relief from the claim filing requirements.  Defendant argues it is not affiliated or related to County of Los Angeles.  Defendant argues Plaintiff was represented by counsel at all times.  Defendant argues Plaintiff also knew the identity of the entity allegedly responsible for her injuries, because the September 9, 2025 claim filed with County of Los Angeles identifies “LA County Metro Driver” as the responsible department and names the Metro bus operator.  Defendant argues it and County of Los Angeles are separate public entities. 

            Defendant argues that the evidence submitted is also insufficient to establish excusable neglect or reasonable mistake as Plaintiff does not submit a declaration but relies solely on a conclusory declaration of counsel.   

            Defendant contends Plaintiff fails to establish that her application to file late claim was presented within a “reasonable time,” as required under Government Code section 911.4 subdivision (b).  Though the outer limit to file an application for late claim is one year after accrual of the cause of action, Defendant argues Plaintiff was still required to establish that the application was brought within a reasonable time and to state the reason for delay in presenting the claim.  Plaintiff did not file the application until June 2, 2026, more than nine months after the August 22, 2025 accident and more than seven months after the County’s October 21, 2025 rejection. 

            Defendant also contends that even if Plaintiff established excusable neglect, it will be prejudiced if the late claim were allowed as it will be deprived of an opportunity to investigate Plaintiff’s allegations properly due to the passage of time. 

            In reply, Plaintiff argues her application for late claim was timely filed within one year of the date of the accident.  Plaintiff filed the application for late claim with Defendant on June 5, 2026.  Plaintiff did not discover that the County was the wrong entity until May 27, 2026, when defense counsel raised the issue.  Plaintiff filed the application to file a late claim with LA MTA days later on June 5, 2026.  Plaintiff argues the application to file a late claim was filed within a reasonable time.

            Plaintiff contends it was reasonable for counsel to mistakenly believe that the County was the responsible entity as the entities carry nearly identical names and that LA MTA’s inclusion of Los Angeles County in its name is misleading.  Plaintiff’s counsel performed a Google search for “LACMTA claim form” and the County’s claim form was the second result.  Plaintiff argues any doubts regarding whether the error was reasonable or excusable should be resolved in favor of relief on the merits.  Finally, Plaintiff contends Defendant also fails to establish prejudice if the late claim were allowed. 

B.  Plaintiff fails to establish that she submitted her application to file a late claim with LACMTA within a “reasonable time” per Government Code section 911.4

            In order to obtain relief pursuant to Government Code section 946.6 from the claim filing requirements of Government Code section 945.4, Plaintiff must first establish that her application to LACMTA under section 911.4 to file a late claim was made within a “reasonable time” not exceeding one year after the accrual of the cause of action.  “Where, as here, plaintiff's mistake was the actual cause of his failure to comply...his application to the board could obviously not have been made until he discovered his mistake, and the delay was, therefore, reasonable under the circumstances.”  (Moore v. State of California (1984) 157 Cal.Ap.3d 715, 725-726 (“reasonable time” inquiry under Government Code section 911.4 measured from date plaintiff discovered she had not complied with the claims requirement when she mistakenly believed that she had complied with that requirement; plaintiff submitted application to file late claim with public entity within a “reasonable time,” three days after she discovered that timely claim she filed did not satisfy the claims requirement).)

            Thus in Moore, the court found plaintiff submitted her application to file late claim to the State Board of Control with a “reasonable time” under sections 911.4 and 946.6, even though it was submitted nearly a year after accrual of her cause of action.  (Moore, supra, 157 Cal.App.3d 715, 725-726.)  Any delay in submitting the application to file late claim with the Board was due to her mistaken belief that she had complied with the claims statute.  (Id.)  Plaintiff did not discover that her previous, timely-filed claim with the Board did not satisfy the claims statute until a demurrer was sustained to her complaint.  (Id.)  Plaintiff submitted her application to file late claim under section 911.4 to the Board three days after the demurrer was sustained.  (Id.) When measured from the date she discovered her erroneous belief that she had complied with the claims statute, the delay of three days was reasonable and the fact that the application for leave to file late claim was filed nearly a year after the action accrued was not grounds to deem it untimely.  (Id.) 

            Plaintiff represents in her reply brief that she only discovered that her claim with the County did not satisfy the claims requirement as to LACMTA on May 27, 2026.  However, no evidence is presented to establish this fact.  Plaintiff’s counsel only testifies, “[o]nce my offices was notified by LACMTA attorney that it was filed in the wrong department, I have filed an Application with the correct form to LACMTA.”  (Notice of Errata, Ex. B, Grigoryan Dec., ¶4.)  Counsel does not attest to the date on which the application to file late claim was submitted to LACMTA.  (Id.)  The application is attached as Exhibit A to counsel’s declaration and is dated June 2, 2025, although Plaintiff represents in her briefing that it was submitted on June 5, 2026.    There is no evidence of when Plaintiff discovered that the claim she submitted to the County did not satisfy the claims requirement as to LACMTA.  Without this information, the reasonableness of her June 2d or 5th, 2026 application to file late claim cannot be evaluated.  The fact that the application was filed less than a year from the accident alone is insufficient to establish that it was submitted to LACMTA within a reasonable time.

            Plaintiff fails to establish the first requirement for relief from the claims requirement under section 945.4.     

B.  Plaintiff has not established that its application to file late claim filed on June 5, 2026 has been denied or deemed denied pursuant to section 911.6

            Plaintiff was required as part of her motion to establish that her application to LACMTA to file late claim under section 911.4 “was denied or deemed denied pursuant to Section 911.6.”  Plaintiff could not establish this fact when the motion was filed, because she filed this motion on June 4, 2026.  Plaintiff submitted her application to file late claim to LACMTA on either June 2, 2026 or June 5, 2026.  Although it has been more than 45 days since either June 2 or June 5, 2026, Plaintiff has not submitted any evidence regarding the response to her June 2026 application to file late claim with LACMTA. 

            Plaintiff fails to establish that her application to file late claim pursuant to section 911.4 has been denied by LACMTA or by operation of law under section 911.6. 

C.  Plaintiff fails to submit prima facie evidence that her failure to file a timely claim with LACMTA was the result of excusable neglect or reasonable mistake of counsel

“Excusable neglect has been defined as neglect that might have been the act or omission of a reasonably prudent person under the same or similar circumstances. Cases in which relief on the basis of excusable neglect is granted generally involve plaintiffs who have diligently acted within the claims-filing limitation period (i.e., six months) to retain counsel.  Generally speaking, it is the neglectful conduct of counsel or counsel's staff, imputed to plaintiff, which is determined to be excusable and the neglectful conduct is deemed relatively minor.  In deciding whether counsel's error is excusable, the reviewing court looks to the nature of the mistake or neglect and whether counsel was otherwise diligent in investigating and pursuing the claim. When examining the mistake or neglect, the court inquires whether a reasonably prudent person might have made the same error under the same or similar circumstances.”  (Munoz v. State of California (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1767, 1782–1783 (in medical malpractice action, plaintiff’s failure to file a timely claim was not excusable where only evidentiary showing of due diligence in investigating the claim were two letters requesting medical records).)  “In addition, unless inexcusable neglect is clear, the policy favoring trial on the merits prevails.”  (Bettencourt v. Los Rios Community College Dist. (1986) 42 Cal.3d 270, 276.)   

“A petitioner or counsel for a petitioner must show more than the mere failure to discover a fact until too late; he or she must establish the failure to discover that fact in the use of reasonable diligence.”  (Munoz, supra, 33 Cal.App.4th at 1783.)  “When there is a readily available source of information from which the potential liability of a government entity may be discovered, a failure to use that source is deemed inexcusable.”  (Department of Water & Power v. Superior Court (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 1288, 1294 (Government Code section 946.6 relief denied where plaintiff and plaintiff’s counsel conducted no investigation whatsoever regarding DWP’s potential liability despite knowledge that DWP was working on water system and caused flooding of roadway where plaintiff’s auto accident occurred).)  The petitioner has the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence the necessary elements for relief.  (Munoz, supra, 33 Cal.App.4th at 1783.) 

The bar to find excusable neglect or reasonable mistake on a petition for relief from the claims filing requirement is extremely low.  For example, in Flores v. Board of Supervisors (1970) 13 Cal.App.3d 480, 482-483, excusable neglect was found based on “the candid admission of appellants’ attorneys that the lateness of the claim was due to their failure to open a file which in turn would have reminded them of the 100-day limitation upon the presentation of appellants’ claim.  (Flores, supra 13 Cal.App.3d at 483.) 

In Bettencourt, counsel simply believed that the employees of Sacramento City College were employees of the state without any investigation.  The Supreme Court found the mistake reasonable for several reasons.  Counsel did not practice law in or near Sacramento and had never lived there.  (Bettencourt, supra, 42 Cal.3d at 276).  The employees were employees of Los Rios Community College District, which was in fact overseen by the state Community College of Governors.  (Id.)  Finally, the Supreme Court acknowledged that the “public higher education in California represents a sometimes confusing blend of state and local control and funding.”  (Id.)

Here, counsel fails to meet the low bar to establish excusable neglect or reasonable mistake under section 946.6 subdivision (c)(1).  Counsel Grigoryan’s declaration is five paragraphs.  (Notice of Errata, Ex. B.)  Grigoryan testifies that he “believe[s] that Jennifer’s failure to timely submit a claim to the [LACMTA] was due to mistake, inadvertence, surprise, excusable neglect.”  (Id. at ¶3.)  He also testifies “[t]he government form was erroneously filed with County of Los Angeles, and based on their rejection letter, it was field in timely manner.”  (Id. at ¶4.)  Grigoryan’s testimony fails to identify any mistake or neglect, e.g. he thought the County was the correct entity because LACMTA is named “Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transit Authority.”  He merely states that he “believes” Plaintiff’s claim was not timely filed due to some unidentified mistake, inadvertence, surprise or excusable neglect.”  (Id. at ¶3.)  Grigoryan’s declaration also fails to attest to when these events occurred, including when he filed the claim with the County and when he filed the application for late claim with LACMTA. 

In addition, Grigoryan’s declaration fails to establish that he acted diligently in all other respects despite his alleged negligence or mistake.  “In determining whether an attorney's error constitutes excusable neglect, the courts also consider the attorney's overall diligence or lack thereof in addition to examining the attorney's error.”  (Bettencourt, supra, 42 Cal.3d at 278.) 

Plaintiff fails to establish that she did not present a timely claim to LACMTA as a result of counsel’s excusable neglect and/or reasonable mistake.  Plaintiff’s motion is denied for failure to establish excusable neglect, reasonable mistake, inadvertence or surprise pursuant to section 946.6 subdivision (c)(1).           

IV.    CONCLUSION

     Plaintiff’s Motion for Order for Relief from Claim Statute is denied.
