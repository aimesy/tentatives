I.                   INTRODUCTION

            This action arises from the alleged sexual abuse suffered by Plaintiffs Jane L.N. Doe and Jane Y.M. Doe (“Plaintiffs”) while they were students at 97th Street Elementary School (“97th Street”), which is within Defendant Los Angeles Unified School District (“LAUSD”), by their teacher, Don Ray Moore (“Moore”). Plaintiffs allege that LAUSD knew or should have known about the sexual abuse committed by Moore against Plaintiffs and that Moore had a history of sexual abuse against students.  

            On June 26, 2024, Plaintiffs initiated this action. 

            On November 12, 2024, Plaintiffs filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”), alleging a single cause of action for Negligence. 

            The FAC alleges that LAUSD (1) failed to adequately hire, supervise, and retain Moore; (2) failed to investigate or otherwise confirm or deny allegations about Moore; (3) ignored clear and obvious signs that Moore was engaged in an inappropriate and harassing relationship with Plaintiffs; (4) allowed Plaintiffs to spend unsupervised time with Moore; (5) failed to report that Moore sexually harassed, molested, and abused Plaintiffs; and (6) failed to implement and/or enforce policies and procedures that were aimed at preventing or detecting sexual abuse of its students. (FAC ¶¶¿60,64.) 

            On May 22, 2026, the Court granted LAUSD’s motion for summary judgment. 

On June 16, 2026, LAUSD filed a Memorandum of Costs (“Costs Memorandum”).

On July 1, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion to tax costs.

On July 31, 2026, LAUSD filed an opposition.

On August 6, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their reply.

II.                LEGAL STANDARD

Except as otherwise expressly provided by statute, a prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs in any action or proceeding. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032(b).) Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5(c)(2), allowable costs “shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation.”¿Subdivision (3) requires: “Allowable costs shall be reasonable in amount.”¿“Items not mentioned in [Section 1033.5] and items assessed upon application may be allowed or denied in the court’s discretion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(4).) There is no requirement that copies of bills, invoices, statements, or any other such documents be attached to the memorandum. Only if the costs have been put in issue via a motion to tax costs must supporting documentation be submitted. (Bach v. County of Butte (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 294, 308.)  

On a motion to tax costs, “[i]f the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary. On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs. Whether a cost item was reasonably necessary to the litigation presents a question of fact for the trial court and its decision is reviewed for abuse of discretion. However, because the right to costs is governed strictly by statute a court has no discretion to award costs not statutorily authorized.” (Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 774, internal citations omitted.) “The court’s first determination, therefore, is whether the statute expressly allows the item, and whether it appears proper on its face. If so, the burden is on the objecting party to show them to be unnecessary or unreasonable.” (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131, internal citations omitted.) The objecting party does not meet this burden by arguing that the costs were not necessary or reasonable but must present evidence and prove that the costs are not recoverable. (Litt v. Eisenhower Med. Ctr. (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1224; Seever v. Copley Press, Inc. (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 1550, 1557.) 

Rule of Court 3.1700 states, “A prevailing party who claims costs must serve and file a memorandum of costs within 15 days after the date of service of the notice of entry of judgment or dismissal by the clerk under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5 or the date of service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, or within 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is first.”

III.             DISCUSSION

Plaintiffs seek an order striking and/or taxing LAUSD’s costs. On June 16, 2026,

LAUSD filed its Costs Memorandum seeking $21,888.87 in Costs comprised of $13,206.62 in Deposition Costs, $5,546.95 in Service of Process Costs, and $3,135.30 in Fees for Electronic Filing or Service.

            In the alternative, Plaintiffs request that the Court order LAUSD to produce competent documentation (invoices and itemized billing records) substantiating each challenged item, and tax any item LAUSD fails to support.

i.                    Item 4 — Deposition Costs ($13,206.62)

            Plaintiffs ask the Court to tax (a) the videotaping/video-recording charges of $2,643.75 (Jane Y.M. Doe — $900.00; Jane L.N. Doe — $1,743.75) as merely convenient where the depositions were also stenographically transcribed; (b) the $2,475.00 "Taking" charge for Kimberly Lakes, Ph.D., to the extent it represents a non-recoverable expert witness fee; and (c) the Sue Spears deposition charge, which is internally inconsistent on the face of the Worksheet ($651.15 in the "Transcribing" column versus a $351.15 subtotal).

            Here, as to the $2,475.00 “Taking” charge for Kimberly Lakes, Ph.D., LAUSD concedes that the costs for this expert deposition were inadvertently included and are not recoverable. Otherwise, Plaintiffs fail to carry their burden. Deposition costs are expressly recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5 subdivision (a)(3), and the remaining deposition costs claimed in LAUSD’s cost bill appear proper on their face as required by Nelson as each cost is identified by the name of the deponent and further broken down into categories (ie. transcribing, taking, and videotaping.) (Memorandum of Costs, pgs. 2, 4.)

            As to the $2,643.75 in video-recording charges for the depositions of Jane Y.M. Doe ($900.00) and Jane L.N. Doe ($1,743.75), the Court finds that these costs appear proper on their face and were reasonably necessary to prepare for trial at the time they were incurred rather than merely convenient. Plaintiffs argue that these amounts should be taxed because the depositions were stenographically transcribed and “the Worksheet offers no reason the additional video record was necessary.” However, Plaintiffs concede that recording of a necessary deposition is an allowable item, and LAUSD was not required to “offer [a] reason the additional video record was necessary” in its Cost Memorandum as it is Plaintiffs’ burden to show that the costs were not reasonable or necessary. (Nelson, supra, 72 Cal.App.4th at 131.)

            As to the Sue Spears entry, while the worksheet lists $651.15 in the “Transcribing” column but a $351.15 subtotal, the $13,206.62 total for Item 4 was calculated using the lower $351.15 figure and Plaintiffs concede that the $651.15 figure appears to be a clerical error. The Court agrees and there is no need to tax any amount of this entry.

            Therefore, the Court taxes $2,475.00 in deposition costs for the “Taking” charge for expert Kimberly Lakes, Ph.D’s deposition.

ii.                  Item 5 — Service of Process ($5,546.95)

            Plaintiffs ask the Court to tax the duplicative and facially excessive registered-process-server charges, including the $307.50 "KFH/SCPMG" charge that appears both on the face of the Worksheet (Item 5c) and again in Attachment 5d, and “the unreconciled face charges” in Items 5a–5c ($877.75 total), together with the unsupported and unreasonable charges itemized in the accompanying Memorandum.

            Here, the Court finds that LAUSD’s service of process costs are proper. The amount actually incurred in effecting service of process by a registered process server is specifically recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5(a)(4)(B). LAUSD set forth in detail in its memorandum of costs each item of service claimed, and in Opposition, established that each item was served by a registered process server. Further, LAUSD showed that the two $307.50 "KFH/SCPMG" entries are not duplicative charges as both Plaintiff Jane L.N. Doe and Plaintiff Jane Y.M. Doe received treatment from Kaiser for the injuries they attributed to LAUSD’s conduct in this lawsuit, and subpoenas for records were thus necessary for both Plaintiffs. In reply, Plaintiffs concede that “[i]n light of the District's newly produced invoices, Plaintiffs no longer press the ‘manner of service’ objection or the challenge to the two $307.50 KFH/SCPMG charges.”

            Further, Plaintiffs challenges multiple service of process charges for the first time in Reply: “Lizzette Vescera — $934.50; Sam Wasfi Family Therapy — $638.60; Dr. Karim Lofty — $377.35; Kryss Castle — $311.10; CTC — $341.70 and $297.50; Comprehensive Psychiatric Services — $155.75 and $200.85; and Sam Wasfi — $206.00. This is improper and the Court will not consider these arguments which should have been raised in the Motion.

            Therefore, the Court will not tax any of LAUSD’s Service of Process costs.

iii.                Item 14 — Fees for Electronic Filing or Service ($3,135.30)

            Plaintiffs ask the Court to tax all amounts not shown to be fees for electronic filing or service that the Court required or ordered, as opposed to optional electronic-service or convenience charges.

            In opposition, LAUSD argues that the Superior Court of Los Angeles County mandates electronic filing of all documents in all civil case types by litigants represented by attorneys. (Decl. Rivera ¿ 11, Ex. K – CA R Los Angeles Superior Ct. Rule 3.4.)

            Here, the prevailing party is allowed to recover the fees for the electronic filing of documents “if a court requires or orders electronic filing.” (Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5(a)(14).) The Court agrees with LAUSD that the requirement that parties file all documents electronically in the LASC is a sufficient ground alone to permit LAUSD to recover costs for such filings in this action. While Plaintiffs argue in reply that LAUSD does not explain how much of these costs are based on electronic service rather than electronic filing, service fees are addressed in Item 5 and Plaintiffs failed to raise this argument there.

Thus, the Court will not tax any of LAUSD’s Fees for Electronic Filing or Service.

IV.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing,

Plaintiffs’ motion to strike and/or tax costs is GRANTED in part. Defendant LAUSD’s cost bill is taxed in the amount of $2,475.00 for the deposition “Taking” charge for Kimberly Lakes, Ph.D. Defendant LAUSD is entitled to the remainder of its costs.

Jeremy D. Lubin, et al. v. Nancy Burkhart-Board (24STCV17833)

 

Counsel for Plaintiffs/moving parties: Keith Turner, et al.

Counsel for Defendant/opposing party: Nancy Burkhart-Board, in pro per

 

Motion for terminating sanctions (filed by Plaintiffs Jeremy and Elizabeth Lubin on July 6, 2026 and advanced via July 9, 2026 Ex Parte order from December 8, 2026. Notice of order served on July 20, 2026.)

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

Plaintiffs’ motion for terminating sanctions is GRANTED. The November 18, 2024, filed answer is struck.

 

Background

 

Plaintiffs Jeremy and Elizabeth Lubin own a certain home located 3437 South Kerckhoff Ave., San Pedro, with Defendant Nancy Burkhard-Board in an adjacent home at 3503 South Kerckhoff Ave., San Pedro. Plaintiffs allege Defendant acquired the property in 2021, and commenced a campaign of harassment, including placement of a “spite fence,” other non-permitted construction projects, and direction of bright lights into Plaintiffs’ home, and challenges to the placement of garbage cans on collection days.

 

On July 14, 2024, Plaintiffs filed their complaint for (1) Spite Fence Abatement (2) Negligence (3) Negligence, (4) Elder Financial Abuse, (5) Declaratory Relief. Defendant answered on November 18, 2024.

 

On September 3, 2025, the court granted the motion of defense counsel to be relieved of representation of Burkhart-Board.

 

Arguments

 

Plaintiffs Jeremy and Elizabeth Lubin move for terminating sanctions or alternatively issue and evidentiary sanctions, due to Defendant’s failure to comply with the May 13, 2026, court order compelling Defendant’s appearance for deposition, and the failure to provide further responses to special interrogatories, and requests for production of documents.

 

ANALYSIS

A.      Legal Standard for Terminating, Issue, and Evidentiary Sanctions

 

“To the extent authorized by the chapter governing any particular discovery method or any other provision of this title, the court, after notice to any affected party, person, or attorney, and after opportunity for hearing, may impose the following sanctions against anyone engaging in conduct that is a misuse of the discovery process: ... (c) The court may impose an evidence sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from introducing designated matters in evidence. (d) The court may impose a terminating sanction by one of the following orders: (1) An order striking out the pleadings or parts of the pleadings of any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process. (2) An order staying further proceedings by that party until an order for discovery is obeyed. (3) An order dismissing the action, or any part of the action, of that party. (4) An order rendering a judgment by default against that party.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030.)

 

Evidence or issue sanctions may be imposed only after parties violated discovery orders compelling further responses, except in exceptional circumstances, including where there was sufficiently egregious misconduct regarding a failure to respond to discovery, or a prior discovery order would be futile. (New Albertsons, Inc. v. Sup. Ct. (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1428.) “The discovery statutes thus ‘evince an incremental approach to discovery sanctions, starting with monetary sanctions and ending with the ultimate sanction of termination.’ (Citation.) Although in extreme cases a court has the authority to order a terminating sanction as a first measure (Citations), a terminating sanction should generally not be imposed until the court has attempted less severe alternatives and found them to be unsuccessful and/or the record clearly shows lesser sanctions would be ineffective.” (Lopez v. Watchtower Bible & Tract Society of New York, Inc. (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 566, 604.) To avoid sanctions, the burden of proving that a discovery violation was not willful is on the party on whom the discovery was served. (Cornwall v. Santa Monica Dairy Co. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 250, 252- 253.)

 

The court hesitantly imposes issue and evidentiary sanctions, where the result leads to a potential windfall for the prevailing party and an effective “avoidance of a decision on the merits.” (Rutledge v. Hewlett-Packard Co. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 1164, 1194 accord McGinty v. Superior Court (1994) 26 Cal.App.4th 204, 214 [“On balance, we conclude that it was excessive and an abuse of discretion for the trial court to impose the preclusive witness and evidence sanction in this case ... where no real harm has been done ..., and where the punishment falls most harshly not on the supposed malefactor but on the petitioners in their ability to have their case decided on the merits”]; Parker v. Wolters Kluwer United States, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 285, 301.)

 

A prerequisite to the imposition of the dismissal sanction is that the party has willfully failed to comply with a court order. (Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsytem (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 262, 279-280; Laguna Auto Body v. Farmers Ins. Exchange (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 481, 487 overruled on other grounds in Garcia v. McCutchen (1997) 16 Cal.4th 469, 478, fn. 4.); Young v. Rosenthal (1989) 212 Cal.App.3d 96, 114.) Preventing parties from presenting their cases on the merits is a drastic measure; terminating sanctions should only be ordered when there has been previous noncompliance with a rule or order and it appears a less severe sanction would not be effective. (Link v. Cater (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1326; Department of Forestry & Fire Protection v. Howell¿(2017) 18 Cal.App.5th 154, 191 [“Terminating sanctions are to be used sparingly because of the drastic effect of their application”].) “The trial court may order a terminating sanction for discovery abuse ‘after considering the totality of the circumstances: [the] conduct of the party to determine if the actions were willful; the detriment to the propounding party; and the number of formal and informal attempts to obtain the discovery.’” (Los Defensores, Inc. v. Gomez¿(2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 377, 390.)  

 

B.      Plaintiff in Pro Per

 

“Although self-represented litigants are not entitled to special treatment, they are entitled to the same treatment as a represented party. ... ‘Trial judges must acknowledge that in propria persona litigants often do not have an attorney's level of knowledge about the legal system and are more prone to misunderstanding the court's requirements.’ (Citation). When one party has counsel and the other does not, the trial court ‘should monitor to ensure the in propria persona litigant is not inadvertently misled, either by the represented party or by the court... [S]pecial care should be used to make sure that verbal instructions given in court and written notices are clear and understandable by a layperson.’” (Petrosyan v. Prince Corp. (2013) 223 Cal.App.4th 587, 594.)

 

C.      Analysis

 

May 13, 2026, the court granted Plaintiffs’ motion to compel the deposition of Defendant, and responses to form interrogatories, special interrogatories and requests for production of documents. The court denied the motion for non-monetary sanctions, but imposed monetary sanctions. The court also granted the motion for terminating, issue and evidentiary sanctions regarding the deposition.

 

Since the withdrawal of counsel, Defendant presents no representation to the court regarding efforts to comply with the court orders or obtain new counsel. Defendant’s failure to appear for deposition and non-compliance with any of the discovery orders compelling responses to discovery indicates an abandonment of any defense to the action. Relinquishment under the circumstances constitutes a willful choice to concede on the merits of the discovery. Given the prior order for sanctions relating to the deposition, further issue and evidentiary sanctions appear ineffective. The material impact of both the prior order and the proposed order for issue and evidentiary sanctions would effectively lead to a concession of a completely meritorious action and no valid defense. The net result leads to an effective judgement in favor of Plaintiffs via trial or default judgment. Rather than impose additional burdens on Plaintiffs and the court, the court orders terminating sanctions.

 

D.     Conclusion

 

The motion for terminating sanctions is granted. The court strikes the November 18, 2024, filed answer to the complaint. Plaintiffs may submit an application for default with the clerk following entry of this order, and proceed with a default judgment pursuant to statutory guidelines.
