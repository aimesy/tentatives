Order Granting and Denying Petition for Writ of Mandate

           

INTRODUCTION

 

             Petitioner Edgar Murguia (“Petitioner”) seeks a writ of mandate directing the Court to compel the Respondent Los Angeles County Civil Service Commission (the “Commission”) to reverse its decision to suspend Petitioner for 10 days.

 

The Court tentatively GRANTS the petition. The Court directs the Commission to vacate its decision and comply with Topanga Assn. for a Scenic Community  v. County of Los Angeles (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506. The Court offers no opinion on whether the Commission imposes discipline on Petitioner, and if so, for how long, but the Commission must make clear why it has chosen a particular level of discipline.

 

However, the Court requests argument on one issue: the Department of Parks and Recreation (the “Department”) argues that Petitioner’s petition fails because Petitioner failed to include the transcript of the Commission’s hearings, which it implies explained why the Commission made its decision. (Opp. at 14:11-18.) Is this a ground to deny the writ, and what is Petitioner’s response to this argument?

 

BACKGROUND

 

A.     Factual Background

 

On November 17, 2021, the Department suspended Petitioner from his permanent position of Senior Lake Lifeguard with the County of Los Angeles for 10 calendar days. (AR 3.) According to the Department, the disciplinary action was a result of a concerted effort to suggest to co-workers that Alexis Kearns, a lifeguard, was involved in a sexual relationship and an inappropriate quid pro quo with her then supervisor, Joaquin Falcon, in order to obtain preferential treatment for herself. (AR 3.) Petitioner was alleged to question the validity of Kearns’ “Outstanding” performance evaluation, which gave Kearns preferable ranking on the seniority list at Castaic Lake. (AR 3.) The Department also accused Petitioner of failing to maintain confidentiality about this matter during the investigation and discussing it with another subject of the investigation. (AR 3.)

 

On March 3, 2022, the Commission granted a hearing to Petitioner. (AR 20.)

 

After hearing dates throughout September, October, and November 2023, the hearing officer released her conclusions of law and recommendations. (AR 111.) The hearing officer made the following findings of fact:

 

Petitioner was promoted to his current position as permanent Senior Lake Lifeguard at Castaic Lake in February 2017. (AR 132.) Kearns started as a Lake Lifeguard in 2018, supervised by two beach Captains, Joaquin Falcon and Beth Chatalyan. (AR 133.) Falcon told Kearns that Petitioner and another Senior Lake Lifeguard Oscar Villagomez were spreading rumors about Kearns and Falcon having an inappropriate relationship. (AR 133.) Kearns received an ‘Outstanding’ rating on her 2019 performance evaluation, given by Falcon and Chatalyan. (AR 133.) Falcon received a thank you gift of a dive watch worth between $400 to $1000. (AR 133.) The watch was bought by Kearns with contributions by other lake lifeguards. (AR 133.)

 

Petitioner raised questions with several employee’s regarding the appropriateness of Kearns’ outstanding rating, questioning whether there was any favoritism, asking ‘what’s going on with Kearns and Falcon.’ (AR 133.) Petitioner had a conversation with Kearns where he expressed concern that the dive watch created an appearance of impropriety and violated a County policy regarding fraud and conflict of interest, prohibiting gifts. (AR 134.)

 

Kearns told her supervisor, Falcon, that she felt uncomfortable, bullied, threatened, and undermined in her receipt of her ‘Outstanding’ rating and that Petitioner and Villagomez spread malicious rumors that she engaged in a quid pro quo in exchange for an ‘Outstanding’ rating. (AR 134.) Villagomez stated that he knew how Falcon took care of guards and made a thrusting motion of his hips. (AR 135.) Petitioner may have laughed at the gesture. (AR 135.) Petitioner later spoke with Villagomez about the fact that he was having an administrative interview, in violation of county policy. (AR 135.)

 

According to the hearing officer, the evidence substantiates allegations Petitioner questioned Kearns’ “Outstanding’ rating, implied there was a quid pro quo, and questioned the relationship between Kearns and Falcon. (AR 131.) Thus, Petitioner was guilty of violating Department policies as alleged. (AR 131.)

 

However, the hearing officer also stated there was insufficient evidence to say that Petitioner suggested or stated there was a sexual relationship between Falcon and Kearns, that Kearns gave an expensive dive watch to Falcon in order to obtain preferential treatment for a quid pro quo of an ‘outstanding relationship’, and that Petitioner intentionally was not forthcoming and honest. (AR 132.) Additionally, there was insufficient evidence to say that Petitioner brought up the question of ‘what’s going on between Kearns and Falcon’ or made statements that they were engaged in an inappropriate relationship. (AR 132.)

 

Because of the lack of evidence, the hearing officer found the discipline inappropriate and suggested the 10-day suspension be reduced to a five-day suspension. (AR 132.)

 

On June 5, 2024, the Civil Service Commission announced its proposed decision to accept the findings and recommendation of the hearing officer and reduce the 10 day suspension to 5 days. (AR 143.) Both petitioner and the Department objected to the proposed decision. (AR 144, 171.)

 

On October 23, 2024, the Commission issued its decision. The Commission overruled the Petitioner’s objections and sustained the Department’s objections. (AR 244-245.) The Commission rejected the recommendations of the hearing officer to reduce the ten day suspension to five days and announced its final decision to impose a ten day suspension. (AR 245.)

 

In announcing its decision, the Commission did not make clear if it was making findings different from those of the Hearing Officer. Nor did the Commission explain why it rejected the five day reduction of the hearing officer and instead imposed a ten day suspension. Here is the full decision by the Commission:

 

On October 23, 2024, the [Commission] overruled the petitioner’s objections and sustained the departments objections. The Commission rejected the recommendation of the Hearing Officer, Trudi Ferguson, to reduce the ten (10) day suspension to five (5) days and announced as its final decision to impose a ten (10) day suspension. Commissioner Huntley dissented. Commission Segal was absent.

 

 

(AR 249.) It is with respect to this decision that Petitioner seeks a writ of mandate, compelling the Commission to set aside its ten-day suspension.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1094.5, subd. (b), the pertinent issues are whether the respondent has proceeded without jurisdiction, whether there was a fair trial, and whether there was a prejudicial abuse of discretion.  An abuse of discretion is established if the agency has not proceeded in the manner required by law, the decision is not supported by the findings, or the findings are not supported by the evidence.  (Code Civ. Pro. § 1094.5, subd. (b).)

 

Because the decision to suspend Petitioner concerns a fundamental vested right, the independent judgment standard applies to the factual findings made by Respondent. (See Wences v. City of Los Angeles (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 305, 314.)  Under the independent judgment test, “the trial court not only examines the administrative record for errors of law, but also exercises its independent judgment upon the evidence disclosed in a limited trial de novo.”  (Bixby v. Pierno (1971) 4 Cal. 3d 130, 143.)  The court may draw its own reasonable inferences from the evidence and make its determinations as to the credibility of witnesses.  (Morrison v. Housing Authority of the City of Los Angeles Board of Commissioners (2003) 107 Cal. App. 4th 860, 868.)  “In exercising its independent judgment, a trial court must afford a strong presumption of correctness concerning the administrative findings, and the party challenging the administrative decision bears the burden of convincing the court that the administrative findings are contrary to the weight of the evidence.”  (Fukuda v. City of Angels (1999) 20 Cal. 4th 805, 817; see also Evid. Code § 664.) 

 

Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate, by citation to the administrative record, that the weight of the evidence does not support the administrative findings.  (Strumsky v. San Diego County Employees Retirement Assn. (1974) 11 Cal.3d 28, 32; Steele v. Los Angeles County Civil Service Commission (1958) 166 Cal. App. 2d 129, 137.)  Petitioner “must identify (with citations to the record) the factual findings made by the board that he or she is challenging and demonstrate (with citations to the record) why those factual findings were against the weight of the evidence.”  (Shenouda v. Veterinary Medical Bd. (2018) 27 Cal.App.5th 500, 513.) A reviewing court “will not act as counsel for either party to an appeal and will not assume the task of initiating and prosecuting a search of the record for any purpose of discovering errors not pointed out in the briefs.” (Fox v. Erickson (1950) 99 Cal.App.2d 740, 742 (Fox).)  When an appellant challenges “the sufficiency of the evidence, all material evidence on the point must be set forth and not merely [his] own evidence.” (Toigo v. Town of Ross (1998) 70 Cal.App.4th 309, 317.) 

 

“On questions of law arising in mandate proceedings, [the court] exercise[s] independent judgment.’”  (Christensen v. Lightbourne (2017) 15 Cal.App.5th 1239, 1251.)  The interpretation of statute or regulation is a question of law.  (See State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Quackenbush (1999) 77 Cal.App.4th 65, 77.)   In addition, “[a] challenge to the procedural fairness of the administrative hearing is reviewed de novo on appeal because the ultimate determination of procedural fairness amounts to a question of law.”  (Nasha L.L.C. v. City of Los Angeles (2004) 125 Cal.App.4th 470, 482.) 

 

 

DISCUSSION

 

 

A.     Petitioner’s first argument presents is not persuasive based on the current record

 

Petitioner’s first argument is that the evidence does not support a finding that Petitioner “spread or initiated malicious rumors suggesting an inappropriate sexual relationship” between Kearns and Falcon. (Opening Brief, 5:6-7.)

 

The hearing officer specifically determined that the “Department failed to prove [Petitioner] spread a malicious rumor that Kearns was engaged in an inappropriate sexual relationship with her supervisor, Falcon.” (AR 137.) The Commission did not specifically say that it was making any other finding. (AR 244.)

 

Petitioner prevailed on this issue before the Hearing Officer. The Court cannot discern whether the Commission had a different point of view.  Accordingly, the Court cannot review a finding that the Court is unsure was made by the Commission.

 

B.     Petitioner’s second argument is unpersuasive

 

Petitioner’s second argument is that a finding that Petitioner “maybe laughed” at a sexual gesture is speculative and unsupported. (Opening Brief, 6:6-7.)

 

Falcon testified Murguia “kind of chuckled.” (AR 656.) The hearing officer found Falcon credible. (AR 128). By contrast, another person in the room did not hear Petitioner laugh. (AR 128). In sum, one person heard the laughter; one person did not.

 

Exercising its independent judgment, the Court finds that the finding that Petitioner “maybe laughed” is supported by the evidence.

 

C.     Petitioner’s third argument is unpersuasive

 

Petitioner’s third argument is that the Department failed to prove any improper conduct related to Kearns’s performance evaluation. (Opening Brief, 6:16-17.)

 

The hearing officer found, and the Commission adopted the following facts:

 

·         Petitioner raised questions with several employees regarding the appropriateness of Kearns’ Outstanding rating, questioned whether there was any favoritism and asked what was going on with Kearns and Falcon. Petitioner said past practice was that employees only received ‘Outstanding’ if they walked on water.

 

·         There was no reason for Petitioner to know about Kearns’ rating. Petitioner had no input in Kearns evaluation and had no reason to question Kearns’ rating.

 

(AR 133.)

 

            The evidence reflects that Petitioner was not within his purview to request information about Kearns’ performance evaluation. (AR 722.) Petitioner was not Kearns’ supervisor so he did not have a business need to know what her evaluation was. (AR 841.) Yet Petitioner “raised questions with several employees… questioning whether there was any favoritism, asking ‘what’s going on…’” [AR 133 (Fact 7).]

 

            Policy 809 states that abusive conduct and respect in the workplace can include misapplication or misuse of authority, which undermines an employee’s right to work with dignity and respect. (AR 87.) Kearns was crushed about the rumors going around about her. (AR 588.) Kearns felt unworthy. (AR 601.)

 

            Petitioner offers no evidence to say that performance evaluations are publicly posted. Indeed, the record reflects that ratings are not publicly posted for other employees to see. (AR 539.)

 

Exercising its independent judgment, the Court finds that substantial evidence supports the finding that Petitioner engaged in improper conduct when it came to Kearns’ performance evaluation, and that improper conduct violated Policy 809.

 

D.    Petitioner’s fourth argument is not supported by any legal authority or record citation

 

Petitioner’s fourth argument is that the Department’s case improperly relied heavily on investigator summaries and secondhand characterizations of witness statements, rather than on verbatim transcripts, recordings, or contemporaneous documents. (Opening Brief, 7:10-14.)

 

This argument is unavailing. Petitioner cited no authority why this would make the investigation defective. Petitioner provided no analysis how any summary or characterization was defective. Petitioner also stated, “multiple witnesses testified in ways that contradicted the Department’s characterization.” (Opening Brief, 7:15-16.) No citation to the record was given for this statement.

 

Accordingly, Petitioner has not met their burden to show why the Department’s case improperly relied heavily on investigator summaries and secondhand characterizations of witness statements.

 

E.     The Commission did not comply with Topanga

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1094.5, subd. (b), an abuse of discretion is established if the decision is not supported by the findings.  In Topanga Assn. for a Scenic Community v. County of Los Angeles, (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506, 515, the Supreme Court held that “implicit in [Code of Civil Procedure] section 1094.5 is a requirement that the agency which renders the challenged decision must set forth findings to bridge the analytic gap between the raw evidence and ultimate decision or order.”  The court explained that “among other functions, … findings enable the reviewing court to trace and examine the agency's mode of analysis.…  Absent such roadsigns, a reviewing court would be forced into unguided and resource-consuming explorations; it would have to grope through the record to determine whether some combination of credible evidentiary items which supported some line of factual and legal conclusions supported the ultimate order or decision of the agency….”  (11 Cal. 3d at 516-517 [fns. omitted].) 

 

“The findings requirement ‘serves to conduce the administrative body to draw legally relevant sub-conclusions supportive of its ultimate decision; the intended effect is to facilitate orderly analysis and minimize the likelihood that the agency will randomly leap from evidence to conclusions.’”  (West Chandler Blvd. Neighborhood Ass’n vs. City of Los Angeles (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1506, 1521-22.)  “Administrative agency findings are generally permitted considerable latitude with regard to their precision, formality, and matters reasonably implied therein.”  (Southern Pacific Transportation Co. v. State Bd. of Equalization (1987) 191 Cal.App.3d 938, 954.)  However, “mere conclusory findings without reference to the record are inadequate.”  (West Chandler, supra, 198 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1521-22.)  If the court “cannot discern the analytic route the [agency] traveled from evidence to action,” the decision does not comply with Topanga.  (Ibid.)  Significantly in this case, Topanga requires the agency to “justify the penalty imposed, including ‘a statement of the factual and legal basis for the decision.’”  (Oduyale v. Cal. State Bd. of Pharmacy (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 101, 113.)  ¿ 

 

            Here, the Commission did not explicitly state it disagreed with any of the hearing officer’s findings but rejected the hearing officer’s decision to decrease the suspension from ten days to five days.  (AR 244-245.) The Commission’s final action, however, did not say why. It consisted of a conclusory approval of the Department’s position that a ten-day suspension was appropriate and a rejection of the Hearing Officer’s recommendation. (AR 244-245.) It contains no independent findings, no explanation for rejecting the Hearing Officer’s credibility determinations, and no analysis connecting the evidence to the ultimate decision.

 

            Absent any findings from the Commission, this reviewing Court is unable to trace and examine the Commission’s mode of analysis. (Topanga, supra, 11 Cal.3d at pp. 516-517.) While this Court does not demand that the Commission elaborate on their decision with great precision or formality, “mere conclusory findings without reference to the record are inadequate.” (West Chandler, supra, 198 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1521-22.) 

 

      In its opposition, the Department suggests that the Court can discern the Commission’s reasoning by reference to the Department’s objections, which it characterizes as being based on the proposition that “veiled insinuations an employee slept with a supervisor for advancement are just as damaging as an explicit statement.” (Opp. 15:5-6.) The Department’s argument compounds the problem, rather than solving it. The Hearing Officer found there was not sufficient evidence that Petitioner insinuated that Kearns had an inappropriate sexual relationship with her supervisor. (AR (AR 137.) The Commission did not say otherwise. (AR 244.) If the Commission was making findings different than those of the Hearing Officer, it could have explained that. If the Commission was adopting the factual findings of the Hearing Officer, but changing the result, the Commission could have explained why.

 

      This lack of reasoning makes review extremely difficult if not impossible. The Department, for example, asserts that the “Commission did not ‘reject the Hearing Officer’s findings.’” (Opp. at 14:19-20.) However, in the argument referenced in the previous paragraph of this ruling, the Department seems to assume that, based on its objection,  the Commission did reject the Hearing Officer’s determination (at AR 137) that there was insufficient evidence of Petitioner insinuating that Kearns was in a sexual relationship with her supervisor. The February 10, 2025 Final Commission action stated that the Commission “approved findings in the above-entitled case.” (AR 247.)  Does that mean it adopted the Hearing Officer’s findings or made different findings? The Court cannot discern which path the Commission took to reach its decision.

 

            Petitioner argues that this Court should decide this case in his favor, rather than remanding the case back to the Commission. The Court finds no basis to decide in Petitioner’s favor without remanding the case. None of the cases cited by Petitioner indicate remand is disfavored in situations like the one before the Court. Skelly v. State Personnel Bd. (1975) 15 Cal.3d 194, 217-218 and Talmo v. Civil Service Commission (1991) 23 Cal.App.3d 210,229-230 do not discuss a trial court’s power to remand a case under C.C.P. § 1094.5, subd. (e).  Topanga, supra, 11 Cal.3d at p. 516 cautions against the Court not remanding the case.  Absent findings from the administrative agency below, “a reviewing court would be forced into unguided and resource-consuming explorations; it would have to grope through the record to determine whether some combination of credible evidentiary items which supported some line of factual and legal conclusions supported the ultimate order or decision of the agency.” (Id., at p. 516.)

 

Accordingly, on remand, the Commission must explain its rulings consistent with Topanga.  

 

CONCLUSION

 

The Court GRANTS the petition for writ of mandate. The Court directs the Commission to vacate its decision and directs the Commission to comply with Topanga Assn. for a Scenic Community  v. County of Los Angeles (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506. The Court offers no opinion on whether the Commission imposes discipline on Petitioner, and if so, for how long, but the Commission must make clear why it has chosen a particular level of discipline.

 

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Order Regarding Motion to Quash Deposition Subpoena

           

INTRODUCTION

 

Respondent Harvard-Westlake School (“Respondent”) seeks an order staying and quashing the June 15, 2026 Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records (“Tyson Subpoena”) served by Petitioner Alex G. Romain (“Petitioner”) on Tyson & Mendes LLP (“Tyson & Mendes”). Respondent also moves for a protective order precluding Petitioner from attempting to take any discovery from Tyson without first obtaining leave of court. Respondent also moves for an order imposing monetary attorneys fees against Petitioner in the amount of $5,977 on the grounds that the Tyson Subpoena was oppressive and/or Petitioner opposed this motion without substantial justification.

 

The Court GRANTS the motion to quash the deposition subpoena. The Court DENIES any request for a protective order and grants the request for attorneys fees in a reduced amount of $3,003.00.

BACKGROUND FACTS

 

            On June 15, 2026, a subpoena for production of business records was issued. (Bordin-Wosk Decl., Exh. 1 at p. 1.) Petitioner served the subpoena on the Custodian of Business Records for Tyson & Mendes LLP. (Bordin-Wosk Decl., Exh. 1 at p. 1.) Tyson & Mendes is former counsel of record for Harvard-Westlake in this matter. (Bordin-Wosk Decl., ¶ 3 & Exh. 7 at ¶ 1.)

 

            In the Tyson Subpoena, Petitioner requests (1) communications between Tyson &Mendes and Lucca V. relating to the juvenile proceeding; and (2) communications between Tyson & Mendes and Lucca V. related to Lucca V.’s alleged sexual or racial misconduct. (Bordin-Wosk Decl., Exh. 1 at p. 4.)

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“Depositions of opposing counsel are presumptively improper, severely restricted, and require ‘extremely good cause’—a high standard. [Citations.] There are strong policy considerations against deposing an opposing counsel. The practice runs counter to the adversarial process and to the state’s public policy to ‘[p]revent attorneys from taking undue advantage of their adversary’s industry and efforts.’ [Citation.]” (Carehouse Convalescent Hospital v. Superior Court (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1558, 1562.)

 

Attorney depositions are considered disruptive and add to the length and expensive of litigation. (Id., at p. 1562.) “Attorney depositions chill the attorney-client relationship, impede civility and easily lend themselves to gamesmanship and abuse. ‘Counsel should be free to devote his or her time and efforts to preparing the client's case without fear of being interrogated by his or her opponent.’” (Id., at p. 1563.)

 

To determine whether an attorney deposition should go forward, California applies a three-prong test. “First, does the proponent have other practicable means to obtain the information? Second, is the information crucial to the preparation of the case? Third, is the information subject to a privilege?” (Id., at p. 1563.)

 

PROCEDURAL HISTORY

 

            Harvard-Westlake filed a Motion to Quash on July 1, 2026. Petitioner has not opposed the motion.           

ANALYSIS

 

This case involves the question of whether Harvard-Westlake has produced all pupil records pertaining to A.R. The requested documents are not related to the fundamental legal question in this case: has Harvard-Westlake produced all of A.R.’s pupil records and if not, what additional records must Harvard-Westlake produce to comply with its legal obligations?

 

There has been no showing that communications relating to the juvenile proceeding involving Lucca V. and communications between Tyson & Mendes and Lucca V. may be A.R.’s pupil records. Moreover, if there are records of Lucca V., those records might be protected by a student’s right to privacy in their pupil records. (Rim of the World Unified School Dist. v. Superior Court (2002) 104 Cal.App.4th 1393, 1398; Porten v. University of San Francisco (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 825, 832.)  If Petitioner did have a legal right to seek these records, he could seek them from Harvard-Westlake.

 

Petitioner has not filed an opposition, so has not overcome the presumption that this request is presumptively improper.

In its notice of motion, Harvard-Westlake requests a protective order as well. The Court declines to impose a protective order because there is no discussion of a protective order in the memorandum of points and authorities.

 

When a party moves to quash a deposition subpoena, “the court may in its discretion award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonable attorney's fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.2.) The Court “shall impose a monetary sanction…against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion for a protective order, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.420, subd. (h).)

 

A deposition of an opposing party’s attorney is an inherently burdensome tactic. (Carehouse Convalescent Hospital, supra, 143 Cal.App.4th at p. 1566.) Therefore, the Court determines sanctions are warranted.

 

Harvard-Westlake seeks $5,977 in sanctions. However, some of this amount is a result of an expectation of reviewing an opposition and preparing a reply, which did not occur for this motion. (See, e.g., Bordin-Wosk Decl., ¶ 12.) The Court therefore cuts $1,000.00 from the time Yousefzadeh was expected to incur on this motion.

 

The Court also cuts the amount of time that Partner Christopher Blanchard worked on this motion from 12.6 hours to 6 hours. It was unreasonable for Harvard-Westlake to have multiple attorneys conducting legal research and drafting a short and straightforward motion.

 

That leaves the following:

 

·         Yousefzadeh - $250/hr at 2.5 hours

·         Blanchard - $290/hr at 6 hours

·         Bordin-Wosk - $290/ hr at 2.2 hours

·         $60 in costs

 

The lodestar for this attorneys fees motion is $3,003.00.

CONCLUSION

 

 

 

 

 

The Court GRANTS the motion to quash the deposition subpoena. The Court DENIES any request for a protective order and grants the request for attorneys fees in a reduced amount of $3,003.00. Petitioner shall pay this amount to Harvard-Westlake within 30 days.

 

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Order Regarding Discovery Motions

           

INTRODUCTION

 

             Petitioner The Westmoreland Firm (“Petitioner”) moves for two orders seeking further responses from Respondent City of Compton (“City”) as to Petitioner’s Request for Production of Documents. Petitioner also requests monetary sanctions against the City in the amount of $5,460.00 (Set One) and $5,460.00 (Set Two). The City opposes and requests attorneys fees in the amount of $2,070.00 (Set One).

 

            The Court DENIES both motions to compel further discovery responses and orders Petitioner to pay attorneys fees of $2,070.00, payable to the City in 30 days.

 

            Petitioner moves for an order compelling the City to (1) produce its employee, Assistant City Manager Tamara Binns, for deposition within fifteen calendar days; (2) designate a person most qualified to testify on the City’s behalf concerning each subject identified in Petitioner’s second notice of deposition; (3) complete the PMK deposition  within 15 calendar days. Petitioner also requests attorneys fees against the City in the amount of $7,865.25. The City opposes.

 

            The Court GRANTS the motion to compel the deposition of Binns, and GRANTS IN PART the motion to compel the deposition of the PMK. The Court denies attorneys fees.

 

PETITIONER’S MOTIONS TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO RFPDs, SET ONE AND SET TWO

 

A.     Procedural History

 

On March 4, 2026, Petitioner filed a verified petition for writ of mandate, seeking two categories of public records: (a) payment and disbursement records reflecting City payments to Petitioner for tax years 2023-2025, and (b) the City’s outside counsel handbook/policies/procedures and billing/audit/payment practices for 2023-2025. (Petition, ¶ 2.)

 

On March 18, 2026, Petitioner sought Requests for Production of Documents (“RFPDs”), Set One. (Westmoreland Decl., ¶ 2.)

 

On April 28, 2026, the City served verified responses to RFPDs, Set One. (Westmoreland Decl., ¶ 4.)

 

On May 4, 2026, Petitioner sought RFPDs, Set Two. (Westmoreland Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.)

 

Petitioner filed two motions to compel further responses on July 6, 2026.

On September 3, 2026, the City served verified responses to Set Two. (Welch Decl., ¶¶ 4–5.)

 

B.     Legal Standard

 

The California Public Records Act, Government Code § 7922.530, provides:

 

(a)   Except with respect to public records exempt from disclosure by express provisions of law, each state or local agency, upon a request for a copy of records that reasonably describes an identifiable record or records, shall make the records promptly available to any person upon payment of fees covering direct costs of duplication, or a statutory fee if applicable. Upon request, an exact copy shall be provided unless impracticable to do so.

 

“When a party seeks pretrial discovery in a proceeding to enforce the California Public Records Act, ‘the trial court must determine whether the discovery sought is necessary to resolve whether the agency has a duty to disclose, and…additionally whether the request is justified given the need for an expeditious resolution.’” (County of San Benito v. Superior Court (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 243, 249 (County of San Benito).)

 

In contrast with civil litigation, “the general scope of discovery in Public Records Act proceedings is limited to a narrow issue: whether the public agency has an obligation to disclose the records that the petitioner has requested.” (Id., at p. 254 [internal quotations removed].)

 

It is appropriate for a Court to grant discovery requests if the requests are “tailored to understanding and assessing the reasonableness of the County’s ongoing search for responsive records.” (Id., at p. 257.)

 

C.     Analysis – RFPDs, Set One

 

C.C.P. § 2031.310, subd. (c) provides:

 

Unless notice of this motion is given within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the demanding party and the responding party have agreed in writing, the demanding party waives any right to compel a further response to the demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling.

 

A court has no power to entertain, let alone grant, an untimely motion. (Sexton v. Superior Court (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1410 [after 45 days, a court is “without authority to rule on motions to compel [a further response] other than to deny them].)

 

The City served its verified responses to RFP Set One on April 28, 2026. (Westmoreland Decl. ¶ 4; Exh. B.) WLF filed this motion on July 6, 2026, which is well over 45 days from service of the verified responses.

 

The only way to extend the deadline to file a motion to compel further discovery responses is for the parties to agree in writing, and for the writing to include a specific new deadline. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (c).)

 

Counsel for Petitioner says that she believed the parties’ settlement discussions included “the deadline to file a motion concerning the City’s deficient RPF responses.” (Westmoreland Decl., ¶ 12.) On reply, Petitioner submitted new evidence, which shows that the City suggested putting discovery on hand to see if they could resolve cases. (Westmoreland Supp. Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. G.)

 

The problem for Petitioner is that this email does not satisfy the requirements of C.C.P. § 2031.31, subd. (c). Petitioner needed a writing that included an extension for filing a motion to compel further discovery responses and for the writing to include a specific new deadline. No specific deadline was included, so as a matter of law, the deadline was not continued. 

 

Therefore, this motion is untimely, and the Court has no authority to compel further discovery responses.

 

Petitioner contends that [a]ny timeliness objection should be rejected based on written agreement, waiver, estoppel, and the City's own conduct.” (Motion at 12:17–18.) Regarding estoppel, the Court of Appeal has suggested that if a responding party fails to raise timeliness and instead permits “the hearing to go forward,” the responding party might be estopped from later arguing timeliness. (Sexton, supra, 58 Cal.App.4th at p. 1410, fn. 4.) That exception has no application here: the City raises timeliness in this very opposition, before any hearing on the merits of Petitioner's motion has occurred.

 

Even if the Court reached the merits of the motion, Petitioner would still not prevail. A petitioner in a public records act proceeding cannot use a motion to compel to obtain the documents already sought by the underlying public records request. (County of San Benito, supra, 96 Cal.App.5th at pp. 255-256.)

 

Requests Nos. 11 and 12 seek “all DOCUMENTS” and “all ESI pertaining to YOUR outside counsel policies, procedures, handbooks, and guidelines.” (Westmoreland Decl., Exh. A.) This is awfully similar to the ultimate request in this CPRA case, where Petitioner seeks the City’s outside counsel handbook/policies/procedures and billing/audit/payment practices for 2023-2025. (Petition, ¶ 2.)

 

Request No. 4 seeks the following:

 

All DOCUMENTS that support the amount of $585,902.18 described in the 2025 1099-NEC that YOU issued to Petitioner.

 

Request No. 5 seeks the following:

 

All DOCUMENTS pertaining to all payments of Petitioner’s invoices presented to YOU from January 1, 2019 to the present.  

 

Request No. 7 seeks the following:

 

All ESI that support the amount of $585,902.18 described in the 2025 1099-NEC that YOU issued to Petitioner.

 

Request No. 8 seeks the following:

 

All ESI pertaining to all payments of Petitioner’s invoices presented to YOU from January 1, 2019 to the present.

 

The Court finds that these requests are substantially similar to the overall CPRA request, which seeks payments and disbursement records reflecting City payments to Petitioner for tax years 2023-2025.

 

There is another problem with the other RFPDs. There is a clear legislative intent that CPRA actions be handled expeditiously. (City of Los Angeles v. Superior Court (2017) 9 Cal.App.5th 272, 290.) Because of this clear legislative intent, “the trial court has discretion to consider whether the petitioner has made an adequate showing that the discovery is likely to aid in the resolution of the particular issues presented in the proceeding.” (Ibid.) Nothing in the separate statement for Requests Nos. 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, and 10 explain why those requests are necessary for addressing the ultimate question: whether the City has a duty to disclose.

 

Thus, even on the merits, the Court would not compel further responses to RFPDs, Set One.

 

The Court is required to impose sanctions on a party who unsuccessfully makes a motion to compel further responses unless the party can show they acted with substantial justification or circumstances would make the imposition of sanctions unjust. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (h).)

 

There was no substantial justification for filing this motion. The documents requested are beyond what is allowed in CPRA discovery and the motion was filed late. Therefore, the Court awards sanctions of $2,070.00, as requested by the City.

 

D.    Analysis – RFPDs, Set Two

 

After the filing of the motion, the City filed responses to Set Two, meaning this motion is now moot. (Welch Decl., ¶¶ 4–5.)

 

A party who fails to serve a timely response to a demand for inspection waives any objection, including privilege or work product, “unless the court, on motion, grants relief from this waiver” upon a showing that the party “has subsequently served a response that is in substantial compliance” with the applicable sections, and that “the party's failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).)

 

Here, the Court finds that the City acted with mistake, inadvertence or neglect. Three days before the June 8, 2026, deadline to serve responses to Set Two, the parties, including Petitioner, jointly filed a Joint Notice of Settlement of this very action, and the City reasonably believed the discovery dispute had been resolved along with the rest of the case.

 

On the merits, the Court cannot compel responses to RFPDs, Set Two. Petitioner does not explain anywhere in the briefing how the requests in Set Two address the ultimate issue in a CPRA case: whether the City has a duty to disclose the documents at issue in the underlying CPRA request.[1] Simply put, a CPRA request is not an opportunity to conduct broad discovery.

 

Therefore, the motion to compel further responses to RFPDs, Set Two is denied.

 

The City does not seek sanctions in connection with Petitioner’s making of a motion to compel further discovery responses to RFPDs, Set Two. Therefore, the Court will not award any sanctions.

PETITIONER’s MOTION TO COMPEL THE DEPOSITION OF TAMARA BINNS AND THE CITY’S PMK

 

A.     Legal Standard

 

C.C.P. § 2025.450, subd. (a) provides:

If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action or an officer, director, managing agent, or employee of a party, or a person designated by an organization that is a party under Section 2025.230, without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice, the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent's attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.

 

            C.C.P. § 2025.450, subd. (b) provides:

 

The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.

 

B.     Analysis

 

The PMK notice identified seven deposition topics: (1) the City's public records request portal; (2) California Public Records Request No. P008110-021826; (3) payments made to WLF; (4) all 1099s issued to WLF; (5) communications between WLF and the City regarding invoices, payments, 1099s, and any other tax matters; (6) documents exchanged between WLF and the City regarding the same; and (7) the City's outside counsel handbooks from January 1, 2010 to present. (Motion, Exh. D at p. 2.)

 

Deposition Topics 3 through 7 do not bear on whether the City has a duty to disclose. As the Court discussed in its analysis of the Motions to Compel Further, discovery in a CPRA case must address this question, and this question alone.

 

Deposition Topics 1 and 2 do have the potential to address whether the City has a duty to disclose records. Accordingly, they are proper subjects for a PMK deposition.

 

The Court also concludes that the Binns deposition should move forward. Contrary to what the City indicates, Binns has previously verified responses  to Petitioner’s Form Interrogatories, Requests for Admission and Requests for Production. (Westmoreland Supp. Decl., ¶ 4.) The Court also disagrees with the City’s contention that Binns can get out of a deposition because she is at the apex of a governmental hierarchy. In the case cited in the opposition, Liberty Mutual Insurance Co. v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1282 the executive declared that he lacked involvement and knowledge. (Id., at pp. 1284-1288.) By contrast, the evidence before the Court suggests Binns does have awareness of some of Petitioner’s requests.

 

The City also never filed a protective order under C.C.P.  § 2025.420, subd. (a), so that issue is not properly before the Court. The Court does not find the City’s argument that there was a lack of a meet-and-confer persuasive. The evidence suggests that the parties did engage in a meet-and-confer process. (Westmoreland Supp. Decl., ¶¶ 8-9.)

 

The City urges the Court to stay the Binns deposition until after the PMK deposition, so Petitioner is not engaged in unreasonably cumulative and duplicative discovery. The Court declines to do that. The Court urges the parties to be reasonable as to the sequencing of the depositions. However, based on the record, the Court does not think two depositions counts as “unreasonably cumulative” or “duplicative” discovery.

 

C.C.P. § 2025.450, subd. (h) requires the Court to impose sanctions if the Court grants a motion to compel discovery, unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.

 

Petitioner requests $7,865.25 in attorneys fees, with Petitioner’s counsel billing $1,200/hr. (Westmoreland Decl., ¶ 24.) This is not reasonable for a CPRA case, where the Court normally sees hourly rates of between $300 and $600/hr.

 

The Court agrees that the City acted with substantial justification in opposing the PMK deposition, especially because many of the items Petitioner wants to depose the PMK on are not relevant to a CPRA action.

 

The Court also agrees that the City acted with substantial justification in opposing the Binns deposition because of the City’s “apex” argument. While the Court rejects the argument, is it a reasonable basis to oppose.

 

Therefore, the Court denies attorney’s fees.

 

CONCLUSION

 

            The Court DENIES both motions to compel further discovery responses and awards attorneys fees of $2,070.00, payable from Petitioner to the City in 30 days.

 

            The Court GRANTS the motion to compel the deposition of Binns, and GRANTS IN PART the motion to compel the deposition of the PMK. The Court denies attorneys fees.

Order Denying Application for Preliminary Injunction

 

 

            Plaintiff Pamela Bates (“Plaintiff”) requesting a preliminary injunction ordering Defendant Mark Leonard (“Leonard”) and Mark Leonard Defined Pension Plan (collectively, “Defendants”) from taking possession of and/or selling the real property located at 510 West Raymond Street, Compton, California 90220 (“Subject Property”).

 

            The Court tentatively DENIES the application for a preliminary injunction for the reasons set forth below.

 

            However, the Court requests argument on the following question:  Plaintiff may have demonstrated a question about the amount of the default.  (See Reply at 2:26-4:5.) Assuming for the sake of argument that she has a likelihood of success on the merits as to the amount of the default, but that she is in default in some significant amount, is she entitled to a preliminary injunction?

LEGAL STANDARD

 

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending a decision on the merits.  (Major v. Miraverde Homeowners Ass’n. (1992) 7 Cal. App. 4th 618, 623.)  In deciding whether or not to grant a preliminary injunction, the court looks to two factors, including “(1) the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits, and (2) the relative balance of harms that is likely to result from the granting or denial of interim injunctive relief.”  (White v. Davis (2003) 30 Cal.4th 528, 553-54.) The factors are interrelated, with a greater showing on one permitting a lesser showing on the other.  (Dodge, Warren & Peters Ins. Services, Inc. v. Riley (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 1414, 1420.)  However, the party seeking an injunction must demonstrate at least a reasonable probability of success on the merits.  (IT Corp. v. County of Imperial (1983) 35 Cal.3d 63, 73-74.)  The party seeking the injunction bears the burden of demonstrating both a likelihood of success on the merits and the occurrence of irreparable harm.  (Savage v. Trammell Crow Co. (1990) 223 Cal.App.3d 1562, 1571.) Irreparable harm may exist if the plaintiff can show an inadequate remedy at law.  (CCP § 526(a).)

 

“The granting or denying of a preliminary injunction does not constitute an adjudication of the ultimate rights in controversy.”  (Cohen v. Board of Supervisors (1985) 40 Cal.3d 277, 286; see also Yee v. American National Ins. Co. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 453, 457-458 [“a preliminary injunction is not a determination on the merits….”].) 

 

DISCUSSION

 

A.     Summary of Facts

 

The application is based on Plaintiff’s causes of action for (1) breach of contract; (2) fraudulent concealment of facts; (3) negligent misrepresentation; (4) accounting; and (5) intentional infliction of emotional distress.

 

Plaintiff is the owner and resides in the Subject Property as her primary residence. (Choi Decl., ¶ 2.)

 

On or about September 17, 2024, Plaintiff entered into Loan Number: R000503 with Leonard. (Choi Decl., ¶ 3.) Plaintiff executed a written promissory note in favor of Leonard. (Choi Decl., ¶ 3.) The note is secured by a deed of trust. (Choi Decl., ¶ 3.)

 

To obtain the loan, Plaintiff certified under the penalty of perjury that the loan proceeds would be used only for business purposes, and certified under the penalty of criminal prosecution that the Property was held for investment purposes and that she would not occupy it. (Leonard Decl., ¶ 4.) The loan required interest-only payments until loan maturity on October 1, 2025, at which point he entire balance would become due and owing. (Leonard Decl., ¶ 5.)

 

Plaintiff asserts that she has made all payments in accordance with the Note, while Leonard has continued to provide payoff demands and payment histories that were incomplete, inaccurate, and misleading. (Choi Decl., ¶ 4.) Plaintiff asserts that Leonard also allegedly imposed late fees, a default interest rate, and other charges not authorized by the Note. (Choi Decl., ¶ 4.)

 

By contrast, Leonard says that Plaintiff agreed to be charged a fee for late payments and the Note allowed a higher default interest rate upon default. (Leonard Decl., ¶ 7.)

 

Leonard states that he started receiving Plaintiff’s monthly payments late in February 2025. (Leonard Decl., ¶¶ 12, 13.)

 

Leonard received the last payment from Plaintiff on June 1, 2025. (Leonard Decl., ¶ 18.) During the summer of 2025, Leonard was in regular contact with Bates on the telephone to discuss her loan default and the upcoming loan maturity date. (Leonard Decl., ¶ 19.) Leonard sent Bates three accountings. (Leonard Decl., ¶¶ 20-22.)

 

The loan matured on October 1, 2025, with no payment. (Leonard Decl., ¶ 26.)

 

Between October 2025 and January 2026, Plaintiff claimed she was trying to refinance her loan, but never went through with the refinancing. (Leonard Decl., ¶¶ 27-36.)

 

On or about March 24, 2026, Lenders TD Service, LLC recorded a Notice of Default and Election to Sell Under Deed of Trust as Document No. 20260202407 in the Recorder’s Office of Los Angeles County, California, against the Subject Property. (Choi Decl., ¶ 5.) Plaintiff did not cure her loan default. (Leonard Decl., ¶ 40.)

 

On July 9, 2026, Lenders TD Service recorded a Notice of Trustee’s Sale Under Deed of Trust against the Subject Property. (Choi Decl., ¶ 6.) The trustee’s sale is currently on hold because of the temporary restraining order. The loan continues to accrue interest at the post-maturity default rate, along with late fees, foreclosure fees, and legal fees. (Leonard Decl., ¶ 42.) Plaintiff has also failed to make timely payments for property taxes, and there are now approximately $5,763.28 in back taxes owed on the Subject Property. (Leonard Decl., ¶ 43.)

 

B.     Judicial Notice

 

The Court grants the request for judicial notice (“RJN”).

 

C.     Plaintiffs’ Likelihood of Success on the Merits

 

The application is based on Plaintiff’s causes of action for breach of contract; fraudulent concealment of facts; negligent misrepresentation; accounting; and intentional infliction of emotional distress.

 

1.      Breach of Contract

 

To establish a claim for breach of contract, a plaintiff must prove: (1) the existence of a contract; (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendant’s breach of the contract; and (4) damages incurred by plaintiff as a result of the breach.  (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1367.) 

 

Plaintiff has alleged that Leonard has breached his obligations under the promissory note by charging the loan for “default interest, late fees, and other charges not authorized by the Note,” failing to properly credit the loan for payments, providing “inaccurate and misleading payoff demands and payoff histories,” failing to provide “a complete and accurate accounting of the Loan,” and “[a]cting in a manner that prevented Plaintiff from refinancing the Loan […].” (FAC, ¶ 39.)

 

Plaintiff’s promissory note authorizes the charging of default interest, late fees, and certain costs incurred by the lender. (Leonard Decl. Ex. 1 [Note, para. 4, 6, 7].) Plaintiff failed to repay the loan as agreed or by its maturity date. (Leonard Decl., ¶¶ 12-18, 26.) The allegation that Leoanrd failed to provide accurate loan information and acted in a manner to prevent a loan refinance are not based on any identifiable contract provisions.

 

Plaintiff can also not establish her performance of obligations under the promissory note, which required her to make timely and full payment on the loan by October 1, 2025. (Leonard Decl. Ex. 1, para. 3.) Plaintiff failed to pay all sums due and the loan is fully matured and in default for nonpayment. (Leonard Decl,, ¶¶ 11-18, 26.)

 

Finally, Leonard cannot be liable for breaching a promissory note because a promissory note is one sided. It is a “form of negotiable instrument—an unconditional promise to pay money signed by the person undertaking to pay, payable on demand, or at a definite time.” (Saks v. Charity Mission Baptist Church (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1132.)

 

Therefore, Plaintiff is unlikely to prevail on the breach of contract claim.

 

2.      Fraudulent Concealment

 

The elements of a cause of action for fraud based on concealment are (1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage. (Bank of America Corp. v. Superior Court (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 862, 870.)

 

Based on the record before the Court, Leonard did not conceal anything related to the loan. Leonard sent loan accountings to Plaintiff on eight separate occasions starting in May 2025. (Leonard Decl., ¶¶ 20-24, 32, 35 and Ex. 6-10, 13 15.) Plaintiff also alleges that Leonard suppressed facts with the intention to prevent her from refinancing the loan. (FAC, ¶ 47.) However, the evidence before the Court indicates that Leonard wanted Plaintiff to refinance the loan, because that would have allowed him to get his money. (Leonard Decl., ¶ 45.)

 

It is also unclear how Plaintiff was damaged. Leonard was not aware of Plaintiff’s attempt to refinance the loan in or around March 2025. (Leonard Decl., ¶ 44.) Because it is difficult to understand how a borrower would refinance her loan without participation from the current lender, this allegation is dubious. (Ibid.)

 

Therefore, Plaintiff is unlikely to prevail on a fraudulent concealment claim.

 

3.      Negligent Misrepresentation

 

The elements of a cause of action for negligent misrepresentation are (1) the misrepresentation of a past or existing material fact, (2) without reasonable ground for believing it to be true, (3) with intent to induce another’s reliance or the fact misrepresented, (4) justifiable reliance on the misrepresentation, and (5) resulting damage. (Borman v. Brown (2021) 59 Cal.App.5th 1048, 1060.)

 

The FAC lists “false representations” (FAC, ¶ 54), but their asserted falsity is not supported by the record The evidence before the Court indicates that Plaintiff was in default, Plaintiff had failed to make required payments, the amounts stated by Leonard were accurate, and late fees and charges were properly due and owing under the Note. (See, generally, Leonard Decl.) Plaintiff’s disagreement with the numbers on the accounting does not mean that Leoanrd engaged in a misrepresentation.

 

Therefore, Plaintiff is unlikely to prevail on a negligent misrepresentation claim.

 

4.      Accounting

 

“An action for an accounting may be brought to compel the defendant to account to the plaintiff for money or property (1) where a fiduciary relationship exists between the parties, or (2) where, even though no fiduciary relationship exists, the accounts are so complicated that an ordinary legal action demanding a fixed sum is impracticable.” (Jolley v. Chase Home Finance, LLC (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 872, 910.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that Leonard must provide a loan accounting because she cannot ascertain what she owes him. (Complaint, ¶ 64.) Leonard has already provided an accounting eight separate times. (Leonard Decl., ¶¶ 20-24, 32, 35 and Ex. 6-10, 13 15.)

 

Therefore, Plaintiff is unlikely to prevail on a claim for an accounting.

 

5.      Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

 

An action for intentional infliction of emotional distress requires that (1) the defendant engages in extreme and outrageous conduct with the intent to cause, or with reckless disregard for the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff suffers extreme or severe emotional distress; and (3) the defendant’s extreme and outrageous conduct was the actual and proximate cause of the plaintiff’s extreme or severe emotional distress. (Ragland v. U.S. Bank National Assn. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 182, 204.)

 

The Complaint alleges that Leonard falsely asserted Plaintiff was in default under the Loan, failed to properly credit Plaintiff’s payments, withheld and concealed accurate loan information, provided misleading and inaccurate payoff demands, and imposed improper interest, fees and charges in an effort to extract additional money from Plaintiff and prevent her from refinancing the loan. (Complaint, ¶ 69.)

As the Court has previously said, all of the admissible evidence indicates that these allegations are not correct.

 

Finally, Leonard’s foreclosure proceedings against the Property are not actionable in tort. (Sierra-Bay Fed. Land Bank Association v. Superior Court (1991) 227 Cal.App.3d 318, 334 [“A commercial lender is not to be regarded as the guarantor of a borrower’s success and is not liable for the hardships which may befall a borrower. (Citation.) It is simply not tortious for a commercial lender to lend money, take collateral, or to foreclose on collateral when a debt is not paid.” (citations omitted)].)

 

Plaintiff is unlikely to prevail on an intentional infliction of emotional distress claim.

 

D.    Balance of Harms

 

For the second factor, the court must consider “the interim harm that the plaintiff would be likely to sustain if the injunction were denied as compared to the harm the defendant would be likely to suffer if the preliminary injunction were issued.”  (Smith v. Adventist Health System/West (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 729, 749.)  “Irreparable harm” generally means that the defendant’s act constitutes an actual or threatened injury to the personal or property rights of the plaintiff that cannot be compensated by a damages award.  (See Brownfield v. Daniel Freeman Marina Hospital (1989) 208 Cal.App.3d 405, 410.)  The showing of harm is even more demanding when a Plaintiff seeks injunctive relief against a public entity because “[t]here is a general rule against enjoining public officers or agencies from performing their duties.” (Tahoe Keys Property Owners’ Assn. v. State Water Resources Control Bd. (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1459, 1471.)

 

Plaintiff argues that if an injunction is denied, Defendants may sell the Subject Property to a third party, which would cause irreparable harm because it would extinguish Plaintiff’s interest. Plaintiff indicates that Defendants are unlikely to face harm because the deed of trust remains on the record and Defendants’ security interest is unimpaired; at most sale is delayed.

 

The Court does not agree that Plaintiff is likely to suffer from irreparable harm. To obtain the loan, Plaintiff certified under penalty of perjury that the loan proceeds would be used only for business purposes, and certified under penalty of criminal prosecution that the Property was held for investment purposes and that she would not occupy it. (Leonard Decl., ¶ 4.) The loss of investment property is compensable in money. (Civil Code § 3387.)

 

Plaintiff now claims the Property is her residence. (Choi Decl., ¶ 2.) This raises the question of whether Plaintiff, not Leonard, engaged in misrepresentations to secure the loan in the first place.

 

 Either way, it does not change the underlying facts: Leonard invested his own money in Plaintiff’s loan (Leonard Decl., ¶ 2.) The loan has been due in full for nearly a year, all the while Leonard is deprived of the money he relies upon. (Id., ¶¶ 26, 42.) Plaintiff has also failed to pay property taxes, placing the Property at risk of a tax lien and eventual sale. (Id., ¶ 43.)

 

CONCLUSION 

 

            The Court DENIES the application for a preliminary injunction.
