PLAINTIFFS’ (4) MOTIONS TO COMPEL RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR ADMISSION AND REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS, SET ONE, AND FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS

 

Motions filed on April 27, 2026.

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiffs Matthew Van Dam and David Van Dam

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendants Contemporary Services Corporation and Los Angeles Philharmonic Association

NOTICE: OK.

RELIEF REQUESTED: Orders compelling Defendants to serve further responses without objection to Requests for Admission and Requests for Production of Documents, Set One, and awarding $2,810.00 in monetary sanctions on each motion.

RULING: Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel CSC’s Further Responses to Requests for Admission, Set One (Res. ID: 119436528097), is GRANTED. The remaining motions are DENIED. All requests for monetary sanctions are DENIED.

 

BACKGROUND

 

On October 27, 2023, Matthew Van Dam and David Van Dam (collectively, Plaintiffs) filed the Complaint against Contemporary Services Corporation (CSC) and Los Angeles Philharmonic Association (LAPA), asserting causes of action for assault and battery, false imprisonment, and intentional infliction of emotional distress.

 

On April 27, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a motion concerning CSC’s responses to Requests for Admission, Set One, supported by the declaration of Geoffrey Bosmans, a separate statement challenging Requests for Admission Nos. 19 through 22, and exhibits.

 

On April 27, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a motion concerning LAPA’s responses to Requests for Admission, Set One, supported by the declaration of Geoffrey Bosmans, a separate statement challenging Request for Admission No. 5, and exhibits.

 

On April 27, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a motion concerning CSC’s responses to Requests for Production of Documents, Set One, supported by the declaration of Geoffrey Bosmans, a separate statement addressing Requests for Production Nos. 1 through 18, and exhibits.

 

On April 27, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a motion concerning LAPA’s responses to Requests for Production of Documents, Set One, supported by the declaration of Geoffrey Bosmans, a separate statement addressing Requests for Production Nos. 1, 3 through 12, and 15 through 18, and exhibits.

 

On July 22, 2026, CSC and LAPA filed oppositions and opposing separate statements to each motion. No timely replies were filed.

 

DISCUSSION

Procedural Issues


Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.280 applies only where the responding party “fails to serve a timely response” to requests for admission. In that circumstance, the responding party waives its objections, and the requesting party may move to have the matters deemed admitted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subds. (a), (b).) Section 2033.290 applies “[o]n receipt of a response” and authorizes a motion to compel a further response where an answer is evasive or incomplete or an objection is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.290, subd. (a).)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.300 likewise applies only where a party “fails to serve a timely response” to an inspection demand. In that circumstance, the party waives its objections, and the demanding party may move for an order compelling a response. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subds. (a), (b).) Section 2031.310 applies “[o]n receipt of a response” where a statement of compliance is incomplete, a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive, or an objection is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).) Section 2031.320 applies only where a party, after serving a response, “fails to permit the inspection, copying, testing, or sampling in accordance with that party’s statement of compliance.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.320, subd. (a).)

 

Here, Plaintiffs invoke the wrong statutory procedures across all four motions. The RFA motions proceed under section 2033.280 and seek responses without objection, even though they acknowledge that CSC and LAPA served responses on December 3, 2025. Their passing reference to section 2033.290 incorrectly describes that statute as governing interrogatories and a failure to respond. The separate statements, however, challenge identified responses and seek further responses. (CSC RFA Mot. at 1-6; LAPA RFA Mot. at 1-6.)

 

The RPD motions contain the same defect. They proceed under section 2031.300 and seek responses without objection, although they acknowledge that Defendants served responses on March 2 and March 10, 2026. Although captioned as motions to compel “compliance,” the papers do not invoke section 2031.320 or identify a failure to produce documents in accordance with a statement of compliance. Instead, they argue that the failure to provide promised supplemental responses resulted in waiver, which does not establish relief under either section 2031.300 or section 2031.320. (CSC RPD Mot. at 1-6; LAPA RPD Mot. at 1-6.)

 

Plaintiffs have not established that Defendants’ initial responses were untimely. Plaintiffs acknowledge that Defendants received extensions before serving the RFA responses on December 3, 2025, and the RPD responses on March 2 and March 10, 2026, but do not identify the extended deadlines or show that the responses were served after those deadlines. The failure to provide later supplemental responses does not result in waiver under sections 2033.280 or 2031.300.

 

Because the separate statements identify the particular responses being challenged and Defendants addressed those disputes on the merits, the Court construes the RFA motions under section 2033.290 and the RPD motions under section 2031.310 as motions to compel further.

 

Motion to Compel CSC’s Further Responses to Requests for Admission Nos. 19 Through 22

 

A party may request an admission concerning a fact, an opinion relating to fact, or an application of law to fact. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.010.) Each answer must be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available permits. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.220, subd. (a).) If only part of a request is objectionable, the remainder must be answered, and each objection must clearly state its specific ground. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.230, subds. (a), (b).) The Court may compel a further response where an answer is evasive or incomplete or an objection is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.290, subd. (a).) The responding party bears the burden of justifying its objections. (Coy v. Superior Court (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220-221.)

 

Requests Nos. 19 and 20 ask whether the CSC personnel who restrained Matthew Van Dam and David Van Dam, respectively, were acting within the course and scope of their employment with CSC. Requests Nos. 21 and 22 ask whether those personnel were acting within the course and scope of an agency relationship with Hollywood Bowl. (Ex. B at 5.) CSC served materially identical objection-only responses to all four requests and advances the same arguments in opposition. (Ex. C at 16-18; CSC Opp. Sep. Stmt. at 4-20.)

 

An ambiguity objection is proper only where the request is so ambiguous that the responding party cannot in good faith frame an intelligent reply. A response is required where the nature of the information sought is apparent. (Cembrook v. Superior Court (1961) 56 Cal.2d 423, 428-430; Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 783.) Here, each request identifies the personnel by their affiliation with CSC, the alleged restraint of a named Plaintiff, and the employment or agency relationship to be admitted or denied. CSC’s earlier responses also identify Plaintiffs’ October 7, 2023 removal and restraint by CSC personnel. (Ex. C at 5-7.) The omission of individual names or repetition of the incident date does not prevent a substantive response. Nor do the terms “personnel,” “restrained,” “course and scope of employment,” or “agency” render the requests unintelligible. The vagueness, ambiguity, and indeterminate-time-and-scope objections are OVERRULED.

 

CSC does not explain how the requests are overbroad. Its undue burden and harassment objections are unsupported by evidence showing the amount of work required to answer or that the burden would be incommensurate with the result sought. (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 549-550; West Pico Furniture Co. v. Superior Court (1961) 56 Cal.2d 407, 417-418.) Lack of foundation or an assumed factual premise does not justify an objection-only response. (West Pico, supra, 56 Cal.2d at p. 421.) If CSC disputes that its personnel restrained either Plaintiff, it may deny or qualify the request. If it cannot admit or deny after reasonable inquiry, it must state that the information known or readily obtainable remains insufficient. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.220, subd. (c); Cembrook, supra, 56 Cal.2d at pp. 429-430; Grace v. Mansourian (2015) 240 Cal.App.4th 523, 529-530.) The overbreadth, burden, harassment, foundation, and speculation objections are OVERRULED.

 

The attorney-client and work-product objections are OVERRULED. CSC expressly asserted those protections as required by section 2033.230, subdivision (b), but it has not established that they apply to the information sought. The attorney-client privilege protects confidential communications, not the underlying facts. (Aerojet-General Corp. v. Transport Indemnity Insurance Co. (1993) 18 Cal.App.4th 996, 1004.) Work-product protection requires a preliminary or foundational showing that answering would reveal counsel’s tactics, impressions, or evaluation of the case or permit opposing counsel to take undue advantage of counsel’s efforts. (Coito v. Superior Court (2012) 54 Cal.4th 480, 502; Curtis v. Superior Court (2021) 62 Cal.App.5th 453, 470-472.) These requests seek CSC’s admission or denial concerning the employment or agency status of the personnel involved. CSC’s assertion that counsel might be consulted in formulating the responses does not establish that the responses themselves would disclose privileged communications or protected work product.

 

The premature expert-discovery objection is OVERRULED. A party may not avoid responding merely because a request may call for expert opinion. Because requests for admission are not limited to matters within the responding party’s personal knowledge, the party must make a reasonable investigation before answering. (Bloxham v. Saldinger (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 729, 751-752; Grace, supra, 240 Cal.App.4th at p. 530.) The requests seek CSC’s position concerning the employment and agency relationships at issue, not disclosure of an expert’s identity, report, communications, or opinions. CSC’s assertion that expert input may bear on its responses does not justify withholding substantive answers.

 

The legal-conclusion objection is OVERRULED. Section 2033.010 expressly permits requests concerning an application of law to fact. This includes whether a third person was an “authorized agent” or acted within the “course and scope of employment.” (Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶ 8:1300.) Requests Nos. 19 through 22 ask CSC to apply those standards to the CSC personnel who allegedly restrained each Plaintiff and to the employment and agency relationships at issue. Morrow v. Los Angeles Unified School District (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1424 is inapplicable here.

 

CSC’s objection under section 2033.060, subdivision (d), is OVERRULED. That subdivision prohibits unauthorized prefaces and instructions, while subdivision (e) contemplates specially defined terms. In any event, Requests Nos. 19 through 22 are full and understandable without reference to the definitions. Each request independently identifies the personnel, conduct, Plaintiff, and employment or agency relationship at issue.

 

CSC also incorporated its Preliminary Statement and General Objections into each response but does not identify any additional objection or explain how it applies to these requests. CSC therefore has not met its burden of justifying those incorporated objections. The incorporated objections are OVERRULED.

 

The motion is GRANTED as to Requests for Admission Nos. 19 through 22. CSC shall serve verified further responses complying with section 2033.220.

 

Motion to Compel LAPA’s Further Responses: Request for Admission No. 5

 

Each request for admission must be separately set forth and full and complete in itself. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.060, subds. (c), (d).) If only part of a request is objectionable, the remainder must be answered. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.230, subd. (a).)

 

Here, Request for Admission No. 5 asks LAPA to admit that it hired or contracted CSC to provide “those security services.” (Ex. B at 3.) The phrase “those security services” has no antecedent within Request No. 5 and requires reference to Request No. 4, which identifies private security services at the Hollywood Bowl for the October 7, 2023 concert. (Ibid.) Request No. 5 therefore is not full and complete in itself as required by section 2033.060, subdivision (d). LAPA expressly asserted that objection in its response. (Ex. C at 7.)

 

Because “those security services” identifies the subject of the requested admission, there is no separable, non-objectionable remainder to answer under section 2033.230, subdivision (a).

LAPA’s objection under section 2033.060, subdivision (d), is SUSTAINED.

 

The motion is DENIED as to Request for Admission No. 5. The remaining objections are not reached.

 

Motions to Compel Further Responses to Requests for Production of Documents

 

Unlike a motion to compel further responses to requests for admission, a motion to compel further responses to requests for production must be supported by specific facts showing good cause for the discovery sought. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) A party may move for further responses where a statement of compliance is incomplete, a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive, or an objection is without merit or too general. (Id., subd. (a).)

 

To establish good cause, the moving party must identify a disputed fact of consequence in the action and explain how the discovery sought will tend to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will do so. (Digital Music News LLC v. Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 216, 224, overruled on another ground in Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 557, fn. 8.) This burden is not onerous and may be satisfied by a fact-specific showing of relevance. (TBG Ins. Services Corp. v. Superior Court (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.) Once the moving party demonstrates good cause for the discovery, the burden is on the responding party to justify any objection or failure to fully respond to the inspection demand.  (Coy v Superior Court (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220.)

 

Here, although the burden is low, Plaintiffs have not made the required fact-specific showing of good cause. Each memorandum identifies Plaintiffs’ claims and states generally that the requested responses are “essential” to evaluating Defendants’ allegations and defenses and preparing for trial. The memoranda, separate statements, and declarations do not identify a particular disputed fact or explain how any category of requested documents would tend to prove or disprove that fact. The separate statements principally identify documents or information that were not produced and explain why Plaintiffs believe the responses or productions are incomplete. The motions otherwise proceed under section 2031.300 and argue that Defendants failed to provide promised supplemental responses and thereby waived their objections. (CSC RPD Mot. at 3-6; LAPA RPD Mot. at 3-6.)

 

Even assuming Plaintiffs have satisfied the minimal good-cause requirement, they have not established a basis for compelling further responses under section 2031.310, subdivision (a).

 

As to CSC Requests Nos. 1, 2, 4, 7 through 12, 17, and 18, CSC stated that, following a diligent search and reasonable inquiry, it possessed no responsive or no nonprivileged responsive documents. Where documents were withheld, CSC identified them in a privilege log. As to Requests Nos. 3, 5, 6, and 13 through 15, CSC identified the documents produced or to be produced. Plaintiffs principally contend that additional documents should exist because of CSC’s reporting policies or because the documents produced do not identify the particular persons or contain the information Plaintiffs expected. CSC responds that it has identified all responsive documents within its possession, custody, or control and that no additional responsive documents exist. (CSC Opp. Sep. Stmt. at 2-45, 49-54.) Plaintiffs’ belief that additional or more informative documents should exist does not establish that CSC’s substantive responses are incomplete or evasive.

 

CSC’s response to Request No. 16 does not warrant a different result. That request broadly seeks personnel files, training records, and disciplinary records of any CSC personnel involved in the incident. CSC objected based on overbreadth, confidentiality, and privacy, stated that it could not comply, described the training provided to its employees, and maintained that it had identified all responsive documents. (CSC Opp. Sep. Stmt. at 45-48.)

 

LAPA’s responses present the same issue. As to Requests Nos. 4, 6 through 9, and 11, LAPA stated that, following a diligent search and reasonable inquiry, it possessed no responsive or no nonprivileged responsive documents. As to Requests Nos. 1, 3, 5, 10, 12, and 15 through 17, LAPA identified documents produced, previously produced, or to be produced. Plaintiffs again argue principally that additional documents should exist or that the identified documents do not fully provide the information sought. LAPA maintains that it has identified all responsive documents within its possession, custody, or control. (LAPA Opp. Sep. Stmt. at 2-40.) When a party objects but also provides complete substantive responses, nothing remains to address on a motion to compel. (American Federation of State, County & Municipal Employees v. Metropolitan Water Dist. (2005) 126 Cal.App.4th 247, 269.)

 

Plaintiffs’ challenge to the privilege logs does not warrant a further response. The logs identify the date, document type, author, and privilege or protection asserted. The Court finds the information presented sufficient.

 

To the extent Plaintiffs contend that Defendants failed to produce documents expressly identified in statements of compliance, that issue was not presented under section 2031.320. To the extent Plaintiffs believe other, more specifically identifiable records exist, that belief does not establish that the present responses are incomplete or evasive. Plaintiffs may pursue appropriately tailored follow-up discovery directed to any particular documents or custodians identified through subsequent discovery.

 

Accordingly, both motions to compel further responses to Requests for Production of Documents are DENIED. No relief is ordered as to LAPA Requests Nos. 2, 13, and 14 because Plaintiffs did not address those requests in their separate statement.

 

Sanctions

 

The Court shall impose a monetary sanction against a party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further responses unless the Court finds substantial justification or other circumstances making sanctions unjust. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2033.290, subd. (d), 2031.310, subd. (h).)

 

Here, Plaintiffs request $2,810 in connection with each motion. Plaintiffs proceeded principally under the no-response statutes even though Defendants had served substantive responses. Plaintiffs prevailed only on the motion concerning CSC’s requests for admission, and CSC raised a reasonable dispute concerning the form and scope of those requests. Plaintiffs did not prevail on the LAPA request-for-admission motion or either production-demand motion.

 

The Court finds substantial justification and that an award would be unjust. All requests for monetary sanctions are DENIED.

 

CONCLUSION

Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel CSC’s Further Responses to Requests for Admission, Set One (Res. ID: 119436528097), is GRANTED. CSC shall serve verified further responses to Requests for Admission Nos. 19 through 22 within 30 days.

 

Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel LAPA’s Further Response to Request for Admission No. 5, Set One (Res. ID: 700759251963), is DENIED.

 

Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel CSC’s Further Responses to Requests for Production of Documents, Set One (Res. ID: 063851897580), is DENIED.

 

Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel LAPA’s Further Responses to Requests for Production of Documents, Set One (Res. ID: 760170050902), is DENIED.

 

All requests for monetary sanctions are DENIED.

DEFENDANTS’ MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT OR, IN THE ALTERNATIVE, SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

Motion filed on January 26, 2026.

MOVING PARTY: PHH Mortgage Corporation (PHH) and Deutsche Bank National Trust Company, as Trustee for Argent Securities Inc., Asset-Backed Pass-Through Certificates, Series 2005-W4 (Deutsche Bank as Trustee) (collectively, Defendants)

RESPONDING PARTY: Jon F. Nesel (Plaintiff)

NOTICE: OK.

RELIEF REQUESTED: Summary judgment on the Complaint or, in the alternative, summary adjudication of each cause of action.

RULING: Granted. Summary judgment is granted in favor of Defendants and against Plaintiff.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff filed the Complaint on June 9, 2025, asserting causes of action for violation of Civil Code sections 2923.5 and 2924.9, wrongful foreclosure, violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200, and cancellation of written instruments. Defendants filed a verified answer on July 21, 2025.

 

Defendants filed the instant Motion for Summary Judgment on January 26, 2026, with a separate statement, the declarations of Kevin Flannigan and Neil J. Cooper, an appendix of evidence, a notice of lodging of three audio recordings, and a request for judicial notice. No timely opposition was filed. Defendants filed notice of non-opposition on July 21, 2026.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

Defendants’ request for judicial notice is GRANTED as to Exhibits 2 through 5, 23, and 26 through 32. (Evid. Code, § 452, subds. (c), (d); Fontenot v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 256, 265.) Judicial notice is taken of the recorded instruments and the bankruptcy filings and orders, but not of the truth of disputed factual assertions contained therein.

 

UNTIMELY OPPOSITION

 

Plaintiff’s opposition was due on July 15, 2026, but was filed on July 30, 2026, without leave of court or a showing of good cause. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (b)(2).) Defendants filed a reply addressing the opposition that same day. The Court exercises its discretion to consider both filings.

 

Plaintiff admits UMFs 1 through 3, 7, and 9. Although Plaintiff purports to dispute UMFs 4 through 6 and 8, his separate statement cites only allegations in the Complaint or no supporting evidence. Plaintiff also identifies no additional material facts. Although the pleadings define the scope of the issues presented on summary judgment, a party’s own allegations, even in a verified pleading, do not constitute evidence creating a triable issue. (Hutton v. Fidelity National Title Co. (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 486, 493; College Hospital, Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 720, fn. 7; Roman v. BRE Properties, Inc. (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 1040, 1054.) Once a moving defendant meets its initial burden, the plaintiff must set forth specific facts supported by evidence and may not rely on the allegations or denials of the complaint. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subds. (b)(3), (p)(2).) Plaintiff’s unsupported responses therefore do not create a triable issue.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

In reviewing a motion for summary judgment, courts must apply a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent’s claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.”  (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.)

 

“[T]he initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.”  (Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) A defendant moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) A moving defendant need not conclusively negate an element of plaintiff’s cause of action.  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854.)

 

To meet this burden of showing a cause of action cannot be established, a defendant must show not only “that the plaintiff does not possess needed evidence” but also that “the plaintiff cannot reasonably obtain needed evidence.” (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 854.) It is insufficient for the defendant to merely point out the absence of evidence. (Gaggero v. Yura (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 884, 891.)  The defendant “must also produce evidence that the plaintiff cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support his or her claim.” (Ibid.)¿The supporting evidence can be in the form of affidavits, declarations, admissions, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and matters of which judicial notice may be taken. (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 855.)

 

“Once the defendant . . . has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)  The plaintiff may not merely rely on allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists, but instead, “shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action.”  (Ibid.)  “If the plaintiff cannot do so, summary judgment should be granted.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467.)

 

DISCUSSION

 

Undisputed Material Facts

 

On or about September 27, 2005, Plaintiff and his late wife obtained a $295,000 loan (Loan) from Argent Mortgage Company, LLC, secured by a deed of trust against real property at 15505 La Valle Street, Sylmar, California 91342 (Property). (UMF 1.) The Loan was transferred to Deutsche Bank as Trustee before PHH began servicing it, and the beneficial interest in the deed of trust was assigned to Deutsche Bank as Trustee in 2009. (UMF 2.)

 

Plaintiff failed to make the December 1, 2023 and later monthly payments. (UMF 3.) On January 10 and February 23, 2024, PHH sent Plaintiff correspondence concerning the default and loss-mitigation options. The February 23 letter also provided HUD housing-counseling information. (UMF 4.)

 

On March 20, 2024, PHH spoke with Plaintiff concerning the default, the amount required to reinstate the Loan, and loss-mitigation options. PHH asked about Plaintiff’s household income, the cause of the default, and whether the hardship had been resolved. PHH informed Plaintiff that it would review the account for available loss-mitigation options and send him the results. (UMF 5.) PHH conducted another financial interview on April 4, 2024. (UMF 6.)

 

A Notice of Default was recorded on May 31, 2024. (UMF 7.) On June 6, 2024, PHH sent Plaintiff a letter stating that he might be evaluated for a foreclosure-prevention alternative, that an application was required, and how he could obtain one. (UMF 8.) A trustee’s sale was held on May 6, 2025, and the Property was sold to a third party. (UMF 9.)

 

Deutsche Bank as Trustee

 

The pleadings define the scope of the issues on summary judgment. (Hinesley, supra, 135 Cal.App.4th at p. 294.)

 

The Complaint names Deutsche Bank as Trustee as a defendant and alleges that it owns the beneficial interest in the deed of trust. Each cause of action, however, is expressly pleaded only against “PHH Defendant.” (Compl. ¶¶ 18-54.) No cause of action is asserted against Deutsche Bank as Trustee.

 

Plaintiff requests leave to amend, asserting that the references to “PHH Defendant” may reflect inadvertent drafting. An opposing party may not defeat summary judgment by advancing an unpleaded theory and should seek leave to amend before filing its opposition. (Champlin/GEI Wind Holdings, LLC v. Avery (2023) 92 Cal.App.5th 218, 224-225.) Here, Plaintiff first requested amendment in an opposition filed three court days before the hearing, submitted no proposed amended pleading, identified no proposed new allegations, and offered no explanation for failing to seek amendment earlier. Moreover, Plaintiff seeks to assert the same claims based on the same alleged statutory violations addressed below and identifies no additional facts or evidence supporting those claims. The request for leave to amend is denied.

 

Summary judgment is granted in favor of Deutsche Bank as Trustee.

 

First Cause of Action for Violation of Civil Code Section 2923.5

 

The version of Civil Code section 2923.5[1] in effect when the Notice of Default was recorded prohibited recordation until “[e]ither 30 days after initial contact is made as required by paragraph (2) or 30 days after satisfying the due diligence requirements.” (Civ. Code, former § 2923.5, subd. (a)(1)(A).) It required the mortgage servicer to “contact the borrower in person or by telephone in order to assess the borrower’s financial situation and explore options for the borrower to avoid foreclosure.” (Id., subd. (a)(2).) During the initial contact, the servicer was required to advise the borrower of the right to request a subsequent meeting and, if requested, to schedule that meeting within 14 days. The servicer was also required to provide HUD’s toll-free housing-counseling number. (Ibid.) The assessment of the borrower’s financial situation and discussion of options could occur during the initial contact or at the subsequent meeting. (Ibid.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that PHH made no contact and sent no communications concerning foreclosure alternatives before recording the Notice of Default. (Compl. ¶¶ 14, 20-21.) The evidence establishes otherwise. PHH’s February 23, 2024 letter advised Plaintiff of available mortgage-assistance options, identified his designated relationship manager, and provided HUD’s housing-counseling number. Plaintiff acknowledged receiving that letter. (UMF 4; Flannigan Decl. ¶ 15, Ex. 19; Cooper Decl., Ex. 33 [Nesel Depo. at 24:8-14].)

 

During March 20 and April 4, 2024 calls, PHH asked about Plaintiff’s income, the cause and expected duration of his hardship, and his ability to continue or increase his monthly payments. PHH identified forbearance, a repayment plan, loan modification, short sale, and deed in lieu of foreclosure as possible options. (UMFs 5-6; Flannigan Decl. ¶¶ 16-17; Notice of Lodging, Exs. 20-21.) The March 20 call therefore included a financial assessment and discussion of foreclosure alternatives. These contacts occurred more than 30 days before the Notice of Default was recorded on May 31, 2024. (UMF 7.) Plaintiff also admitted that he received correspondence and spoke with PHH several times before recordation. (Cooper Decl., Ex. 33 [Nesel Depo. at 25:14-23, 26:18-21, 48:11-50:10].)

 

PHH has therefore met its initial burden of establishing compliance with former section 2923.5, subdivision (a)(2). Although Plaintiff disputes whether the communications satisfied the statute, his separate statement cites only allegations in the Complaint and no evidence contradicting the letters or recorded calls. Plaintiff’s unsupported assertions do not create a triable issue. Summary adjudication is granted as to the first cause of action.


Second Cause of Action for Violation of Civil Code Section 2924.9

 

Unless the borrower has previously exhausted the applicable first-lien loan-modification process, Civil Code section 2924.9 requires a mortgage servicer that offers one or more foreclosure-prevention alternatives to send the borrower, within five business days after recording a notice of default, written notice that the borrower may be evaluated for a foreclosure-prevention alternative. (Civ. Code, § 2924.9, subd. (a)(1).) The notice must also state whether an application is required and identify the means and process by which the borrower may obtain one. (Id., subd. (a)(2)-(3).)

 

Here, the Notice of Default was recorded on May 31, 2024. (UMF 7.) On June 6, 2024, the fourth business day after recordation, PHH sent Plaintiff a letter stating that he may be evaluated for a foreclosure-prevention alternative, that a completed application was required, and that he could obtain an application through PHH’s website or by telephone. (Flannigan Decl. ¶ 19, Ex. 24.) Plaintiff testified that he recalled receiving the June 6 letter. (Cooper Decl., Ex. 33 [Nesel Depo. at 26:22-27:5].)

 

This evidence establishes that PHH timely provided each item required by section 2924.9, subdivision (a). PHH has therefore met its initial burden. Although Plaintiff disputes the sufficiency of the letter, his separate statement cites no evidence that it omitted any of the three statutory requirements. Plaintiff further argues that the notice was required to identify the particular foreclosure-prevention alternatives for which he might qualify, but section 2924.9 imposes no such requirement. Plaintiff raises no triable issue. Summary adjudication is granted as to the second cause of action.

 

Third Cause of Action for Wrongful Foreclosure

 

A wrongful foreclosure claim requires that “(1) the trustee or mortgagee caused an illegal, fraudulent, or willfully oppressive sale of real property pursuant to a power of sale in a mortgage or deed of trust; (2) the party attacking the sale was prejudiced or harmed; and (3) in cases where the trustor or mortgagor challenges the sale, the trustor or mortgagor tendered the amount of the secured indebtedness or was excused from tendering.” (Sciarratta v. U.S. Bank National Assn. (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 552, 561-562.) “[M]ere technical violations of the foreclosure process will not give rise to a tort claim; the foreclosure must have been entirely unauthorized on the facts of the case.” (Id. at p. 562.)

 

Here, Plaintiff predicates the claim on PHH’s alleged violations of Civil Code sections 2923.5 and 2924.9. (Compl. ¶¶ 27-30.) As discussed above, PHH establishes compliance with both statutes. To the extent the claim also rests on the incorporated allegation that the Notice of Default was void because the accompanying declaration was not executed under penalty of perjury, that theory fails because the declaration required by section 2923.5 need not be executed under penalty of perjury. (Compl. ¶ 13; Mabry v. Superior Court (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 208, 233-234.)

 

PHH has therefore met its initial burden of negating the alleged illegality underlying the sale. In opposition, Plaintiff relies on the same asserted statutory violations and submits no evidence establishing any independent basis on which the sale was illegal, fraudulent, willfully oppressive, or entirely unauthorized. Plaintiff raises no triable issue. Because the first element cannot be established, the Court need not address tender.

 

Summary adjudication is granted as to the third cause of action.

 

 

Fourth Cause of Action for Violation of Business and Professions Code Section 17200

 

The Unfair Competition Law (UCL) prohibits “any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200.) Its remedies, however, are limited. “Prevailing plaintiffs are generally limited to injunctive relief and restitution” and “may not receive damages.” (Zhang v. Superior Court (2013) 57 Cal.4th 364, 371.) “The object of restitution is to restore the status quo by returning to the plaintiff funds in which he or she has an ownership interest,” and “nonrestitutionary disgorgement of profits is not an available remedy in an individual action under the UCL.” (Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1134, 1149, 1152.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that PHH engaged in unlawful, unfair, and fraudulent practices by failing to comply with Civil Code sections 2923.5 and 2924.9, failing to provide meaningful loss-mitigation assistance, ignoring his communications, prolonging the process to accumulate interest and fees, providing misleading information, and violating title 15 United States Code section 1641(g). (Compl. ¶¶ 34-47.)

 

As discussed above, PHH shows compliance with Civil Code sections 2923.5 and 2924.9. PHH’s evidence also show that Plaintiff stopped making monthly payments beginning December 1, 2023, and that PHH thereafter sent him written information concerning loss-mitigation options and conducted two financial interviews concerning his income, hardship, and available alternatives. (UMFs 3-6.) This evidence negates the pleaded allegations that PHH ignored Plaintiff, provided no loss-mitigation assistance, or purposefully prolonged the process while withholding information. The Loan was assigned to Deutsche Bank as Trustee in 2009, before PHH began servicing it. Because title 15 United States Code section 1641(g) imposes its notice obligation on the new owner or assignee of the debt, the alleged violation does not provide an unlawful predicate against PHH. (UMF 2.)

 

PHH has therefore met its initial burden as to the pleaded unlawful, unfair, and fraudulent theories. In opposition, Plaintiff relies on the asserted violations of Civil Code sections 2923.5 and 2924.9 and states generally that unfair or fraudulent conduct presents a factual question. He cites no evidence contradicting PHH’s communications or identifying any independent false statement, intentional delay, or other unfair or fraudulent practice. Plaintiff therefore raises no triable issue as to UCL liability.

 

Plaintiff argues that Defendants conflate standing with the availability of restitution. The two inquiries are distinct, and ineligibility for restitution does not defeat standing. (Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court (2011) 51 Cal.4th 310, 336-337.) Even assuming Plaintiff has standing, however, standing alone does not establish entitlement to relief. Restitution requires both that Plaintiff lost money or property and that PHH acquired it by means of the alleged unfair competition. (Id. at p. 336.)

 

The Complaint alleges continuing interest charges, late fees, foreclosure costs, and diminished equity and seeks restitution and disgorgement. (Compl. ¶¶ 40, 48, 50.) In opposition, Plaintiff argues that these amounts are recoverable as restitution but cites only the allegations of the Complaint. Plaintiff submits no evidence identifying any such amount he paid to PHH, any money or property PHH acquired from him through the challenged practices, or any causal connection between such an acquisition and the alleged unfair competition. His assertion that the record “does not foreclose” such losses does not create a triable issue. Plaintiff therefore fails to show that the claimed losses are recoverable as restitution rather than compensatory damages.

 

Prospective injunctive relief is likewise unavailable. “An injunction must seek to prevent harm, not to punish the wrongdoer,” and should not issue “in the absence of any evidence that the acts are likely to be repeated in the future.” (Feitelberg v. Credit Suisse First Boston, LLC (2005) 134 Cal.App.4th 997, 1012.) The trustee’s sale was completed, and the Property was sold to a third party. (UMF 9.) There is no continuing foreclosure activity involving Plaintiff, the Loan, or the Property to enjoin.

 

PHH has met its initial burden of showing that Plaintiff cannot establish a UCL violation. Plaintiff raises no triable issue and identifies no available UCL remedy supported by evidence. Summary adjudication is granted as to the fourth cause of action.

 

Fifth Cause of Action for Cancellation of Written Instrument

 

Civil Code section 3412 permits cancellation of a written instrument where “there is a reasonable apprehension that if left outstanding it may cause serious injury to a person against whom it is void or voidable.”

 

Plaintiff seeks cancellation of the Notice of Default and Notice of Trustee’s Sale based on PHH’s alleged violations of Civil Code sections 2923.5 and 2924.9. (Compl. ¶¶ 51-54.) As discussed above, PHH negates those alleged violations. To the extent Plaintiff also relies on the incorporated allegation concerning the absence of penalty-of-perjury language, the declaration required by section 2923.5 need not be executed under penalty of perjury. (Compl. ¶ 13; Mabry, supra, 185 Cal.App.4th at pp. 233-234.) The pleaded grounds therefore do not establish that either instrument is void or voidable.

 

Plaintiff raises no triable issue. Summary adjudication is granted as to the fifth cause of action.

 

CONCLUSION

 

Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment is granted. Summary judgment is granted in favor of PHH Mortgage Corporation and Deutsche Bank National Trust Company, as Trustee for Argent Securities Inc., Asset-Backed Pass-Through Certificates, Series 2005-W4, and against Plaintiff Jon F. Nesel.

 

Defense is ordered to file and serve a proposed judgment within 10 days of this order.

BACKGROUND

 

On September 5, 2025, Plaintiff Bryan Cioffi filed this action. On March 17, 2026, the Court set the Final Status Conference for October 29, 2027, and the Jury Trial for November 8, 2027. On May 26, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel, Arpi Galstian, Esq., filed the instant motion to be relieved as counsel on Judicial Council forms MC-051, MC-052, and MC-053. On July 10, 2026, Defendant filed a motion to compel Plaintiff’s deposition, which is set for hearing on September 10, 2026. No timely opposition to the motion to be relieved was filed.

 

DISCUSSION

 

The Court has discretion to allow an attorney to withdraw, and such a motion should be granted, provided that there is no prejudice to the client and it does not disrupt the orderly process of justice. (See Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 904, 915 (Ramirez); People v. Prince (1968) 268 Cal.App.2d 398, 403–407.)

 

A motion to be relieved as counsel must be made on Judicial Council Form MC-051 (Notice of Motion and Motion), MC-052 (Declaration), and MC-053 (Proposed Order). (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subds. (a), (c), (e).) The requisite forms must be served “on the client and on all other parties who have appeared in the case.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (d).) The proposed order must specify all hearing dates scheduled in the action, including the trial date, if known. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (e).)

 

Grounds for permitting an attorney to withdraw from representation include a breakdown in the attorney-client relationship. (Estate of Falco (1987) 188 Cal.App.3d 1004, 1014 (Falco).)

 

Here, counsel states that there has been an irreparable breakdown in the attorney-client relationship involving a lack of trust, communication, and cooperation, which has rendered continued representation unreasonably difficult. The Court finds counsel has shown sufficient grounds to withdraw.

 

However, no proof of service has been filed establishing service of the moving papers on Plaintiff and all other parties who have appeared in the action, as required by California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362(d). In addition, the MC-052 and proposed MC-053 do not reflect the September 10, 2026 hearing on Defendant’s motion to compel Plaintiff’s deposition. The proposed MC-053 therefore does not identify all currently scheduled hearings as required by rule 3.1362(e). A continuance is warranted to permit counsel to provide updated forms and establish proper service.

 

CONCLUSION

Counsel must also file proof of service establishing service of the updated forms on Plaintiff and all other parties who have appeared in the action.
