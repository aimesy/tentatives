LEGAL STANDARDS

“The court may, for good cause, grant any defendant a protective order requiring the plaintiff to produce evidence of a prima facie case of liability for damages pursuant to Section 3294, prior to the introduction of evidence of:  ¶  (1) The profits the defendant has gained by virtue of the wrongful course of conduct of the nature and type shown by the evidence.  ¶  (2) The financial condition of the defendant.”  (Civ. Code, § 3295, subd. (a).)

“No pretrial discovery by the plaintiff shall be permitted with respect to the evidence referred to in paragraphs (1) and (2) of subdivision (a) unless the court enters an order permitting such discovery pursuant to this subdivision.  However, the plaintiff may subpoena documents or witnesses to be available at the trial for the purpose of establishing the profits or financial condition referred to in subdivision (a), and the defendant may be required to identify documents in the defendant’s possession which are relevant and admissible for that purpose and the witnesses employed by or related to the defendant who would be most competent to testify to those facts.  Upon motion by the plaintiff supported by appropriate affidavits and after a hearing, if the court deems a hearing to be necessary, the court may at any time enter an order permitting the discovery otherwise prohibited by this subdivision if the court finds, on the basis of the supporting and opposing affidavits presented, that the plaintiff has established that there is a substantial probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim pursuant to Section 3294.  Such order shall not be considered to be a determination on the merits of the claim or any defense thereto and shall not be given in evidence or referred to at the trial.”  (Civ. Code, § 3295, subd. (c), emphasis added.)

“The court shall restrict the frequency or extent of use of a discovery method provided in Section 2019.010 if it determines either of the following:  ¶  (1) The discovery sought is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative, or is obtainable from some other source that is more convenient, less burdensome, or less expensive.  ¶  (2) The selected method of discovery is unduly burdensome or expensive, taking into account the needs of the case, the amount in controversy, and the importance of the issues at stake in the litigation.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2019.030, subd. (a).)  “The court may make these determinations pursuant to a motion for a protective order by a party or other affected person.  This motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2019.030, subd. (b).)

“The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion for a protective order, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2019.030, subd. (c).)

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.090, “[w]hen interrogatories have been propounded, the responding party, and any other party or affected natural person or organization may promptly move for a protective order.  This motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.  ¶  (b) The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other natural person or organization from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense.  This protective order may include, but is not limited to, one or more of the following directions:  ¶  (1) That the set of interrogatories, or particular interrogatories in the set, need not be answered.  ¶  (2) That, contrary to the representations made in a declaration submitted under Section 2030.050, the number of specially prepared interrogatories is unwarranted.  ¶  (3) That the time specified in Section 2030.260 to respond to the set of interrogatories, or to particular interrogatories in the set, be extended.  ¶  (4) That the response be made only on specified terms and conditions.  ¶  (5) That the method of discovery be an oral deposition instead of interrogatories to a party.  ¶  (6) That a trade secret or other confidential research, development, or commercial information not be disclosed or be disclosed only in a certain way.  ¶  (7) That some or all of the answers to interrogatories be sealed and thereafter opened only on order of the court.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.090, subds. (a)-(b).)

“The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion for a protective order, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.090, subd. (c).)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030, subdivision (a), “The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.  The court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney who advised that assertion, or on both.  If a monetary sanction is authorized by any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (a).)  “Misuses of the discovery process include … (a) Persisting, over objection and without substantial justification, in an attempt to obtain information or materials that are outside the scope of permissible discovery.  ¶  (b) Using a discovery method in a manner that does not comply with its specified procedures.  ¶  (c) Employing a discovery method in a manner or to an extent that causes unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense.  …  (h) Making or opposing, unsuccessfully and without substantial justification, a motion to compel or to limit discovery.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subds. (a)-(c), (h).)

“The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though no opposition to the motion was filed, or opposition to the motion was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1348(a).)

DISCUSSION

BD Management moves for a protective order to avoid responding further to Special Interrogatories, Set Three, which includes Special Interrogatories Nos. 142 through 157.  This request is made in accordance with Code of Civil Procedure sections 1987.1, 2030.030, 2030.040, 2030.050, and 2030.090, as well as Civil Code section 3295.  (Motion, pp. 2:2-5, 3:9-5:14.)

However, the Court observes that Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.1 applies only to situations involving “the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition[,]” through the use of a subpoena.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)  In light of the fact that there is no subpoena involved in this case, the Court will refrain from evaluating the merits of the motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.1.  Rather, the analysis will focus on Code of Civil Procedure sections 2030.030, 2030.040, 2030.050, and 2030.090, as well as Civil Code section 3295.

Timeliness

Plaintiffs contend that the motion should be denied on the grounds that BD Management “did not promptly move for a court order.”  (Opp., p. 6:17.) 

Initially, Plaintiffs refer to an unnamed “practice guide” that states a “motion for protective order must be made within the time period that the response to the discovery was due, i.e. within the thirty days.”  (Opp., p. 6:18-19.)  However, Plaintiffs fail to identify this practice guide for the Court’s examination.  Furthermore, the Court notes that practice guides do not constitute compelling or binding authority.  As articulated in Aixtron, Inc. v. Veeco Instruments, Inc. (2020) 52 Cal.App.5th 360, 400, “While secondary sources like practice guides and treatises are not compelling authority, they may be persuasive when there is an absence of precedent.  [Citation.]  But such sources ‘certainly are not binding’ [citation], especially when, as here, they do not cite any authority for the asserted rule.  In the absence of any relevant precedent, we return to the language of the statute.”

Second, Plaintiffs cite Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.090, subdivision (b)(1), which states, “The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other natural person or organization from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense.  This protective order may include, but is not limited to, one or more of the following directions:  ¶  (1) That the set of interrogatories, or particular interrogatories in the set, need not be answered.”  (Opp., p. 6:19-21.)

Based on this statute, Plaintiffs assert that “[t]he court cannot make an order that the interrogatory need not be answered, when in fact the defendant already answered the interrogatories, albeit with objections.”  (Opp., p. 6:21-23.)  This interpretation is misplaced.  The statute does not imply that a protective order may only be granted if responses have not been provided.  Rather, the language indicates that the Court possesses the authority to provide directions beyond those explicitly stated in the statute.  Accordingly, the Court may determine that BD Management is not required to provide further responses.

Finally, Plaintiffs contend that “[a]ll potential objections to a discovery request, including the right to a protective order must be asserted within the time allowed by law for the defendant to respond to the discovery.”  (Opp., p. 6:23-25.)  They cite Scottsdale Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 263, asserting that it holds “that such an objection must be made before responses to the discovery are due and not after.”  The Court observes that the Court of Appeal did not specifically address whether the right to a protective order may be asserted within the statutory time frame for a response to discovery.  Instead, the Court of Appeal concluded that “the objection based on the protective order must be withdrawn because the court had denied the protective order.”  (Scottsdale Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 263, 268.)

Consequently, the Court does not find the motion to be untimely.

Civil Code section 3295

BD Management asserts that “Civil Code § 3295 explicitly prohibits the conduct [Nick Saba, Sr.] clearly commits and the information he seeks: the financial condition of the defendant.”  (Motion, p. 5:13-14.) 

“No pretrial discovery by the plaintiff shall be permitted with respect to the evidence referred to in paragraphs (1) and (2) of subdivision (a) unless the court enters an order permitting such discovery pursuant to this subdivision.  However, the plaintiff may subpoena documents or witnesses to be available at the trial for the purpose of establishing the profits or financial condition referred to in subdivision (a), and the defendant may be required to identify documents in the defendant’s possession which are relevant and admissible for that purpose and the witnesses employed by or related to the defendant who would be most competent to testify to those facts.  Upon motion by the plaintiff supported by appropriate affidavits and after a hearing, if the court deems a hearing to be necessary, the court may at any time enter an order permitting the discovery otherwise prohibited by this subdivision if the court finds, on the basis of the supporting and opposing affidavits presented, that the plaintiff has established that there is a substantial probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim pursuant to Section 3294.  Such order shall not be a determination on the merits of the claim or any defense thereto and shall not be given in evidence or referred to at the trial.”  (Civ. Code, § 3295, subd. (c), emphasis added.)

Upon reviewing Nick Saba, Sr.’s Special Interrogatories, Set Three, the Court observes that these interrogatories are confined to the exceptions permitted by Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c).  Nick Saba, Sr. seeks that BD Management identify documents in its possession that are relevant and admissible for the purpose of establishing its profits or financial condition, the identities of witnesses employed by or associated with BD Management who would be competent to testify regarding these matters, and the duration of time these individuals have worked with/for the company.  (Alvarez Decl., Exh. A.)

However, because Nick Saba, Sr.’s Special Interrogatories, Set Three, does not define “identify” in the context of the documents, the Court will apply the plain meaning of the term, interpreting “identify” to mean naming and providing a basic description of the relevant document.  This interpretation does not compel BD Management to disclose comprehensive information regarding its financial status when identifying the documents, as such disclosure would contradict Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c).

Additionally, BD Management argues that the motion should be granted on the grounds that Nick Saba, Sr. has not filed a motion to compel BD Management to disclose its financial condition as mandated under Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c).  (Motion, p. 6:3-28.)  The Court does not concur with this argument.

Nick Saba, Sr. would only be required to file such a motion if he were seeking “evidence of:  ¶  (1) The profits the defendant has gained by virtue of the wrongful course of conduct of the nature and type shown by the evidence.  ¶  (2) The financial condition of the defendant[,]” outside of the exceptions permitted by Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c).  (Civ. Code, § 3295, subds. (a), (c).)  However, as Nick Saba, Sr. is not pursuing this information, there is no necessity for him to file a motion to compel.

Furthermore, BD Management contends that Nick Saba, Sr.’s interrogatories are premature, stating that a “trial court may [only] permit the discovery of a defendant’s financial condition after liability has been determined.”  (Motion, p. 6:22-23.)  This assertion holds merit only if Nick Saba, Sr. is seeking forbidden financial information without having filed a motion to compel it.  However, Nick Saba, Sr. is not requesting this information, thus rendering his interrogatories neither premature nor inappropriate.

Code of Civil Procedure sections 2030.040 and 2030.050

BD Management maintains that Nick Saba, Sr. has not complied with Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.050(8) because his attorney has failed to explain why “the complexity or the quantity of issues in the instant lawsuit” necessitates BD Management’s responses to Special Interrogatories Nos. 142 through 157.  (Motion, p. 7:3-17.)  The Court disagrees.

“Except as provided in Section 2030.070, no party shall, as a matter of right, propound to any other party more than 35 specially prepared interrogatories.  If the initial set of interrogatories does not exhaust this limit, the balance may be propounded in subsequent sets.  ¶  Unless a declaration as described in Section 2030.050 has been made, a party need only respond to the first 35 specially prepared interrogatories served, if that party states an objection to the balance, under Section 2030.240, on the ground that the limit has been exceeded.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.030, subds. (b)-(c).)

In the present case, Nick Saba, Sr. has issued more than 35 special interrogatories, as indicated by their numbering, Nos. 142 through 157.  (Alvarez Decl., Exh. A.)  Consequently, a declaration as outlined in Section 2030.050 is necessary.

In relevant part, Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.050(8) stipulates that the declarant must assert: “This number of questions is warranted under Section 2030.040 of the Code of Civil Procedure because __________.  (Here state each factor described in Section 2030.040 that is relied on, as well as the reasons why any factor relied on is applicable to the instant lawsuit.)”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.050(8).)  Moreover, “[s]ubject to the right of the responding party to seek a protective order under Section 2030.090, any party who attaches a supporting declaration as described in Section 2030.050 may propound a greater number of specially prepared interrogatories to another party if this greater number is warranted because of any of the following:  ¶  (1) The complexity or the quantity of the existing and potential issues in the particular case.  ¶  (2) The financial burden on a party entailed in conducting the discovery by oral deposition.  ¶  (3) The expedience of using this method of discovery to provide to the responding party the opportunity to conduct an inquiry, investigation, or search of files or records to supply the information sought.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.040, subds. (a)-(b).)

In this instance, declarant Ogochukwu Victor Onwaeze states: “This number of questions is warranted under Section 2030.040 of the Code of Civil Procedure because of  ¶  1. the complexity or the quantity of issues in the instant lawsuit.  The lawsuit involves the landlord and tenant relationship between the parties that spanned several years.  Plaintiffs complaint includes a prayer for punitive damages and these interrogatories are narrowly limited to the issue of networth and financial condition of responding party as permitted by Civil Code section 3295.  ¶  2. The expedience of using this method of discovery to provide to the responding party the opportunity to conduct an inquiry, investigation, or search of files or records to supply the information sought.”  (Alvarez Decl., Exh. A, Declaration of Ogochukwu Victor Onwaeze re: SROG (“Onwaeze Decl. re: SROG”), ¶ 8.)

While the second factor is deemed insufficient due to its failure to clarify its application to this case, the Court finds the first factor to be adequate.  Onwaeze states: “The lawsuit involves the landlord and tenant relationship between the parties that spanned several years.  Plaintiffs complaint includes a prayer for punitive damages and these interrogatories are narrowly limited to the issue of networth and financial condition of responding party.”  (Onwaeze Decl. re: SROG, ¶ 8.)  This explanation is more substantive than a mere assertion of complexity in the case.

Overboard, Oppressive, Burdensome, and Privacy Issues

BD Management asserts that “[i]t is clear that these demands, spanning ‘January 1, 2016 to the date of the responses’ and therefore over a decade, are extremely overbroad, burdensome, and grossly infringe on BD Management s employees’ privacy rights.”  (Motion, p. 8:1-3.)  The Court disagrees.

Initially, the Court determines that the requests are not overbroad or burdensome solely due to their decade-long span.  The information sought is pertinent and will enable Nick Saba, Sr. to better assess BD Management’s financial condition as appropriate.

Furthermore, the Court concludes that BD Management has not met its burden of proof to justify withholding its employees’ identities and dates of employment.

Article 1 of the California Constitution recognizes a constitutional right to privacy, which “protects an individual’s reasonable expectation of privacy against a serious invasion.”  (Faunce v. Cate (2013) 222 Cal.App.4th 166, 171.)  Under California law, even highly relevant, non-privileged information may be shielded from discovery if its disclosure would violate a person’s inalienable right to privacy.  (See¿Britt v. Superior Court (1978) 20 Cal.3d 844, 855-856.)  The right to privacy protects the individual’s reasonable expectation of privacy against a serious invasion.  (Pioneer Electronics (USA), Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 40 Cal.4th 360, 370.)  In general, when evaluating a discovery dispute that involves a privacy claim, the trial court should apply the framework outlined in Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1 (Hill).

First, a claimant must demonstrate that they have a “legally protected privacy interest.”  (Hill, supra, 7 Cal.4th at p. 35.)  Second, the claimant must show that they have a reasonable expectation of privacy given the circumstances, which include the customs, practices, and physical settings surrounding particular activities.  (Id. at pp. 36-37.)  Third, the invasion of privacy must be serious in terms of its nature, scope, and actual or potential impact.  (Id. at p. 37.)  If there is a reasonable expectation of privacy and the invasion of privacy is serious, then the court must balance the privacy interest at stake against other competing or countervailing interests.  (Id. at pp. 37-40.)

The California Supreme Court has clarified that when applying the Hill analysis, the initial burden of demonstrating a privacy interest rests with the party asserting such an interest.  (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552.)  The Court has refuted case law that mandates a party seeking the discovery of private information to prove a compelling interest; instead, the responsibility lies with the party opposing the discovery to delineate the extent and seriousness of the anticipated invasion of privacy.  (Id. at p. 557.)  “What suffices to justify an invasion will…vary according to the context.”  (Ibid.)  Only obvious invasions of interests fundamental to personal autonomy must be supported by a compelling interest.  (Id. at p. 556.)

“The custodian [of private information] has the right, in fact the duty, to resist attempts at unauthorized disclosure and the person who is the subject of [it] is entitled to expect that his right will be thus asserted.  [Citation.]  And, of course, the custodian of such private information may not waive the privacy rights of persons who are constitutionally guaranteed their protection.”  (Board of Trustees v. Superior Court (1981) 119 Cal.App.3d 516, 525-526, internal citation and quotations omitted.)

BD Management fails to demonstrate the existence of a “legally protected privacy interest” that would warrant the exclusion of its employees’ identities and dates of employment.  BD Management provides no pertinent statutes or case law.  Additionally, BD Management does not attempt to demonstrate that its employees have a reasonable expectation of privacy given the circumstances, which include the customs, practices, and physical settings surrounding particular activities, and that the invasion of privacy is serious in terms of its nature, scope, and actual or potential impact.

Accordingly, the Court DENIES BD Management’s motion for a protective order.

ORDERS

1)      Defendant BD Management Company, LLC’s Motion for Protective Order is DENIED.

2)      BD Management is ordered to give notice of the Court’s ruling.

LEGAL STANDARD

A defendant may serve and file a notice of motion to quash service of summons on the grounds of lack of jurisdiction of the court over them.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a).)  Code of Civil Procedure section 418.10 provides the exclusive procedure for challenging personal jurisdiction at the outset.  (Roy v. Superior Court (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 337, 342.)  The court may dismiss without prejudice the complaint in whole, or as to that defendant, when dismissal is made pursuant to section 418.10.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 581, subd. (h).)

“A court of this state may exercise jurisdiction on any basis not inconsistent with the Constitution of this state or of the United States.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 410.10.)  “The Due Process Clause protects an individual’s liberty interest in not being subject to the binding judgments of a forum with which he has established no meaningful ‘contacts, ties, or relations.’” (Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz (1985) 471 U.S. 462, 471-472.)  A state court may not exercise personal jurisdiction over a party under circumstances that would offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.”  (Asahi Metal Industry Co., Ltd., v. Superior Court of California, Solano County (1987) 480 U.S. 102, 113.)

When a defendant moves to quash service of the summons and complaint, the plaintiff has “the burden of proving the facts that did give the court jurisdiction, that is the facts requisite to an effective service.”  (Coulston v. Cooper (1966) 245 Cal.App.2d 866, 868.)  The plaintiff must establish the facts of jurisdiction by a preponderance of the evidence.  (Aquila, Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 556, 568.)  “When a nonresident defendant challenges personal jurisdiction the burden shifts to the plaintiff to demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence that all necessary jurisdictional criteria are met.  [Citation.]  This burden must be met by competent evidence in affidavits and authenticated documentary evidence.  An unverified complaint may not be considered as an affidavit supplying necessary facts.”  (Jewish Defense Organization, Inc. v. Superior Court (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 1045, 1054-1055.)

“Personal jurisdiction may be either general or specific.”  (Vons Companies, Inc. v. Seabest Foods, Inc. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 434, 445 (Vons).)  “A defendant that has substantial, continuous, and systematic contacts with the forum state is subject to general jurisdiction in the state, meaning jurisdiction on any cause of action.”  (HealthMarkets, Inc. v. Superior Court (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1160, 1167.)  “General jurisdiction, as its name implies, extends to any and all claims brought against a defendant.  Those claims need not relate to the forum State or the defendant’s activity there; they may concern events and conduct anywhere in the world.  But that breadth imposes a correlative limit: Only a select set of affiliations with a forum will expose a defendant to such sweeping jurisdiction.  In what [the Supreme Court] ha[s] called the paradigm case, an individual is subject to general jurisdiction in her place of domicile.  And the equivalent forums for a corporation are its place of incorporation and principal place of business.” (Preciado v. Freightliner Custom Chassis Corp.¿(2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 964, 976, internal citation omitted.)

A nonresident may be subject to specific jurisdiction if a three-prong test is met.  “First, the defendant must have purposefully availed itself of the state’s benefits.  Second, the controversy must be related to or arise out of the defendant’s contacts with the state.  Third, considering the defendant’s contacts with the state and other factors, California’s exercise of jurisdiction over the defendant must comport with fair play and substantial justice.”  (Gilmore Bank v. AsiaTrust New Zealand Ltd. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 1558, 1568, internal citations and emphasis omitted.)  “When a defendant moves to quash service of process on jurisdictional grounds, the plaintiff has the initial burden of demonstrating facts justifying the exercise of jurisdiction.  Once facts showing minimum contacts with the forum state are established, however, it becomes the defendant’s burden to demonstrate that the exercise of jurisdiction would be unreasonable.”  (Vons, supra, 14 Cal.4th at p. 449, internal citations omitted.)

DISCUSSION

Defendant is seeking to quash the service of the summons, contending that the Court lacks the authority to exercise personal jurisdiction.  (Motion, p. 1:2-5.)  Defendant asserts that (1) it is not incorporated in California; (2) its principal place of business is not in California; and (3) any contacts it may have with California do not give rise to Plaintiffs’ claims.  (Motion, p. 1:5-8.)

Conversely, Plaintiffs assert both general and specific personal jurisdiction over Defendant.  (Opp., pp. 6:2-12:27.)  In the alternative, should the Court determine that additional factual information is necessary, Plaintiffs request that the Court continue the motion and allow for jurisdictional discovery.  (Opp., pp. 13:1-14:8.) 

Specific Jurisdiction

First Prong: Purposeful Availment

Plaintiffs maintain that Defendant purposefully availed itself by (1) intentionally engaging in commercial activities aimed at California through their websites and reservation system, (2) maintaining a registered agent for franchises and operating a California specific entity, and (3) providing benefits to Plaintiffs through the Marriott Bonvoy membership of the CEO of Sabio Holding.  (Opp., pp. 6:19-7:17.) 

“An out-of-state defendant purposefully avails itself of a forum state’s benefits if the defendant (1) purposefully directs its activities at the forum state’s residents, (2) purposefully derives a benefit from its activities in the forum state, or (3) purposefully invokes the privileges and protections of the forum state’s laws by (a) purposefully engaging in ‘significant activities’ within the forum state or (b) purposefully creating ‘continuing [contractual] obligations’ between itself and the residents of the forum state.  [Citations.]  Purposeful availment can occur from afar; the out-of-state defendant’s physical presence in the forum state is not required.  [Citation.]”  (Jacqueline B. v. Rawls Law Group, P.C. (2021) 68 Cal.App.5th 243, 253 (Jacqueline B.), internal citations omitted.)

Website

“In assessing whether an out-of-state defendant’s operation of a website constitutes purposeful availment, we examine (1) whether the website targets California residents (Snowney, supra, 35 Cal.4th at p. 1063, 29 Cal.Rptr.3d 33, 112 P.3d 28; cf. Yue v. Yang (2021) 62 Cal.App.5th 539, 547, 276 Cal.Rptr.3d 718 [out-of-state defendant sends ‘ “California-focused” social media messages “directly” to California residents,’ knowing their residency; purposeful availment]; Zehia, supra, 45 Cal.App.5th at pp. 556-557, 258 Cal.Rptr.3d 778 [same]; Moncrief v. Clark (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 1000, 1006-1007, 189 Cal.Rptr.3d 864 [out-of-state resident made false representations to California-based attorneys to induce purchase of farm equipment; purposeful availment]), and, if it does not, (2) where the website falls on a ‘sliding scale’ of ‘interactivity’ with Internet users, with websites allowing the out-of-state defendant to conduct business with California residents at one end of the scale and websites that passively make information available at the other end (Snowney, at pp. 1063-1064, 29 Cal.Rptr.3d 33, 112 P.3d 28; Pavlovich, supra, 29 Cal.4th at p. 274, 127 Cal.Rptr.2d 329, 58 P.3d 2)”  (Jacqueline B., supra, 68 Cal.App.5th at pp. 254-255.)  “The middle ground is occupied by interactive Web sites where a user can exchange information with the host computer.  In these cases, the exercise of jurisdiction is determined by examining the level of interactivity and commercial nature of the exchange of information that occurs on the Web site.”  (Zippo Mfg. Co. v. Zippo Dot Com, Inc. (W.D.Pa. 1997) 952 F.Supp. 1119, 1124.)

Plaintiffs contend that Defendant has “purposefully directed commercial activity toward California” through its websites.  (Opp., p. 6:5-6.) 

Similar to the case of Jacqueline B., “the evidence shows that Defendant did not target California residents (and hence that any effect on those residents was not intended)[.]”  (Jacqueline B., supra, 68 Cal.App.5th at p. 255.)  Plaintiffs provide evidence indicating that Defendant advertised Planet Hollywood Costa Rica by Royalton, An Autograph Collection All-Inclusive Resort, globally through its website, including to California residents.  (Declaration of Adam J. Reis (“Reis Decl.”), ¶ 3, Exh. 2.)  Furthermore, Defendant’s privacy choices page permitted California residents to exercise their Do Not Sell rights under the California Consumer Privacy Act.  (Reis Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. 3.)  Exhibit 4 demonstrates that individuals worldwide can reserve rooms via a website purportedly managed by Defendant.  (Reis Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. 4.)  However, the Court notes that the website does not indicate whether individuals can make payments for their rooms online.  Exhibit 5 outlines the terms and conditions of Defendant’s Loyalty Program.  (Reis Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. 5.)  In Exhibit 6, California is mentioned only in a brief statement: “California Residents: AGA Service Company is doing business in California as Allianz Global Assistant Insurance Agency, License # 0B01400.  California offers a toll-free consumer hotline at 1800927-4357.”  (Reis Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. 6.)

However, none of these documents convincingly demonstrate that Defendant targeted California residents.  Allowing access to individuals globally and providing limited information specifically for California residents does not establish that Defendant directed any activities toward California.  Rather, it suggests that Defendant seeks to serve a broad and diverse audience.

Further, Plaintiffs contends that Snowney v. Harrah’s Entertainment, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1054 (Snowney) is on point.  (Opp., pp. 6:22-7:6.) 

In Snowney, the Supreme Court of California found that “[b]y touting the proximity of their hotels to California and providing driving directions from California to their hotels, defendants’ Web site specifically targeted residents of California.  [Citation.]  Defendants also concede that many of their patrons come from California and that some of these patrons undoubtedly made reservations using their Web site.  As such, defendants have purposefully derived a benefit from their Internet activities in California [citation] and have established a substantial connection with California through their Web site [citation].”  (Snowney, supra, 35 Cal.4th at pp. 1064-1065.)

Here, the Defendant has not promoted the proximity of their hotels to California nor provided driving directions from California to their hotels.  The sole similarity lies in the fact that individuals may reserve a hotel room through one of the alleged websites associated with Defendant.  This does not adequately demonstrate that Defendant “purposefully directed commercial activity toward California.”

As such, Plaintiffs fail to show purposeful availment under this theory.

Autograph Collection Franchise

Moreover, Plaintiffs contend that “[Defendant’s] Autograph Collection franchise disclosures also reveal that at the relevant time [Defendant] availed itself to the benefits of doing business in California by maintaining a franchise registered agent and regulatory authority in California and that [Defendant] appeared to operate a California specific entity for franchise-related California transactions[.]  (Reis Decl. Ex. 8 at pp. 311, 314, 478.)”  (Opp., p. 6:17-21.)

However, the Court observes that, following the sustainment of Defendant’s objection to Exhibit 8, Plaintiffs lack the necessary supporting evidence for their assertion.  As such, Plaintiffs fail to show purposeful availment under this theory.

Benefits Membership

“[W]here the defendant ‘deliberately’ has engaged in significant activities within a State, [citation], or has created ‘continuing obligations’ between himself and residents of the forum, [citation], he manifestly has availed himself of the privilege of conducting business there, and because his activities are shielded by ‘the benefits and protections’ of the forum’s laws it is presumptively not unreasonable to require him to submit to the burdens of litigation in that forum as well.”  (Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz (1985) 471 U.S. 462, 475-476, emphasized added.)

Plaintiffs further contend that “[Defendant] also created continuing obligations running to a California resident.  Annex A of the contract designates Sabio’s California-based CEO — by name, Marriott Bonvoy membership number, and Sabio email address — as the Marriott Bonvoy Events member entitled to benefits in connection with the event, under program terms constituting an agreement with and modifiable at the sole discretion of [Defendant].  (Rahimtoola Decl., ¶ 8; Cmpl., Ex. A, Annex A, Reis MII Decl., Ex. 6.)  A defendant that ‘creat[es] continuing obligations between [itself] and residents of the forum’ has availed itself of the forum’s benefits and may be required to answer there.  (Burger King, supra, 471 U.S. at pp. 475–476.)  [Defendant’s] own loyalty program, embedded in the contract’s benefits provisions and directed at an identified Californian, is precisely such an obligation — and [Defendant’s] declarant concedes the conduit: [Defendant] licenses its ‘intellectual property and systems’ for use at the Resort.  (Tamburello Decl., ¶ 6.)”  (Opp., p. 7:7-17.) 

However, the loyalty program does not constitute a continuing obligation.  According to the terms set forth in Annex A of the contract referenced in the Complaint, members, including the CEO of Sabio Holding, are granted points or miles upon the booking of an event or stay.  (Compl., Exh. A., Annex A.)  Once the event or stay has concluded, the obligation is deemed fulfilled, and the member receives the corresponding points or miles.  A new obligation towards the CEO of Sabio Holding arises only when a subsequent booking for another event or stay is made.  Consequently, the Court cannot categorize this arrangement as a continuing obligation, as Defendant’s alleged obligation is contingent upon future bookings.

As such, the Plaintiffs failed to show purposeful availment under this theory.  Absent satisfaction of the first prong, Plaintiff has not established specific jurisdiction.

General Jurisdiction

Plaintiffs contend that Defendant’s connections to California were both continuous and systematic, establishing general jurisdiction.  (Opp., p. 12:1-27.) 

“General (also called all-purpose) jurisdiction means that a defendant’s contacts with a state are sufficiently extensive that the ‘defendant is “essentially at home,” ’ and the court may exercise jurisdiction over the defendant regardless of whether the claims relate to the forum state.”  (Doe v. Dameron (2021) 70 Cal.App.5th 684, 689-690.)  “[T]he proper inquiry, this Court has explained, is whether a foreign corporation’s ‘affiliations with the State are so “continuous and systematic” as to render [it] essentially at home in the forum State.  [Citation.]”  (Daimler AG v. Bauman (2014) 571 U.S. 117, 119, internal citation omitted.)

Plaintiffs contend that “[t]he record shows that Sabio’s Chief Executive Officer transacted with [Defendant’s] interactive booking platforms from California in the very transaction at issue (Rahimtoola Decl., ¶ 6), and that those platforms maintain consumer disclosures directed specifically to California residents [(Reis MII Decl., Exs. 2–6) —underscoring that MII maintains an ongoing California facing enterprise.”  (Opp., p. 12:15-19.)  The Court, however, finds this argument unpersuasive.

The Court finds that the record indicates the CEO interacted with the booking platform, but it does not substantiate that he booked any room or made any transaction through one of Defendant’s alleged websites.  (Declaration of Aziz Rahimtoola, ¶ 8.)  Moreover, as discussed above, the Court finds that Defendant did not directly target California residents.

Furthermore, Plaintiffs contend that Defendant’s “franchise disclosures also reveal that at the relevant time [Defendant] maintained a franchise registered agent and regulatory authority in California and that [Defendant] appeared to operate a California specific entity for franchise-related California transactions[.]  (Reis Decl. Ex. 8 at pp. 311, 314, 478.)”  (Opp., p. 12:20-23.)  However, this claim lacks merit because Plaintiffs have not provided admissible evidence to support their position.

Lastly, Plaintiffs contend that “during the relevant time frame, Marriott maintained over 500 properties in California.”  (Opp., p. 12:19-20.)  Yet, it is unclear why this information is relevant, as Plaintiffs do not demonstrate that Defendant, rather than “Marriott,” owned, maintained, or otherwise controlled these properties.

As such, Plaintiff has failed to show personal jurisdiction based on these theories.

Jurisdictional Discovery

If the Court determines that personal jurisdiction cannot be established based on the evidence presented, as it already has, Plaintiffs request that the Court continue the hearing and grant permission for jurisdictional discovery.  (Opp., p. 13:1-14:8.)  The Court DENIES this request.

“A plaintiff attempting to assert jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant is entitled to an opportunity to conduct discovery of the jurisdictional facts necessary to sustain its burden of proof.  [Citation.]  In order to prevail on a motion for a continuance for jurisdictional discovery, the plaintiff should demonstrate that discovery is likely to lead to the production of evidence of facts establishing jurisdiction.  [Citation.]  …  A ruling on a motion to continue in order to allow additional time to discover jurisdictional facts lies in the trial court’s discretion.”  (In re Automobile Antitrust Cases I & II (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 100, 127, internal citations omitted.)

Plaintiffs assert that the following discovery requests, propounded on July 10, 2026, will facilitate their evaluation of the jurisdictional questions presented by the motions: (1) “the franchise agreement and related intercompany agreements;” (2) “documents defining [Defendant’s] rights and role concerning reservations, marketing, brand standards, and the licensed ‘systems’;” and (3) “Marriott Bonvoy participation documents for the Resort; and reservation channel and booking records for Sabio’s event.”  (Opp., pp. 13:27-14:3.)  But, Plaintiffs fail to articulate how these specific discovery requests are likely to lead to the production of evidence of facts establishing jurisdiction.  As such, this request is DENIED.

Accordingly, the Defendant’s motions to quash service of the summons is GRANTED.

ORDERS

1)      Specially Appearing Defendant Marriot International, Inc.’s Motion to Quash Plaintiffs’ Service of Summons and Complaint for Lack of Personal Jurisdiction is GRANTED.

2)      Plaintiffs Sabio Holdings Inc. and Sabio Inc.’s Requests to Continue the Hearing and Permit Jurisdictional Discovery are DENIED.

3)      Defendant is ordered to provide notice of this Court’s ruling.
