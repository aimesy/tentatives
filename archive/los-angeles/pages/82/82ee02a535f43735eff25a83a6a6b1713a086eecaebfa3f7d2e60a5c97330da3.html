Case Number: 20STCV32549    Hearing Date: July 10, 2026    Dept: 406

 

Denise Greene, an individual,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

CITY OF LOS ANGELES, a Municipal Corporation; and DOES 1 through 50,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  20STCV32549

  Hearing Date:  July 10, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

(1)   Defendant’s motion to compel answers at deposition (CRS# 134340551488)

 

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On August 26, 2020, Plaintiff Denise Greene (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendant City of Los Angeles (“Defendant”) for sexual harassment. Plaintiff alleges that she was subject to sexual harassment as an employee of Defendant and seeks damages for emotional distress, including depression, anxiety, related emotional injuries, and a 2018 miscarriage that is part of her emotional distress claim.  

            On February 25, 2026, Defendant City of Los Angeles deposed Plaintiff Denise Greene. During the deposition, Plaintiff’s counsel repeatedly instructed Greene not to answer approximately 21 questions based on objections of “scope” and “privacy.” The unanswered questions concerned, among other topics: (1) Greene’s pregnancy and miscarriage history; (2) her medical treatment and healthcare providers; (3) alleged domestic violence and other intervening stressors; (4) the identity and relationship with the father of her first child; and (5) her work history and return to the Wilmington Recreation and Parks location.

            On June 3, 2026, Defendant filed a motion (CRS# 134340551488) to compel Plaintiff’s answers to the above questions and the ordering of sanctions. Plaintiff filed an opposition on June 26, 2026.  On June 30, 2026, Defendant filed a reply to Plaintiff’s opposition.

LEGAL STANDARD

            “If a deponent fails to answer any question or to produce any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing under the deponent’s control that is specified in the deposition notice or a deposition subpoena, the party seeking discovery may move the court for an order compelling that answer or production.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480(a).) “If the court determines that the answer or production sought is subject to discovery, it shall order that the answer be given or the production be made on the resumption of the deposition.” (Id., § 2025.480(i).)

DISCUSSION

I. Deposition Timeline

            One of the Plaintiff’s points is that the Defendant’s motion is untimely under Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480(b). In calculating the statutory deadline, the Court notes that the parties agreed to extend Plaintiff's time to review and execute the deposition errata sheet, which was ultimately served on April 16, 2026. (Freeman Decl. ¶ 7.) Accordingly, the Defendant’s motion was timely.

II. Discovery

            Plaintiff reads Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court, 22 Cal.4th 245 (2000) as limiting Defendant's renewed deposition to updated damages to preclude duplicative discovery. While Fairmont recognizes that discovery after remand remains subject to statutory limitations, it also makes clear that discovery reopens anew following a remand for a new trial. (Id. at 252) Notably, Fairmont expressly distinguishes a new trial from a continuance in that one constitutes delay whereas the other begins proceedings anew. (Id.) That distinction underlies the Court's conclusion that discovery reopens following remand as though a trial never happened. (Id.)

III. Privacy and Privilege Objections

            A key premise of Plaintiff’s objections is that the deposition must provide information only on updated damages. However, Fairmont is unequivocal that a new trial restarts discovery de novo to help the parties probe new issues and gaps in evidence. (Id. at 253) All information relating to Plaintiff's alleged miscarriage, including her pregnancy history and the number of miscarriages she has experienced, is relevant to the nature and extent of her claimed damages. Similarly, the abuse Plaintiff has suffered at the hands of her spouse is relevant to the existence of alternative sources of her emotional injuries. Likewise, Plaintiff's possible return to the Wilmington Recreation and Parks location and her experiences there are relevant to how the workplace contributed to her injuries.

            Defendant is entitled to discover the identities of healthcare providers who treated Plaintiff for the physical or emotional injuries she attributes to Defendant's alleged conduct.

            However, some of the deposition questions that were unanswered are unrelated to the claims at issue. The identity of Plaintiff's first child, whether the child's father is incarcerated, and when Plaintiff last saw him bear no apparent relevance to Plaintiff's sexual harassment claims or the damages she seeks.

CONCLUSION

            Defendant’s motion is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART.

COSTTREE HOLDINGS, LLC,

ECIVIS, INC., et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

 

           AND RELATED MATTERS.

 

  Case No.:  22STCV23458

defendants’ motion for summary judgment or adjudication

 

 

I. BACKGROUND

a. The Underlying Complaint

On July 20, 2022, Plaintiff CostTree Holding, LLC (CostTree) filed this action against Defendants eCivis, Inc. (eCivis), GTY Technology Holdings, Inc. (GTY), and James Ha (Ha). On March 15, 2024, Plaintiffs CostTree and Capital Accounting Partners, LLC (CAP) filed the operative Second Amended Complaint (SAC), alleging (1) breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (2) fraud, (3) negligent misrepresentation, (4) civil conspiracy, (5) breach of contract, and (6) intentional interference with contractual relations.    

            The SAC alleges that CostTree agreed to sell its assets to eCivis through an Asset Purchase Agreement (APA) with eCivis. One of these assets was a certain program developed by CostTree, called Allocate. In exchange for the sale, the APA provided that CostTree would make earn-out payments from Allocate until a certain date and that eCivis would assume certain liabilities of CostTree, including a License Agreement with CAP. eCivis has allegedly failed to adequately promote sales or renewals of Allocate and has not assumed any of CostTree’s liabilities.

eCivis allegedly acted to minimize CostTree’s earn-out payments from Allocate until CostTree’s right to earn-out payments expired under the APA. Meanwhile, Ha, the CEO of eCivis, sought to maximize his own earn-out payments from eCivis’s programs under an agreement with GTY, which acquired eCivis and its programs. Despite increased resources from the GTY acquisition to market eCivis’s programs, eCivis allegedly refused to market or sell Allocate until CostTree’s right to earn-out payments expired. This allegedly allowed eCivis to effectively acquire CostTree’s assets for little to no consideration. Defendants allegedly concocted this scheme to remove a competitor from the market and maximize their own profits.

Defendants also allegedly terminated the Licensing Agreement with CAP without following the procedures for termination outlined in the Licensing Agreement. Defendants allegedly cut off CAP’s access to Allocate, preventing CAP from servicing its clientele for nearly a month. Defendants allegedly did this as retaliation for CostTree filing this action, as Nicolie Wickwire (Wickwire) is the CEO of both CostTree and CAP.

Ha has since been dismissed.  

b. Wickwire’s Related Action

            On October 6, 2023, Wickwire filed the action Nicolie Wickwire v. eCivis, Inc., et al. (23CMCV01613), alleging (1) breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (2) constructive discharge in violation of public policy, (3) defamation, and (4) declaratory relief. The Wickwire action was consolidated with this action, with this action as the lead case. However, Wickwire subsequently dismissed her complaint with prejudice. 

c. The Cross-Complaint

            On April 29, 2024, eCivis and GTY (dba Euna Solutions) cross-complained against CostTree, CAP, Wickwire, and Stephanie Ratajczak (Ratajczak). eCivis and GTY filed the operative Third Amended Cross-Complaint (TACC) on September 15, 2025, alleging (1) conversion, (2) intentional interference with prospective economic advantage, (3) breach of employment agreement, (4) breach of the APA, (5) breach of the CAP Licensing Agreement, (6) fraud, and (7) civil conspiracy. 

            The TACC alleges that Allocate underperformed as compared to Wickwire’s promises, despite assistance from eCivis and GTY. Upon Wickwire’s job title change, she allegedly refused to sign the new employment agreement and directed her subordinate, Ratajczak, to not sign her agreement either. Ratajczak was allegedly working for CAP at the same time as working for eCivis. Wickwire, Ratajczak, and CAP allegedly sold Allocate services to third party customers who should have contracted with eCivis and GTY. Wickwire, Ratajczak, and CAP allegedly allowed third parties to access and use Allocate without a proper license and without paying fees to eCivis and GTY. These actions allegedly violated the APA, Licensing Agreement, and employment agreements.    

When Wickwire and Ratajczak simultaneously resigned, they allegedly left eCivis and GTY without any knowledgeable employees to manage Allocate. Wickwire allegedly failed to train any other employees on Allocate as was her duty prior to resigning, leaving Allocate with little value and thereby damaging eCivis and GTY. Since their resignations, Wickwire and Ratajczak have allegedly continued to impermissibly access or grant unpermitted access to Allocate beyond the limited access permitted for customers of CAP.

d. The Instant Motion

            On March 13, 2026, eCivis and GTY (collectively, Defendants) filed the instant motion for summary judgment or adjudication of the SAC. CostTree and CAP (collectively, Plaintiffs) filed an opposition on June 18, 2026. Defendants filed a reply on June 29, 2026.

 

 

II. LEGAL STANDARD

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” (Leyva v. Garcia (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1095, 1101.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce “substantial responsive evidence.” (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)

IV. DISCUSSION

a. Abandoned Claims

As an initial matter, Plaintiffs have abandoned their claims for fraud, negligent misrepresentation, and civil conspiracy. Defendants’ motion establishes that there are no triable issues as to those claims, and Plaintiffs do not contest that showing. Therefore, the Court proceeds on the remaining causes of action.

b. Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

                        1. Legal Standard

“The covenant of good faith and fair dealing, implied by law in every contract, exists merely to prevent one contracting party from unfairly frustrating the other party’s right to receive the benefits of the agreement actually made.” (Guz v. Bechtel National, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 349-350.) “The covenant of good faith and fair dealing imposes obligations on the contracting parties separate and apart from those consensually agreed to.” (Bodenhamer v. Superior Court (1987) 192 Cal.App.3d 1472, 1477.) “The obligations imposed by the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing are not those set out in the terms of the contract itself, but rather are obligations imposed by law.” (Id. at p. 1478.) Therefore, “breach of a specific provision of the contract is not a necessary prerequisite to a claim for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.” (Schwartz v. State Farm Fire & Casualty Co. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 1329, 1339.)

            Nonetheless, “the scope of conduct prohibited by the covenant of good faith is circumscribed by the purposes and express terms of the contract.” (Avidity Partners, LLC v. State of California (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 1180, 1204.) “The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing does not impose substantive terms and conditions beyond those to which the parties actually agreed.” (Ibid.) “It cannot impose substantive duties or limits on the contracting parties beyond those incorporated in the specific terms of their agreement.” (Ibid.) 

                        2. Allegations

Here, the SAC alleges that “Defendants intentionally interfered with Costtree Plaintiff's right to receive the benefit of the contract by refusing to market and promote the Program.” (SAC ¶ 79.) The SAC alleges that “Defendants intentionally framed the APA in such a way that it would be impossible for Costtree Plaintiff to receive any benefits.” (Id., ¶ 80.) Plaintiffs contend that Defendants failed to provide “critical software development” and “sales leadership.”

                        3. Express Terms

Defendants argue that they did not breach the APA because “[t]he express terms of the APA do not require Defendants to engage in any specific conduct regarding Allocate, including but not limited to ‘critical software development’ or ‘sales leadership.’” (Def.’s Stmt. of Undisputed Facts (UF) 3.) Defendants contend that “critical software development” and “sales leadership” are undefined and not incorporated into the APA. (UF 2, 11.)

However, an implied covenant claim does not depend on breach of an express contract term. (Schwartz, supra, 88 Cal.App.4th at p. 1339.) The implied covenant is specifically distinguished from a breach of contract claim in that it relies on obligations imposed by law rather than contractual text. (Bodenhamer, supra, 192 Cal.App.3d at p. 1478.) Thus, Defendants cannot satisfy their initial burden on the motion by merely pointing out that the APA does not expressly require “critical software development” or “sales leadership.” Defendants have not precluded the possibility that a failure to provide critical software development or sales leadership “unfairly frustrat[ed] the other party’s right to receive the benefits of the agreement actually made.” (See Guz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at pp. 349-50.)  

The evidence also suggests that the parties contemplated Defendants would provide critical software development and sales leadership. Under the APA, Wickwire received no payment upfront for Allocate. (Wickwire Decl. ¶ 15.) Instead, Wickwire was entitled to earnout payments conditioned on the marketing and sale of Allocate. (Ibid.) “After the sale, control over the Allocate software's development, improvements, and upgrades belonged exclusively to eCivis.” (Id., ¶ 38.) Prior to signing the APA, Ha created a document stating that “eCivis would provide critical software development and sales leadership to establish product market fit and a realistic go-so-market strategy.” (Id., ¶ 7, Ex. 2.)  

A reasonable trier of fact may infer from this evidence that Defendants’ provision of software development and sales leadership were implied obligations consistent with “the purposes and express terms of the contract.” (See Avidity Partners, LLC, supra, 221 Cal.App.4th at p. 1204.) Put differently, the failure to provide software development and sales leadership may have “unfairly frustrat[ed] the other party’s right to receive the benefits of the agreement” by hindering sales of Allocate and depriving Plaintiffs of earnout payments. (See Guz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at pp. 349-50.) Defendants have not precluded this possibility for summary judgment purposes.

4. Defendants’ Performance

Defendants argue that they in fact provided software development and sales leadership to assist with Allocate. Specifically, Defendants point out that they funded Allocate’s development and hired personnel including a Chief Technology Officer, a support lead, two software developers, and a senior project manager/graphic designer. (Mtn. 17:13-20.) Defendants also hired a senior vice president of sales, hired an employee to assist with selling Allocate, and contracted with third-party Hinderliter, de Llamas & Associates (HdL) to facilitate additional sales of Allocate. (Mtn. 18:10-23.) GTY also supported Ha when he asked for support of Allocate. (Ibid.) Defendants also contend that their hiring of Wickwire is itself proof of their dedication to the development and sale of Allocate. (Mtn. 18:23-27.)

On the other hand, Wickwire avers that she “repeatedly asked eCivis management, including Craig Ross and Art Elizarov, for additional sales and development resources for Allocate” but was “consistently ignored.” (Wickwire Decl., ¶¶ 27-28.) Wickwire claims that “eCivis did not dedicate any new marketing or sales staff to the Allocate program, nor did it expand the small team of employees who had transitioned from CostTree.” (Id., ¶ 29.) Wickwire claims that “[t]he existing eCivis sales team was never trained on how to sell Allocate, despite [her] repeated offers to train them.” (Id., ¶ 30.) “eCivis removed the Allocate software from the sales team's commission schedule around the end of 2019 or the beginning of 2020, removing any financial incentive for staff to sell the product.” (Id., ¶ 32.) According to Wickwire, the partnership with HdL “lacked coordination,” and HdL “quoted improperly low prices for Allocate.” (Id., ¶ 35.) Wickwire further avers that over five years, “Allocate received only three minor maintenance updates and zero feature enhancements.” (Wickwire Decl. ¶ 39.)

These conflicting facts raise a triable issue over whether Defendants adequately provided support for the Allocate software. “The issue of whether the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing has been breached is ordinarily ‘a question of fact unless only one inference [can] be drawn from the evidence.’” (Moore v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 280, 292.) Here, the evidence is subject to more than one inference. A reasonable trier of fact may find that Defendants failed to provide sufficient software development and sales expertise, thus hindering sales of Allocate and depriving Plaintiffs of earnout payments under the APA. The trier of fact may conclude that this constituted a breach of the implied covenant. Therefore, summary judgment is unwarranted.  

c. Breach of Contract

            The SAC alleges that “Defendants breached the CAP Licensing Agreement by terminating access without written notice and without following the requirements of Section 6.2 of the CAP Licensing Agreement.” (SAC ¶ 115.) Specifically, Section 6.2(ii) of the Licensing Agreement provides: “Either party may terminate this Agreement immediately by written notice if the other party materially breaches this Agreement and fails to cure its breach after receipt of written notice within (a) 15 days in the case of nonpayment of any fees, or (b) 30 days in the case of all other breaches.” (Wickwire Decl., Ex. 4.)

            It is undisputed that CAP was denied access to Allocate from August 23, 2023 to September 14, 2023, a period of 22 days. (UF 17-18.) Plaintiffs contend that this was a termination of the Licensing Agreement even though Defendants failed to follow the termination procedure outlined in Section 6.2(ii).

            However, Plaintiffs fail to explain how a 22-day interruption constitutes a termination of the agreement. Plaintiffs argue that because “access to the Allocate platform is the very subject matter and entire consideration of the Agreement,” cutting off such access constitutes termination. (Opp. 15:27-28.) Yet, it is undisputed that Plaintiffs ultimately retained access to Allocate. (UF 18.) This shows as a matter of law that the agreement was not terminated. If the agreement had been terminated, Defendants would have had no obligation to provide access to Allocate. (See Merrill v. Continental Assur. Co. (1962) 200 Cal.App.2d 663, 670 [a party who terminates a contract “is not liable after termination for further transactions thereunder”].) If an agreement to provide access to Allocate is terminated, then Plaintiffs would no longer have access to Allocate.

Plaintiffs identify no basis besides the Licensing Agreement for their continued access to Allocate. According to Plaintiffs themselves, the Licensing Agreement “allow[ed] CAP to continue using the Allocate software for its consulting clients after the sale.” (Opp. 7:13-14.) It follows that without the Licensing Agreement, CAP would have retained no such access. Thus, the agreement clearly survived the 22-day interruption. Because the agreement was not terminated, Section 6.2 is not implicated. Plaintiffs identify no other provision of the Licensing Agreement that was breached as a result of the 22-day interruption. Their complaint and argument rely solely on Section 6.2. (See SAC ¶ 115; Opp. 15:22-31.)

Thus, the breach of contract claim fails as a matter of law. 

d. Interference with Contractual Relations

The elements of intentional interference with contractual relations are: “(1) a valid contract between plaintiff and a third party; (2) defendant’s knowledge of this contract; (3) defendant’s intentional acts designed to induce a breach or disruption of the contractual relationship; (4) actual breach or disruption of the contractual relationship; and (5) resulting damage.” (Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. Bear Stearns & Co. (1990) 50 Cal.3d 1118, 1126.)

Here, the SAC alleges that the 22-day interruption interfered with CAP’s contracts with its clients. (SAC ¶¶ 120-124.)

Defendants argue that the interference claim “fails along with [the fifth] cause of action for the reasons set forth above. There can be no intentional interference because Defendants, under the terms of the License Agreement Plaintiffs drafted provides no guarantee that access to Allocate will be uninterrupted.” (Mtn. 28:25-28.) However, just because Defendants did not breach their own agreement with CAP, does not mean Defendants did not interfere with CAP’s other contracts with its clients. This argument fails to preclude a triable issue.

Defendants also argue that they “had good reason to believe Ms. Wickwire and Ms. Ratajczak were absconding with confidential, proprietary customer information – i.e. shutting down access was not per se wrongful.” (Mtn. 29:1-2.) However, interference with contract does not require an independently wrongful act. (See Ixchel Pharma, LLC v. Biogen, Inc. (2020) 9 Cal.5th 1130, 1141 [“It is generally not necessary that the defendant's conduct be wrongful apart from the interference with the contract itself”].) The independent wrong requirement only applies to interference with business relations. (Ibid.) Thus, this argument fails to preclude a triable issue.

Lastly, Defendants argue that “Plaintiffs have failed to produce any contract between Plaintiffs and any third parties, nor have they produced any evidence that Defendants knew of the existence of such contract. (See Richardson Decl., ¶ 14.).” (Mtn. 29:3-5.) The Richardson declaration merely states that “[t]o date, Plaintiffs CostTree Holdings, LLC and Capital Accounting Partners, LLC have not produced any documents to Defendants.” (Richardson Decl. ¶ 14.) However, there is no indication that Plaintiffs were asked to produce CAP’s contracts with its clients or otherwise admitted that they possessed no such contracts. Richardson’s perfunctory statement, devoid of explanation or context, fails to preclude a triable issue. Similarly, Defendants present no evidence that they lacked knowledge of CAP’s contracts.

“[S]ummary judgment law in California requires the defendant to present evidence, and not simply point out through argument, that the plaintiff does not possess and cannot reasonably obtain the needed evidence.” (Lona v. Citibank, N.A. (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 89, 110.) Here, Defendants have failed to meet their initial burden on the motion because they have not shown, with evidence, that Plaintiff cannot prove the elements of the claim. Therefore, summary judgment is unwarranted.

e. Punitive Damages

            “In an action for the breach of an obligation not arising from contract, where it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice, the plaintiff, in addition to the actual damages, may recover damages for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant.” (Civ. Code, § 3294(a).)

“An employer shall not be liable for damages pursuant to subdivision (a), based upon acts of an employee of the employer, unless the employer had advance knowledge of the unfitness of the employee and employed him or her with a conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others or authorized or ratified the wrongful conduct for which the damages are awarded or was personally guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. With respect to a corporate employer, the advance knowledge and conscious disregard, authorization, ratification or act of oppression, fraud, or malice must be on the part of an officer, director, or managing agent of the corporation.” (Civ. Code, § 3294(b).)

Defendants argue that “[t]here is no evidence of any officer, director, or managing agent committed any act of oppression, fraud, or malice,” citing to the depositions of Wickwire and Ha. (Mtn. 29:18-27.) However, Defendants have cited no evidence to preclude a triable issue for purposes of their initial burden on the motion.

Wickwire was merely asked, “who, specifically, at eCivis and/or GTY, do you think, intended to harm you?” and “is there anybody specific that you believe should be punished?” (Richardson Decl., Ex. 1 at 196:17-22, 198:4-11.) That is not an inquiry pertinent to the punitive damages analysis. Wickwire was not asked anything that would reveal whether a director, officer, or managing agent engaged in or ratified malice, oppression, or fraud, nor did she say anything that would preclude this possibility. Similarly, Ha provided no testimony relating to the topic. (See Ex. 4 at pp. 57-60, 77.)

Therefore, Defendants have failed to satisfy their initial burden on the motion. Accordingly, summary adjudication of punitive damages is not warranted.

 

 

V. CONCLUSION

            Defendants’ motion for summary judgment is DENIED. Defendants’ motion for summary adjudication is GRANTED as to the second, third, fourth, and fifth causes of action and DENIED in all other respects.

 


Case Number: 24STCV05932    Hearing Date: July 10, 2026    Dept: 406

 

5379 WHITTIER BOULEVARD LLC,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

YOLANDA PADILLA, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  24STCV05932

  Hearing Date:  July 10, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

cross-complainants’ motion to permit discovery of financial condition (CRS# 0688)

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On March 8, 2024, Plaintiff 5379 Whittier Boulevard LLC (Whittier) filed this action against Defendants Yolanda Padilla (Padilla) and Jonathan Flores (Flores), asserting causes of action for (1) trespass, (2) negligent interference with prospective economic advantage, (3) intentional interference with prospective economic advantage, and (4) declaratory and injunctive relief.

            Whittier owns a property adjoining that of Padilla and Flores. The dispute arises from Whittier’s attempt to conduct renovations on a part of its property close to the boundary line between the two properties. After Padilla and Flores declined Whittier access to their property to conduct the renovations, Whittier began the renovations supposedly without encroaching onto the other property. However, Padilla and Flores allegedly engaged in a campaign of harassment to prevent the renovations from proceeding, based on the false assertion that the work encroached onto their property. This allegedly included trespassing onto Whittier’s property and engaging in physical violence.

            On April 3, 2024, Padilla and Flores filed a cross-complaint against Whittier, Logan Altman, Duval Campbell, Armed Elite Services, Raul Alvarez Ramirez, and Caribe Construction. Padilla and Flores filed the operative First Amended Cross-Complaint on February 10, 2026. The FACC adds Tekoa E. Burdine as a cross-defendant. The FACC asserts claims for (1) assault and battery, (2) terrorist threats, (3) trespass, (4) intentional infliction of emotional distress, (5) negligent infliction of emotional distress, and (6) negligent retention. Padilla and Flores allege that Whittier’s renovations encroached onto their property and that the construction workers refused to leave. Whittier hired a security guard, Campbell, who allegedly helped the workers trespass onto the other property and allegedly assaulted Padilla when she attempted to stop him.

            On June 5, 2026, Padilla and Flores filed the instant motion to permit discovery into Cross-Defendant Logan Altman’s financial condition.

LEGAL STANDARD

            “No pretrial discovery by the plaintiff shall be permitted with respect to the [defendant’s financial condition] unless the court enters an order permitting such discovery pursuant to this subdivision.” (Civ. Code, § 3295(c).) “Upon motion by the plaintiff supported by appropriate affidavits and after a hearing, if the court deems a hearing to be necessary, the court may at any time enter an order permitting the discovery otherwise prohibited by this subdivision if the court finds, on the basis of the supporting and opposing affidavits presented, that the plaintiff has established that there is a substantial probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim pursuant to Section 3294.” (Ibid.)

            “In this context, a ‘substantial probability’ of prevailing on a claim for punitive damages means that it is ‘very likely’ that the plaintiff will prevail on such a claim or there is ‘a strong likelihood’ that the plaintiff will prevail on such a claim.” (Kerner v. Superior Court (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 84, 120.) “The purpose of this requirement is to protect defendants' financial privacy and prevent defendants from being pressured into settling nonmeritorious cases in order to avoid disclosure of their financial information.” (Ibid.)

DISCUSSION

            Altman is the alleged owner of the Whittier property. (FACC ¶ 16.) Padilla and Flores seek punitive damages against Altman for his alleged role in trespassing onto their property, for hiring an armed guard to escalate the situation, and for purportedly demonstrating a disregard for their wellbeing.

            a. Trespass

            Padilla and Flores begin their argument by pointing out that Altman knew of the encroachment problem and intentionally trespassed onto their property. (Mtn. 4:17-7:26.) However, the mere commission of a tort such as trespass is insufficient to justify punitive damages. None of the arguments in the motion or the cited evidence indicates malice, oppression, or fraud. At most, Altman was aware of the property lines and disregarded them to proceed with the construction. This is a plain tort with no aggravating circumstances warranting punitive damages. Thus, the alleged intentional trespass does not support a “substantial probability” or “strong likelihood” of succeeding on the claim for punitive damages. (See Kerner, supra, 206 Cal.App.4th at p. 120.)

            b. Armed Security

            Next, Padilla and Flores argue that Altman intentionally hired armed security to escalate a volatile situation. (Mtn. 7:26-8:15.) However, the cited evidence does not demonstrate any malice, oppression, or fraud. Altman’s testimony shows that he was aware of an altercation in which the police were called. Thus, Altman testified that he “call[ed] Armed Elite Services to request their guard at the site to protect the workers.” (Mtn. 8:1-6.) This is not egregious or aggravating. Hiring security after learning of a serious confrontation is arguably a reasonable response. Even the motion acknowledges that Altman “understood at that moment that the situation was sufficiently contentious that armed professional security was needed before he sent workers back the next day.” (Mtn. 8:4-6.)

            Padilla and Flores argue that Altman “did not try to resolve the dispute through discussion, seek a court order, or pause the project.” (Mtn. 8:6-11.) Instead, he hired armed security, which Padilla and Flores claim “was a choice made in conscious disregard of the homeowners' rights.” (Ibid.) However, the fact that Altman did not take a more prudent approach to the situation does not mean he engaged in malice, oppression, or fraud. There is nothing particularly egregious about hiring security after learning of an altercation involving the police. The fact that the guard may have subsequently harmed Padilla or Flores does not prove that Altman acted with malice, oppression, or fraud in hiring security. There is no evidence that Altman hired security with the intent to harm. Even the testimony cited in the motion shows that Altman was concerned for the workers and had no reason to assume that Campbell would act inappropriately. (See Mtn. 10:27-11:3.)

            Therefore, the fact that Altman hired Armed Elite Services does not support a “substantial probability” or “strong likelihood” of succeeding on the claim for punitive damages. (See Kerner, supra, 206 Cal.App.4th at p. 120.)

            c. Concern for Padilla

            The motion argues that Altman demonstrated a disregard for Padilla’s wellbeing: “When Altman arrived at the property after the February 22 incident, he did not ask about Ms. Padilla. He did not ask whether she had been hurt. He was asked at deposition whether, when he arrived and saw that Campbell was armed, he criticized Campbell or told him he should not have been armed. His answer was ‘No.’ His explanation: ‘When I arrived on the site, I was mostly concerned for his well-being because he was visibly bleeding and injured from being attacked by the neighbor. So I was concerned about his injuries and I was surprised that he didn't press charges.’ (Altman Depo., Vol. I, p. 78.).” (Mtn. 8:20-26.)

            This testimony does not demonstrate malice, oppression, or fraud. To the contrary, Altman testified that he was concerned about Campbell, the guard, because Campbell was visibly injured. The fact that Altman did not express equal concern for Padilla, the other person allegedly injured in the altercation, does not mean Altman acted with malice, oppression, or fraud. There is no evidence that Altman was aware of Padilla’s alleged injuries at the time, intended for Padilla to be harmed, prevented Padilla from obtaining medical treatment, or otherwise caused Padilla’s alleged injuries. Padilla and Flores merely take issue with Altman’s subjective reaction after the incident had already occurred. This is not sufficient to establish a “substantial probability” or “strong likelihood” of succeeding on the claim for punitive damages. (See Kerner, supra, 206 Cal.App.4th at p. 120.)     

            d. Condemning Campbell’s Actions

            Padilla and Flores further take issue with the fact that Altman did not sufficiently condemn Campbell’s statements and actions, and even suggested that Campbell should have pressed charges against Padilla. (Mtn. 9:7-10:24.) As with above, Altman’s subjective reaction after the fact does not show that he engaged in malice, oppression, or fraud. As the motion acknowledges, Altman testified that he witnessed Campbell’s injuries. The motion also acknowledges that Altman testified he was unaware Campbell had thrown something at Padilla: “He answered that he had not known about the wood-throwing at the time.” (Mtn. 9:23-25.) Thus, it was arguably reasonable for Altman to believe that Padilla or Flores were in the wrong. The evidence does not show that Altman confirmed Campbell had engaged in wrongdoing and then ratified or encouraged it.

            Moreover, Altman did testify that he would have preferred if Campbell had not been involved in the situation, and testified that he does not “condone throwing boards at each other.” (Plntf.’s Ex. K at 88:4-11, 185:10-12.) This is the opposite of approving or ratifying Campbell’s alleged actions. Altman is not guilty of malice, oppression, or fraud merely because he did not condemn Campbell to the degree that Padilla and Flores would prefer. This is not sufficient to establish a “substantial probability” or “strong likelihood” of succeeding on the claim for punitive damages. (See Kerner, supra, 206 Cal.App.4th at p. 120.)     

             In sum, Padilla and Flores have failed to meet the showing required under Code of Civil Procedure section 3295. Therefore, their request to conduct financial discovery must be denied.

CONCLUSION

            Cross-Complainants Yolanda Padilla’s and Jonathan Flores’ motion to compel financial discovery is DENIED.

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

TENDA TECHNOLOGY, INC., et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  24STCV33669

  Hearing Date:  July 10, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant deng ping quan’s motion to quash service of summons

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On December 19, 2024, Plaintiff Marco Davalos filed this employment action against Defendants Tenda Technology, Inc. (Tenda), Deng Ping Quan (Quan), and Will Wang (Wang).

            Wang was personally served on June 24, 2025.

Tenda was served on October 31, 2025 through personal delivery to the California Secretary of State, as authorized by the Court’s order on October 29, 2025.

            Quan was served by publication in the San Gabriel Valley Tribune as of November 3, 2025, as authorized by the Court’s order on October 3, 2025.

            On March 5, 2026, the Court entered default against all three Defendants.

            On June 5, 2026, Quan filed the instant motion to quash service of summons. Plaintiff filed an opposition on June 26, 2026. Quan filed a reply on July 2, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

“[T]he court in which an action is pending has jurisdiction over a party from the time summons is served on him as provided by Chapter 4 (commencing with Section 413.10).” (Code Civ. Proc, § 410.50(a).) “[A] court acquires jurisdiction over a party by proper service of process or by that party's general appearance.” (In re Jennifer O. (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 539, 547.) Actual notice of a lawsuit is not a substitute for proper service of process. (Abers v. Rohrs (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 1199, 1206.) A defendant may serve and file a notice of motion to quash service of summons on the ground that the court lacks jurisdiction. (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10(a).)

DISCUSSION

            The sole basis of Quan’s motion is that Quan was not served according to the Hague Convention. (Mtn. 2:20-3:10.) However, the Convention “shall not apply where the address of the person to be served with the document is not known.” (People v. Mendocino County Assessor's Parcel No. 056-500-09 (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 120, 125.) “Hence, the Convention does not apply to situations in which the whereabouts of the defendant cannot be ascertained despite reasonable diligence.” (Ibid.)

            “The only method of service under California law which does not require the transmission of documents abroad, and consequently does not implicate the Hague Service Convention, is service of summons by publication where the party's address remains unknown during the publication period despite the exercise of reasonable diligence.” (Kott v. Superior Court (1996) 45 Cal.App.4th 1126, 1136.) Thus, where a trial court permits service by publication, and the defendant’s whereabouts remain unknown throughout the publication period, the Convention is not implicated. (People v. Mendocino County, supra, 58 Cal.App.4th at p. 125.)

            Here, the Court granted Plaintiff’s request to serve Quan by publication because Plaintiff established that Quan could not be located through reasonable diligence. Quan was in fact not located throughout the publication period. Thus, the Hague Convention is not implicated. The service was proper. 

            In reply, Quan argues that Plaintiff never attempted to locate him in China even though Quan has never concealed the fact that he resides in China. However, Quan cites no evidence that Plaintiff actually knew or should have known that Quan was in China. Plaintiff is not imputed with knowledge of Quan’s whereabouts just because Quan did not actively conceal his location. The law does not require plaintiffs to be clairvoyant. The Court already found that Quan could not be located despite reasonable diligence when it granted Plaintiff’s application to serve Quan through publication. The application was supported by the thorough declaration of Plaintiff’s counsel. (See 10/02/25 Declaration of Alvin Chang.)

            Therefore, the Court accepts that Quan’s whereabouts were unknown to Plaintiff and could not be ascertained with reasonable diligence. As the court in People v. Mendocino County stated: “The trial court granted the request [for publication] and, during the publication period, appellant's whereabouts continued to remain unknown. The Hague Service Convention was therefore not implicated.” (People v. Mendocino County, supra, 58 Cal.App.4th at p. 125.) This is the precise situation here. Accordingly, service by publication was proper, and Plaintiff was not required to comply with the Hague Convention.

CONCLUSION

            Defendant Deng Ping Quan’s motion to quash service of summons is DENIED.

BACKGROUND



            On December 19, 2024, Plaintiff
Marco Davalos filed this employment action against Defendants Tenda Technology,
Inc. (Tenda), Deng Ping Quan (Quan), and Will Wang (Wang).



            Wang was personally served on June

Tenda was served on October 31, 2025
through personal delivery to the California Secretary of State, as authorized

            Quan was served by publication in

default against all three Defendants.



            On June 4, 2026, Tenda and Quan
filed the instant motion to set aside the default. Plaintiff filed an
opposition on June 26, 2026. Defendants filed a reply on July 2, 2026.



LEGAL STANDARD



“The court may, upon any terms as may be
just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment,
dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or
her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., §
473(b).) Because the law favors resolution on the merits, doubts are resolved
in favor of the party requesting relief, and only “slight evidence” is needed
to justify relief. (Caldwell v. Methodist Hospital (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th
1521, 1524.) “[C]ourts have often granted relief pursuant to the discretionary
relief provision of section 473 if no prejudice to the opposing party will
ensue.” (Comunidad en Accion v. Los Angeles City Council (2013) 219
Cal.App.4th 1116, 1132-1133.)



DISCUSSION



            Tenda terminated its operations in
January 2024, approximately one year prior to the filing of this lawsuit. (Quan
Decl. ¶ 11.) All of Tenda’s U.S. employees were laid off, and Tenda does not
maintain a physical address. (Id., ¶¶ 12-13.) Quan has been Tenda’s sole
acting agent, but he resides in China. (Id., ¶¶ 3-4, 14.) Quan first
discovered this lawsuit in March 2026 upon being informed by Tenda’s former
CFO. (Id., ¶¶ 21, 25.) Being in China, Quan has faced difficulty finding
legal representation for this action. (Id., ¶ 27.)



            The record sufficiently demonstrates
that the default was caused by Defendants’ mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or
excusable neglect. Plaintiff does not explain how he would be prejudiced from
setting aside the default. Plaintiff only argues that he “will be prejudiced if
he is not reimbursed for the amount of time and costs expended in obtaining
Defendants’ entry of default.” (Opp. 8:17-18.) However, that is a separate
issue and not the type of prejudice that would prevent setting aside the
default. 



            In sum, the Court finds that the
default was caused by Defendants’ mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable
neglect. The Court further finds that Plaintiff would not be prejudiced from
setting aside the default. Therefore, relief is warranted.



CONCLUSION



            Defendants Tenda Technology, Inc.’s
and Deng Ping Quan’s motion to set aside default is GRANTED. 


Case Number: 26STCV00679    Hearing Date: July 10, 2026    Dept: 406

 

TUYET PHAM,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

CONTINUEM, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  26STCV00679

  Hearing Date:  July 10, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendants’ motions to compel further responses (CRS# 9641, 7450, 4522, 7161, 8880)

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On January 8, 2026, Plaintiff Tuyet Pham filed this action against Defendants ContinuEM; ContinuEM Physicians Group Corporation, Inc.; ContinuEM Care Institute, Inc.; Freddy Sotelo, MD; and Bobby Massoudian, MD. The complaint alleges retaliation in violation of the Labor Code and wrongful termination in violation of public policy. Plaintiff alleges that he was fired after filing a class action wage lawsuit against Defendants.

            On June 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant five motions to compel further responses. Plaintiff filed an omnibus opposition on June 26, 2026. Defendants filed replies on July 2, 2026.

            The motion CRS# 8880 (Requests for Production) is advanced and heard on this date.

LEGAL STANDARD

Upon receiving responses to its discovery requests, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if the responses are incomplete or evasive, or objections are without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a), 2031.310(a), 2033.290(a).)

 

DISCUSSION

I. Special Interrogatories (CRS# 4522)

            A party may not propound more than 35 special interrogatories unless the quantity is justified by the needs of the case. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.030-2030.050.) A court may restrict discovery that is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative, or that is unduly burdensome or expensive in relation to the needs of the case. (Id., § 2019.030(a).)

            Here, the Court finds that 238 SROGs are excessive and overly burdensome in relation to the issues in litigation. Defendants have not demonstrated the need for additional SROGs. While Defendants are entitled to find evidence pertaining to Plaintiff’s contentions, the complaint is only 46 paragraphs long and primarily alleges that Plaintiff was terminated as retaliation for filing a class action lawsuit. Plaintiff also alleges that Defendants fabricated patient complaints as a pretext. This is a relatively straightforward theory which does not warrant 238 SROGs.

            Therefore, Plaintiff shall respond to the first 35 SROGs.

II. Form Interrogatories (CRS# 9641, 7450)

            A party may propound “[a]ny additional number of official form interrogatories . . . that are relevant to the subject matter of the pending action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.030(a)(2).) A party “is entitled to demand answers to its interrogatories, as a matter of right . . . [and] the burden of justifying any objection and failure to respond remains at all times with the party resisting an interrogatory.” (Williams v. Sup. Ct. (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541.)

            Here, Plaintiff has failed to justify his refusal to respond to the FROGs. FROGs are standardized interrogatories drafted by the Judicial Council, and Plaintiff does not identify any FROGs as improper or irrelevant.

            Therefore, Plaintiff shall respond to the FROGs.

 

 

III. Requests for Production (CRS# 8880)

            The party seeking production of documents bears the initial burden of showing good cause through a fact-specific showing of relevance. (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98.) Once this showing is made, the burden shifts to the responding party to justify any objections. (Ibid.)

            Defendants fail to establish good cause for the information sought in the RFPs. Defendants’ separate statement only argues that Plaintiff’s objections are improper. But Defendants bear the initial burden of demonstrating good cause before the burden shifts to Plaintiff to justify his objections. Some of the requests are also facially improper. For example, RFP No. 9 seeks “ALL non-privileged COMMUNICATIONS between YOU and any attorney regarding the CLASS ACTION.” Communications between Plaintiff and his attorney regarding the class action would necessarily be privileged.

            Therefore, the motion is denied as to RFPs.

IV. Requests for Admission (CRS# 7161)

            The RFAs are excessive and unduly burdensome in that they seek to force Plaintiff to make conclusions and evidentiary admissions before discovery has been completed. For example, over a dozen RFAs ask Plaintiff to admit that “YOU have no evidence of” various contentions made in the complaint, or some variation thereof. Therefore, the RFAs are disproportionate to the needs of the case.

            Therefore, the motion is denied as to RFAs.

CONCLUSION

            Defendants’ motions to compel further responses are GRANTED as to the Form Interrogatories and the first 35 Special Interrogatories and DENIED in all other respects.

plaintiffs’ motion to enforce settlement

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On July 13, 2018, Plaintiffs Carlous Parker and Renee Parker filed this action against Defendants Parker’s Towing & Salvage, Inc., et al.

            On June 5, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion to enforce settlement.

DISCUSSION

            The proof of service indicates that Plaintiffs served the motion by email to attorney Lorraine Anderson. Thus, proper service requires that Plaintiffs personally serve the motion on Defendants directly.

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiffs’ motion to enforce settlement is DENIED without prejudice to Plaintiffs filing a properly served motion.
