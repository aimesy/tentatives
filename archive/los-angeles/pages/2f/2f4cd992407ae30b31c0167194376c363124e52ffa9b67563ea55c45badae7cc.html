[TENTATIVE] RULING ON DEMURRER TO VERIFIED PETITION FOR WRIT OF MANDATE

 

Dept. 836 (Hon. Curtis A. Kin)

 

 

 

 

 

 

            Respondent County of Los Angeles, erroneously sued as “Los Angeles County IHSS,” (“County”) demurs to the Verified Petition for Writ of Mandate.  The demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

 

I.       BACKGROUND

 

Petitioner Susanbar Margaryan alleges that she is a 79-year-old disabled woman with advanced Alzheimer’s disease, cortical atrophy, and severe cognitive impairments (Pet. at 1:18-19.) On September 1, 2023, County denied petitioner’s request for protective supervision. (Pet. at 3:7-8.) On July 23, 2024, respondent California Department of Social Services (“CDSS”) adopted the County’s decision. (Pet. at 3:9.) On January 2, 2025, petitioner filed a rehearing request with various medical documents to support petitioner’s cognitive decline; petitioner alleges there was good cause to consider the late-filed rehearing request because the medical documents were not available until late 2024. (Pet. at 3:10-14, 16 & Exs. 1-3.) On February 21, 2025, CDSS denied petitioner’s rehearing request as untimely. (Pet. at 3:15.)

 

Petitioner alleges that CDSS violated her due process rights when it denied her rehearing request for protective supervision on the grounds that the decision was arbitrary; ignored new, material evidence; and failed to safeguard a vulnerable adult under Welfare & Insurance Code § 1230.21 (Pet. at 2:5-12.) Petitioner requests, inter alia, that the Court set aside CDSS’s decision and order CDSS to grant petitioner’s rehearing request. (Pet. at 6:10-16, Prayer for Relief ¶ 1.)

 

          On July 23, 2025, petitioner filed a Verified Petition for Writ of Mandate (CCP § 1094.5). On July 30, 2026, County filed a demurrer to the Petition. As of August 3, 2026, no opposition has been received to the instant demurrer.

 

II.      DISCUSSION

 

A.           Lack of Legal Representation

 

As a preliminary matter, County points out that it had attempted to meet and confer in good faith with petitioner in compliance with CCP § 430.41. (Brenner Decl. ¶¶ 2-4, 9) Any communication made by petitioner was through Mary Khamtrashyan, who identified herself as petitioner’s daughter and “authorized representative.” (Brenner Decl. ¶¶ 5-7.) Ultimately, no substantive meet-and-confer conference was held because Khamtrashyan withdrew the agreement to hold a telephonic conference on April 24, 2026, preferring any communication to transpire through email. (Brenner Decl. ¶¶ 7-8.)

 

The issue here is that, even as an “authorized representative,” Khamtrashyan does not have the authority to represent petitioner. (See Drake v. Superior Court (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 1826; see also Hansen v. Hansen (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 618, 621 [reasoning that a representative of the decedent’s estate may not appear in propria persona on behalf of the estate in matters outside the probate proceedings].) Petitioner concedes that she suffers from cognitive impairments (Pet. at 1:18-19), and it is unclear to the Court whether petitioner has the mental capacity to represent herself in this action. There is no indication that anyone has attempted to apply to be petitioner’s guardian ad litem or has pursued a conservatorship action through probate court. Further, even if a guardian ad litem or conservator were appointed, neither of them would be permitted to appear in pro per on petitioner’s behalf for the purpose of litigating this action, as so doing would violated the prohibition on the unauthorized practice of law. (Drake,  21 Cal.App.4th 1826; see also Hansen, 114 Cal.App.4th at 621.)

 

Accordingly, because the Court harbors genuine doubt and concern as to whether petitioner is capable of prosecuting this action on her own accord, the Court shall issue an Order to Show Cause as to why this matter should not be stayed or dismissed for petitioner’s lack of capacity and legal representation. 

 

B.           The Petition Suffers from a Defect of Parties

County also demurs to the petition pursuant to CCP § 430.10(d) on the ground that it is not the appropriate party. (Demurrer at 5.) The Court agrees that County is improperly named as a respondent in this petition.

 

A party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may file a special demurrer on the ground that there is a defect or misjoinder of parties. (CCP § 430.10(d); Union Carbide Corp. v. Superior Court (1977) 36 Cal. 3d 15, 21-22.) This defect must appear on the face of the complaint or in matters judicially noticed. (Royal Surplus Lines Ins. Co., Inc. v. Ranger Ins. Co. (2002) 100 Cal. App. 4th 193, 198.) Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 389, “defect” means a necessary or indispensable party is absent from the action, whereas “misjoinder” means that a party has been improperly joined to an action.

 

Here, as shown by the petition, CDSS is the agency that denied petitioner’s rehearing request on February 21, 2025. (Pet. at 3:15, 19 [Rehearing Denial Letter]) and appears to be an appropriate respondent.  By contrast, the petition is devoid of any allegations that would support a claim against County. Accordingly, as alleged, the only proper respondent is CDSS, not County.  

 

Accordingly, because the petition suffers from a defect of parties and County is improperly named as a respondent, the demurrer is SUSTAINED per CCP § 430.10(d).  County, however, concedes that it stands in the position of a real party in interest (Demurrer at 8:9-11), and the Court will provide petitioner with the opportunity to so amend.

 

III.     CONCLUSION

 

            The demurrer to the Petition is SUSTAINED per CCP § 430.10(d) with leave to amend. Petitioner shall file an amended petition within 20 days of receipt of this order, removing County as a respondent and identifying it as a real party in interest.

 

Further, the hearing on the Court’s Order to Show as to why this matter should not be stayed or dismissed for petitioner’s lack of capacity and legal representation shall be held on September 24, 2026, at 1:30 p.m., in Department 836.

Dept. 836 (Hon. Curtis A. Kin)

 

 

 

 

 

 

Petitioner Brian Yun Kim petitions for a writ of mandate directing respondent Department of Motor Vehicles (“Department” or “DMV”) to set aside the order suspending petitioner’s driving privileges. For the reasons stated below, the Petition is DENIED.

 

I.       BACKGROUND

 

A.           Petitioner’s Arrest

 

On October 19, 2024, at around 1:06 a.m., California Highway Patrol (“CHP”) Officers Ortiz and Welch were traveling along the US-101 Highway, north of Lankershim Avenue in the No. 2 Lane when they observed petitioner’s vehicle drift left into the No. 1 Lane. (AR 9, 16.) Officer Ortiz further observed petitioner’s vehicle brake and drift to the right into the No. 3 Lane. (AR 9, 16.) The MVARS video also captured petitioner’s vehicle weaving in the No. 2 Lane before the officers initiated an enforcement stop. (MVARS at 01:07:10 – 01:07:25.) Officer Welch instructed petitioner to exit the freeway using the patrol vehicle’s public address system, but, because petitioner was slow to comply with this order, Officer Welch had to repeat the instructions.[1] (AR 9, 16; MVARS at 01:07:25 – 01:11:15.)

 

Upon making contact with petitioner, Officer Ortiz observed the strong odor of alcohol emitting from petitioner’s breath, petitioner’s red, watery eyes, and his slurred speech. (AR 7, 14, 16.) When questioned, petitioner admitted to having consumed alcohol earlier in the evening. (See AR 16.) During the administration of the Field Sobriety Tests, petitioner displayed impairment and was unable to complete them as explained and demonstrated. (AR 14, 15, 16.) Officer Ortiz requested that petitioner submit to a Preliminary Alcohol Screening test, but petitioner declined to submit to that test. (AR 14, 15.) Due to petitioner’s objective signs of intoxication, his poor driving, his poor performance during the Field Sobriety Tests, and his admission of drinking, Officer Ortiz determined that petitioner had operated a motor vehicle under the influence of alcohol and that it would be unsafe for petitioner to continue to operate the vehicle, resulting in Ortiz placing petitioner under arrest at 1:35 a.m. for violating Vehicle Code § 23152(a). (AR 16-17)

 

While still at the scene, Officer Ortiz advised petitioner of the requirement to submit to a chemical test but did not advise him of the potential consequences of not submitting at that point. (AR 17; MVARS at 01:40:20 – 01:41:40.) Petitioner initially agreed to submit to a blood test (MVARS at 01:41:40 – 01:41:45.) Immediately after agreeing, petitioner asked about the difference between the blood and breath test; the differences were explained to him, and the officers informed petitioner that he could think about his choice. (MVARS at 01:40:45 – 01:43:00.) When re-questioned near 2 a.m., Officer Welch indicated that a refusal to agree to a chemical test would require them to procure a warrant, and, after being informed that a chemical test was required, petitioner expressed a willingness to submit to a blood test. (MVARS at 01:58:00 – 02:00:45.) When informed that he would need to sign a medical consent form before blood could be drawn, petitioner expressed dissatisfaction and refused to comply. (MVARS at 02:00:45 – 02:01:10.) Specifically, petitioner stated that he was “not going to submit to anything until I get my lawyer.” (MVARS at 02:01:00 – 02:01:10.) Officer Welch advised petitioner that he did not have a right to talk with an attorney and informed him of the consequences for refusing to submit to a chemical test. (MVARS at 02:01:10 – 02:01:40.) Thereafter, the officers transported petitioner to CHP’s Central LA station. (AR 17.)

 

After arriving at the station, an officer read to petitioner the Chemical Test Admonition at around 2:23 a.m. (AR 8, 17; MVARS at 02:23:50 – 02:26:48.) After being read the admonishment, petitioner remained quiet when asked if he would take a breath or blood chemical test, which resulted in the officers informing him that silence amounted to a refusal. (AR 8, 17; MVARS at 02:26:48 – 02:27:10.) Based on petitioner’s decision to remain silent, Officer Ortiz submitted an affidavit in support of a search warrant for a non-consensual blood draw, which was approved. (AR 17-18; MVARS at 02:27:10 – 02:27:30.) Officer Ortiz then transported petitioner to East L.A. Doctor’s Hospital at around 4:15 a.m., where he observed a medical professional draw blood from petitioner’s arm before recording the blood draw and booking it into evidence (AR 17, 19-21.)

 

On October 19, 2024, petitioner was issued a DMV DS-367 form, which indicated that his license was being suspended for Chemical Test Refusal. (AR 11.)

 

B.           Administrative Per Se Hearing

 

Prior to the Administrative Per Se (“APS”) hearing, on October 29, 2024, respondent requested records from the CHP, namely, the DMV DS-367 form, any chemical test results, and the arrest report (AR 113). On November 18, 2024, petitioner subpoenaed audio and video records from the CHP. (AR 70-74.)  (NAR 131.)

 

On February 6, 2025, an APS Hearing was held in this matter before DMV Hearing Officer Dennis Haley. (AR 48.) At the start of the APS Hearing, the hearing officer indicated that he would be acting as a neutral factfinder and that he was prohibited from acting as an advocate for the DMV. (AR 48-49.) The hearing officer then read the issues of the hearing into the record.[2] (AR 49-50) Petitioner was not present for the hearing but was represented by counsel. (AR 49.)

 

The hearing officer admitted three exhibits into evidence: (1) Exhibit #1 [Age 21 & Older Officer’s Statement (DS-367)] (AR 7-12, 50); (2) Exhibit # 2 [Driving Under the Influence Arrest and Investigation Report by Officer Ortiz] (AR 13-26, 50); and (3) Exhibit #3 [petitioner’s driving record] (AR 41-44, 50). Petitioner did not object to the admission of these exhibits. (AR 50.) Petitioner’s counsel requested to admit the MVARS video into the record. (AR 50.) She sought to do so because she argued that the recording showed that petitioner had expressed a willingness to submit to a blood test on two separate instances. (AR 53-54, 56-57.) Because the hearing officer did not have a copy of the video, he indicated that he would neither move the recording into evidence nor accept this argument unless he received a copy of the recording within five business days. (AR 54.) Petitioner’s counsel provided a digital copy of the recording via DropBox to the hearing officer, and the hearing officer instructed petitioner to send a physical disc to the DMV’s Oxnard office. (AR 59-61, 63-64.)

 

C.           The Decision

On February 11, 2025, the hearing officer issued his Notification of Findings and Decision (“Decision”), suspending petitioner’s driver license for refusing to submit to a chemical test.  (AR 3-5.) In the Decision, the hearing officer first found that Officer Ortiz[3] lawfully detained petitioner because there was a reasonable articulable suspicion to detain petitioner based on the observation of petitioner’s behavior, which consisted of weaving while driving.  (AR 3.) The Decision also found that petitioner was lawfully arrested based on petitioner’s objective signs of intoxication (slurred speech, red and/or water eyes, odor of alcohol, and unsteady gait), as well as petitioner’s unsatisfactory performance on several field sobriety tests. (AR 3-4.)

 

The Decision also found that petitioner was properly advised that his driving privileges would be suspended if he refused or failed to complete one of the required chemical tests. (AR 4.) With respect to the argument by petitioner’s counsel that the MVARS video footage shows that petitioner had expressed a willingness to submit to a blood test on two separate occasions, the Decision concluded that this was not the case. (AR 4.) As for the first instance, the Decision indicated that petitioner’s initial indication of willingness was not formalized beyond his initial statement. As for the second instance, the Decision reasoned that any willingness was contradicted by petitioner’s refusal to sign any consent form without his attorney. (AR 4.) The Decision further points to the DS-367 form that reflects petitioner’s documented silence when asked to submit to a chemical test. (AR 4.) Thus, the Decision contained the determination that petitioner refused to submit to a chemical test in that he did not demonstrate “a consistent willingness to comply with chemical testing procedures” because his “initial verbal indications of compliance were not sustained in subsequent interactions.” (AR 4.)

 

Based on these findings, the Department imposed a one-year license suspension, effective February 24, 2025, through February 23, 2026.[4] (AR 2-3, 5.)

 D.          The Petition for Writ of Mandamus

 

On March 11, 2025, petitioner filed his Verified Petition for Writ of Mandate in the Kern County Superior Court. On August 8, 2025, the case was transferred to the Los Angeles County Superior Court.

 

On November 14, 2025, respondent filed its Answer. On March 13, 2026, petitioner filed his opening brief. On April 10, 2026, respondent filed its brief in opposition.  On April 27, 2026, petitioner filed his reply brief. The Court has received an electronic copy of the administrative record and an electronic copy of the MVARS recording.

 

II.        DISCUSSION

 

A.           Standard of Review

 

Under CCP § 1094.5(b), the pertinent issues are whether the respondent has proceeded without jurisdiction, whether there was a fair trial, and whether there was a prejudicial abuse of discretion. An abuse of discretion is established if the agency has not proceeded in the manner required by law, the decision is not supported by the findings, or the findings are not supported by the evidence. (CCP § 1094.5(b).) “[T]he party challenging the administrative decision bears the burden of convincing the court that the administrative findings are contrary to the weight of the evidence.” (Fukuda v. City of Angels (1999) 20 Cal.4th 805, 817; see also Bixby v. Pierno (1971) 4 Cal.3d 130, 139.)

 

When a driver petitions for a writ of mandate following an order suspending his driver’s license, the trial court is required to determine, based on its independent judgment, whether the weight of the evidence supports the administrative decision. (Lake v. Reed (1997) 16 Cal.4th 448, 456.) Under the independent judgment test, “the trial court not only examines the administrative record for errors of law, but also exercises its independent judgment upon the evidence disclosed in a limited trial de novo.” (Bixby, 4 Cal.3d at 143.) The Court must draw its own reasonable inferences from the evidence and make its own credibility determinations. (Morrison v. Housing Authority of the City of Los Angeles Board of Commissioners (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 860, 868.)  However, “[i]n exercising its independent judgment, a trial court must afford a strong presumption of correctness concerning the administrative findings, and the party challenging the administrative decision bears the burden of convincing the court that the administrative findings are contrary to the weight of the evidence.” (Fukuda, 20 Cal.4th at 817 [internal quotations omitted].)

  

B.           The Claim that the DMV Exceeded Its Authority in Failing to Set Aside the License Suspension was Waived

 

            Petitioner argues that the DMV exceeded its authority in obtaining arrest records and evidence against petitioner after petitioner had made a request for an APS hearing, because the Vehicle Code required the arresting officer to immediately forward the information to the DMV.  Thus, reasons petitioner, the suspension should have been set aside because the DMV had no evidence in its possession to support the suspension at the time petitioner had requested an APS Hearing. (Pet. Br. at 12-15.) Petitioner made no such argument during the APS Hearing and has accordingly waived it here. (See AR 49-65.)

 

            “Before seeking judicial review a party must show that he has made a full presentation to the administrative agency upon all issues of the case and at all prescribed stages of the administrative proceedings.” (Edgren v. Regents of University of California (1984) 158 Cal.App.3d 515, 520.) Because this argument was not first raised before the hearing officer, the Court finds that it was waived. In any event, even if petitioner had preserved this argument, it lacks merit.  Petitioner cites no legal authority for the novel proposition he advances here.  Indeed, in failing to address this argument on reply or respond to the DMV’s arguments in opposition, petitioner effectively concedes it is without merit.

 

C.           The Weight of the Evidence Supports a Finding that Petitioner was Lawfully Detained

 

Petitioner also argues that the weight of the evidence does not support a finding that he was lawfully detained.[5] (Pet. Br. at 15-17.) The Court disagrees.

 

“[U]nless a person is ‘lawfully arrested’. . . , he or she is not subject to license suspension or revocation” for failing to submit to a chemical test per Vehicle Code §§ 23612 and 13353. (Mercer v. Department of Motor Vehicles (1991) 53 Cal. 3d 753, 760; Gikas v. Zolin (1993) 6 Cal. 4th 841, 847-848 (1993) [“[F]or the DMV to suspend the driver's license, the underlying arrest must have been lawful.”]; Veh. Code § 23612(a)(1)(C) [“The testing shall be incidental to a lawful arrest. . . .”].) In order to conduct a lawful investigatory stop for the purpose of detaining a driver, an officer must have “reasonable suspicion that the driver has violated the law.” (People v. Wells (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1078, 1082.) Reasonable suspicion is “a particularized and objective basis for suspecting the particular person stopped of criminal activity,” and it “requires specific, articulable facts which, together with objective and reasonable inferences, form a basis for suspecting that a particular person is engaged in criminal conduct.” (People v. White (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 636, 641.) The standard for reasonable suspicion is less demanding than what is required to establish probable cause, but the circumstances must be reasonably consistent with criminal activity, not on mere curiosity, rumor, or hunch. (Arburn v. Department of Motor Vehicles (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 1480, 1484.)

 

“Weaving within a lane is a widely recognized characteristic of an intoxicated driver and recognizing a weaving driver is undoubtedly within the province of even the most junior officer.” (Arburn, 151 Cal.App.4th at 1485.) Multiple California courts have determined that:

 

“weaving” within a lane provides sufficient cause to conduct an investigatory stop. (See People v. Bracken (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th Supp. 1, 3–4 [weaving within lane for one-half mile]; People v. Perez (1985) 175 Cal.App.3d Supp. 8, 10–11 [“‘pronounced weaving’ within the lane” for three-quarters of a mile; citing additional cases holding that weaving from one lane to another justifies an investigatory stop]; People v. Perkins (1981) 126 Cal.App.3d Supp. 12, 14 [driving 20 miles per hour under speed limit and “weaving abruptly from one side of [the] lane to the other”]; see also People v. Russell (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 96, 104 [concluding summarily that fact that defendant was “drifting around in his lane” justified an investigatory stop].)

 

(Ibid.)

 

            Here, at around 1:06 a.m., on October 19, 2024, CHP Officers Ortiz and Welch observed petitioner’s vehicle drifting into the No. 1 Lane from the No. 2 Lane before overcorrecting by braking and drifting into the No. 3 Lane along the US-101 Highway. (AR 9, 16; MVARS at 01:07:05-01:07:15.) Thereafter, petitioner’s vehicle  can be observed weaving within the No. 2 Lane in a pronounced fashion by ping-ponging between the lane’s line boundaries before the officers initiate an enforcement stop.[6] (MVARS at 01:07:15 – 01:07:25.) Petitioner also denied that there was anything mechanically wrong with his vehicle. (AR 14.) Given petitioner’s repeated weaving movements occurring over a period of more than 20 seconds while traveling on the freeway after 1 a.m., it was reasonable for the officers to suspect that petitioner was impaired while driving.[7] Indeed, the officers had a duty to investigate what was causing petitioner’s vehicle to weave and whether his driving posed a safety risk. (Arburn, 151 Cal.App.4th at 1486; see also Brierton v. Department of Motor Vehicles (2005) 130 Cal.App.4th 499, 509.)

 

Moreover, the Court is unpersuaded by petitioner’s attempts to distinguish the present case from other cases in which courts have found that weaving supported an officer’s reasonable suspicion to conduct a traffic stop.  (See Reply at 7-9.)  Whatever differences there may be between the facts of those cases and this one is immaterial,[8] as petitioner’s weaving on the road provided ample reasonable suspicion in this case for law enforcement to stop him and investigate. Accordingly, the Court finds in its independent judgment that the weight of the evidence supports a finding that petitioner was lawfully detained.

 

D.          The Weight of the Evidence Supports a Finding that Petitioner Refused a Chemical Test After Being Arrested

 

Petitioner argues that he consented to a blood test and that refusing to sign a blood draw consent form did not amount to a chemical test refusal. (Pet. Br. at 18-20.) The Court disagrees.

 

“The law of implied consent mandates that an arrestee is required to submit to and complete one of the three tests upon their first having been offered to him by an arresting officer.” (Espinoza v. Shiomoto (2017) 10 Cal. App. 5th 85, 112.) “Consent which is not clear and unambiguous may be deemed a refusal. The determinative factor as to whether there is a refusal is not the arrestee’s subjective state of mind, but rather the objective, fair meaning to be distilled from his words and conduct.” (Carrey v. Department of Motor Vehicles (1986) 183 Cal.App.3d 1265, 1270.) Before a license may be suspended, a driver must be “notified of the consequences of refusal to submit.” (Hughey v. Department of Motor Vehicles (1991) 235 Cal.App.3d 752, 757-758; see also Munro v. Department of Motor Vehicles (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 41, 50-51 [“the Vehicle Code is clear that an arresting officer must instruct a suspected drunk driver of the consequences of refusing chemical testing . . ., and case law has consistently required arresting officers to at least attempt to provide that mandatory admonition even to uncooperative suspects.”].)

 

Petitioner contends that he agreed to submit to a blood test when first asked (Pet. Br. at 18:13-16), but the MVARS recording reveals that petitioner did not unambiguously agree to submit to that test. Immediately after stating this, petitioner asked about the difference between the breath and blood chemical test; the differences were explained to him, and the officers gave petitioner an opportunity to think about his decision before agreeing. (MVARS at 01:40:45 – 01:43:00.) Petitioner’s consent to the required test was thus not clear and unambiguous.

 

Next, petitioner asserts that his reaffirmation to submit to a blood test should qualify as consent and that his refusal to sign a consent form should not undermine that reaffirmation. (Pet. Br. at 18:16-22, 19:9-20:4; see MVARS at 01:57:58 – 02:02:48.) However, petitioner’s indication that he would not sign the blood draw consent form precludes his statement that he would take a blood test from constituting clear and unambiguous consent. (Carrey, 183 Cal.App.3d at 1270.) As held in Carrey, a driver’s “refusal to sign the medical consent form was the pragmatic equivalent of a refusal to consent to the procedure involved,” and a driver’s “purported willingness to take the blood test was meaningless in absence of his signature on the form.” (Id. at 1271.) While the Court finds that petitioner did not clearly and unambiguously consent to the required chemical test at this point, the Court acknowledges that petitioner was not sufficiently advised of the consequences of his refusal because the officers had not recited the entire Chemical Test Admonition at this juncture. Thus, petitioner’s refusal at that moment did not trigger a license suspension.

 

However, the officers eventually read the Chemical Test Admonition to petitioner at the station, at which point petitioner was fully warned of the consequences of his refusal and asked whether he would submit to either a breath test or a blood test. (AR 8, 17; MVARS at 02:23:50 – 02:27:30.) Petitioner remained silent when asked whether he would submit to either test, which constituted a refusal to submit to a chemical test within the meaning of Vehicle Code § 23612.

 

            Accordingly, the Court concludes in its independent judgment that the weight of the evidence supports the finding that petitioner refused to submit to a chemical test after being lawfully arrested for driving under the influence of alcohol and fully informed of the consequences of refusing to do so.

 

E.           Petitioner was Sufficiently Advised that the Right to an Attorney Did Not Apply to the Chemical Test

 

Petitioner also argues that the officers failed to sufficiently explain to him that he did not have a right to speak with a lawyer before submitting to a chemical test. (Pet. Br. at 20-21.)  At the scene, the officer informed petitioner that he did not have a right to speak to an attorney before submitting to a chemical test and that, by refusing, the officers would proceed with the test after procuring a search warrant regardless of petitioner’s position. (MVARS at 2:00:48 – 2:01:48.) At the station, the officers also advised petitioner that staying silent amounted to a refusal and read him the Chemical Test Admonition, which indicated he did not have a right to consult with an attorney in connection with taking the required chemical test. (MVARS at 02:23:43 – 2:27:53.) All of these statements sufficiently dispel any possible confusion petitioner could claim over whether he was properly informed about his right to consult with an attorney. (See Goodman v. Orr (1971) 19 Cal.App.3d 845, 855 [“‘The rule only requires that the officer erase any possible confusion caused the allegedly drunken laymen by the officer’s own statements.’”].)

 

            Accordingly, the record shows that petitioner was clearly and fully informed about his right to consult an attorney and his obligation to submit to a chemical test.

 

III.      CONCLUSION

 

The petition for writ of mandate is DENIED. Pursuant to Local Rule 3.231(n), respondent Department of Motor Vehicles shall prepare, serve, and ultimately file a proposed judgment.

Petitioner suggests these cases stand for the proposition that movement within one’s lane is not sufficient to support an officer’s reasonable suspicions. (Pet. Br. at 17:8-13.) However, those cases involved alien smuggling (not driving under the influence), and the Court determined that movements within one’s lane did not support the inference of that particular criminal activity, i.e., alien smuggling.

 

[8]           For example, while this case did not involve a near collision with a curb (or any other object or driver) as in Arburn (see Reply at 7:12-18), the lack of any near collision does not diminish the potential safety risk that an impaired driver may pose to other drivers, particularly where, as here, the MVARS show many other vehicles and drivers were on the freeway with petitioner.


Case Number: 25STCV35056    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: 836

APPLICATION FOR RIGHT TO ATTACH ORDER

                                    

Date:                           8/11/26 (1:30 PM)                                         

Case:                           Shahram Pascal Abrar v. Tayfun Paul Kadioglu., et al.

                                    (25STCV35056)

  

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Plaintiff Shahram Pascal Abrar’s UNOPPPOSED application for right to attach order as to defendant Paloma Palm Springs LLC is DENIED.

 

Although he contends otherwise, plaintiff Shahram Pascal Abrar filed only one notice of application and one application for writ of attachment on May 6, 2026, even though his memorandum of points and authority claims to seek writ of attachment against defendants (1) Tayfun Paul Kadioglu, (2) Paloma Palm Springs LLC, and (3) Bellevue Oasis LLC (see Notice of Mtn. 1) and his Reply Brief claims he filed “two separate 186-page Applications for Right to Attach Order—one directed to BELLEVUE OASIS LLC and one directed to PALOMA PALM SPRINGS” (Reply at 3-4).

 

As required by CCP § 484.050, a notice of application for writ of attachment “shall inform” a defendant against whom attachment is sought various required information.  Here, the single notice of application is directed only to defendant Paloma Palm Springs LLC.  Defendants Kadioglu and Bellevue Oasis LLC have thus not received proper notice as required by CCP § 484.050.  Because a writ of attachment is a special statutory remedy, courts have found that the statutory requirements must be strictly followed. (Gow v. Marshall (1891) 90 Cal. 565, 567; Yosemite Growers Co-op Ass'n v. Case-Swayne Co. (1946) 73 Cal.App.2d 806, 811.) Plaintiff did not comply with the statutory requirements, and the Court accordingly considers only plaintiff’s request for attachment against defendant Paloma Palm Springs LLC.

 

With respect to Paloma Palm Springs LLC, plaintiff moves for a right to attach order in the amount of $694,751.00 based on theory that it is an alter ego of defendant Kadioglu. By way of background, plaintiff and Kadioglu entered into a residential lease agreement on May 23, 2024 in connection with property located at 5227 Los Hermosos Way, Los Angeles, California 90027, wherein Kadioglu agreed to pay plaintiff monthly rent of $18,500. (Abrar Decl. ¶¶ 2-3 & Ex. A.) Per an addendum to the lease agreement, plaintiff permitted Kadioglu to use the subject property for short-term rentals, and, in exchange, plaintiff would be entitled to collect the income derived from these rentals and retain a management fee of 30%. (Abrar Decl. ¶ & Ex. A [Addendum No. 2].)  Plaintiff claims that he has been damaged by Kadioglu’s failure to repay outstanding rent and to honor the lease addendum, as well as by causing plaintiff to incur regulatory fines and HOA penalties. (Abrar Decl. ¶¶ 4, 7, 13 & Exs. B, G.) Plaintiff claims that Kadioglu used defendant Paloma Palm Springs LLC as a “conduit[] or repositor[y] for short-term-rental revenues connected to the [subject property].” (Mtn. at 3:24-26, 4:9-13; Abrar Decl. & Ex. C [Verified Compl.].)

 

Pursuant to CCP § 484.090, in order to issue a writ of attachment, the Court must find, among other things that plaintiff has established the probable validity of its claim against the defendant.  A claim has “probable validity” if it is shown to be more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.  (CCP § 481.190.)  Here, while attachment may be had upon a valid showing that the alter ego theory should apply (Meizlisch v. San Francisco Wool Sorting & Scouring Co. (1931) 213 Cal. 668, 670 [finding sufficient evidence to warrant attachment as alter ego]), the Court does not find on this record that plaintiff is likely to establish its claim of alter ego against defendant Paloma Palm Springs LLC.

 

 “In California, two conditions must be met before the alter ego doctrine will be invoked. First, there must be such a unity of interest and ownership between the corporation and its equitable owner that the separate personalities of the corporation and the shareholder do not in reality exist. Second, there must be an inequitable result if the acts in question are treated as those of the corporation alone.” (Sonora Diamond Corp. v. Superior Court (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 523, 538-539.) “Among the factors to be considered in applying the doctrine are commingling of funds and other assets of the two entities, the holding out by one entity that it is liable for the debts of the other, identical equitable ownership in the two entities, use of the same offices and employees, and use of one as a mere shell or conduit for the affairs of the other.” (Ibid., internal citations omitted.) “Other factors which have been described in the case law include inadequate capitalization, disregard of corporate formalities, lack of segregation of corporate records, and identical directors and officers.” (Ibid.; see also Zoran Corp. v. Chen (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 799, 811-12 [enumerating the various factors])

 

Even if attachment were appropriate against Kadioglu, the Court finds the evidence before it insufficient to support plaintiff’s alter ego theory of liability against defendant Paloma Palm Springs LLC. Plaintiff’s allegations that Paloma Palm Springs LLC was used in connection with the short-term rental scheme is based purely on information and belief. (See Abrar Decl. Ex. C [Verified Compl. ¶¶ 42-43].) The same is true for the allegations regarding plaintiff’s alter ego theory. (See Abrar Decl. Ex. C [Verified Compl. ¶¶ 49-50].) Thus, such allegations are insufficient to support attachment against Paloma Palm Springs.

 

Moreover, plaintiff submits evidence that merely shows that Kadioglu solely owns and controls the entity defendant, uses the same management property company for different hospitality businesses, and uses the same registered address for his entities. (Mtn. at 7:17-26, 8:4-15, 9:8-15; Abrar Decl. ¶¶ 9-10, 17 & Exs. D-E, I.) There is no evidence that the defendants are commingling funds or that Paloma Palm Springs is undercapitalized or holding itself out for the debts of Kadioglu. Plaintiff’s reliance on Kadioglu’s use of the same management company for the subject property as he does for his other hospitality businesses does not demonstrate that there is no financial separation or independent accounting as between the entities owned by Kadiglu. (Mtn. at 8:4-15, 8:18-9:6.) There is also no indication that Kadioglu has been disregarding corporate formalities by keeping hospitality management as an in-house enterprise. Furthermore, plaintiff’s assertion that Kadioglu does not hold real property does not support a finding that it would be an injustice to prevent the piercing of the corporate veil. Lastly, plaintiff’s reliance on the fact that a different action claimed that Bellvue Oasis is a dba for Paloma Palm Springs LLC is irrelevant because that complaint is not verified and no other evidence has been submitted to corroborate this assertion. (Mtn. at 10:20-25; Abrar Decl. Ex. H.)

 

Accordingly, because sufficient evidence has not been submitted to establish plaintiff’s alter ego theory of liability against defendant Paloma Palm Springs LLC, the Court DENIES plaintiff’s request for an order of attachment.

Plaintiff OX Biomed LLC requests that the Court issue a right to attach order as to defendant David Feldmar in the amount of $9,221,423.94.  For the reasons that follow, plaintiff’s application is DENIED.

 

I.       BACKGROUND

 

            A.        Factual Background

           

Plaintiff OX Biomed LLC sells human cell, tissue, and xengraft products (“Products”) for the treatment of serious injuries, burns and wounds. (Sutherland Decl. ¶ 4.) According to plaintiff, it entered into a Service Agreement with defendant David Feldmar, dba Feldmar Aesthetics, on January 27, 2025, pursuant to which defendant agreed to purchase plaintiff’s Products. (Sutherland Decl. ¶ 4 & Ex. 1.) Plaintiff further represents that Feldmar Aesthetics is defendant’s fictitious business name for his medical practice and that the Service Agreement was signed by defendant in his individual capacity. (Sutherland Decl. ¶¶ 3, 5.)

 

Plaintiff claims that, during the period from January 27, 2025 through March 26, 2025, defendant, doing business as Feldmar Aesthetics, ordered $12,779,011.78 worth of Products. (Sutherland Decl. ¶ 8.) Plaintiff received payments for a certain set of invoices totaling $3,557,577.84 but not for invoices that totaled $9,221,423.94. (Sutherland Decl. ¶ 8 & Exs. 3-5.) Plaintiff indicates that, in July 2025, Cole MacPherson, who is associated with defendant, requested the account to be changed from Feldman Aesthetics to “Feldmar Medical Corporation.” (Sutherland Decl. ¶ 9; see also MacPherson Decl. ¶¶ 11-12 & Exs. 1-2.) In August 2025, plaintiff indicated to MacPherson and Danny Gefen, who is also associated with defendant, that the account would be changed only if plaintiff received weekly payments of $500,000 towards the outstanding balance. (Sutherland Decl. ¶¶ 11-12 & Ex. 6.) On August 21, 2025, plaintiff requested that defendant provide a proposal for payment of the outstanding invoices but never received a response. (Sutherland Decl. ¶ 13 & Ex. 7.) The balance of $9,221,423.94 remains outstanding. (Sutherland Decl. ¶13.)

 

According to defendant, Feldmar Aesthetics is the fictitious business name for Feldmar Cosmetic Inc., which provides plastic surgery procedures, and Feldmar Cosmetic Inc. did not purchase Products from plaintiff. (Feldmar Decl. ¶¶ 7-9, 37 & Ex. 1.) Defendant further represents that he has a separate business, Feldmar Medical Corporation (“FMC”), which provides wound care surgical medical services to patients. (Feldmar Decl. ¶¶ 10, 12.) In January 2025, defendant spoke with Will Shiebler, who is plaintiff’s sales representative, about the benefits of plaintiff’s Products. (Feldmar Decl. ¶¶ 14-16.) Defendant informed Shiebler that FMC would be the purchaser for the Products. (Feldmar Decl. ¶¶ 17-19.) Defendant admits signing the Service Agreement but states that he had not realized that it incorrectly identified the customer as Feldman Aesthetics. (Feldmar Decl. ¶¶ 21-25 & Ex. 2.) Defendant claims that he told Shiebler of the error and requested that the documents be corrected. (Feldmar Decl. ¶ 25.) Defendant claims that Shiebler represented that the documents would be corrected and resubmitted for signature. (Feldmar Decl. ¶ 27.) Defendant also represents that, on January 31, 2025, a Business Associate Agreement (“BAA”) was provided by plaintiff to FMC and that defendant executed the BAA as an officer of FMC. (Feldmar Decl. ¶ 29 & Ex. 3.) Per the BAA’s preamble, the BAA is made part of the agreements existing between the parties. (Feldmar Decl. ¶ 30 & Ex. 3 at pg. 1.)

 

Defendant presumed that the documents would be corrected because the BAA included the corrected customer information. (Feldmar Decl. ¶ 33.) However, by April or May 2025, FMC’s COO MacPherson informed defendant that a corrected Service Agreement was never provided. (Feldmar Decl. ¶ 34; MacPherson Decl. ¶ 7.) All payments were made by FMC. (MacPherson Decl. ¶¶ 15-17 & Exs. 3-4.)

 

             B.       Procedural Background

 

            On December 2, 2025, plaintiff initiated this breach of contract action against defendant. On December 22, 2026, plaintiff filed the instant application for writ of attachment. On July 28, 2026, defendant filed its opposition. Per CCP § 484.060, plaintiff was required to file a reply by July 31, 2026, but no such reply was filed.  Even though on August 3, 2026 the Court on its own motion continued the hearing on the instant application from August 4, 2026 to August 11, 2026, the Court ordered that the briefing remain as scheduled in accordance with the original hearing date.

  

II.      ANALYSIS

 

          A.      Legal Standard

 

            “Upon the filing of the complaint or at any time thereafter, the plaintiff may apply . . . . for a right to attach order and a writ of attachment by filing an application for the order and writ with the court in which the action is brought.” (CCP § 484.010.) The application shall be executed under oath and must include: (1) a statement showing that the attachment is sought to secure the recovery on a claim upon which an attachment may be issued; (2) a statement of the amount to be secured by the attachment; (3) a statement that the attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based; (4) a statement that the applicant has no information or belief that the claim is discharged or that the prosecution of the action is stayed in a proceeding under the Bankruptcy Act (11 U.S.C. § 101, et seq.); and (5) a description of the property to be attached under the writ of attachment and a statement that the plaintiff is informed and believes that such property is subject to attachment. (CCP § 484.020.)

 

The Court shall issue a right to attach order if it finds all of the following:

 

(1) The claim upon which the attachment is based is one upon which an attachment may be issued.

 

(2) The plaintiff has established the probable validity of the claim upon which the attachment is based.

 

(3) The attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based.

 

(4) The amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.

 

(CCP § 484.090(a)(1-4).) “The Attachment Law statutes are subject to strict construction….” (Epstein v. Abrams (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1168.)

 

          B.      Analysis

 

1.            Basis of Attachment

 

“[A]n attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney’s fees.”  (CCP § 483.010(a).)

 

            Plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against defendant is based on the Service Agreement. (Sutherland Decl. ¶¶ 4-5 Ex. 2.) Plaintiff has shown that its claim is for a fixed amount of $9,221,423.94. (Sutherland Decl. ¶ 8 & Exs. 4-5.) Defendant does not dispute that plaintiff’s claim is the type upon which an attachment may be issued. Rather, defendant asserts that he is not a proper party to the Service Agreement, as discussed below.

 

Accordingly, plaintiff demonstrates a valid basis for attachment pursuant to the Service Agreement.

 

2.            Probable Validity of Plaintiff’s Claims

 

“A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.” (CCP § 481.190.) “If the defendant opposes the application, ‘the court must then consider the relative merits of the positions of the respective parties and make a determination of the probable outcome of the litigation.’ [Citations.]” (Pech v. Morgan (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 841, 855.)

 

Plaintiff’s application for attachment is based on its breach of contract claim. The elements for a breach of contract are: (1) the existence of a contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach of the contract, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff. (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman¿(2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.)

 

Based on the record before the Court, it is unclear whether a contract exists between plaintiff and defendant David Felmar. The two interrelated agreements before this Court—i.e., the Service Agreement and the BAA—provide contradictory information as to who are the contracting parties. The initial Service Agreement states that Feldman Aesthetics is the customer and was signed by defendant in his individual capacity. (Sutherland Decl., Ex. 1.) By contrast, the BAA, which states that it was made part of the existing contractual agreements between the parties, identifies FMC as the customer and was signed by defendant on behalf of FMC. (Feldmar Decl., Ex. 3.)

 

In addition, the record does not support plaintiff’s contention that defendant failed to disclose that FMC was the intended contracting party, thereby purportedly creating personal liability for defendant Feldmar as the agent of the undisclosed principal. As attested by Feldmar, he informed plaintiff’s sales representative that FMC would be the purchaser, and, after learning of the mistake in the Service Agreement, he requested that the agreement be updated and corrected. (Feldmar Decl. ¶¶ 16-18, 25-27; see also MacPherson Decl. ¶¶ 7-8.) Notably, plaintiff has not proffered the declaration of the sales representative to rebut defendant’s evidence. Defendant further attests that, before any orders could be approved, FMC provided to plaintiff its patient demographic and insurance eligibility information. (Feldmar Decl. ¶¶ 19, 31.) Taken together, this evidence suggests that defendant disclosed FMC’s identity to plaintiff and is thus not personally liable for the amount due under the Service Agreement. (See W.W. Leasing Unlimited v. Commercial Standard Title, Inc. Co. (1983) 149 Cal.App.3d 792, 796 [citing 3 Am. Jur. 2d Agency, § 320, pp. 676 – 678].)

 

Accordingly, because questions exist as to whether an agreement exists between the parties, plaintiff has not demonstrated the probable validity of its breach of contract claim against defendant Feldmar.

 

III.     CONCLUSION

 

The application for a writ of attachment against defendant David Feldmar is DENIED.
