SUMMARY JUDGMENT/ADJUDICATION

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, Phillips 66 Company

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, Faviola Nino

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Summary Judgment or, in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Faviola Nino has been employed as an operator at Defendant Phillips 66 Company’s oil refinery in Wilmington, CA for over a decade. On or about April 23, 2021, Plaintiff suffered a workplace injury when she struck her left thigh on a valve manifold, causing her to twist upon impact and injuring her shoulder, bicep, back, neck and hip. Plaintiff notified Phillips of her injury and it sent her to its workers’ compensation provider, Defendant CareOnSite, Inc., for medical treatment.

 

Plaintiff alleges that instead of receiving medical treatment for her injury, CareOnSite administered a drug test. Plaintiff claims that neither Phillips nor CareOnSite had reasonable suspicion that she had been intoxicated while at work. Following the test, Phillips and CareOnSite demanded Plaintiff to provide them with a prescription for medications detected in the test. Plaintiff alleges that Phillips sent her to CareOnSite to take a drug test, hoping that she would fail so it could use the failed drug test as a pretext for firing her due to her disability. Plaintiff ultimately passed the drug test.

 

Upon returning to work, Plaintiff still experienced pain and discomfort due to her injury and made multiple requests to Phillips to see its workers’ compensation doctors for a second opinion and/or re-evaluation. However, Phillips allegedly ignored these requests and Plaintiff continued working without any restrictions or accommodation, worsening her condition. Plaintiff then decided to use her personal vacation time to heal and seek treatment. Her new doctor placed her on restrictions of no heavy lifting, pushing, or pulling. Plaintiff informed Phillips of these restrictions.

 

Plaintiff claims that despite Phillips’ ability to provide her with available work that she was capable of performing, Phillips refused to allow her to return to work based on an inability to accommodate her medical restrictions. Plaintiff was instead placed on medical leave between August to September 2021. Plaintiff claims that after she made complaints, a Phillips supervisor contacted her in October 2021 and informed her that it was Phillips’ policy to keep injured employees on medical leave until they were healed, even though job functions could be performed with work restrictions, to prevent reinjury. Plaintiff alleges that since then, Phillips has kept her on leave despite Plaintiff’s continued requests to return to work. She contends that Phillips kept her on leave to force her out of her job due to her disability.

 

On November 9, 2023, Plaintiff filed a Second Amended Complaint for: (1) Disability Discrimination; (2) Failure to Reasonably Accommodate; (3) Failure to Engage in the Interactive Process; (4) Failure to Take All Reasonable Steps to Prevent Discrimination and Retaliation; (5) Retaliation in Violation of FEHA; (6) Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy; (7) Wrongful Constructive Termination in Violation of Public Policy; (8) Aiding and Abetting in Violation of FEHA; (9) Unlawful Medical Inquiry in Violation of FEHA; and (10) Violation of Constitutional Right to Privacy.

 

RULING: Granted in part.

Evidentiary Objections: The Court only rules on those objections material to disposition of the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (q).) Phillips’ Objections No. 31 and 32 are overruled.

 

Phillips moves for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication as to the first through seventh and ninth through tenth causes of action in Plaintiff’s Second Amended Complaint. It argues that Plaintiff’s claims fail as a matter of law. It also claims that Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages has no merit because there is no evidence of oppression, fraud, or malice committed or ratified by a managing agent. Plaintiff opposes the motion, maintaining triable issues of material facts as to her claims. Phillips’ motion is granted in part and summary adjudication is granted as to the disability discrimination, failure to accommodate, failure to engage in the interactive process, and invasion of privacy causes of action.  

 

The pleadings frame the issues for motions, “since it is those allegations to which the motion must respond. (Citation.)” (Scolinos v. Kolts (1995) 37 Cal. App. 4th 635, 640-641; FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 382-83; Jordan-Lyon Prods., LTD. v. Cineplex Odeon Corp. (1994) 29 Cal.App.4th 1459, 1472.) The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atl. Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) A defendant moving for summary judgment “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) “Once the defendant . . . has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Ibid.)

 

“When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) “An issue of fact can only be created by a conflict in the evidence. It is not created by speculation, conjecture, imagination or guesswork.”  (Lyons v. Security Pacific National Bank (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 1001, 1041 (citation omitted)

 

First Cause of Action: Disability Discrimination

Phillips argues that Plaintiff’s disability discrimination claim fails because she was not qualified to do her job and Phillips had legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for her termination. Again, the Court declines to consider Plaintiff’s unpled theories, including those premised on a “nationwide policy” prohibiting medical personnel from prescribing physical work restrictions to injured employees. (Laabs v. City of Victorville (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1253.) The only alleged policies identified in Plaintiff’s Second Amended Complaint are a policy to keep injured employees on medical leave until they were fully healed and a policy to subject disabled employees to drug testing for the purpose of finding a pretextual reason to fire them. [SAC ¶¶ 31, 37-40.] These are very different from an alleged policy whereby employees were returned to work without restrictions to circumvent the employer’s accommodation obligations under the FEHA. [See Opp. at pp. 10, 13.] The Court therefore analyzes Plaintiff’s and Phillips’ respective burdens based on discrimination theories framed by the amended complaint.    

 

“A prima facie case of disability discrimination under FEHA requires the employee to show he or she (1) suffered from a disability, (2) was otherwise qualified to do his or her job, and (3) was subjected to adverse employment action because of the disability.” (Choochagi v. Barracuda Networks, Inc. (2020) 60 Cal.App.5th 444, 458.)

 

Phillips has met its burden to show that Plaintiff was not qualified for her position. The essential functions of Plaintiff’s operator position included unloading railcars containing toxic and explosive substances, pulling quart-size sample containers containing these substances, climbing ladders and structures, and opening and closing valves. [Lagunas Decl. ¶¶ 2-3, Ex. E, Ex. A: Plaintiff’s Depo. pp. 35:6-21, 85:24-89:8; Negrotto Decl. ¶ 13.] From September 2021 to April 2022, Plaintiff submitted to Phillips Employee Health Reports and doctor’s notes completed by her physician, Dr. Bradley Thomas, showing that Plaintiff could not walk, operate a motor vehicle or heavy machinery, climb stairs or ladders, work at heights, use a computer, operate foot controls, twist/bend/stoop, squat/kneel/crawl, or lift/push/carry more than 10 pounds. [Compendium Exs. 8-14.] Although from May 2022 to January 2023, these restrictions eased with Plaintiff being allowed to do desk work without restriction, she still could not climb stairs/ladders/work at heights, twist/bend/stoop, or squat/kneel/crawl, and could perform the other activities for no more than 3 hours. [Id. Exs. 15-23.] After January 2023, Dr. Thomas reinstituted all restrictions. [Id. Exs. 24-27.] After reviewing the requests, Bulk Superintendent Khalid Ali and Bulk Process Supervisor Andre Magee determined that the 10-pound restriction alone precluded Plaintiff from performing the essential function of opening and closing valves. [Id. Ex. 1-C: Ali Depo. pp. 91:13-20, 94:3-6, 96:3-17, 125:21-126:33.] There were no administrative tasks or other positions Plaintiff could perform at the refinery. [Id. Ex. 1-C: Ali Depo. pp. 125:21-126:33; Id. Ex. 1-B: Negrotto Depo. p. 121:4-23; Negrotto Decl. ¶ 18, Ex. 3-A.] Phillips has therefore shown that Plaintiff was not qualified for her position or another vacant position and the burden shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue of material fact.

 

Plaintiff fails to satisfy her burden. She presents no evidence that she was otherwise qualified for her position, instead providing records from her appointment with Dr. Thomas where he noted that Plaintiff could not engage in repetitive pushing/pulling/lifting over 10 pounds and that she would be placed on temporary total disability because her employer was unable to accommodate “lite duty.” [Walker Decl. ¶ 33, Exs. AG-AH.] This does not raise a triable issue because Plaintiff’s weight restriction was the reason she was not qualified as she was unable to perform the essential function of opening and closing valves. Because Plaintiff is unable to establish an element of her discrimination claim, summary adjudication is granted as to this cause of action.  

 

Second Cause of Action: Failure to Reasonably Accommodate

Phillips is also entitled to summary adjudication on the failure to accommodate cause of action. Phillips contends this claim fails because Plaintiff was not qualified for her position. “The elements of a failure to accommodate claim are (1) the plaintiff has a disability under the FEHA, (2) the plaintiff is qualified to perform the essential functions of the position, and (3) the employer failed to reasonably accommodate the plaintiff’s disability.” (Lui v. City and County of S.F. (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 962, 971.) “As with a FEHA discrimination claim, the plaintiff bears the burden of proving that he or she had the ‘ability to perform the essential functions of a job with accommodation.’” (Id. at p. 971.) As mentioned above, Plaintiff has failed to raise a triable issue as to whether she was qualified to perform the essential functions of her position or another position. Thus, summary adjudication is granted as to this claim.

 

Third Cause of Action: Failure to Engage in the Interactive Process

Phillips is entitled to summary adjudication as to Plaintiff’s failure to engage in the interactive process claim. It argues this cause of action fails because Plaintiff cannot identify a reasonable accommodation. “[T]o succeed on a cause of action for failure to engage in an interactive process, ‘an employee must identify a reasonable accommodation that would have been available at the time the interactive process should have occurred.’” (Shirvanyan v. Los Angeles Community College Dist. (2020) 59 Cal.App.5th 82, 96.) Phillips has met its burden because it has shown that Plaintiff could not perform the essential functions of her position or a vacant position. In response, Plaintiff fails to identify an available reasonable accommodation. Thus, summary adjudication is granted as to this claim.

 

Fifth Cause of Action: Retaliation in Violation of FEHA

Phillips has failed to meet its burden as to Plaintiff’s retaliation claim. Phillips contends that Plaintiff’s retaliation claim fails because her termination was due to her inability to perform the essential functions of her position after she exhausted her paid leave (i.e., that the termination was based on a legitimate, nonretaliatory reason). (See Moore v. Regents of Univ. of Cal. (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 216, 236 [employer may meet burden on summary judgment by either showing one or more prima facie elements lacking or legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for adverse employment action].) However, Plaintiff asserts multiple retaliation theories based on different purported adverse employment actions in addition to her ultimate termination. This includes forcing her on unnecessary medical leave and refusing to conduct investigations. [SAC ¶ 77.] To meet its burden, Phillips must “negate all liability theories plaintiff alleges.” (Swanson v. Morongo Unified School Dist. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 954, 970.) Because Phillips has not done so, it is not entitled to summary adjudication on this claim.   

 

Fourth, Sixth, and Seventh Causes of Action: Failure to Take All Reasonable Steps to Prevent Discrimination and Retaliation, Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy, and Wrongful Constructive Termination in Violation of Public Policy

Phillips fails to meet its burden as to Plaintiff’s failure to prevent and wrongful termination causes of action. Phillips argues these claims fall with Plaintiff’s discrimination and retaliation claims. An employer cannot be held liable for failing to prevent discrimination or retaliation that never occurred. (Trujillo v. North County Transit Dist. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 280, 289.) Similarly, no liability for wrongful termination in violation of public policy can exist where the FEHA policies against discrimination and retaliation are the basis for the claim and such policies are not violated. (Cf. Rope v. Auto-Chlor System of Wash., Inc. (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 635, 660 [FEHA’s policy against discrimination can support claim for wrongful termination in violation of public policy].) However, the failure to prevent claim stands because, as discussed above, Phillips is not entitled to summary adjudication on the retaliation cause of action. The wrongful termination claims also stand because, although duplicative, they are both additionally based on retaliatory acts that Plaintiff alleges constituted “constructive termination.” [SAC ¶¶ 85, 91.] This is true even if Phillips has shown it later actually terminated Plaintiff for nonretaliatory reasons. Phillips is therefore not entitled to summary adjudication on these causes of action.

 

Ninth Cause of Action: Unlawful Medical Inquiry in Violation of FEHA

Phillips contends that Plaintiff’s unlawful medical inquiry cause of action fails because the drug test performed was lawful. For the same reasons discussed in the Court’s ruling on CareOnSite’s motion, Phillips is not entitled to summary adjudication on this claim because it has not met its burden to show that its drug test was lawful.

 

Tenth Cause of Action: Violation of Constitutional Right to Privacy

Phillips argues that Plaintiff’s invasion of privacy claim fails because she had no reasonable expectation of privacy, she did not suffer a serious invasion of privacy, and Phillips had a legitimate safety reason for drug testing. As explained in the Court’s granting of CareOnSite’s motion for summary adjudication on this claim, Phillips’ motion is likewise granted because it had a legitimate safety interest for the drug test.

 

Punitive Damages

Phillips is not entitled to summary adjudication as to Plaintiff’s claim for punitive damages. Phillips contends that Plaintiff has no claim for punitive damages because she cannot show by clear and convincing evidence that an officer, director, or managing agent of Phillips acted with malice, oppression, or fraud in any action taken against her. However, Phillips presents no evidence in support of its argument, nor does it connect the evidence presented in its separate statement to its argument. It has therefore failed to meet its burden and its motion for summary adjudication as to this claim is denied.

 

In sum, Phillips is entitled to summary adjudication as to the disability discrimination, failure to accommodate, failure to engage in the interactive process, and invasion of privacy claims.

 

Phillips to give notice.

Evidentiary

CareOnSite
moves for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication as to Plaintiff’s
causes of action for disability discrimination, aiding and abetting, unlawful
medical inquiry, and violation of constitutional right to privacy. It argues
that each of these claims fail as a matter of law. It further seeks summary
adjudication of Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages because there is no
clear and convincing evidence that an officer, director, or managing agent of
CareOnSite was guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. Plaintiff opposes the
motion, maintaining triable issues of material facts as to her claims.
CareOnSite’s motion is granted in part and summary adjudication is granted as
to the disability discrimination, aiding and abetting, and invasion of privacy
causes of action as well as the punitive damages claim.  

 

The pleadings frame the issues for motions, “since it is those
allegations to which the motion must respond. (Citation.)” (Scolinos v. Kolts (1995) 37 Cal. App.
4th 635, 640-641; FPI Development, Inc.
v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 382-83; Jordan-Lyon Prods., LTD. v. Cineplex Odeon Corp. (1994) 29
Cal.App.4th 1459, 1472.) The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or
summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the
parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations,
trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atl. Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) “Code of
Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to
grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences
reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences
or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and
that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler
v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

 

“On
a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party
to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material
fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc.
(2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) A defendant moving for summary judgment
“has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if
the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . .
cannot be established.”  (Code Civ.
Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) “Once the defendant . . . has met that burden,
the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or
more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Ibid.)

 

“When
deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the
evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has
sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn
from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary
judgment.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico
Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467; see also Code
Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) “An issue of fact can only be created by a
conflict in the evidence. It is not created by speculation, conjecture,
imagination or guesswork.”  (Lyons v. Security Pacific National Bank
(1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 1001, 1041 (citation omitted)

 

First
Cause of Action: Disability Discrimination

CareOnSite
first argues that Plaintiff’s disability discrimination claim fails because it
did not employ Plaintiff.

 

CareOnSite’s
own evidence creates a triable issue on whether it is an “employer” under the
FEHA. The FEHA provides that the term “‘employer’ includes any person regularly
employing five or more persons, or any person acting as an agent of an employer,
directly or indirectly . . . .” (Gov. Code, § 12926, subd. (d).) This provision
“permits a business entity acting as an agent of an employer to be held
directly liable as an employer for employment discrimination in violation of
the FEHA in appropriate circumstances when the business-entity agent has at
least five employees and carries out FEHA-regulated activities on behalf of an
employer.” (Raines v. U.S. Healthworks Medical Group (2023) 15 Cal.5th
268, 291.) CareOnSite presents the declaration of its President, Helen Tang,
who states that under its Master Professional Services Agreement with Phillips,
CareOnSite provided “occupational health care services” for Phillips employees,
including “drug and alcohol testing.” [Tang Decl. ¶¶ 4-5, Ex. B.] Medical
examinations, and particularly drug and alcohol testing, are FEHA-regulated
activities. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (f); Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11071,
subd. (d)(2).) CareOnSite therefore fails to meet its burden to show that it is
not an employer under the FEHA.

 

CareOnSite
next contends that the discrimination claim fails because it did not subject
Plaintiff to an adverse employment action and did not knowingly administer the
drug test for the purpose of Phillips terminating her because of her
disability. Framed by her pleadings, Plaintiff’s theory of CareOnSite’s
liability for discrimination is the administration of a post-accident drug test
for the purposes of providing a pretext to fire her for her disability. [SAC ¶¶
19, 37-40.] The Court declines to consider unpled theories, including those
based on a “nationwide policy” prohibiting medical personnel from prescribing
physical work restrictions to injured employees. (Laabs v. City of
Victorville (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1253.)  

 

“A
prima facie case of disability discrimination under FEHA requires the employee
to show he or she (1) suffered from a disability, (2) was otherwise qualified
to do his or her job, and (3) was subjected to adverse employment action
because of the disability.” (Choochagi v. Barracuda Networks, Inc.
(2020) 60 Cal.App.5th 444, 458.) “An ‘adverse employment action’ is one that ‘materially
affects the terms, conditions, or privileges of employment.’” (Doe v. Dept.
of Corrections & Rehabilitation (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 721, 734.)
This includes “ultimate employment actions such as termination or demotion” as
well as “actions that are reasonably likely to adversely and materially affect
an employee’s job performance or opportunity for advancement.” (Id. at
p. 734.)

 

CareOnSite
has satisfied its burden to show that it did not subject Plaintiff to an
adverse employment action via its testing. Plaintiff ultimately passed the drug
test, and no action was taken against her due to the test. [Tang Decl. ¶ 10.]
Indeed, the adverse employment action identified in Plaintiff’s amended
complaint is her being forced to remain on medical leave. [SAC ¶ 48.] The
burden therefore shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue of material fact
on this point.

 

Plaintiff
fails to meet her burden. Plaintiff claims that she suffered an adverse
employment action because she was subject to an unlawful medical inquiry in the
form of the drug test and request to disclose medical information such as her
current prescriptions. But she presents no evidence on the impact this had on
the conditions of her employment nor any authority that these actions alone can
constitute adverse employment actions. Plaintiff therefore fails to raise a
triable issue of material fact and CareOnSite is entitled to summary
adjudication on this claim.

 

Eighth
Cause of Action: Aiding and Abetting in Violation of FEHA

CareOnSite
argues that Plaintiff’s aiding and abetting cause of action fails because
Phillips did not use the drug test results to terminate Plaintiff because of
her disability, CareOnSite had no knowledge of Phillips’ alleged scheme to use
drug test results to terminate disabled employees, and CareOnSite did not
knowingly administer the drug test for the purpose of Phillips terminating her
due to her disability.

 

Under
the FEHA, it is unlawful for “any person to aid, abet, incite, compel, or
coerce the doing of any of the acts forbidden under this part, or to attempt to
do so.” (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (i).) A person is liable under the FEHA for
aiding and abetting an employer’s discrimination against an employee only if:
(1) the employer subjected the employee to discrimination; (2) the person knew
that the employer’s conduct violated the FEHA; and (3) the person gave the
employer “substantial assistance or encouragement” to violate the FEHA. (Smith
v. BP Lubricants USA Inc. (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 138, 146.)

 

CareOnSite
is entitled to summary adjudication on Plaintiff’s aiding and abetting cause of
action. Plaintiff alleges that CareOnSite aided and abetted Phillips’
discrimination against her based on her disability. [SAC ¶¶ 98-104.] As
explained above, Plaintiff suffered no adverse employment action from the drug
test and thus Phillips did not use the test to discriminate against her. Thus, CareOnSite
did not aid or abet in discrimination, and it is granted summary adjudication
as to this claim.

 

Ninth
Cause of Action: Unlawful Medical Inquiry in Violation of FEHA

CareOnSite
contends that Plaintiff’s unlawful medical inquiry claim fails because
Plaintiff was not subjected to an unlawful medical examination and Phillips’
legitimate safety interests for suspicionless drug testing of its operators
complied with law.

 

CareOnSite
fails to meet its burden as to this claim. “An employer or other covered entity
may maintain and enforce rules prohibiting employees from being under the
influence of alcohol or drugs in the workplace and may conduct alcohol or drug
testing for this purpose if they have a reasonable belief that an employee may
be under the influence of alcohol or drugs at work.” (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2,
§ 11071, subd. (d)(2).) CareOnSite presents evidence that it had maintained,
and that Plaintiff was aware of, a post-accident drug testing policy. [Sherrod
Decl. ¶ 3, Ex. A: Plaintiff’s Depo. pp. 111:12-114:6.] The same evidence shows
that Plaintiff was subjected to a post-accident drug test following her April
23, 2021 injury. [Ibid.] Nowhere does CareOnSite present evidence that
it or Phillips had a reasonable belief that Plaintiff was under the influence
of alcohol or drugs at work. CareOnSite therefore fails to show that
Plaintiff’s post-accident drug test was lawful. CareOnSite cites to Loder v.
City of Glendale to support the legality of its test. But that case is
inapposite because it did not address the legality of drug testing of current
employees as opposed to pre and post-offer testing. (Loder v. City of Glendale
(1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 865.) CareOnSite is therefore not entitled to summary
adjudication as to this claim.

 

Tenth
Cause of Action: Violation of Constitutional Right to Privacy

CareOnSite
argues that Plaintiff’s right to privacy cause of action fails because Plaintiff
had no reasonable expectation of privacy under the circumstances and she did
not suffer a serious invasion of privacy rights. It further claims that any
expectation of privacy is outweighed by Phillips’ legitimate safety-related
interests for suspicionless drug testing of its operators.

 

“[A]
plaintiff alleging an invasion of privacy in violation of the state
constitutional right to privacy must establish each of the following: (1) a
legally protected privacy interest; (2) a reasonable expectation of privacy in
the circumstances; and (3) conduct by defendant constituting a serious invasion
of privacy.” (Hill v. NCAA (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1, 39-40.) “A defendant may
prevail in a state constitutional privacy case by negating any of the three
elements just discussed or by pleading and proving, as an affirmative defense,
that the invasion of privacy is justified because it substantively furthers one
or more countervailing interests.” (Id. at p. 40.)

 

CareOnSite
has sufficiently shown that the intrusion on Plaintiff’s privacy was justified
by Phillips’ legitimate safety interests. The advance notice Plaintiff had of
the drug-testing program diminished her expectation of privacy and minimized
the intrusion on her privacy interests. (Smith v. Fresno Irrigation Dist.
(1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 147, 162.) The substance abuse policy provided for
post-accident drug testing and Plaintiff testified in her deposition that she
had been subject to such testing by CareOnSite for a prior accident. [Sherrod
Decl. ¶ 3, Ex. A: Plaintiff’s Depo. pp. 111:12-114:6, 118:9-119:21, 120:4-11.] The
hazardous environment Plaintiff worked in and the hazards inherent in her work justify
Phillips’ testing policy. (Smith, supra, 72 Cal.App.4th at p. 166.)
As an operator at Phillips’ oil refinery, Plaintiff regularly worked with
flammable, explosive, and toxic substances. [Sherrod Decl. ¶ 3, Ex. A:
Plaintiff’s Depo. pp. 35:6-21, 52:18-53:20, 106:8-109:6.] Plaintiff herself
agreed in her deposition that safety was particularly important given the
nature of her job. [Id. at p. 110:2-15.] Phillips therefore had a
legitimate interest in enforcing a post-accident testing policy to ensure that
employees were not impaired at work and to protect Plaintiff and her fellow
co-workers. The burden therefore shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue
of material fact.

 

Plaintiff
fails to raise a triable issue of material fact as to the legitimacy of
Phillips’ post-accident testing policy. Rather than rebutting Phillips’
assertion of safety interests “by showing there are feasible and effective
alternatives to defendant’s conduct which have a lesser impact on privacy
interests,” Plaintiff focuses on the illegality of the testing. (Smith, supra,
72 Cal.App.4th at p. 159.) But the mere fact that the testing program violates
FEHA is insufficient. (All of US or None-Riverside Chapter v. Hamrick
(2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 751, 800-01 [statutory violations do not establish
violation of privacy as a matter of law].) Plaintiff has therefore failed to
meet her burden and CareOnSite is entitled to summary adjudication as to the
invasion of privacy cause of action.  

 

Punitive
Damages

CareOnSite
moves for summary adjudication as to Plaintiff’s claim for punitive damages on
the basis that there is no clear and convincing evidence that an officer,
director, or managing agent of CareOnSite was guilty of oppression, fraud, or
malice against Plaintiff.

 

Summary
adjudication may be granted as to a claim for punitive damages only if it
completely disposes of the entire claim. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd.
(f)(1).) A claim for punitive damages requires clear and convincing evidence “that
the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice” in the
commission of a tort. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) “Malice” is “conduct
which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or
despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and
conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Id. § 3294,
subd. (c)(1).) “Oppression” is “despicable conduct that subjects a person to
cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.” (Id.
§ 3294, subd. (c)(2).) Despicable conduct is conduct that is “so vile,
base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or loathsome that it would be looked
down upon and despised by ordinary decent people.” (Scott v. Phoenix
Schools, Inc. (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 702, 715.) “Such conduct has been
described as ‘[having] the character of outrage frequently associated with
crime.’” (Id. at p. 715.)

 

An
employer is not liable for punitive damages based on the acts of an employee “unless
the employer had advance knowledge of the unfitness of the employee and
employed him or her with a conscious disregard of the rights or safety of
others or authorized or ratified the wrongful conduct for which the damages are
awarded or was personally guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. With respect
to a corporate employer, the advance knowledge and conscious disregard,
authorization, ratification or act of oppression, fraud, or malice must be on
the part of an officer, director, or managing agent of the corporation.” (Civ.
Code, § 3294, subd. (b).)    

 

The
remaining cause of action that can form the basis for Plaintiff’s punitive
damages claim against CareOnSite is her cause of action for unlawful medical
inquiry. Tang states in her declaration that CareOnSite “did not knowingly
administer Plaintiff’s April 23, 2021 drug test for the purpose of Phillips 66
terminating her because of her disability” nor did it have knowledge that
Phillips was engaged in such a scheme. [Tang Decl. ¶¶ 10, 12.] CareOnSite thus
sufficiently shows lack of ratification of any scheme to discriminate against
Plaintiff via the drug test. Plaintiff presents no argument on this point in
opposition and the Court declines to make the argument for her. (Badie v.
Bank of America (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 779, 784-85 [“When [a party]
fails to raise a point, or asserts it but fails to support it with reasoned
argument and citations to authority, we treat the point as waived”].)
CareOnSite is thus entitled to summary adjudication as to Plaintiff’s punitive
damages claim against it.

 

In
sum, CareOnSite’s motion for summary adjudication is granted as to the
disability discrimination, aiding and abetting, and invasion of privacy causes
of action as well as Plaintiff’s claim for punitive damages.

VACATE DEFAULT JUDGMENT

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendants, Progressive Casualty Insurance Company and United Financial Casualty Company

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiffs, Philip Addington and Xavier Nailing

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion to Vacate Default Judgment

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Xavier Nailing hit a coyote while driving Philip Addington’s car, causing the airbags to deploy. Addington then filed a claim with his insurer, Progressive Casualty Insurance Company. Nailing and Addington allege a claims adjustor stated the car was totaled because the airbag was deployed. Nailing and Addington disagree with this determination and Progressive’s estimate of the car’s worth. They filed a complaint against Progressive and its underwriter, United Financial Casualty Company, for breach of contract, breach of fiduciary duty, fraud in the inducement, intentional interference with contract, and conversion. Default judgment was entered against Defendants on December 8, 2025 for $10,692.49.  

 

RULING: Granted.

Request for Judicial Notice: Granted.

The Court takes judicial notice of the existence of documents filed in this case and other cases. (Lockley v. Law Office of Cantrell, Green, Pekich, Cruz & McCort (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 875, 882.) Judicial notice is also taken of the existence of the district court order and the truth of the results reached therein. (Id. at pp. 885-86.)

 

Defendants move to vacate the default judgment entered against them on grounds of improper service and lack of actual notice. Plaintiffs oppose the motion, challenging its timeliness. Defendants’ motion is granted because they have shown the default judgment is void for lack of service.

 

A court may set aside any void judgment or order. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (d).) “[A] default judgment entered against a defendant who was not served with a summons in the manner prescribed by statute is void.” (Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1444.) Where service is proper but does not result in actual notice to a party in time to defend the action, a court has discretion to grant relief from a default judgment. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5; Anastos v. Lee (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 1314, 1319.) “The notice of motion shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of: (i) two years after entry of a default judgment against the party; or (ii) 180 days after service on the party of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5, subd. (a).) The motion must be accompanied by “an affidavit showing under oath that the party’s lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by the party’s avoidance of service or inexcusable neglect. The party shall serve and file with the notice a copy of the answer, motion, or other pleading proposed to be filed in the action.” (Id. § 473.5, subd. (b).)

 

Defendants sufficiently show that they were not served. The proofs of service indicate that service was made on Defendants’ registered agent, CT Corporation System, on August 25, 2025. Defendants submit the declaration of Tamara Kling, a Representation Services Advisor at CT “familiar with the records CT creates in the ordinary course of business related to CT’s receipt, processing, storage, and transmittal of the legal process it receives.” [Kling Decl. ¶ 2.] Kling states that after searching CT’s records, she was unable to find any record of CT receiving the summons and complaint as well as other documents purportedly served on Defendants. [Id. ¶ 5.] Plaintiffs present no evidence in response confirming valid service. The lack of records evidencing receipt of the summons and complaint indicates CT was not served with the documents.

 

Plaintiffs’ timeliness challenge is unavailing. Where, as here, a judgment is valid on its face, but uncontested evidence shows that service was improper, a motion to vacate the judgment as void may be made at any time. (Thompson v. Cook (1942) 20 Cal.2d 564, 569-73.) Defendants’ motion is thus timely and the motion is granted.

 

The default and default judgment are vacated.
