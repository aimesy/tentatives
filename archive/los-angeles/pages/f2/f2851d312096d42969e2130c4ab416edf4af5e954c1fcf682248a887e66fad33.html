            Defendant argues that he was terminated because he is black, but the pretext was that he entered into a contract with a company in which Plaintiff and his wife have interests.  

            Defendants Unbounded Learning Inc. and Consortium on Reaching Excellence In Education Inc. move to compel arbitration.

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendants Unbounded Learning Inc. and Consortium on Reaching Excellence In Education Inc.’s motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED. The case is ordered stayed pending arbitration. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.) The Court sets a status conference re: arbitration for January 25, 2027 at 8:30 a.m. Status conference briefs are due 7 days prior.

ANALYSIS

Motion To Compel Arbitration and Stay Action

Discussion

            Defendants Unbounded Learning Inc. and Consortium on Reaching Excellence In Education Inc. move to compel arbitration.

Existence of Agreement To Arbitrate

            Under California law, arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract. (Blake v. Ecker (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 728, 741 overruled on other grounds by Le Francois v. Goel (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1094.) A party petitioning to compel arbitration has the burden of establishing the existence of a valid agreement to arbitrate and the party opposing the petition has the burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, any fact necessary to its defense. (Banner Entertainment, Inc. v. Superior Court (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 348, 356-57.)

            Defendants present the arbitration agreement in a document entitled “UNBOUNDED LEARNING, INC. Inventions, Non-Disclosure and Non-Solicitation Agreement.” The Agreement is dated February 27, 2023 and entered into between UnboundEd Learning, Inc. as Company and Plaintiff Robert Sheffield as employee.

            The arbitration provision is found at ¶ 5 as follows:

(a)Except as provided in section 5(b) below, I agree that any dispute or controversy arising out of or relating to any interpretation, construction, performance or breach of this agreement, or relating to my employment with the company (including compensation) or termination therefrom, shall be settled by arbitration to be held in New York, New York, in accordance with the employment arbitration rules of the American Arbitration Association before a single arbitrator who shall have experience in the area of the matter in dispute. The arbitrator may grant relief in the nature of injunctions or other relief in such dispute or controversy. The decision of the arbitrator shall be final, conclusive and binding on the parties. Judgment may be entered on the arbitrator’s decision in any court having jurisdiction. The Company and I will each pay one-half of the costs and expenses of such arbitration unless the arbitrator orders otherwise, and each of the Company and I will separately pay our own counsel fees and expenses and other costs of the arbitration. I understand and acknowledge that by agreeing to the arbitration provisions I have waived the right to a trial by jury.

(Maltes Decl., Exh. A; Agreement, ¶ 5(a) [bold emphasis added].)

            Per the Declaration of Nathan Maltes:

4. Following months of corporate acquisition activities, on September 12, 2022, UnboundEd finalized their acquisition of all the stock for CORE. Also on September 12, 2022, Plaintiff Robert Sheffield entered into an agreement with CORE stating that his employment would be retained. Per this agreement, Mr. Sheffield retained his role as President of CORE in addition to establishing his role as Chief Growth Officer of UnboundEd.

Indeed, Plaintiff alleges at ¶ 4 of the Complaint that UEL acquired CORE in 2023 and merged into a single business. As such, Plaintiff is estopped from denying that CORE is entitled to enforce the arbitration agreement.

“Nonsignatory defendants may enforce arbitration agreements ‘where there is sufficient identity of parties.’ [Citation.] Enforcement is permitted where the nonsignatory is the agent for a party to the arbitration agreement [citation], or the nonsignatory is a third party beneficiary of the agreement [*9]  [citation]. In addition, a nonsignatory may enforce an arbitration agreement under the doctrine of equitable estoppel. The doctrine applies where, for example, a signatory plaintiff sues a nonsignatory defendant for claims that are based on an underlying contract. In such instance, the plaintiff may be equitably estopped to deny the nonsignatory defendant's right to enforce an arbitration clause that is contained within the contract that the plaintiff has placed at issue.” (Marenco, supra, 233 Cal.App.4th at p. 1417.)

(Jenks v. DLA Piper Rudnick Gray Cary US LLP (2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 1, 8-9.) 

Plaintiff’s signature appears on the agreement. Plaintiff asserts three cause of action: breach of contract, breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing and wrongful termination. All of these are common law causes of action. As such, the Court need not address the Armendariz factors because those only apply where a party seeks to arbitrate nonwaivable statutory rights in the workplace (Fitz v. NCR Corp. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 702, 711-12) 

The Court finds that Defendants are entitled to enforce the arbitration agreements as to the claims asserted in the Complaint. The burden shifts to Plaintiff to demonstrate that the arbitration agreement is unenforceable. 

In failing to file an opposition, Plaintiff has failed to demonstrate that the arbitration agreement should not be enforced. 

As such, the motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED. The case is ordered stayed pending arbitration. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.) The Court sets a status conference re: arbitration for January 25, 2027 at 8:30 a.m. Status conference briefs are due 7 days prior.

Plaintiff alleges that Defendant could not repair the vehicle to conform to warranties.

The parties settled the case. Plaintiff moves for an award of attorney’s fees.

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff Chris Jordan G. De Jesus’s motion for attorney’s fees is GRANTED in the amount of $12,000.

ANALYSIS

Motion For Attorney Fees

Discussion

            The Court has reviewed the moving, opposing and reply briefs filed by the parties, but only addresses the points which the Court deems to be material to the disposition of this motion.

Plaintiff Chris Jordan G. De Jesus moves for an award of attorney’s fees.

The parties settled the case. Plaintiff moves for an award of attorney’s fees in the amount of (1) $11,966.00 in attorneys’ fees for Quill & Arrow, LLP; (2) a 1.25 multiplier enhancement on the attorney fees (or $2,991.50); and (3) an additional $4,000.00 for Plaintiff’s counsel to review Defendant’s Opposition; draft the Reply; and attend the hearing on this Motion.

            Plaintiff brought causes of action pursuant to the Song-Beverly Act, Civil Code § 1790, et seq. Civil Code, § 1794(d) provides:

(d) If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.

 

Plaintiff obtained a settlement against VWGA in the amount of $50,092.97 plus, attorneys’ fees, costs, and expenses as the prevailing party.

The determination of reasonable amount of attorney fees is within the sound discretion of trial courts. (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095; Akins v. Enterprise Rent-A-Car Co. (2000) 79 Cal. App. 4th 1127, 1134.) “The determination of what constitutes a reasonable fee generally ‘begins with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate….’”  “[T]he lodestar is the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community; it may be adjusted by the court based on factors including, as relevant herein, (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award….”  (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.) In setting the hourly rate for an attorney fees award, courts are entitled to consider the rate of “‘fees customarily charged by that attorney and others in the community for similar work.’" (Bihun v. AT&T Information Systems, Inc. (1993) 13 Cal. App. 4th 976, 997 [affirming rate of $450 per hour], overruled on other grounds by Lakin v. Watkins Associated Indus. (1993) 6 Cal. 4th 644, 664.)  The burden is on the party seeking attorney fees to prove reasonableness of the fees. (Center for Biological Diversity v. County of San Bernardino (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 603, 615.)

The Court has broad discretion in determining the amount of a reasonable attorney's fee award which will not be overturned absent a “manifest abuse of discretion, a prejudicial error of law, or necessary findings not supported by substantial evidence.” (Bernardi v. County of Monterey (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1379, 1393-94.)  The Court need not explain its calculation of the amount of attorney’s fees awarded in detail; identifying the factors considered in arriving at the amount will suffice. (Ventura v. ABM Industries Inc. (2012) 212 Cal.App.4th 258, 274-75.)

Here, the Complaint was filed on June 25, 2025. Defendant Volkswagen Group of America, Inc. filed its Answer on July 16, 2025 and Defendant LAD-V, LLC filed its Answer on October 9, 2025. Plaintiff filed the motion for attorney’s fees on May 26, 2026 before the notice of settlement was filed on June 3, 2026. There was no other law and motion.  

In general, Lemon Law litigation presents certain routine issues which are applicable to other consumers’ vehicles, which would trigger economies of scale in terms of Plaintiff’s counsel’s efficiency in litigating this type of Lemon Law case. The Complaint included four causes of action, none of which were fraud-based.

The Court has reviewed the billing summary attached as Exhibit 40 to the Declaration of Kevin Y. Jacobson. The Court approves the hourly rates of $395, $425, $450, $500, $595 sought by Plaintiffs’ counsel’s firm to be reasonable for counsel with similar experience in the field.   (Jacobson Decl., ¶¶ 46 – 55.) However, the Court reads into these hourly rate experience in consumer and lemon law litigation which would account for efficiency and knowledge that would be expected of counsel billing at these rates, which in turn should minimize the amount of legal research and drafting of discovery requests and responses required for this Lemon Law case.

“A trial court is not required to state each charge it finds reasonable or unreasonable. A reduced award might be fully justified by a general observation that an attorney overlitigated a case.” (Karton v. Ari Design & Constr., Inc (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 734, 744-45.)

The Court acknowledges that Plaintiff’s counsel conducted written discovery and prepared for a deposition. The Court also notes that Defendant’s third CCP § 998 offer of $50,092.97 was more than double the first CCP § 998 offer of $23,509.83, so Plaintiff’s counsel cannot be faulted for failing to accept the first offer and continuing to conduct discovery. The Court declines to reduce the amount of fees to $5,215 as requested by Defendant in the opposition. However, the Court finds that the reasonable amount of attorney’s fees incurred in this case, including bringing this motion for attorney’s fees, is $12,000. The Court declines to apply a multiplier. The Court reads into the hourly rates charged by counsel the factors of risk and delay in payment. As such, the Court does not find any justification for the application of a multiplier and declines to apply such. 

[E]ven though the trial court did not specifically mention other factors open to consideration when a multiplier is under consideration, [an appellate] court must presume that the trial court considered all factors in reaching its decision, “even though the court may not have mentioned or discussed them in its written ruling.” (Citation omitted.)

     (Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 255.)

Plaintiff’s motion for attorney’s fees is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $12,000.

            Plaintiff alleges that Defendant failed to fully fund a construction loan borrowed by Plaintiff. In a lawsuit between the parties, the case proceeded as a reference proceeding, which resulted in a ruling against lender Cathay Bank and in favor of borrower Crossroads Action.

            Crossroads seeks to sell the property but requests cancellation of the promissory note based upon the running of the statute of limitations, and return the of the monies paid under the Holdback Agreement with Cathay Bank and Agreement following the sale of the Property.

Defendant Cathay Bank moves for a stay of this action pending determination of Crossroads Greenville Properties LTD, et al. v. Cathay Bank, Court of Appeal Case No. B348425 and the underlying Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. BC610411. 

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant Cathay Bank’s motion for a stay of this action pending determination of Crossroads Greenville Properties LTD, et al. v. Cathay Bank, Court of Appeal Case No. B348425 and the underlying Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. BC610411 is GRANTED in the exercise of this Court’s discretion.

The Court sets a status conference re: appeal for December 7, 2026 at 8:30 a.m. Status conference briefs are due 7 days prior.

ANALYSIS

Motion To Stay

Request For Judicial Notice

            Defendant requests that the Court take judicial notice of the following:

 

1. Complaint filed in Crossroads Greenville Properties v. Cathay Bank, Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. BC610411.

 

2. Cross-Complaint filed in Crossroads Greenville Properties v. Cathay Bank, Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. BC610411.

 

3. Statement of Decision filed in Crossroads Greenville Properties v. Cathay Bank, Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. BC610411.

 

4. Docket in Crossroads Greenville Properties v. Cathay Bank, Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. BC610411.

 

5. Opening Brief filed in Crossroads Greenville Properties et al. v. Cathay Bank, California Second District Court of Appeal Case No. B348425.

 

6. Docket in Crossroads Greenville Properties et al. v. Cathay Bank, California Second District Court of Appeal Case No. B348425.

 

Requests Nos. 1 – 6 are GRANTED per Evid. Code § 452(d)(court records).

Discussion

Defendant Cathay Bank moves for an order staying the instant case pending the disposition of the appeal in Crossroads Greenville Properties LTD, et al. v. Cathay Bank, Court of Appeal Case No. B348425 and the underlying Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. BC610411.

“Trial courts generally have the inherent power to stay proceedings in the interests of justice and to promote judicial efficiency. (Citations omitted.) “(Freiberg v. City of Mission Viejo (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1484, 1489.) “The granting or denying of [a]  . . . stay necessarily requires a court to exercise its equitable discretion.” (Webster v. Superior Court (1988) 46 Cal.3d 338, 345.)

“[T]he general rule [is] that trial courts have inherent power to stay trials awaiting final appellate resolution of related actions that might conclusively determine all or some of the issues in the stayed action.” (Koch-Ash v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 689, 696.)

By this proceeding they seek to stay a trial of the action until the issue raised on their appeal is determined. On the record now before us it would seem that a very substantial question is presented by the appeal, and that the case ought not to be tried until that question is decided. The possibility that the case might have to be tried twice, in the event of a reversal, should be avoided.

(Molybdenum Corp. of America v. Wright (1956) 143 Cal.App.2d 288, 289.)

            The Court agrees with Defendant Cathay Bank that a stay pending the appeal in Crossroads Greenville Properties LTD, et al. v. Cathay Bank, Court of Appeal Case No. B348425 and the underlying Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. BC610411 is appropriate. If the Court of Appeal reverses the Referee’s judgment in favor of Crossroads against Cathay Bank, including as to the Cross-Claim for breach of the promissory note, then the basis for cancellation in this action may no longer exist.

As such, the motion to stay is GRANTED in the exercise of this Court’s discretion. The Court sets a status conference re: appeal for December 7, 2026 at 8:30 a.m. Status conference briefs are due 7 days prior.

            Plaintiffs alleged that Defendants sold Plaintiffs inventory, equipment and other personal property in connection with a commercial lease. Plaintiffs were sued by the former tenants, who claimed that Plaintiffs did not have a right to sell or otherwise dispose of the inventory, equipment and other personal property. Plaintiffs settled the dispute and now sued Defendants for indemnity.

Defendants A.R.D. Services, Inc. and Amir Damavandi demur to the First Amended Complaint and move to strike portions thereof. 

TENTATIVE RULING

            Defendants A.R.D. Services, Inc. and Amir Damavandi’s demurrer to the First Amended Complaint is SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to the sole cause of action.

            Based on the ruling on the demurrer, the motion to strike is MOOT.

            Plaintiffs are given 30 days’ leave to amend.

ANALYSIS

Demurrer

Request For Judicial Notice

            Defendants request that the Court take judicial notice of the following:

Complaint (including Exhibit 5 record; Evid. Code, claims asserted in thereto - the lease for 920 S. Maple Avenue) filed August 16, 2021 in Michael Levine, Inc., et al. v. A.R.D. Services, Inc., et al., U.S. Bankruptcy Court, Central District of California, Adv. Proc. No. 2:21-ap 01169-ER, Doc. 29

Defendants Sohrab Issakharian's and Connect Apparel, LLC's Answer to Complaint and Crossclaim Against A.R.D. Services, Inc., filed in Adv. Proc. No. 2:21-ap-01169-ER

The requests are GRANTED per Evid. Code § 452(d)(court records).

Meet and Confer

            The Declaration of Andrew Ritholz that Defendants’ counsel satisfied the meet and confer obligation set forth in Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41.

Discussion

Defendants A.R.D. Services, Inc. and Amir Damavandi demur to the First Amended Complaint as follows:

1.         Sole Cause of Action (Implied Equitable Indemnity).

            The First Amended Complaint alleges as follows:

10. At all relevant times, Plaintiffs were engaged in the business of fabric sales and related commercial operations in Los Angeles County, California.

 

11. Defendants engaged in leasing commercial retail space to Plaintiffs.

 

12. At the time Plaintiffs entered into the certain leases with Defendants, Defendants sold certain inventory, equipment and other personal property, tangible and non-tangible (the “PROPERTY”) located at or associated with the leased premises to Plaintiffs. Defendants issued an invoice to Plaintiffs for the sale of the PROPERTY, which Plaintiffs paid. A true and correct copy of this invoice is attached hereto as Exhibit “A”.

 

13. Subsequent to the sale of the PROPERTY by Defendants to Plaintiffs, the prior tenant of the leased premises filed suit against Plaintiffs and Defendants herein, claiming that Plaintiffs did not have a right to sell or otherwise dispose of the PROPERTY, claiming damages of approximately $6,000,000.

 

14. At the time Plaintiffs took possession of the PROPERTY, they neither knew or had any reason to know that any third party had a claim to the PROPERTY, or the Defendants did not have marketable title to the PROPERTY or the right to sell it.

 

15. The lawsuit by the prior tenant was originally venued in the District Court for the Central District of California (case number 2:21-cv-03668-MWF-SK) (the “District Court Case”). It was later re-filed in the Bankruptcy Court for the Central District of California (Adversary Proceeding case number 2:21-ap-01169-ER) (the “Adversary Proceeding”).

 

16. Plaintiffs tendered the defense of both the District Court Case and Adversary Proceeding to Defendants, but Defendants refused the tender.

 

17. Plaintiffs filed a cross-claim against Defendants herein in the Adversary Proceeding, seeking the same relief as it is seeking here, namely equitable indemnity and contribution.

 

18. In the middle of 2025, after nearly 5 years of litigation, Plaintiffs settled the Adversary Proceeding with the Chapter 7 Trustee, who was the plaintiff in that action. Since the Chapter 7 Trustee, and therefore the bankruptcy estate, was no longer implicated in the Adversary Proceeding, which after the settlement involved only the competing cross-claims between the parties hereto, the Bankruptcy Court lost jurisdiction over the case. The Adversary Proceeding was thus dismissed, without prejudice.

 

19. Plaintiffs allege that any defects, irregularities, statutory violations, or wrongful conduct relating to the PROPERTY which was sold and transferred by Defendants to Plaintiffs were caused by the acts, omissions, and/or misconduct of Defendants, without the knowledge of Plaintiffs.

 

20. Plaintiffs did not participate in, direct, or control any wrongful conduct, if any, relating to the acquisition, handling, or disposition of the PROPERTY.

 

21. As a result of Defendants’ conduct in the sale of the PROPERTY to Plaintiffs, Plaintiffs have been exposed to claims and demands, and have incurred substantial damages, including attorneys’ fees and costs of suit, in defending and ultimately settling the District Court Case and the Adversary Proceeding.

 

22. Plaintiffs bring this action to complete its litigation of the cross-claims previously brought in the Adversary Proceeding against Defendants.

 

. . .

 

24. Plaintiffs are informed and believe, and on that basis allege, that any and all damages, injuries, or losses arising out of the sale and transfer of the PROPERTY from Defendants to Plaintiffs were directly and proximately caused by the acts, omissions, and/or wrongdoing of Defendants, to the detriment of Plaintiffs.

 

     (1AC, ¶¶ 13 – 22, 24.) 

It is well settled in California that equitable indemnity is only available among tortfeasors who are jointly and severally liable for the plaintiff's injury. (Citations omitted.) With limited exception, there must be some basis for tort liability against the proposed indemnitor. (Citation omitted.) “Generally, it is based on a duty owed to the underlying plaintiff [citations], although vicarious liability [citation] and strict liability [citation] also may sustain application of equitable indemnity. In addition, implied contractual indemnity between the indemnitor and the indemnitee can provide a basis for equitable indemnity. [Citation.]”   (Citation omitted.)

 

(Stop Loss Ins. Brokers, Inc. v. Brown & Toland Medical Group (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1036, 1040 [italics in original].)

            Here, Plaintiffs do not articulate the underlying theory whereby Defendants should be considered joint tortfeasors as to the former tenant who claimed the right to the inventory, equipment and other personal property. Plaintiffs must articulate an underlying legal theory whereby Defendants herein are liable for defects, irregularities, statutory violations or wrongful conduct which caused Plaintiffs to incur damages, settlement payments, attorney fees, expenses and costs incurred in their defense of the District Court Case and the Adversary Proceeding. (1AC, ¶¶ 19, 27.) The Court is not saying that Defendants are blameless. Rather, Plaintiffs must articulate what Defendants did wrong under a cognizable legal theory.

            The demurrer to the sole cause of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

Motion To Strike

            Based on the ruling on the demurrer, the motion to strike is MOOT.

            Plaintiffs are given 30 days’ leave to amend.
