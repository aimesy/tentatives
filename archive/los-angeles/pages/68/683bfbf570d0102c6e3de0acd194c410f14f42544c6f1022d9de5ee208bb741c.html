SUMMARY OF FACTS:
Plaintiffs Richard Vartanian and Elvira Vartanian allege that they are an elderly couple and that in December of 2019 defendant Lazaros Georgiadis (Lazaros), on behalf of defendant Lending Star Capital, offered plaintiffs an attractive reverse mortgage on their real property located in Altadena. At the time plaintiffs were in active Chapter 13 bankruptcy due to the fact that the subject property was under foreclosure by the second lienholder. Plaintiffs allege that Lazaros falsely represented himself as a licensed mortgage broker, sent a loan package, and, together with defendant Ivan Torres, proposed that plaintiffs could be provided with a bridge second mortgage loan until plaintiffs could obtain a reverse mortgage to pay off the second deed of trust, provided the plaintiffs would first exit Chapter 13 bankruptcy. 

Plaintiffs allege that in January of 2020, Lazaros sent plaintiffs a quote for a reverse mortgage, which was a fabrication since defendants did not open any reverse mortgage case with any reverse mortgage lender, and that plaintiffs were pushed to obtain mandatory counseling and sign the mortgage loan documents, although plaintiffs informed defendants repeatedly that they had no ability to repay the proposed loan.

In February of 2020, upon receiving the escrow papers, plaintiffs learned the identity of the investors, defendants Alex Dourbetas and Christina Dourbetas, who were not licensed mortgage brokers, and it became clear to plaintiffs that the transaction was not a bridge loan at all, but a second mortgage backed by a deed of trust. Plaintiffs then noticed that the payee in the escrow documents had been left blank, and brought that to the attention of Jamie Aguilar, who emailed plaintiffs with a copy of the escrow documents with the Dourbetas defendants typed in as payee.

Plaintiffs allege that at the insistence of defendants they filed a motion for voluntary dismissal of their bankruptcy case. Over the next several months, Lazaros attempted to get plaintiff Richard Vartanian to sign reverse loan documents, falsely assuring plaintiff that his wife could be added later on, when she could not. Plaintiffs learned that Lazaros never opened a reverse mortgage application for them.

In March of 2021, defendant Alex Dourbetas called plaintiffs and left a message stating, “we have a loan on your property, and you cannot pay for some time.” Dourbetas indicated he was considering foreclosing on the property in the next couple of days and wanted to give plaintiffs the opportunity to come up with some arrangement to make the loan. Plaintiffs attempted to meet with Dourbetas to come to an arrangement, but Dourbetas refused, stating that the account had been transferred to a collection agency, and on March 2, 2022, Dourbetas issued a notice of default, with a sale date set for July 12, 2022, which date has been postponed to August 2, 2022. Plaintiffs allege causes of action for elder abuse, IIED, fraud, cancellation of deed of trust, and quiet title.

On February 15, 2023 the Dourbetas defendants, as cross-complainants, filed and served a Cross Complaint against their co-defendants, alleging that the cross-complainants lent money to plaintiffs at the recommendation of the cross-defendants, who represented they had done proper due diligence as to plaintiffs’ suitability as borrowers, and falsely represented they were licensed to handle this sort of investment placement. Cross-complainants also allege that at no time prior to the subject transaction did the cross-complainants know that the Vartanian plaintiffs had requested a reverse mortgage from cross-defendants. The cross-complaint alleges causes of action for declaratory relief, fraud and concealment, and negligence.

ANALYSIS:
“After a party has completed his presentation of evidence in a trial by the court, the other party, without waiving his right to offer evidence in support of his defense or in rebuttal in the event the motion is not granted, may move for a judgment. The court as trier of the facts shall weigh the evidence and may render a judgment in favor of the moving party, in which case the court shall make a statement of decision as provided in Sections 632 and 634, or may decline to render any judgment until the close of all the evidence. The court may consider all evidence received, provided, however, that the party against whom the motion for judgment has been made shall have had an opportunity to present additional evidence to rebut evidence received during the presentation of evidence deemed by the presenting party to have been adverse to him, and to rehabilitate the testimony of a witness whose credibility has been attacked by the moving party. Such motion may also be made and granted as to any cross-complaint.” (CCP, § 631.8 subd. (a).) 

“If it appears that the evidence presented supports the granting of the motion as to some but not all the issues involved in the action, the court shall grant the motion as to those issues and the action shall proceed as to the issues remaining. Despite the granting of such a motion, no final judgment shall be entered prior to the termination of the action, but the final judgment in such action shall, in addition to any matters determined in the trial, award judgment as determined by the motion herein provided for.” (CCP, § 631.8 subd. (b).) “If the motion is granted, unless the court in its order for judgment otherwise specifies, such judgment operates as an adjudication upon the merits.” (CCP, § 631.8 subd. (c).)

Issue No. 1: Whether The Bridge Loan Is a Consumer Loan or a Commercial Loan
A consumer loan is defined as “a loan, whether secured by either real or personal property, or both, or unsecured, the proceeds of which are intended by the borrower for use primarily for personal, family, or household purposes.” (Financial Code, § 22203.) 

A commercial loan is defined as “a loan of a principal amount of five thousand dollars ($5,000) or more, or any loan under an open-end credit program, whether secured by either real or personal property, or both, or unsecured, the proceeds of which are intended by the borrower for use primarily for other than personal, family, or household purposes.” (Financial Code, § 22502.) 

The Vartanian’s argue that the bridge loan obtained from the Dourbetas Defendants was a consumer loan, as it was used for personal, family or household purposes. They argue that the Vartanians, facing foreclosure, “filed for bankruptcy and were attempting to obtain a reverse mortgage when they were contacted by Lazaros and Torres.” (Vartanian Mot., p. 5:15-16.)  The Vartanians assert that the only reason they “even agreed to the bridge loan was because Lazaros and Torres promised them that they would obtain a reverse mortgage on their behalf and the bridge loan was the only way to save their home.” (Vartanian Mot., p. 5:16-17.) Lastly, the Vartanian’s assert that they “never received any of the proceeds from the bridge loan.” (Vartanian Mot., p. 5:18-19.)

In their motion, the Dourbetas Defendants assert that the bridge loan was used to pay off an antecedent loan for “legal expenses associated with litigation for Mr. Vartanian’s aircraft business.” (Dourbetas Mot., p. 5:9-10.) Mr. Vartanian testified that he incurred the original second loan to cover legal fees and expenses arising from litigation related to his airplane business. (Vartanian Depo., pp. 71:2-73:3.) He testified that the litigation “massively disrupted our finances and had to the get the second mortgage.” (Vartanian Depo., p. 72:22-23.)  

“It is clear that a large number, if not the vast majority, of individuals who obtain a mortgage do so for the purpose of purchasing a personal or family residence. This would fall within the literal terms of [Financial Code section 22203’s] definition of debt that is acquired “for personal, family, or household purposes.” (Davidson v. Seterus, Inc. (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 283, 298.) Notwithstanding, California courts and Federal courts applying California law use a primary purpose test to classify loans, examining the “transaction as a whole” and “paying particular attention to ‘the purpose for which the credit was extended in order to determine whether the transaction was primarily consumer or commercial in nature.” (Slenk v. Transworld Systems, Inc. (2001) 236 F.3d 1072, 1075.) This inquiry requires courts to “elevate[] substance over form,” the Court in Slenk holding that “neither the lender’s motives nor the fashion in which the loan is memorialized are dispositive of this inquiry.” (Ibid.) Courts must therefore “look to the substance of the transaction and the borrower’s purpose in obtaining the loan, rather than the form alone.” (Ibid.) 

Here, the Vartanians obtained the bridge loan to pay off their second mortgage loan which was incurred for commercial business litigation expenses. Thus, the second mortgage was unambiguously obtained for a business or commercial purpose, rather than a personal, family or household purpose. When the primary purpose of a loan is to pay off a prior loan, the classification of the prior loan’s purpose is key to classifying the new loan. (See Slenk, supra, 236 F.3d at p. 1075.) Thus, as the primary purpose of the bridge loan was to pay off the second mortgage loan, the bridge loan inherits the second mortgage’s commercial purpose. 

Accordingly, the Court concludes that the bridge loan is a commercial loan, not a consumer loan. 

To the extent any findings or conclusions made in this ruling conflict with those set forth in the prior Statement of Decision, the ruling supersedes the prior Statement of Decision.  

Issue No. 2: Whether the Dourbetas Defendants Need to be Licensed
“No person shall engage in the business of a finance lender or broker without obtaining a license from the commissioner.” (Financial Code, § 22100 subd. (a).)

The Dourbetas Defendants assert that they do not need to be licensed as they were not “in the business” of making loans. Pursuant to the Business and Professions Code, in the business means “[t]he making of eight or more loans in a calendar year.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 10131.1 subdivision (b)(1)(C).) In support of this contention, the Dourbetas Defendants present expert testimony from Pamela Sosa (“Sosa”). Sosa testified that the Dourbetas’ were not required to have a license for two distinct reasons: (1) they were represented by a licensed broker, Mr. Albanez, at the time the transaction was conducted; and (2) they had not made eight or more loans in a calendar loan. (Sosa Depo., pp. 10:21-11:8.) 

The Vartanians argue that Albanez and his office were not involved in the negotiations of the bridge loan. At trial, Albanez testified that he was not involved with the Dourbetas’ hard money loan. (Trial Transcript, p.18:11-13.) By contrast, Torres and Georgiadis testified that they helped arrange the loan and were involved in the negotiations. (Trial Transcript, pp. 23:11-16, p. 48:20-27.) Neither Torres nor Georgiadis are licensed brokers. (Vartanian’s Mot., p. 4:10-11.) Thus, based on the testimony from trial, the Court does not find that the Dourbetas’ were represented by a licensed broker at the time the transaction was conducted. 

The Vartanians also argue that the definition of those “in the business of” on which Sosa relies does not apply here. Business and Professions Code section 10131.1 provides that, “as used in this section, [i]n the business means” “[t]he making of eight or more loans in a calendar year.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 10131.1 subd. (b)(1)(C).) Because the Financial Code does not separately define the phrase “in the business of,” the Court finds that the Business and Professions Code definition provides useful guidance in interpreting the relevant Financial Code provision. Moreover, the testimony and evidence presented does not demonstrate that the Dourbetas’ engaged in a regular commercial pattern of making loans, as opposed to the occasional investment-driven lending transaction. Accordingly, based on the definition in the Business and Professions Code, the expert testimony of Pamela Sosa, and the evidence and testimony presented at trial, the Court finds that the Vartanians are not “in the business of” making commercial loans.  

Thus, the Court concludes that the Dourbetas Defendants did not need a license.

Issue No. 3: Penalty Provisions
Lastly, Plaintiffs argue that Torres and Georgiadis willfully violated the Financial Code and argue that the Dourbetas loan should be void and unenforceable under Financial Code section 22750. As discussed above, the Court has concluded that the bridge loan is a commercial loan, not a consumer loan. Accordingly, Section 22750 does not apply. 

Moreover, as Georgiadis, Torres, Albanez dba The Lending Capital, and Lending Star Capital correctly point out in their motion, there is no private right of action for violations of the licensing requirements under Financial Code section 22100. In Lagrisola v. North American Financial Corp. (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 1178, the plaintiff brought a cause of action against defendant, a Nevada based mortgage lender, based on the allegation that the defendant lacked a license to lend money to California borrowers. (Lagrisola, supra, 96 Cal.App.5th at p. 1196.) The plaintiff argued that the “law specifically commands that an unlicensed lender is to forfeit all interest and finance charges made on any unlicensed loan.” (Ibid.) The Court of Appeal found that the statutory scheme does not contemplate a private right of action to pursue violations of Financial Code sections 22750 or 22100. (Id., at pp. 11195-1198.) The Court of Appeal noted that “when regulatory statutes, like the Financial Code, provide a comprehensive scheme for enforcement by an administrative agency, the courts ordinarily conclude that the Legislature intended the administrative remedy to be exclusive unless the statutory language or legislative history clearly indicates an intent to create a private right of action.” (Id. at p. 1196.) The Court found that Financial Code section 22752, subdivision (a), did not support a private right of action, particularly when read in context with Financial Code section 22713, which specifically provides that the commissioner or Attorney General may bring an action to enjoin a person from violating a provision of the California Financing Law (§§ 22000-2278.1.) (Id., at pp. 1196-1198.)

Accordingly, there is no private right of action to pursue violations of the licensing requirements set forth in Financial Code section 22100 or the penalty provisions applicable to consumer loans under Financial Code section 22750 or commercial loans under Financial Code section 22780. 
Thus, based on the foregoing, Richard and Elvira Vartanian’s Motion for Partial Judgment is DENIED.

Alex Dourbetas and Christina Dourbetas’s Motion for Partial Judgment is GRANTED.

Lazaros Georgiadis, Gersom Ivan Torres, Gabriel Angel Albanez dba The Lending Capital, and Lending Star Capital’s Motion is GRANTED.

Plaintiffs Richard and Elvira Vartanian’s Motion for Partial Judgment is DENIED.

Defendants/Cross-Complainants’ Alex Dourbetas and Christina Dourbetas’ Motion for Partial Judgment is GRANTED.

Case Name: Chandler v. Finch, et al.

MOTIONS TO COMPEL DISCOVERY

Moving Party: Defendant, Norma Lynn Finch  
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Anthony Chandler [Unopposed]

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Compel Plaintiff’s responses to special interrogatories and requests for production and request for sanctions. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Form Complaint
1) Motor Vehicle
2) General Negligence 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
This action arises out of an alleged motor vehicle accident. Anthony Chandler (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on June 30, 2022, at the intersection of Highland Oaks Drive and Foothill Boulevard in Arcadia, CA, Norma Lynn Finch (“Defendant”) negligently, wantonly, unlawfully, and carelessly failed to stop for the red light ahead, rear-ending Plaintiff’s vehicle, causing property damage and personal injury to Plaintiff.

ANALYSIS:
A motion to compel an initial response can be made on the ground that a party did not serve a timely response to interrogatories or a demand to produce. (CCP §§ 2030.290, subd. (a) [interrogatories], 2031.300, subd. (a) [demand to produce]; Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 404.)  The discovering party can also make a motion to deem as admitted any unanswered requests for admission or any requests answered in a late or unverified response. (CCP § 2033.280, subd. (b); CCP, § 2033.240, subd. (a) [RFA responses must be signed by responding party under oath]; Appleton v. Superior Court (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 632, 636 [unsworn response to RFAs is treated like no response].) These requests are not automatically deemed admitted; the discovering party must make the motion. (CCP § 2033.280, subd. (b).)

To establish this ground, a movant must show:
(1) Proper service (CCP §§ 2030.080, subd. (a) [interrogatories], 2031.040 [demand to produce]); § 2033.070 [requests for admission])
(2) Expiration of the deadline for the initial response 30 days after service or on date agreed to by parties (CCP §§ 2030.260, subds. (a), (b) [interrogatories], 2031.260, subds. (a), (b) [demand to produce] 2033.250, subds. (a), (b) [requests for admission]); and
(3) No timely response. (CCP §§ 2030.290 [interrogatories], 2031.300 [demand to produce] § 2033.280, subd. (b) [requests for admission])

A court must deny a motion to compel initial discovery where the discovery sought is outside the scope of discovery. (CBS, Inc. v. Superior Court (1968) 263 Cal.App.2d 12, 19; CCP § 2017.010 [scope of discovery].)

The SROGs and RFPs were served via electronic service on May 28, 2026. (Ortega Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. 1.) The parties agreed on a response deadline of July 20, 2026. (Ortega Decl., ¶ 5.) As of the date of the hearing on these motions, there is no evidence before the Court that Plaintiff has responded to the discovery requests. Thus, the motions are GRANTED. 

Request for Sanctions 
The Court must impose monetary sanctions against anyone—party, nonparty, or attorney—who unsuccessfully makes or opposes the motion, unless it finds that the person to be sanctioned acted with substantial justification or other circumstances make the imposition of the sanctions unjust. (CCP §§ 2030.290, subd. (c) [interrogatories], 2031.300, subd. (c) [demand to produce]; Sinaiko, supra, 148 Cal.App.4th at 404 [interrogatories and demand to produce].)

The Court must award sanctions when a party’s response to request for admissions is untimely, and the discovering party makes a motion to deem the requests admitted. (CCP, § 2033.280, subd. (c); Appleton, supra, 206 Cal.App.3d at 635-636 [sanctions are mandatory].)

“The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though no opposition to the motion was filed, or opposition to the motion was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.” (Rules of Court, rule 3.1348, subd. (a).)

Even after a party provides discovery responses, a party can keep its motion on calendar and the court has authority to grant sanctions, even if it denies the motion to compel responses “as essentially unnecessary, in whole or in part.” (Sinaiko, supra, 148 Cal.App.4th at 409.)
 
The burden is on the party subject to sanctions to show substantial justification or injustice. (Mattco Forge, Inc. v. Arthur Young & Co. (1990) 223 Cal.App.3d 1429, 1436.)

Defendant requests sanctions in the amount of $1,260.00 for the motion to compel responses to special interrogatories and $860.00 for the motion to compel responses to requests for production. The amount requested is slightly high given that no opposition or reply has been filed. The sanctions are adjusted accordingly. Sanctions are awarded as follows: $1,060.00 for the motion to compel responses to special interrogatories and $660.00 for the motion to compel responses to requests for production. 

RULING:
Defendant’s Motion to Compel Responses to Special Interrogatories is GRANTED. Plaintiff is ordered to serve full, Code-compliant, verified responses, without objections within 20 days.

Monetary sanctions sought by the moving party:
Utilizing a lodestar approach, and in view of the totality of the circumstances, the Court finds that the reasonable amount of attorney’s fees and costs incurred for the work performed in connection with the pending motion is $1,000.00 (2.5 hours @ $400/hour) [3.0 hours requested] plus $60.00 in filing fees for a total award of $1,060.00 [Amount Requested $1,260.00], which sum is to be awarded in favor of Defendant Norma Lynn Finch and against Plaintiff Anthony Chandler payable within 20 days. (CCP, § 2030.290, subd. (c); CRC Rule 3.1348(a).) 

Defendant’s Motion to Compel Responses to Requests for Production is GRANTED. Plaintiff is ordered to serve full, Code-compliant, verified responses, without objections within 20 days.

Monetary sanctions sought by the moving party:
Utilizing a lodestar approach, and in view of the totality of the circumstances, the Court finds that the reasonable amount of attorney’s fees and costs incurred for the work performed in connection with the pending motion is $600.00 (1.5 hours @ $400/hour) [2.0 hours requested] plus $60.00 in filing fees for a total award of $660.00 [Amount Requested $860.00], which sum is to be awarded in favor of Defendant Norma Lynn Finch and against Plaintiff Anthony Chandler payable within 20 days. (CCP, § 2031.300, subd. (c); CRC Rule 3.1348(a).)

Counsel for moving party is ordered to prepare an order for sanctions and submit it on eCourt by 3:00 PM today in accordance with this order.

Case Name: Mageno v. LKATEE Collective, Inc., et al.

MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES
[42 U.S.C. § 12205]

Moving Party: Defendant, Lkatee Collective, Inc. 
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Perla Mageno

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Grant attorneys’ fees in the amount of $35,685.00. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint
1) Violations of the Americans with Disabilities Act
2) Violations of the Unruh Civil Rights Act

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE:
The Court may take judicial notice of “[r]ecords of (1) any court of this state or (2) any court of record of the United States or of any state of the United States.” (Evidence Code, § 452, subd. (d).) 

Pursuant to Plaintiff’s request, the Court takes judicial notice of the following: (1) Plaintiff’s Complaint, filed October 31, 2024; (2) Plaintiff’s Amendment to Complaint naming OFD America, LLC as Doe Defendant 1, filed January 29, 2026; (3) The Court’s Minute Order dated March 13, 2026, adopting its tentative ruling and granting Defendant Lkatee Collective, Inc.’s Motion for Summary Judgment; (4) Notice of Entry of Judgment, filed April 1, 2026, with the Order of Dismissal and Judgment entered March 17, 2026; (5) Defendant’s Memorandum of Costs, filed April 1, 2026; (6) Declaration of Woochan Kwon in Support of Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment or, in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication, with Exhibit A thereto, filed December 23, 2025; (7) Supplemental Declaration of Woochan Kwon in Support of Defendant’s Reply, with Exhibit A thereto, filed March 2, 2026. 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
Plaintiff Perla Mageno (“Plaintiff”) is a visually-impaired and legally blind person who requires screen-reading software (“SRS”) to read website content using a computer. Plaintiff alleges that defendant Lkatee Collective, Inc. (“Lkatee”) fails to operate its website, www.oldferrydonutus.com, to be fully accessible to, and independently usable by, Plaintiff and other blind or visually-impaired people. Plaintiff alleges that during separate visits to the website, Plaintiff encountered multiple access barriers which denied Plaintiff full and equal access to the facilities, goods, and services offered to the public and made available to the public on Lkatee’s website. Plaintiff alleges that due to the widespread access barriers Plaintiff encountered on Lkatee’s website, Plaintiff has been deterred from obtaining full and equal access to the website.

ANALYSIS:
“In any action or administrative proceeding commenced pursuant to this chapter, the court or agency, in its discretion, may allow the prevailing party, other than the United States, a reasonable attorney’s fee, including litigation expenses, and costs, and the United States shall be liable for the foregoing the same as a private individual.” (42 U.S.C. § 12205.) 

The court, in its discretion, may award to the prevailing party reasonable attorney’s fees and costs if it finds that the plaintiff’s action is “frivolous, unreasonable or groundless, or that the plaintiff continued to litigate after it clearly became so.” (Christianburg Garment Co. v. EEOC (1978) 434 U.S. 412, 422.) The award of attorneys’ fees to prevailing defendants in meritless lawsuits is intended to “protect defendants from burdensome litigation having no legal or factual basis.” (Ibid., at p. 420.)  A fee award turns on whether the plaintiff had a colorable claim; California courts award attorneys’ fees where plaintiff’s claims were without foundation or completely groundless.  (See, e.g., Bond v. Pulsar Video Productions (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 918, 921-925 [awarding fees where plaintiff’s claims were frivolous and without foundation]; Cummings v. Benco Building Service (1992) 11 Cal.App.4th 1383.)

Lkatee moves for an order awarding attorneys’ fees as the prevailing party in this action on the grounds that Plaintiff’s action was frivolous, unreasonable and groundless. On March 13, 2026, the Court granted Lkatee’s motion for summary judgment on the basis that Lkatee had no right or duty to modify the allegedly discriminatory website, nor any ability to control or direct its contents. (3/13/2026 Minute Order.) 

 Lkatee argues that Plaintiff continued to assert her claims against Lkatee after discovery established that Lkatee did not own, operate, maintain, or have control over the website’s content or accessibility features. (Mot., pp. 4:28-5:2.) Thus, Lkatee argues that “Plaintiff’s claims against Defendant lacked a factual foundation as to a necessary element of liability.” (Mot., p. 5:3-4.) 

In the opposition, Plaintiff asserts that the franchisee agreement identifying OFD America, LLC as the franchisor, is not a publicly available document and the public records identified Lkatee as the operator of the store. (RJN Ex. F [Kwon Decl.] ¶ 5, Ex. A; Jankowski Decl., ¶ 2.) Plaintiff further asserts that it did not see the franchisee agreement until Lkatee filed the motion for the summary judgment. (RJN Ex. F, ¶¶ 5–6, Ex. A; Jankowski Decl., ¶ 7.) Lkatee did not serve its full verified discovery responses, including the franchisee agreement, until February 18, 2026, two days before the opposition to the motion for summary judgment was due. (Jankowski Decl., ¶¶ 3–8, Exs. 1–2.) 

The Court does not find that Plaintiff’s claims against Lkatee were frivolous, unreasonable, or without foundation. At the time this action was filed and up through the summary judgment motion, Plaintiff had a reasonable factual basis for assuming that Lkatee oversaw the website. Indeed, the Court did not indicate that Plaintiff’s claims were frivolous in its ruling on the motion for summary judgment. To the contrary, the Court acknowledged that the specific issue presented—whether a franchisee can be held liable for accessibility violations on a website that is wholly controlled by the franchisor—was a novel one, and that neither the parties nor the Court had identified any authority directly addressing that particular circumstance. (3/13/2026 Minute Order.)

Under Christianburg, Courts must avoid exactly what Lkatee demands here: “[I]t is important that a district court resist the understandable temptation to engage in post hoc reasoning by concluding that, because a plaintiff did not ultimately prevail, the action must have been unreasonable or without foundation.” (Christianburg Garment Co. v. EEOC (1978) 434 U.S. 412, 421-422.)

Thus, the motion is DENIED. 

RULING:
Defendant Lkatee Collective, Inc.’s Motion for Attorney Fees is DENIED.

Case Name: Lefkowitz v. Dulat, et al.

MOTIONS FOR ATTORNEY FEES
[CCP § 425.16 subd. (c)(1)]

Moving Party: Defendant, Lawrence Lishner
Defendant, Pierre Mazenod (erroneously sued and served as Pierre Dulat)
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Gary Lefkowitz [Unopposed]

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Grant attorneys’ fees and costs for defendant Lawrence Lishner in the amount of $28,795.21.

Grant attorneys’ fees and costs for defendant Pierre Mazenod in the amount of $17,059.99.

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint 
1) Fraud 
2) Forcible Detainer 
3) Trespass 
4) IIED 
5) Violation of the Unruh Act 
6) Breach of Contract 
7) ADA Violations 
8) Disability Laws 
9) Rehabilitation Act 
10) Elder Abuse 
11) Violation of B&P 17200

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
Plaintiff Gary Lefkowitz alleges that he has resided at an apartment unit on Arch Drive in Los Angeles since 2017. The complaint alleges that defendant Pierre Joseph Dulat owns the subject property and had MRI Realty Management as the property manager.  The complaint alleges that MRI Realty Management is not a corporation and that it is believed that no California real estate broker runs MRI Realty Management. ANALYSIS:
Pursuant to CCP section 425.16 subdivision (c)(1), “a prevailing defendant on a special motion to strike shall be entitled to recover his or her attorney's fees and costs.” (See also Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1141-1142.) Parties may seek attorney fees and costs in connection with a special motion to strike (1) in the moving papers, (2) in a subsequently filed motion, or (3) as part of a cost memorandum. (Melbostad v. Fisher (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 987, 992.)¿The moving party has the burden of proof in a motion for attorney’s fees. (ComputerXpress, Inc. v. Jackson (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 993, 1020.) The successful party is also entitled to fees incurred in filing the motion for anti-SLAPP fees, also referred to as “fees on fees.” (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 1141 [in anti-SLAPP, CCP § 425.16 context].)

Entitlement to Attorney’s Fees
It is undisputed that Lishner and Mazenod are the prevailing parties on the Anti-SLAPP Motions and are thus entitled to attorney fees. 

Reasonableness of Fees
In determining whether the attorneys’ fees requested under Section 425.16 are “reasonable,” the Court “may and should consider the nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, the skill required and the skill employed in handling the litigation, the attention given, the success of the attorney’s efforts, his learning, his age, and his experience in the particular type of work demanded . . . [,] the intricacies and importance of the litigation, the labor and necessity for skilled legal training and ability in trying the cause, and the time consumed.” (Church of Scientology v. Wollersheim (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 628, 659, disapproved on other grounds [quotation marks and emphasis omitted.]) 

Lishner Motion
Lishner seeks to recover attorney’s fees for two attorneys who worked on this matter. Their hourly rates are as follows: (1) Rinat Klier Erlich: $800/hour; and (2) Jennie Raphelt: $500/hour.

(Erlich Decl., ¶ 21.A.)

Case Name: Schlenz v. Santo Tomas Dental Group, Inc, et al.

MOTION TO QUASH DEPOSITION SUBPOENA
[CCP § ]

Moving Party: Plaintiff, Edith Felicitas Schlenz
Responding Party: Defendant, Victor Israel, DMD, DDS [Unopposed]

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Quash deposition subpoena for production of Medicare records.

CAUSES OF ACTION: from First Amended Complaint 
1) Dental Malpractice
2) Lack of Informed Consent
3) Financial Elder Abuse [Welfare & Institutions Code § 15610.30]
4) Unfair Business Practices [Business & Professions Code § 17200] 
5) Fraud
6) Medical Battery
7) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
Edith Felicitas Schlenz (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on April 25, 2025, Plaintiff, a 75-year-old senior citizen, visited defendants Santo Tomas Dental Group, Inc. and Evan Farr DDS, Inc. dba Santo Tomas Dental Group’s office for a dental consultation for an estimate to replace her partial upper dentures. Plaintiff alleges that despite her request for a quote to replace her partial upper dentures only, Defendants prepared an estimate for a treatment plan that included full upper dentures with two dental implants, and full lower dentures with six dental implants costing approximately $35,000.00. Plaintiff alleges that without her consent, defendant Victor Israel, DMD, DDS (“Israel”) extracted two of Plaintiff’s healthy, natural teeth, and placed two dental implants. 

The Complaint further alleges that, without Plaintiff’s knowledge or consent, Defendants secured a loan in Plaintiff’s name in the amount of $30,000.00 with an APR of 20.49% and monthly payments of $675.07, totaling $56,705.88 in payments over the life of the loan. The Complaint alleges that Defendants also secured an additional line of credit in the amount of $3,000.00 to be repaid over 60 months at an APR of 17.99%, without Plaintiff’s consent. Plaintiff alleges that she did not agree to finance any dental work, did not authorize the creation of a credit account, and was not informed about the nature of these financial agreements. Plaintiff alleges that she is on a fixed income from Social Security and should never have qualified for these loans. She alleges that Defendants fraudulently obtained and misused her financial information to secure these credit lines for services to which she did not knowingly agree. Plaintiff alleges that the debts have caused her severe emotional distress, physical pain and financial hardship. 

ANALYSIS:
“When a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, …, upon motion reasonably made by the party, the witness, or any consumer described in Section 1985.3, or upon the court's own motion after giving counsel notice and an opportunity to be heard, may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon such terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders.” (CCP § 1987.1) There is no requirement that the motion contain a meet-and-confer declaration demonstrating a good-faith attempt at informal resolution. (CCP § 1987.1) A motion to quash requires a separate statement, unless the party being subpoenaed did not provide any response to the subpoena. (Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(a)(5), (b)(6).)  

“Unless otherwise limited by order of the court in accordance with this title, any party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or to the determination of any motion made in that action, if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. Discovery may relate to the claim or defense of the party seeking discovery or of any other party to the action. Discovery may be obtained of the identity and location of persons having knowledge of any discoverable matter, as well as of the existence, description, nature, custody, condition, and location of any document, electronically stored information, tangible thing, or land or other property.” (CCP § 2017.010.) 

Plaintiff moves to quash the deposition subpoena for production of Medicare records issued and served by defendant Israel on the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, San Francisco Regional Office. Plaintiff argues that subpoena violates Plaintiff’s right to privacy, seeks documents not reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence, and is overbroad and burdensome. 

The subpoena seeks “Any and all documents pertaining to the subject. This request includes, but is not limited to the entire official and unofficial file, reports, forms, memoranda, claims, handwritten memoranda, written records of any kind, telephone memoranda, statements, tapes, photographs, videotapes, and any other information, media or data stored or transmitted electronically pertaining to this insurance file. Please include copies of any medical records that may be contained in the file. Please provide copies of any photographs in digital format such as JPG or JPEG on cd, flash drive or via email. MEDICARE SUBSCRIBER # 3QR3-YK6-CP92” (Marjiya Decl., Ex. A.) 

Plaintiff first argues that the subpoena violates her right to privacy. A plaintiff has a reasonable expectation of privacy in their medical information. (See John B. v. Superior Court (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1177, 1198 [finding medical records are protected by the California right to privacy].) Medical history is protected by the California constitutional right to privacy. (See John B., supra 38 Cal.4th at p. 1198.) Yet the privacy protection is not absolute; medical histories are discoverable to the extent the need for discovery outweighs the patient’s privacy concerns. (Id. at p. 1199.) All medical histories relating to a plaintiff’s claimed injuries¿in a personal injury action are discoverable. (See Evid. Code, §§ 996, 1016; Britt v. Sup.Ct. (San Diego Unified Port Dist.) (1978) 20 Cal.App.3d 844, 864.) However, medical records pertaining to an unrelated condition are discoverable on a showing of “good cause” if the condition is relevant to the issue of proximate causation. (See Evid. Code, § 999; Slagle v. Superior Court (1989) 211 Cal.App.3d 1309, 1313.) Accordingly, Defendants may only discover injuries or conditions not directly caused by the incident if they provide good cause for doing so (i.e. by demonstrating the injury is relevant to proximate causation).

Here, Plaintiff’s claimed injuries concern dental treatment. However, the subpoena seeks all Plaintiff’s Medicare records, not limited or tailored to dental work. Defendants have not filed any opposition to this motion or otherwise explained their request for Plaintiff’s entire Medicare records unrelated to the claimed injuries. Accordingly, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s medical records are protected by the California constitutional right to privacy, and Defendants have not met their burden to show good cause for the medical records sought. 

Thus, the motion is GRANTED. 

RULING:
Plaintiff Edith Felicitas Schlenz’s Motion to Quash is GRANTED. 

The subpoena issued to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, San Francisco Regional Office is hereby QUASHED.

Case Name: Reed v. White Pickle, LLC, et al.

DEMURRER AND MOTION TO STRIKE
[CCP § 430.10 subd. (e)]

Moving Party: Defendants, White Pickle LLC and Adam Grandmaison, erroneously sued as Adam Grandmaison dba No Jumper Podcast
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Jermel Reed 

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Sustain demurrer to Second Amended Complaint and strike the claims for punitive damages.

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Second Amended Complaint 
1) Premises Liability
2) General Negligence
3) Negligent Hiring, Retention, Supervision, and/or Entrustment
4) Battery
5) Assault
6) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
The Second Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleges that on December 26, 2024, Jermel Reed (“Plaintiff”) was lawfully present at the subject premises located at 2508 N. Ontario St., Burbank, CA for a business meeting with defendants Termaine Williams (“Williams”), Cash Jones (“Jones”), and Adam Grandmaison (“Grandmaison”). Grandmaison is the principal, owner, operator, manager, alter ego, and/or controlling person of White Pickle, LLC (“WP”) and the No Jumper business premises and operations. Plaintiff alleges that while he was engaged in conversation with Jones and Williams about their contemplated business deal, Williams suddenly and violently struck Plaintiff in the face. 

DEMURRER:
A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; CCP, § 430.10, subd. (e).) 

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) “[E]ach evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.) 

In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-67.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) A demurrer, however, “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)  

Procedural Issues
As an initial matter, the Court notes that Defendants filed a single joint opposition addressing both the instant demurrer and the demurrer filed in the related action, Siler v. White Pickle, LLC, et al. (LASC case no: 25NNCV06763.) The opposition was only filed in the Siler action, and not in the instant case. Accordingly, no opposition was technically filed to the instant demurrer. Although the two actions have been deemed related and arise from the same incident, they involve distinct plaintiffs, different allegations and separate complaints asserting different causes of action. The related designation does not consolidate the actions or otherwise permit the parties to treat filings in one action as filings in the other. As this is the first occurrence, the Court will consider the opposition in connection with the instant demurrer. Defendants are admonished that, going forward, filings must be separately and properly filed in each action to which they pertain. 

First Cause of Action, Premises Liability
The elements of a negligence claim and a premises liability claim are the same: a legal duty of care, breach of that duty, and proximate cause resulting in injury. (Kesner v. Superior Court (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1132, 1158.) Unlike the elements of breach, causation, and injury, all of which are fact-specific issues for the trier of fact, the existence and scope of duty are questions of law. (Id. at pp. 1142, 1144.) 

California law requires landowners to maintain land in their possession and control in a reasonably safe condition.¿(Ann M. v. Pacific Plaza Shopping Center (1993) 6 Cal.4th 666, 674 [disapproved on other grounds in Reid v. Google, Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 512, 527, fn. 5].)¿ Moreover, “foreseeability is a ‘crucial factor’ in determining the existence and scope of a legal duty.” (Delgado v. Trax Bar & Grill (2005) 36 Cal.4th 224, 237, citing Ann M., supra, 6 Cal.4th at pp. 674, 676.)

The Demurring Defendants, WP and Grandmaison, demur to the premises liability cause of action on grounds that: (i) they owed no legal duty to prevent third party criminal conduct; (ii) the SAC alleges neither misfeasance nor any special relationship that could create a legal duty; (iii) the SAC alleges no specific facts establishing the “extraordinarily high degree of foreseeability” required to hold WP and Grandmaison liable for third-party criminal acts; and (iv) the SAC fails to allege any defective, unsafe, or hazardous condition. 

When analyzing duty in the context of third-party acts, as in cases of misfeasance or when a special relationship exists, “the court considers the foreseeability of risk from the third party conduct.” (Melton v. Boustred (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 521, 532.) Generally, “a duty to take affirmative action to control the wrongful acts of a third party will be imposed only where such conduct can be reasonably anticipated.” (Ann M. v. Pacific Plaza Shopping Center (1993) 6 Cal.4th 666, 676, disapproved on other grounds in Reid v. Google, Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 512, 527, fn. 5.) And “in the case of criminal conduct by a third party, an extraordinarily high degree of foreseeability is required to impose a duty on the landowner, in part because ‘it is difficult if not impossible in today's society to predict when a criminal might strike.’ ” (Garcia v. Paramount Citrus Assn., Inc. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1448, 1457, quoting Wiener v. Southcoast Childcare Centers, Inc. (2004) 32 Cal.4th 1138, 1150).)  

“Misfeasance exists when the defendant is responsible for making the plaintiff’s position worse, i.e., defendant has created a risk.” (Weirum v. RKO General, Inc. (1975) 15 Cal.3d 40, 49.) A legal duty may arise from affirmative acts “where the defendant, through his or her own action (misfeasance) has made the plaintiff’s position worse and has created a foreseeable risk of harm from the third person. In such cases the question of duty is governed by the standards of ordinary care.” (Pamela L. v. Farmer (1980) 112 Cal.App.3d 206, 209; Weirum, supra, 15 Cal.3d at p. 49.) 

Here, the SAC alleges that “White Pickle and Grandmaison arranged, approved, authorized, facilitated, and/or knowingly permitted the meeting that brought Plaintiff to the premises for their business benefit.” (SAC, ¶ 24.) The SAC alleges that “White Pickle and Grandmaison had the right, ability, and authority to control access to the premises, approve or disapprove meetings and gatherings there, regulate who was invited onto the property for business purposes, and supervise security, crowd management, and guest conduct at the premises.” (SAC, ¶ 26.) The SAC alleges that “Defendants knew or should have known that bringing Plaintiff into that environment for a business meeting with Jones and Williams created a foreseeable and unreasonable risk of harm, particularly given the nature of the premises, the personalities involved, and the lack of reasonable protective measures.” (SAC, ¶ 28.) Lastly, the SAC alleges that “Defendants knew or should have known that invitees brought to the premises for business discussions could be exposed to sudden violence in the absence of proper supervision, security, access control, and separation of hostile or volatile individuals.” (SAC, ¶ 36.) 

These assertions are insufficient to allege a legal duty owed by WP or Grandmaison under the circumstances. Even accepting Plaintiff’s allegations as true, the SAC does not allege any facts demonstrating that WP or Grandmaison knew, or should have known, that inviting Plaintiff, Jones and Williams would result in Williams assaulting Plaintiff. There are no allegations that WP or Grandmaison took any actions to stimulate criminal conduct. There is nothing inherently risky about hosting a business meeting at a podcast studio. There are no allegations or inferences that violence was a necessary component of the meeting or that WP or Grandmaison encouraged the attack. There are no allegations that WP or Grandmaison ordered Williams to commit the assault or were in any way involved in the attack. Rather, based on the allegations in the SAC, Plaintiff’s theory of liability is that Plaintiff, Jones and Williams were all invitees at property owned or controlled by WP and Grandmaison, that Jones and Williams were volatile individuals, and that WP and Grandmaison did not have adequate security measures in place. Such allegations do not constitute misfeasance.

Thus, Plaintiff has not alleged facts demonstrating that WP or Grandmaison owed a duty of care to protect Plaintiff from third-party criminal conduct. 

In the opposition, Plaintiff asserts that the police report for the incident “identifies Jones and Williams as persons with gang affiliations and describes defendant, Williams as associated with the Eight Tray Gangster Crips and Rollin’ 60s Neighborhood Crips. The report also states that Grandmaison hosted numerous admitted Blood and Crip gang members on No Jumper, some of whom became violent against Grandmaison or other people present at the podcast studio during interviews.” (Opp., p. 8:11-15.) These allegations, however, are not contained in the SAC, and the Court therefore cannot consider them in ruling on the demurrer. Nevertheless, Plaintiff has demonstrated a manner in which the SAC can be amended that could change the legal effect of the pleading. 

Accordingly, the demurrer to the first cause of action is SUSTAINED. 

Second Cause of Action, General Negligence
The necessary elements for negligence are: (1) that the defendant owed a legal duty to the plaintiff to use due care; (2) a breach of that duty; (3) causation; and (4) damages to the plaintiff.¿ (County of Santa Clara v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 292, 318.) 

For the same reasons as discussed above, the Court finds that Plaintiff has not sufficiently alleged that WP or Grandmaison owed a duty of care under the circumstances here. 

The demurrer to the second cause of action is SUSTAINED.

Third Cause of Action, Negligent Hiring/Retention/ Supervision
The elements of a cause of action for negligent hiring, retention, or supervision are: (1) the employer’s hiring, retaining, or supervising an employee; (2) the employee was incompetent or unfit; (3) the employer had reason to believe undue risk of harm would exist because of the employment; and (4) harm occurs. (Evan F. v. Hughson United Methodist Church (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 828, 836-837.)

The SAC alleges that a few months before the alleged assault, “Plaintiff met with Jones and Williams at a hotel restaurant to discuss business opportunities relating to the proposed show, related media ventures, and the parties’ anticipated working relationship.” (SAC, ¶ 13.) The SAC alleges that “During that hotel meeting, Jones spoke on behalf of both himself and Grandmaison concerning the contemplated business relationship, the proposed show, and the parties’ anticipated collaboration.” (SAC, ¶ 14.) Lastly, the SAC alleges that “Jones’s participation in those discussions and his speaking on Grandmaison’s behalf were consistent with Jones’s business association with Grandmaison through “The Adam & Wack Show,” which was associated with No Jumper and its related media operations.” (SAC, ¶ 15.)

While these allegations suggest a business association between the parties, they do not allege an employee-employer relationship between Grandmaison and Jones. The SAC does not allege that Jones is an employee of Grandmaison or that Jones was acting under Grandmaison’s employment, direction, control or authorization. Accordingly, the SAC does not allege facts sufficient to support a claim against Grandmaison for negligently hiring, retaining or supervising Jones.  

Thus, the demurrer to the third cause of action is SUSTAINED. 

Sixth Cause of Action, Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress
“The elements of a prima facie case for the tort of intentional infliction of emotional distress are: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct. Conduct to be outrageous must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.” (Wilson v. Hynek (2012) 207 Cal.App.4th 999, 1009, citation and ellipses omitted.) 

WP and Grandmaison demur to the sixth cause of action on the basis that Plaintiff amended the FAC to add the claim without leave of the Court. The Court agrees that adding the emotional distress was beyond the scope of the Court’s order granting leave to amend the FAC. Sustaining the demurrer to the FAC with leave to amend granted Plaintiff leave to amend only the causes of action in the FAC, not leave to add an entirely new cause of action. (Patrick v. Alacer Corp. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 995, 1015.) “Following an order sustaining a demurrer or a motion for judgment on the pleadings with leave to amend, the plaintiff may amend his or her complaint only as authorized by the court’s order. The plaintiff may not amend the complaint to add a new cause of action without having obtained permission to do so, unless the new cause of action is within the scope of the order granting leave to amend.” (Zakk v. Diesel (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 431, 456 [quoting Harris v. Wachovia Mortgage, FSB (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1018, 1023].)

Moreover, Plaintiff does not address this argument or this cause of action in the opposition. 

Thus, the demurrer to the sixth cause of action is SUSTAINED.

The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (a) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (b) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (CCP § 436, subds. (a), (b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].)

Because the Court has sustained the Demurrer in its entirety, the motion to strike is MOOT. 

RULING:
Defendants White Pickle LLC and Adam Grandmaison’s Demurrer is SUSTAINED, with 15 days leave to amend. 

The motion to strike is MOOT.

Case Name: Siler v. White Pickle, LLC, et al.

DEMURRER AND MOTION TO STRIKE
[CCP § 430.10 subd. (e)]

Moving Party: Defendants, White Pickle LLC and Adam Grandmaison, erroneously sued as Adam Grandmaison dba No Jumper Podcast
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Eban Siler

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Sustain demurrer to Second Amended Complaint and strike the claims for punitive damages. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Second Amended Complaint
1) Premises Liability
2) Negligence [Operational Negligence]
3) Negligent Retention, Negligent Entrustment and Negligent Supervision
4) Battery 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE:
The Court may take judicial notice of “[r]ecords of (1) any court of this state or (2) any court of record of the United States or of any state of the United States.” (Evidence Code, § 452, subd. (d).) 

The court, however, may not take judicial notice of the truth of the contents of the documents. (Herrera v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1366, 1375.) Documents are only judicially noticeable to show their existence and what orders were made such that the truth of the facts and findings within the documents are not judicially noticeable. (Lockley v. Law Office of Cantrell, Green, Pekich, Cruz & McCort (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 875, 885.)   
Pursuant to Defendants’ request, the Court takes judicial notice of the following: (1) Plaintiff Jermel Reed’s Second Amended Complaint for Damages, filed March 30, 2026, in Jermel Reed v. White Pickle LLC, et al., Case No. 25NNCV05431; and (2) Notice of Ruling and Continuance of CMC and OSC re Related Case Determination, dated March 20, 2026, including the Court’s March 20, 2026 Minute Order in Jermel Reed v. White Pickle LLC, et al., Case No. 25NNCV05431. 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
Eban Siler (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on December 26, 2024, he was on the premises located at 2508 N. Ontario St. in Burbank when he was assaulted and battered by agents/employees/guests/affiliates of Defendant White Pickle LLC (“WP”), owned/controlled by Defendant Adam Grandmaison (“Grandmaison”). Plaintiff also alleges that Defendants WP, Grandmaison, Cash Jones (also known as Luce Cannon, “Jones”), and Termain Williams (also known as Wack 100, “Williams”), by and through their agents/employees, intentionally touched Plaintiff without his consent such that he suffered multiple injuries to his body and face.

DEMURRER:
A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; CCP, § 430.10, subd. (e).) 

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) “[E]ach evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.) 

In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-67.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) A demurrer, however, “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)  

First Cause of Action, Premises Liability
The elements of a negligence claim and a premises liability claim are the same: a legal duty of care, breach of that duty, and proximate cause resulting in injury. (Kesner v. Superior Court (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1132, 1158.) Unlike the elements of breach, causation, and injury, all of which are fact-specific issues for the trier of fact, the existence and scope of duty are questions of law. (Id. at pp. 1142, 1144.) 

California law requires landowners to maintain land in their possession and control in a reasonably safe condition.¿(Ann M. v. Pacific Plaza Shopping Center (1993) 6 Cal.4th 666, 674 [disapproved on other grounds in Reid v. Google, Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 512, 527, fn. 5].)¿ Moreover, “foreseeability is a ‘crucial factor’ in determining the existence and scope of a legal duty.” (Delgado v. Trax Bar & Grill (2005) 36 Cal.4th 224, 237, citing Ann M., supra, 6 Cal.4th at pp. 674, 676.)

The Demurring Defendants, WP and Grandmaison, demur to the premises liability cause of action on grounds that: (i) they owed no legal duty to prevent third party criminal conduct; (ii) the SAC alleges neither misfeasance nor any special relationship that could create a legal duty; (iii) the SAC alleges no specific facts establishing the “extraordinarily high degree of foreseeability” required to hold WP and Grandmaison liable for third-party criminal acts; and (iv) the SAC fails to allege any defective, unsafe, or hazardous condition. 

When analyzing duty in the context of third-party acts, as in cases of misfeasance or when a special relationship exists, “the court considers the foreseeability of risk from the third party conduct.” (Melton v. Boustred (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 521, 532.) Generally, “a duty to take affirmative action to control the wrongful acts of a third party will be imposed only where such conduct can be reasonably anticipated.” (Ann M. v. Pacific Plaza Shopping Center (1993) 6 Cal.4th 666, 676, disapproved on other grounds in Reid v. Google, Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 512, 527, fn. 5.) And “in the case of criminal conduct by a third party, an extraordinarily high degree of foreseeability is required to impose a duty on the landowner, in part because ‘it is difficult if not impossible in today's society to predict when a criminal might strike.’ ” (Garcia v. Paramount Citrus Assn., Inc. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1448, 1457, quoting Wiener v. Southcoast Childcare Centers, Inc. (2004) 32 Cal.4th 1138, 1150).)  

“Misfeasance exists when the defendant is responsible for making the plaintiff’s position worse, i.e., defendant has created a risk.” (Weirum v. RKO General, Inc. (1975) 15 Cal.3d 40, 49.) A legal duty may arise from affirmative acts “where the defendant, through his or her own action (misfeasance) has made the plaintiff’s position worse and has created a foreseeable risk of harm from the third person. In such cases the question of duty is governed by the standards of ordinary care.” (Pamela L. v. Farmer (1980) 112 Cal.App.3d 206, 209; Weirum, supra, 15 Cal.3d at p. 49.) 

Here, the SAC alleges that Defendants exercised control over who was invited to the subject premises, which individuals were permitted to participate in podcast activities and meetings, whether participants and invitees were separated or monitored, whether any security or supervision was provided, and whether business invitees were exposed to known dangerous individuals. (SAC, ¶ 17.) The SAC alleges that Jones and Williams “were regularly engaged podcast participants and business affiliates who were deliberately brought into Defendants’ business environment.” (SAC, ¶ 19.) The SAC alleges that “Jones and Williams had publicly claimed gang affiliations and had publicly known violent propensities” and that Defendants knew or should have known “of these dangerous propensities through their repeated on-air and off-air association with Jones and Williams, Grandmaison’s hosting of gang-affiliated guests on the No Jumper podcast and related media operations, their public statements and media appearances, and prior violent incidents occurring in connection with Defendants’ podcast and studio operations.” (SAC, ¶¶ 21-22.) The SAC further alleges that “Defendants allowed a post-podcast congregation of numerous associates, participants, and invitees in an unsecured exterior breezeway and parking area, and failed to control interactions or provide reasonable safeguards in that area.” (SAC, ¶ 24.) Lastly, the SAC alleges that “While Plaintiff was at the Subject Premises or in the course of leaving it, Plaintiff was violently attacked, beaten, struck, and battered by Jones, Williams, and other individuals acting in concert with them.” (SAC, ¶ 25.) 

These assertions are insufficient to allege a legal duty owed by WP or Grandmaison under the circumstances. The Court accepts as true Plaintiff’s allegations that WP and Grandmaison exercised control over who was invited to the subject business premises, that Jones and Williams had publicly known violent propensities, and that WP and Grandmaison allowed podcast associates to congregate outside the studio post-podcast. However, WP and Grandmaison’s general awareness of Jones’ and Williams’ violent propensities and permission for them to conduct podcast business at the studio did not create the peril that harmed Plaintiff. 

There are no allegations that WP or Grandmaison took any actions to stimulate criminal conduct. There is nothing inherently risky about recording a podcast. There are no allegations or inferences that violence was a necessary component of the podcast event or that WP or Grandmaison encouraged the attack. There are no allegations that WP or Grandmaison ordered Jones and Williams to commit the assault, were present at the scene of the incident, or were in any way involved in the attack. Rather, based on the allegations in the SAC, Plaintiff’s theory of liability is that Plaintiff, Jones and Williams were invitees at property owned or controlled by WP and Grandmaison and that WP and Grandmaison did not have adequate security measures in place. Such allegations do not constitute misfeasance.

Thus, Plaintiff has not alleged facts demonstrating that WP or Grandmaison owed a duty of care to protect Plaintiff from third-party criminal conduct.

In the opposition, Plaintiff asserts that the police report for the incident states that “Grandmaison had hosted numerous admitted Blood and Crip gang members on his show, some of whom had become violent against Grandmaison or others present at the podcast studio during interviews.” (Opp., p. 2:22-24.) This allegation, however, is not contained in the SAC, and the Court therefore cannot consider it in ruling on the demurrer. Nevertheless, Plaintiff has demonstrated a manner in which the SAC can be amended that could change the legal effect of the pleading. 

Accordingly, the demurrer to the first cause of action is SUSTAINED. 

Second Cause of Action, Operational Negligence
The necessary elements for negligence are: (1) that the defendant owed a legal duty to the plaintiff to use due care; (2) a breach of that duty; (3) causation; and (4) damages to the plaintiff.¿ (County of Santa Clara v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 292, 318.) 

For the same reasons as discussed above, the Court finds that Plaintiff has not sufficiently alleged that WP or Grandmaison owed a duty of care under the circumstances here. 

The demurrer to the second cause of action is SUSTAINED.

Third Cause of Action, Negligent Hiring/Retention/ Supervision
The elements of a cause of action for negligent hiring, retention, or supervision are: (1) the employer’s hiring, retaining, or supervising an employee; (2) the employee was incompetent or unfit; (3) the employer had reason to believe undue risk of harm would exist because of the employment; and (4) harm occurs. (Evan F. v. Hughson United Methodist Church (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 828, 836-837.)

The SAC alleges that Jones and Williams were each a “retained podcast participant, affiliate, associate, invitee, representative, and/or agent” of WP and Grandmaison. (SAC, ¶¶ 4-5.) The SAC further alleges that “Defendants retained, authorized, and permitted Jones and Williams to participate in Defendants’ business operations and to interact with invitees at the Subject Premises.” (SAC, ¶ 45.) These allegations are unclear as to whether Jones and Williams were actually WP or Grandmaison’s employees or merely guests or affiliates present at the property. The SAC does not sufficiently allege that the assailants were acting under the employment, directive, control or authorization of WP or Grandmaison such that they could be held liable for negligently hiring, retaining or supervising them. 

Thus, the demurrer to the third cause of action is SUSTAINED. 

MOTION TO STRIKE: 
The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (a) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (b) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (CCP § 436, subds. (a), (b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].)

Because the Court has sustained the Demurrer in its entirety, the motion to strike is MOOT. 

RULING:
Defendants White Pickle LLC and Adam Grandmaison’s Demurrer is SUSTAINED, with 15 days leave to amend. 

The motion to strike is MOOT.

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
The Complaint alleges that on May 30, 2024, Emadia Fayad (“Plaintiff”) was admitted to Defendants’ residential care facility for the elderly suffering from dementia. Plaintiff alleges that during the time she was a resident at the facility, she suffered multiple severe witnessed and unwitnessed and avoidable falls. As a result of the multiple falls, Plaintiff suffered from significant injuries, including a fractured hip which required surgical repair. The Complaint alleges that upon Plaintiff’s admission to the facility, Defendants were aware that Plaintiff suffered from dementia, was at risk of falls, and required special care and assistance including 24-hour supervision and monitoring, assistance with ambulation and transferring, and the provision of safety and assistance devices to prevent accidents. ANALYSIS:
A motion to compel a further response is used when a party gives unsatisfactory answers or makes untenable objections to interrogatories, demands to produce, or requests for admission. (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (a); Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 403.)

To compel a further response to interrogatories, the movant can show that: (1) the responding party’s answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete (CCP, § 2030.300, subd. (a)(1)); (2) the responding party’s exercise of the option to produce documents in response to an interrogatory was unwarranted or the required specification of those documents was inadequate (CCP, § 2030.300, subd. (a)(2)); and (3) the responding party’s objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general (CCP, § 2030.300, subd. (a)(3); see, e.g., Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 550 [defendant’s argument that plaintiff was required to establish good cause or prove merits of underlying claim before propounding interrogatories without merit]).

To request further production, a movant must establish: (1) good cause for the production (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1); Sinaiko, supra, at p. 403); and (2) that a further response is needed because (a) the responding party’s statement of compliance with the demand to produce is incomplete (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (a)(1)), (b) the responding party’s representation that it is unable to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (a)(2)), (c) the responding party’s objection in the response is without merit or is too general (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (a)(3); Catalina Island Yacht Club v. Superior Court (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1127), or (d) if the responding party objected to the production of ESI on the ground that it is not reasonably accessible the movant can show that the (i) ESI is reasonably accessible or (ii) there is good cause for production of the ESI regardless of its accessibility. (CCP, § 2031.310, subd. (e).)

In the reply, Plaintiff withdraws the motion as to Special Interrogatory Nos. 3, 5, 8-9, 24, 35, 41, 44-46, and 51. This leaves Special Interrogatories Nos. 1-2, 4, 6-7, 10-24, 26-34, 36-40, 42-43, 47-50, 52-68, and 70-77 at issue. 

Plaintiff also withdraws the motion as to Requests for Production No. 37. This leaves Requests for Production Nos. 1-36, 38-87, and 100-112. 

The Court notes that the parties have five additional discovery motions on calendar aside from the instant two motions to compel further. The Court reminds counsel that discovery is a self-executing process that is designed to require little or no court intervention. (See Clement v. Alegre (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1277, 1291-1292.) Given the volume of the parties’ discovery disputes, the Court strongly encourages counsel to engage in meaningful meet and confer efforts to narrow the issues before seeking further judicial intervention. “[A]¿reasonable and good faith attempt at informal resolution entails something more than bickering with [opposing] counsel….¿ Rather, the law requires that counsel attempt to talk the matter over, compare their views, consult, and deliberate.” (Id. at p. 1294.)

Case Name: Gonzalez v. BMW of North America, et al.

MOTION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER
[CCP § 2025.420 subd. (b)]

Moving Party: Defendant, BMW of North America, LLC
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Gina M. Gonzalez

RELIEF REQUESTED:
A protective order allowing for the deposition of BMW NA’s PMK to take place remotely on a mutually agreeable date.

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint 
1) Breach of Express Warranty Under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act
2) Breach of Implied Warranty Under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act
3) Violation of the Song-Beverly Act Section 1793.2(b)

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
This case is a lemon-law action. Gina M. Gonzalez (“Plaintiff”) allege that on June 3, 2024, Plaintiff purchased a 2023 BMW 760i, for which BMW of North America, LLC (“BMW”) issued express warranties. Plaintiff alleges that the vehicle was delivered with defects and nonconformities to warranties. Plaintiff alleges that the defects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves within the express warranty period. The Complaint alleges that notwithstanding Plaintiff’s entitlement, Defendant has failed to either promptly replace the new motor vehicle or to promptly make restitution in accordance with the Song-Beverly Act. 

ANALYSIS:
“Before, during, or after a deposition, any party, any deponent, or any other affected natural person or organization may promptly move for a protective order. The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (CCP, § 2025.420 subd. (a).)

“The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party, deponent, or other natural person or organization from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense. This protective order may include, but is not limited to, one or more of the following directions: […] That the deposition be taken only on certain specified terms and conditions.” (CCP, § 2025.420 subd. (b)(5).)

With respect to protective orders generally, the burden of showing good cause is ordinarily on the party seeking the protective order. (Beverly Hills Nat. Bank & Trust Co. v. Superior Court (1961, 2nd Dist.) 195 Cal.App.2d 861, 866-867.) The granting of a protective order is within the discretion of the trial court. (Id.) The granting or denial of a protective order is reviewed for abuse of discretion. (See Meritplan Insurance Co. v. Superior Court (1981) 124 Cal.App.3d 237, 242.) When the record shows facts on which the trial court exercised its discretion, this exercise will not be disturbed on appeal. (Foster v. Gillette Co. (1979) 100 Cal.App.3d 569, 578.) 

BMW moves for a protective order allowing for the deposition of BMW’s PMK to take place remotely. Plaintiff noticed the deposition of BMW’s PMK to take place in person at Plaintiff’s counsel’s office on August 25, 2026. (Mot., Ex. A.) BMW requested that the deposition be remote, however Plaintiff’s counsel insists on an in-person deposition. BMW argues that good cause exists for a remote deposition because “BMW NA’s PMK handles cases all over the state, so it is impossible to always be within the county of every action.” (Mot., p. 4:21-22.) BMW further argues that “[t]o require the PMK to appear in person for every deposition would unduly burden BMW NA and unnecessarily increase expenses where technology has advanced such that it is very easy to do these remotely.” (Mot., p. 4:25-27.) 

“The deposition of an organization that is a party to the action shall be taken at a place that is, at the option of the party giving notice of the deposition, either within 75 miles of the organization’s principal executive or business office in California, or within the county where the action is pending and within 150 miles of that office.” (CCP, § 2025.250 subd. (b).) “If an organization has not designated a principal executive or business office in California, the deposition shall be taken at a place that is, at the option of the party giving notice of the deposition, either within the county where the action is pending, or within 75 miles of any executive or business office in California of the organization.” (CCP, § 2025.250 subd. (d).)

The Court finds that BMW has not met its burden to show good cause for a protective order. While the Court understands that it is burdensome for BMW’s PMK to appear for an in-person deposition, BMW has not demonstrated that, based on any specific facts or circumstances pertaining to this case, a protective order is warranted here. Pursuant to CCP section 2025.250, Plaintiff is entitled to take the deposition within 75 miles of BMW’s principal executive or business office in California, or within LA County and within 150 miles of that office, unless the parties agree to conduct the deposition remotely. (CCP, § 2025.250 subd. (b).) BMW has not demonstrated that the deposition as noticed is not compliant with the statute or would other subject BMW to unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense. 

In the reply, BMW asserts that on two recent occasions in other cases, BMW has been forced to suspend in-person depositions at Plaintiff’s Counsel’s office due to Plaintiff’s counsel’s uncivil behavior. (Sheehan Decl., ¶¶ 7-9.) Plaintiff’s counsel is strongly admonished that any irregular or unprofessional conduct during the deposition of BMW’s PMK may constitute grounds for a protective order requiring future depositions in this matter to proceed remotely, as well as any other appropriate sanctions or relief. 

The motion is DENIED. 

RULING:
Defendant BMW of North America, LLC’s Motion for a Protective Order is DENIED.

[CCP § 430.10 subds. (e) and (f)]

Moving Party: Defendants, Rodrigo Tonetto and Solange Girardelle 
Responding Party: Plaintiff, HUSPRF 290 North Hudson Ave LP

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Sustain demurrer to First Amended Complaint.

CAUSES OF ACTION: from First Amended Complaint
1) Unlawful Detainer 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE
The Court may take judicial notice of “[r]ecords of (1) any court of this state or (2) any court of record of the United States or of any state of the United States” and of “[o]fficial acts of the legislative, executive, and judicial departments of the United States and of any state of the United States.” (Evidence Code, § 452, subds. (c)-(d).) 

Pursuant to Defendants’ request, the Court takes judicial notice of the following: (1) The FAC filed in this action, including the attached Three-Day Notice to Pay Rent or Quit; (2) Initial Notice of Non-Compliance issued by the City of Pasadena Rent Stabilization Department, dated March 24, 2025, concerning the subject property, stating that the property was out of compliance with Pasadena Charter Article XVIII due to its failure to register for fiscal years 2024 and 2025, and advising that no rent increase or upward adjustment of rent was authorized during the period of noncompliance; and (3) Rental Registration Certification issued by the City of Pasadena Rent Stabilization Department on June 17, 2025, concerning the subject property and stating that the property was then in compliance with its registration obligations under Pasadena Charter Article XVIII. (RJN, Exs. 1-3.) 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
HUSPRF 290 North Hudson Ave LP (“Plaintiff”) is the owner of real property located at 290 N. Hudson Avenue #103E, in Pasadena, CA 91101. Rodrigo Tonetto and Solange Girardelle (collectively, “Defendants”) are tenants in the property pursuant to a June 1, 2025 lease agreement. Plaintiff served Defendants with a 3-day notice to pay rent or quit on April 13, 2026. The FAC alleges that Defendants failed to comply with the requirements of the notice.  

ANALYSIS:
A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; CCP, § 430.10, subd. (e).) 

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) “[E]ach evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.) 

In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-67.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) A demurrer, however, “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)  

A demurrer for uncertainty lies where the pleading is uncertain, ambiguous, or unintelligible. (CCP, § 430.10, subd. (f).)

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616 [disapproved on other grounds].) As a result, a special demurrer for uncertainty is not intended to reach failure to incorporate sufficient facts in the pleading, but it rather is directed only at uncertainty existing in the allegations already made. (People v. Taliaferro (1957) 149 Cal.App.2d 822, 825, disapproved on other grounds in Jefferson v. J.E. French Co. (1960) 54 Cal.2d 717, 719-720 [statute of limitations question].)

Where a complaint is sufficient to state a cause of action and to apprise a defendant of issues he is to meet, it is not properly subject to a special demurrer for uncertainty. (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 643 [“A special demurrer [for uncertainty] should be overruled where the allegations of the complaint are sufficiently clear to apprise the defendant of the issues which he is to meet”].) 

Unlawful Detainer
A tenant is guilty of unlawful detainer when “the tenant continues in possession, in person or by subtenant, without the permission of the landlord, […], after default in the payment of rent, pursuant to the lease or agreement under which the property is held, and three days’ notice, excluding Saturdays and Sundays and other judicial holidays, in writing, requiring its payment, stating the amount that is due, the name, telephone number, and address of the person to whom the rent payment shall be made, and, if payment may be made personally, the usual days and hours that person will be available to receive the payment […], or the number of an account in a financial institution into which the rental payment may be made, and the name and street address of the institution […], or if an electronic funds transfer procedure has been previously established, that payment may be made pursuant to that procedure, or possession of the property, shall have been served upon the tenant and if there is a subtenant in actual occupation of the premises, also upon the subtenant.” (CCP, § 1161 subd. (2).) 

Accordingly, the basic elements of unlawful detainer for nonpayment of rent contained in CCP section 1161 subdivision (2) are “(1) the tenant is in possession of the premises; (2) that possession is without permission; (3) the tenant is in default for nonpayment of rent; (4) the tenant has been properly served with a written notice; and (5) the default continues after the three-day notice period has elapsed.” (Kruger v. Reyes (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th Supp. 10, 16.) 

“Due to the summary nature of such an action, a [notice to pay rent or quit] is valid only if the landlord strictly complies with the provisions of section 1161, subdivision 2.” (Levitz Furniture Co. v. Wingtip Communications, Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1035, 1038.) 

Defendants argue that the unlawful detainer claim fails because the FAC does not plead the facts necessary to establish Plaintiff’s right to recover possession.

First, Defendants argue that the FAC fails to plead facts showing whether the $2,288.00 demanded in the notice to pay rent or quit, comprised of eleven $208 monthly delinquent payments, is for unpaid rent or other non-rent obligations. (Demurrer, pp. 6:22-9:9; RJN, Ex. 1.) Defendants argue that the FAC thus does not establish that the $2,880.00 demanded constitutes a monetary default under the lease agreement which supports a lawful claim for unlawful detainer. 

In the opposition, Plaintiff argues that the notice or pay rent or quit includes all the statutorily required elements. The notice to pay rent or quit describes the amounts as delinquent rent. (RJN, Ex. 1.) Plaintiff argues that Defendants’ contention that the $208 monthly amounts “might” be for “utilities, RUBS charges, parking fees, late fees, passthroughs, trash charges, pest-control charges, or some other category of charge” and thus not actually delinquent rent or a proper basis for an unlawful detainer claim, is a factual dispute not properly resolved on demurrer. (Opp., p. 8:2-8.) 

On the face of the FAC, the $2,880.00 in allegedly delinquent rent is not clearly improper. A demurrer lies only to defects that appear on the face of the pleading or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true. (Aubry, supra, 2 Cal.4th at p. 967.)

If Plaintiff in fact imposed an improper rent increase, or if the $208 monthly amounts constitute some category of charge unrelated to rent, either circumstance could constitute grounds to conclude that the unlawful detainer is not proper. However, nothing on the face of the FAC or in the judicially noticed documents establishes either proposition. These issues present factual disputes that are more appropriately resolved through discovery, and if necessary, at trial or on summary judgment, rather than on demurrer. 

Second, Defendants argue that the FAC is deficient on its face because Plaintiff alleges that the tenancy is not subject to the Tenant Protection Act of 2019 (“TPA”), however fails to identify the specific subpart supporting why tenancy is exempt. (Compl., § 7.) Defendants argue that neither the exemption provided by Civil Code section 1946.2, subdivision (e)(7) or (e)(8) apply to the subject property. (Demurrer, p. 9:18-28.) 

In the opposition, Plaintiff argues that neither CCP section 1161 subdivision (2) nor Civil Code section 1946.2 require a plaintiff to plead a specific TPA exemption. Plaintiff argues that the “Judicial Council’s optional form UD-100, while revised to include a field for the ‘specific subpart’ of Civil Code § 1946.2, does not create a new element of the cause of action.” 

Undoubtedly, when checking the box on the Judicial Council form UD-100 stating that a tenancy is not subject to the TPA, the better and more legally sound practice is also to complete the subpart identifying the applicable exemption. The TPA enumerates nine categories of residential properties and circumstances to which the section does not apply, including transient hotels, nonprofit and care facilities, dormitories, certain owner-occupied residences, properties with certificates of occupancy issued within the last 15 years, single-family homes or condominiums meeting specified ownership criteria, and housing restricted as affordable housing. (Civil Code § 1946.2, subd. (e)(1)-(9).) Based on the facts alleged in the FAC, it is not clear which, if any, of these exemptions would apply to the subject premises. The property is not alleged to be a hotel, care facility, dormitory, or owner-occupied property. Nor does the FAC allege that the rental unit is alienable separately from the title to any other unit in the building, or that the property is subject to a recorded restriction designating it as affordable housing. Finally, the FAC alleges that the premises were constructed in 2003. (FAC, § 3.b.(1).) Accordingly, it does not appear from the allegations that a certificate of occupancy for the premises would have been issued within the last 15 years. 

Accordingly, given the strict pleading requirements governing unlawful detainer actions, and the requirement that a complaint affirmatively allege all facts necessary to support recovery, the Court finds that the FAC is uncertain. Merely checking the box indicating that the TPA does not apply, without identifying the applicable exemption, is insufficient, particularly where, as here, the facts alleged in the FAC do not indicate that any exemption applies. 

The demurrer is SUSTAINED, with leave to amend. 

RULING:
Defendants Rodrigo Tonetto and Solange Girardelle’s Demurrer is SUSTAINED, with 15 days leave to amend.

A demurrer for uncertainty lies where the pleading is uncertain, ambiguous, or unintelligible. (CCP, § 430.10, subd. (f).)

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616 [disapproved on other grounds].) As a result, a special demurrer for uncertainty is not intended to reach failure to incorporate sufficient facts in the pleading, but it rather is directed only at uncertainty existing in the allegations already made. (People v. Taliaferro (1957) 149 Cal.App.2d 822, 825, disapproved on other grounds in Jefferson v. J.E. French Co. (1960) 54 Cal.2d 717, 719-720 [statute of limitations question].)

Where a complaint is sufficient to state a cause of action and to apprise a defendant of issues he is to meet, it is not properly subject to a special demurrer for uncertainty. (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 643 [“A special demurrer [for uncertainty] should be overruled where the allegations of the complaint are sufficiently clear to apprise the defendant of the issues which he is to meet”].) 

Unlawful Detainer
A tenant is guilty of unlawful detainer when “the tenant continues in possession, in person or by subtenant, without the permission of the landlord, […], after default in the payment of rent, pursuant to the lease or agreement under which the property is held, and three days’ notice, excluding Saturdays and Sundays and other judicial holidays, in writing, requiring its payment, stating the amount that is due, the name, telephone number, and address of the person to whom the rent payment shall be made, and, if payment may be made personally, the usual days and hours that person will be available to receive the payment […], or the number of an account in a financial institution into which the rental payment may be made, and the name and street address of the institution […], or if an electronic funds transfer procedure has been previously established, that payment may be made pursuant to that procedure, or possession of the property, shall have been served upon the tenant and if there is a subtenant in actual occupation of the premises, also upon the subtenant.” (CCP, § 1161 subd. (2).) 

Accordingly, the basic elements of unlawful detainer for nonpayment of rent contained in CCP section 1161 subdivision (2) are “(1) the tenant is in possession of the premises; (2) that possession is without permission; (3) the tenant is in default for nonpayment of rent; (4) the tenant has been properly served with a written notice; and (5) the default continues after the three-day notice period has elapsed.” (Kruger v. Reyes (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th Supp. 10, 16.) 

“Due to the summary nature of such an action, a [notice to pay rent or quit] is valid only if the landlord strictly complies with the provisions of section 1161, subdivision 2.” (Levitz Furniture Co. v. Wingtip Communications, Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1035, 1038.) 

Defendant argues that the unlawful detainer claim fails because the FAC does not plead the facts necessary to establish Plaintiff’s right to recover possession.

First, Defendant argues that the FAC fails to plead facts showing whether the $1,969.00 demanded in the notice to pay rent or quit, comprised of eleven $179 monthly delinquent payments, is for unpaid rent or other non-rent obligations. (Demurrer, pp. 6:3-8:13; RJN, Ex. 1.) Defendant argues that the FAC thus does not establish that the $1,969.00 demanded constitutes a monetary default under the lease agreement which supports a lawful claim for unlawful detainer. 

In the opposition, Plaintiff argues that the notice or pay rent or quit includes all the statutorily required elements. The notice to pay rent or quit describes the amounts as delinquent rent. (RJN, Ex. 1.) Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s contention that the $179 monthly amounts “might” be for “utilities, RUBS charges, parking fees, late fees, passthroughs, trash charges, pest-control charges, or some other category of charge” and thus not actually delinquent rent or a proper basis for an unlawful detainer claim, is a factual dispute not properly resolved on demurrer. (Opp., p. 8:2-8.) 

On the face of the FAC, the $1,969.00 in allegedly delinquent rent is not clearly improper. A demurrer lies only to defects that appear on the face of the pleading or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true. (Aubry, supra, 2 Cal.4th at p. 967.)

If Plaintiff in fact imposed an improper rent increase, or if the $179 monthly amounts constitute some category of charge unrelated to rent, either circumstance could constitute grounds to conclude that the unlawful detainer is not proper. However, nothing on the face of the FAC or in the judicially noticed documents establishes either proposition. These issues present factual disputes that are more appropriately resolved through discovery, and if necessary, at trial or on summary judgment, rather than on demurrer. 

Second, Defendant argues that the FAC is deficient on its face because Plaintiff alleges that the tenancy is not subject to the Tenant Protection Act of 2019 (“TPA”), however fails to identify the specific subpart supporting why tenancy is exempt. (Compl., § 7.) Defendants argue that neither the exemption provided by Civil Code section 1946.2, subdivision (e)(7) or (e)(8) apply to the subject property. (Demurrer, pp. 8:14-9:7.) 

In the opposition, Plaintiff argues that neither CCP section 1161 subdivision (2) nor Civil Code section 1946.2 require a plaintiff to plead a specific TPA exemption. Plaintiff argues that the “Judicial Council’s optional form UD-100, while revised to include a field for the ‘specific subpart’ of Civil Code § 1946.2, does not create a new element of the cause of action.” (Opp., p. 12:6-8.) 

Undoubtedly, when checking the box on the Judicial Council form UD-100 stating that a tenancy is not subject to the TPA, the better and more legally sound practice is also to complete the subpart identifying the applicable exemption. The TPA enumerates nine categories of residential properties and circumstances to which the section does not apply, including transient hotels, nonprofit and care facilities, dormitories, certain owner-occupied residences, properties with certificates of occupancy issued within the last 15 years, single-family homes or condominiums meeting specified ownership criteria, and housing restricted as affordable housing. (Civil Code § 1946.2, subd. (e)(1)-(9).) Based on the facts alleged in the FAC, it is not clear which, if any, of these exemptions would apply to the subject premises. The property is not alleged to be a hotel, care facility, dormitory, or owner-occupied property. Nor does the FAC allege that the rental unit is alienable separately from the title to any other unit in the building, or that the property is subject to a recorded restriction designating it as affordable housing. Finally, the FAC alleges that the premises were constructed in 2003. (FAC, § 3.b.(1).) Accordingly, it does not appear from the allegations that a certificate of occupancy for the premises would have been issued within the last 15 years. 

Accordingly, given the strict pleading requirements governing unlawful detainer actions, and the requirement that a complaint affirmatively allege all facts necessary to support recovery, the Court finds that the FAC is uncertain. Merely checking the box indicating that the TPA does not apply, without identifying the applicable exemption, is insufficient, particularly where, as here, the facts alleged in the FAC do not indicate that any exemption applies. 

The demurrer is SUSTAINED, with leave to amend.

Defendant Sallie Allen’s Demurrer is SUSTAINED, with 15 days leave to amend.
