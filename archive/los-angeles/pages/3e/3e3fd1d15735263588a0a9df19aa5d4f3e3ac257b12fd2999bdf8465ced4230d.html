DEPARTMENT P LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 26SMCV01433    Hearing Date: August 5, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Sharon Crystal, Inc., et al. v. Sharon Crystal, Case No. 26SMCV01433

Motions:

(1)   Plaintiff’s Demurrer to Defendants’ Cross-Complaint

(2)   Defendants’ Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Complaint

Hearing Date: August 5, 2026

On March 17, 2026, Plaintiffs Sharon Crystal, Inc. and Shannon Bankosh (“Bankosh”) sued Defendants Sharon Crystal (“Sharon”), Holly Crystal (“Holly”), Darla Smith (“Smith”), and Chad Miller (“Miller”) for twenty claims including conversion, theft, intentional interference with contractual relations, breach of fiduciary duty, defamation, fraud, and breach of lease. Plaintiffs allege that Sharon is Bankosh and Holly’s mother. (Complaint, ¶ 12.) Plaintiffs allege that Sharon and her husband Terry Crystal (“Terry”) started Sharon Crystal, Inc. (“the Company”) to sell clothing and other goods at Renaissance Faires throughout the country in the 1970s. (Id. at ¶ 9.) Plaintiffs allege that Sharon and Terry gave control of the company to Bankosh when they decided to retire in 2022, though Sharon remained an active employee. (Id. at ¶ 19.) Plaintiffs allege that Holly was not involved in Crystal until January 2026, when she, Sharon, Smith, and Miller took over the Company and locked Bankosh out of the Company’s Culver City offices. (Complaint, ¶ 34.)

On April 24, 2026, Sharon and Holly filed a cross-complaint against Bankosh alleging financial elder abuse, fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, cancellation of instruments, and conversion.

On April 27, 2026, Sharon and Holly filed an ex parte application for a temporary restraining order removing Bankosh as Crystal’s Director and enjoining her from interfering with the Company’s operations at the Colorado Renaissance Festival. On April 27, 2026, the Court denied Sharon and Holly’s ex parte application.

On April 27, 2026, Defendants filed a demurrer with a motion to strike portions of Plaintiffs’ complaint. On July 23, 2026, Plaintiffs filed opposition. On July 31, 2026, Defendants filed a reply.

On April 30, 2026, Sharon and Holly filed a motion for trial preference. On June 3, 2026, the Court denied Sharon and Holly’s motion for trial preference. Sharon and Holly appealed. On June 16, 2026, the Court received the Court of Appeals’ tentative order directing the court to either vacate the order denying preference and enter a new order granting preference or show cause why a peremptory writ should not issue.

On June 26, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a demurrer to Defendants’ cross-complaint. On July 23, 2026, Defendants filed opposition. On July 29, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a reply.

On July 1, 2026, the Court vacated the June 3 ruling and granted Sharon and Holly’s Motion for Trial Preference. It also advanced the hearings on these demurrers to August 5, 2026, ahead of the September 28, 2026 trial date.

On July 23, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a motion to bifurcate to try the question of the Company’s ownership first.

Sharon and Holly’s Demurrer to Plaintiffs’ Complaint

Sharon and Holly demur to Plaintiffs’ claims for (1) conversion, (2) misappropriation and theft under Penal Code § 496, (3) Intentional Interference with Contractual Relations, (4) intentional interference with prospective economic relations; (5) breach of fiduciary duty; (6) aiding and abetting breach of fiduciary duty; (7) breach of the duty of loyalty; (8) defamation per se; (9) trespass to chattel; (10) claim and delivery; (11) fraudulent concealment; (12) negligent misrepresentation; (13) breach of lease contract; (14) breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing; (15) civil conspiracy; (16) unjust enrichment; (17) accounting; (18) constructive trust; (19) declaratory relief; and (20) injunctive relief.

Governing Law

In a demurrer proceeding, the defects in the complaint must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

Before filing a demurrer, parties must meet and confer in person or by phone. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41.)

Discussion

a.     Procedural Issues

The parties met and conferred on this demurrer on May 21, 2026, by phone. (Montgomery Decl., ¶ 4.) The meet and confer requirement is satisfied.

b.     Conversion

Sharon and Holly argue that the complaint fails to state a claim for conversion against Defendant Miller because the complaint does not allege that Miller actually took property from Plaintiffs. (Demurrer, p. 3.) In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the complaint alleges that Miller took property, pointing specifically to paragraphs 44 and 45. (Opposition, p. 9.)

A complaint states a claim for conversion where it alleges facts showing that defendant disposed of plaintiff’s property inconsistent with plaintiff’s rights and caused damages. (PCO, Inc. v. Christensen, Miller, Fink, Jacobs, Glaser, Weil & Shapiro, LLP (2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 384, 395, 397.)

“It is not necessary that there be a manual taking of the property; it is only necessary to show an assumption of control or ownership over the property, or that the alleged converter has applied the property to his own use.” …’ ” (Shopoff & Cavallo LLP v. Hyon (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1489, 1507.)

The complaint alleges that Miller was taking company property away from the area where Madison Bankosh was collecting it on behalf of the Company. (Complaint, ¶ 45.) This allegation is sufficient to claim that Miller disposed of the Company’s property inconsistently with the Company’s wishes.

Defendants demurrer to Plaintiffs’ claim for conversion is OVERRULED.

c.      Misappropriation & Theft

Defendants argue that the complaint does not allege that Miller took possession of the allegedly stolen property and fails to allege that Miller took part in the theft or knew about it. (Demurrer, p. 3.) In opposition, Plaintiffs point to paragraphs 44-46 and 64 of the complaint and argue that it sufficiently alleges that Miller stole property and knew it was stolen. (Opposition, p. 10.) In reply, Defendants argue that Plaintiffs’ allegations against Miller are conclusory. (Reply, p. 4.)

Civil theft occurs where (1) property was stolen or obtained in a manner constituting theft; (2) defendant knew the property was so stolen or obtained; (3) defendant received or had possession of the stolen property. (Bell v. Feibush (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 1041, 1049 [upholding civil liability under Penal Code section 496].) Civil liability for theft under Penal Code § 496 requires specific intent to steal. (Garrabrants v. Erhart (2023) 98 Cal.App.5th 486, 506 [finding that a jury instruction excluding specific intent was prejudicial error in a civil theft case].).

The complaint does not allege that Miller specifically intended to steal the Company’s property or receive stolen property.

Because the deficiency arises from a lack of detail, it is reasonably likely that Plaintiffs will be able to amend the complaint to state a claim against Miller.

The demurrer to the civil theft cause of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to Miller.

d.     Intentional Interference with Contractual Relations

Defendants argue that the complaint does not state a claim for intentional interference with contractual relations against Miller because it does not claim that he took any act to disrupt a contract he was a party to. (Demurrer, p. 4.) Plaintiffs argue that Miller sent the board resolution he signed to the Company’s festivals and vendors to induce them to treat Bankosh as removed. (Id. at p. 10.) In reply, Defendants argue that Plaintiffs do not identify the particular contract or economic relationship that Miller allegedly interfered with and do not allege that Miller knew about those relationships. (Reply, p. 4.)

To state a claim for intentional interference with contractual relations, the complaint must allege facts showing (1) Plaintiff had a valid contract with a third party; (2) defendant had knowledge of the contract; (3) defendant committed intentional and unjustified acts designed to interfere with or disrupt the contract; (4) actual interference with or disruption of the relationship; and (5) resulting damages. (Davis v. Nadrich (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1, 10; Shamblin v. Berge (1985) 166 Cal.App.3d 118, 123. See also Sole Energy Co. v. Petrominerals Corp. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 212, 238 [“Proof the interfering conduct was wrongful, independent from the interference itself, is not required to recover for interference with contractual relations.”].)

The complaint states a claim for intentional interference with contractual relations because it alleges that Miller interfered with the Company’s contracts with multiple specific festivals and fairs by forwarding them a fraudulent board resolution purporting to remove Bankosh as head of the Company. (See Complaint, ¶ 44.)

Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiffs’ claim for intentional interference with contractual relations is OVERRULED.

e.      Intentional Interference with Prospective Economic Relations

The parties make the same argument on Plaintiffs’ claim for intentional interference with prospective economic relations.

To state a claim for intentional interference with prospective economic relations, the complaint must state facts showing (1) an economic relationship between plaintiff and third party; (2) probability of future economic benefit to the plaintiff; (3) defendants’ knowledge of the relationship; (4) defendants’ intentional acts designed to disrupt the relationship; (5) defendant engaged in an independently wrongful act in disrupting the relationship beyond just inducing disruption of economic advantage; (6) actual disruption; and (7) economic harm caused by the acts. (Salma v. Capon (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 1275, 1290; Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 330 [“The tort has traditionally protected the expectancies involved in ordinary commercial dealings….”].)

For the reasons described in section (d), the complaint states a claim for intentional interference with prospective economic relations.

Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiffs’ claim for intentional interference with prospective economic relations is OVERRULED.

f.       Breach of Fiduciary Duty and Breach of Duty of Loyalty

Defendants argue that Sharon and Darla Smith did not owe Plaintiffs a fiduciary duty after they resign or are terminated from the company, so Plaintiff cannot state a claim for breach of fiduciary duty against them. (Demurrer, p. 5.) In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that Sharon remained an employee during the time the alleged incident occurred, though she was no longer a director, and that she owed the company duties as an employee. Plaintiffs argue that though Darla Smith was an independent contractor, she owed Plaintiffs fiduciary duties because she was in a position of authority at the company when the events occurred. (Opposition, p. 5-7.) In reply, Defendants argue that these labels are conclusory and identify no facts establishing the scope of Sharon or Smith’s authority. (Reply, p. 3.)

A breach of fiduciary duty occurs when there is (1) a fiduciary duty, (2) breach of the duty, and (3) damage caused by the breach. (Charnay v. Cobert¿(2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 170, 182; Stanley v. Richmond (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1070, 1086.)

“In determining the existence of an employee’s fiduciary duty arising by operation of law, what matters is not one’s title, but rather the levels of trust, confidence, and discretion reposed by the employer.” (Guild Mortgage Co., LLC v. CrossCountry Mortgage LLC (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 885, 895 [finding that an employee with managerial authority entrusted with confidential information owed the corporation a fiduciary duty].)

Plaintiffs allege that Sharon remained the face of the company and a trusted agent of the company, was paid as an employee, and “often exercised a dominant role.” (Complaint, ¶¶ 21-22.) Plaintiffs allege that Smith was hired as an independent contractor to prepare for a Shop Manager’s retirement and had access to confidential financial and operational information. (Id. at ¶ 25, 92.) The complaint alleges a sufficient level of trust and confidence imposed in both Sharon and Smith to allege that they owed the company fiduciary duties. Both Sharon and Smith are clearly alleged to have had substantial authority and access to confidential and operational information while they were employed at the company at the time the alleged events occurred.

The complaint states a claim for breach of fiduciary duty against Sharon and Darla Smith.

Defendants’ demurrers to Plaintiffs’ claims for breach of fiduciary duty and breach of the duty of loyalty are OVERRULED.

g.     Aiding and Abetting Breach of Fiduciary Duty

Defendants argue that the complaint does not sufficiently allege that the other defendants aided and abetted Sharon in breaching her fiduciary duties. (Demurrer, pp. 5-6.) Plaintiffs argue that the aiding and abetting claim is sufficiently plead. (Opposition, p. 7.)

A complaint states a claim for aiding and abetting where it states facts showing that (1) defendant aids and abets commission of an intentional tort; (3) knowing the other’s conduct constitutes breach of duty; or (1) giving substantial assistance or encouragement to the other to so act or in accomplishing a tortious result; and the person’s own conduct, separately considered, constitutes breach of duty to the third person. (Das v. Bank of America, N.A. (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 727, 744; Schulz v. Neovi Data Corp. (2007)152 Cal.App.4th 86, 95 [to plead aiding and abetting, ultimate facts are required, not detailed facts].)

The complaint alleges that Darla Smith aided and abetted Sharon’s breach. (Complaint, ¶¶ 33-34.) The allegations against Miller do not show that he knew that Sharon’s conduct was a breach of her duties or that he breached any duties he owed the corporation.

It is reasonably likely that Plaintiffs will be able to allege sufficient detail to state a claim against Miller if given leave to amend, so leave to amend is required.

Defendants’ demurrer is OVERRULED as to Darla Smith, and SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to Miller.

h.     Defamation Per Se

Defendants argue that the complaint does not state a claim for defamation against Miller because it does not allege that he published any false statements about Plaintiffs. (Demurrer, p. 6.) In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the board resolution Miller signed constituted a publication sufficient to state a claim for defamation per se. (Opposition, p. 10.) In reply, Defendants argue that Plaintiffs do not claim that Miller authorized a specific publication. (Reply, p. 5.)

A complaint states a claim for defamation per se where it alleges facts showing (1) defendant intentionally published (2) a statement of fact (3) that is false, (4) defamatory, (5) unprivileged; and (6) that causes special damages. ((E.g., Taus v. Loftus (2007) 40 Cal.4th 683, 720; Smith v. Maldonado (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 637, 645 [“Reprinting or recirculating a libelous writing has the same effect as an original publication.”]. See also Okun v. Superior Court (1981) 29 Cal.3d 442, 458 [exact words of the defamatory statement need not be alleged, but only the substance]; Palm Springs Tennis Club v. Rangel (1999) 73 Cal.App.4th 1, 7 [pleading libel or defamation per quod requires alleging that people had special knowledge of facts from which they could discern that statements were defamatory]; Barnes-Hind, Inc. v. Superior Court (1986) 181 Cal.App.3d 377, 384 [special damages alleged as “decline of business, loss of goodwill, and injury to business reputation” may be sufficient to withstand a demurrer].)

The complaint describes specific, allegedly false, statements Miller made in emails he signed and sent to the Arizona Renaissance Festival and festivals in Georgia and Scarborough. (Complaint, ¶ 100, [“Shannon Bankosh, . . ., has colluded with Sharon’s attorney in an attempt to take control of the business from Sharon;].) Plaintiffs allege that Miller knew these statements were false, and made these representations with the intent to steal the company. (Complaint, ¶ 109.) Plaintiffs allege special damages: appropriating company renevnue, loss of business space at certain venues, etc. (Complaint, ¶ 75.)

The complaint states a claim for defamation per se.

Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiffs’ claim for defamation per se is OVERRULED.

i.       Trespass to Chattel

Defendants argue that the complaint does not claim that Miller harmed the company’s property in some way that decreased the value of the chattel or deprived the company of the chattel’s use for a substantial time. (Demurrer, p. 6.) Plaintiffs argue that the complaint does claim that Miller harmed the company by interfering with its use of its property. (Opposition, p. 9.)

A complaint states a claim for trespass to chattel where it alleges facts showing (1) intentional interference, (2) with the possession of personal property; (3) injury. (Jamgotchian v. Slender (2009) 170 Cal.App.4th 1384, 1400-1401; Intel Corp. v. Hamidi (2003) 30 Cal.4th 1342, 1350-1351.) 

Plaintiffs allege that Defendants, including Miller, intentionally prevented them from accessing their inventory and business spaces beginning in February 2026, and have not allowed them to access that inventory. (Complaint, ¶ 43, 111.)  Plaintiffs allege that they were harmed as a result of the trespass. The complaint states a claim for trespass to chattel.

Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiffs’ claim for trespass to chattel is OVERRULED.

j.       Claim and Delivery

Defendants argue that claim and delivery is only a remedy, so Plaintiffs’ claim for claim and delivery must be dismissed. (Demurrer, pp. 6-7.) In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the complaint pleads the substance of a claim to recover personal property. (Opposition, p. 12.) In reply, Defendants argue that Plaintiffs concede that this claim is only a remedy and not a separate cause of action. (Reply, p. 2.)

“A secured party may bring an action in replevin or proceed under the statutory success to replevin, an action of claim and delivery. [citation] ‘Claim and delivery is a remedy by which a party with a superior right to a specific item of personal property (created, most commonly, by a contractual lien) may recover possession of that specific property before judgment.’” (Simms v. NPCK Enterprises, Inc. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 233, 241, citing Waffer Interntational Corp. v. Khorsandi (1999) 69 Cal.App.4th 1261, 1271.) The Court goes on the describe the specific procedure available under California’s claim and delivery law, which authorizes a pre-judgment writ of possession in certain situations. (Id. at 242.)

A complaint states a claim for claim and delivery/replevin where it alleges facts showing (1) wrongful exercise of dominion by defendant; (2) over the property as to which plaintiff is the owner or entitled to possession; (3) demand for return of property; (4) description of property; and (5) request for recovery of property or damages. (Law v. Heiniger (1955) 132 Cal.App.2d Supp. 898, 899 [“‘action to recover possession of personal property will not lie unless at the time the action is commenced defendant has the possession of the property or the power to deliver it in satisfaction of a judgment for its possession’”….];  Hillyer v. Eggers (1917) 32 Cal.App. 764, 766 [money is not the subject of an action for claim and delivery unless it is marked or designated to make it capable of identification].) 

The complaint alleges that Defendants are wrongfully retaining the company’s property at the McCune property in Los Angeles and at Sharon’s property in Texas and requests the property’s return via writ of possession. (Complaint, ¶¶116-117, prayer for relief).

The complaint states a claim for claim and delivery.

Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiffs’ claim for claim and delivery is OVERRULED.

k.      Fraudulent Concealment

Defendants argue that Plaintiffs’ fraud claim fails because the complaint does not sufficiently allege a duty that Sharon or the Trust had toward plaintiff Shannon Bankosh and therefore cannot claim that Sharon fraudulently failed to disclose that the company was party to a commercial guaranty for the McCune Property. (Demurrer, p. 7.) In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the complaint alleges a duty under LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, because it alleges that Sharon had exclusive knowledge that the company was bound by a commercial guaranty on a thirty-year mortgage and concealed the fact from Bankosh. (Opposition, p. 8.) In reply, Defendants argue that Plaintiffs do not identify facts creating a disclosure duty connected to a particular relationship, transaction, or partial representation. (Reply, p. 3.)

“The well-known elements of a cause of action for fraud or concealment are: (1) a misrepresentation, which includes a concealment or nondisclosure; (2) knowledge of the falsity of the misrepresentation, i.e., scienter; (3) intent to induce reliance on the misrepresentation; (4) justifiable reliance; and (5) resulting damages.” (Cadlo v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (2004) 125 Cal.App.4th 513, 519-520.)   

Plaintiffs alleging fraud must describe, with particularity, the specific statements defendants made that constituted fraud, or facts defendants omitted that constitute alleged fraud. Pleadings must allege facts as to “‘how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.’” (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 73. Accord Wald v. Truspeed Motorcars, LLC (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 378, 393; Morgan v. AT & T Wireless Services, Inc. (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1235, 1262. Cf. also Goldrich v. Natural Y Surgical Specialties, Inc. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th772, 783 [“conclusory allegations offer no facts at all and it is impossible to determine what was said or by whom or in what manner.”].)

LiMandri holds that a failure to disclose material facts is not actionable fraud unless there is some relationship between the parties that gives rise to a duty to disclose those facts, namely a relationship arising from a transaction. (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336-337.) It affirms that even the duty arising from a failure to disclose confidential information that Plaintiffs invoke arises from a contractual agreement:

Clearly, there was no fiduciary relationship between Judkins as counsel for Security and LiMandri as counsel for the Deddehs.3 Each of the other three circumstances in which nondisclosure may be actionable presupposes the existence of some other relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise. As set forth in BAJI No. 12.36 (8th ed.1994), “where material facts are known to one party and not to the other, failure to disclose them is not actionable fraud unless there is some relationship between the parties which gives rise to a duty to disclose such known facts.” (Italics added.)

As a matter of common sense, such a relationship can only come into being as a result of some sort of transaction between the parties. [citation] Thus, a duty to disclose may arise from the relationship between seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement. (Civ.Code,4§ 1572, subd. 3.)

(Id. at 336-337.)

Plaintiffs’ claim for fraudulent concealment must be brought by Bankosh as an individual, not Sharon Crystal, Inc. because Sharon Crystal, Inc. is a party to the guaranty and is therefore aware of it.

As applied to Plaintiffs’ claim against Sharon, the complaint alleges that Sharon’s duty arose out of the transfer of stock in the company to Shannon Bankosh. (Complaint, ¶ 120.) However, this transfer was not a contract, it was a gift. (Complaint, ¶19 [“The transfer of the Company to Bankosh constituted a deliberate donative act by her parents during their lifetimes,”].) Plaintiffs cite no case law showing that a donative act gives rise to a duty.

As applied to the claim against Holly as Trustee of the Crystal Family Trust, the complaint alleges no contract between Bankosh and the Trust. The complaint alleges that in 2015, prior to the transfer of the company, Sharon and Terry Crystal transferred ownership of the McCune Property to the Crystal Family Trust. The complaint does not allege that the Crystal Family Trust held any interest in the company, so there is no contract or transaction of any kind that could create an actionable duty between Holly, as trustee of the Crystal Family Trust, and Sharon for an alleged failure to disclose the guaranty agreement.

Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiffs’ claim for fraudulent concealment is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

l.       Negligent Misrepresentation

Negligent misrepresentation occurs when a defendant (1) makes an untrue statement; (2) that defendant believes to be true, (3) without reasonable ground for the belief, (4) with intent to induce Plaintiff’s reliance on the representation; (5) plaintiff did rely, and (6) plaintiff was damaged. (Melican v. Regents of Univ. of Cal. (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 168, 182.) It is not settled if claims for negligent misrepresentation require the same level of specificity at the pleading stage as fraud claims, Shulman’s misrepresentation allegations are almost entirely conclusory. (See dissent, B.L.M. v. Sabo & Deitsch (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 823, 845.)

The negligent misrepresentation claim fails for the same reason the fraudulent concealment claim fails.

Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiffs’ claim for negligent misrepresentation is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

m.    Breach of Lease

Defendants argue that Plaintiffs fail to allege the terms of the lease or attach the lease to the complaint. (Demurrer, p. 8.) In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the complaint alleges the essential terms of the lease agreement. (Opposition, p. 4.) In reply, Defendants argue that the complaint does not adequately plead the terms of the lease. (Reply, p. 4.)

A complaint properly pleads breach of contract when it states facts demonstrating (1) the existence of a contract; (2) plaintiffs’ performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendants’ breach (or anticipatory breach); and (4) resulting damage. (Wall Street Network, Ltd. v. N. Y. Times Co. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1171, 1178.) A written contract may be pleaded by its terms, or by pleading the legal effect of the terms. (Maxwell v. Dolezal (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 93, 98.)

The complaint alleges the existence of a written lease agreement and includes the address of the property, the parties, and the term of the lease. Plaintiffs alleged breach is that they locked the leasee out of the property. If the alleged breach were a type of breach that required consideration of the terms of the lease, the facts plead would be insufficient because it would not put Defendants on notice of the provisions of the lease that they breached. However, by their very nature, a lease gives the Plaintiff the right to occupy the premises. The allegations are therefore sufficient.  

Defendants’ demurer to Plaintiffs’ claim for breach of lease is OVERRULED.

n.     Breach of the Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

The parties make the same arguments on their claims for breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing as they make on the breach of lease claims.

Breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing occurs where there is a contractual relationship, an implied duty, breach of that duty, and causation of damages. ((E.g., Smith v. San Francisco (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 38, 49. See also Careau & Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1371, 1395 [“allegations which assert such a claim must show that the conduct of the defendant…demonstrates a failure or refusal to discharge contractual responsibilities, prompted … by a conscious and deliberate act, which unfairly frustrates the agreed common purposes and disappoints the reasonable expectations of the other party thereby depriving that party of the benefits of the agreement. Just what conduct will meet this criteria must be determined on a case by case basis….”].)

The complaint alleges a bad act. (See Complaint, ¶ 145.)

Defendants’ demurrer to plaintiffs’ claim for breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing is OVERRULED.

o.     Civil Conspiracy

Defendants contend that civil conspiracy is not an independent cause of action.

A complaint pleads civil conspiracy where it alleges facts showing (1) defendants’ agreement to the objective and course of action to injure; (2) wrongful act pursuant to such agreement; (3) and resulting damage. (Berg & Berg Ent., LLC v. Sherwood Partners, Inc. (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 802, 823 [noting the elements, and contrasting with aiding and abetting]; State of Cal. ex rel. Metz v. CCC Information Services, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 402, 419 [conclusory allegations are insufficient to allege the elements of conspiracy]. See also Quelimane Co., Inc. v. Stewart Title Guar. Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 47 [general allegations are permitted as to the pleading of conspiracy]; Klistoff v. Superior Court (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 469, 479 [“The existence of a civil conspiracy makes each participant in the wrongful act responsible as a joint tortfeasor for all damages resulting from the wrong, whether or not a participant was a direct actor and regardless of the degree of his activity” and a coconspirator cannot be liable unless he or she owes a duty to the plaintiff as recognized by law].)

Civil conspiracy is a theory of liability that plaintiffs may bring in the alternative. The complaint states a claim for civil conspiracy.

Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiffs’ claim for civil conspiracy is OVERRULED.

p.     Unjust Enrichment

Unjust enrichment occurs when a defendant (1) receives a benefit, (2) retains the benefit wrongfully or unjustly, (3) at the expense of another.  (Peterson v. Cellco Partnership (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1583, 1593.) Unjust enrichment is a theory of recovery applicable to quasi-contract claims.

Plaintiffs have no quasi contract claims, so the demurrer to their claim for unjust enrichment is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

q.     Accounting

A complaint states a claim for an accounting if it alleges facts showing (1) the existence of a relationship requiring an accounting, (2) some unliquidated and unascertained balance is owed. (St. James Church of Christ Holiness v. Superior Court (1955) 135 Cal.App.2d 352, 359.)

The complaint does not allege an unascertained balance owed to Plaintiffs by Defendants, and therefore does not properly seek an accounting. The information Plaintiffs seek, Sharon Crystal’s current financials, is likely discoverable in this action and would go to Plaintiffs’ damages. Additionally, as the sole shareholder, Bankosh has the right to demand inspection of the company’s books.

Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiffs’ claim for accounting is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

r.      Constructive Trust

“[A] constructive trust may only be imposed where the following three conditions are satisfied: (1) the existence of a res (property or some interest in property); (2) the right of a complaining party to that res; and (3) some wrongful acquisition or detention of the res by another party who is not entitled to it.” (Burlesci v. Petersen (1998), 68 Cal. App. 4th 1062, 1069.) A Constructive trust is an equitable remedy available to a plaintiff seeking recovery of property in any number of situations. (Olson v. Toy (1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 818, 823.) To state a claim for constructive trust, a plaintiff must state a claim for an underlying cause of action against the person possessing the property (the constructive trustee) that entitles plaintiff to the property at issue. (Michaelian v. State Comp. Ins. Fund (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 1093, 1114; see also Burlesci v. Petersen (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 1062, 1069 [holding that a plaintiff may seek a constructive trust against the involuntary trustee of property, the person or entity who gained possession of property by fraud or wrongful act].)

The complaint states a claim for constructive trust because it states a claim for conversion, breach of lease, etc.

Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiffs’ claim for Constructive Trust is OVERRULED.

s.      Declaratory Relief and Injunctive Relief

Aimco Venezia, LLC (2d Dist. 2007) 154 Cal.App.4th 154, 162 ["An injunction is a remedy, not a cause of action.”]; County of Los Angeles v. Construction Laborers Trust Funds for Southern California Admin. Co. (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 410, 414, 416 [An equitable lien on plaintiff’s property not in their possession is available on a showing that the plaintiff has a right to the property not in their possession based on a contract showing intent to charge property with a debt, estoppel, unjust enrichment, or other equitable considerations. “An equitable lien is a restitutionary remedy given by a court of equity.”]; Southern Counties Gas Co. v. Ventura Pipeline Const. Co. (1971) Cal.App.3d 372 [declaratory relief is available as a remedy if the complaint states a claim for prospective harm].)

Demurrer to Defendants’ Cross-Complaint with Motion to Strike

Plaintiffs demur to all five of Defendants’ claims in their cross-complaint. Plaintiffs argue that the Defendants lack standing to assert claims on behalf of Terry Crystal and Sharon Crystal, Inc., so the claims that claim injuries on behalf of those non-defendants should be dismissed. (Demurrer, p. 7.) Plaintiffs also argue that the cross-complaint fails because its claims are time-barred because the complaint establishes that Sharon Crystal had inquiry notice that the 2022 transfer of Sharon Crystal, Inc. shares was to Shannon Bankosh as an individual, not as a trustee. (Id. at pp. 8-12.) Plaintiffs argue that amendment would be futile because the exhibits to the complaint conclusively establish inquiry notice. (Id. at p. 12.) Plaintiffs also argue that each of Defendants’ causes of action fail to state a claim because they do not identify property interests, and are insufficiently specific.

In opposition, Defendants argue that the cross-complaint is not time-barred because it alleges that Shannon Bankosh forged the signature on the stock assignment agreement in August, 2022, less than four years before the cross-complaint was filed. (Opposition, p. 5.) Defendants also argue that the cross-complaint does not allege derivative claims because they relate to Sharon’s property, not the company’s property. (Id. at p. 6.) Defendants argue that the only claim that could be a derivative claim is the third claim for breach of fiduciary duty, but argues that the cross-complaint brings that claim directly as a shareholder of Sharon Crystal, Inc. (Ibid.) Defendants argue that the cross-complaint states a claim for each of its five pleaded causes of action, that they do not seek relief on behalf of Terry Crystal, and that the demurrer for uncertainty should be overruled. (Id. at pp. 7-9.)

In reply, Plaintiffs argue that Defendant misstates the accrual standard by conflating the wrongful act with the resulting harm. Plaintiffs argue that Defendants had inquiry notice of wrongdoing in January 2022. Plaintiffs argue that Defendants’ standing argument that they were divested of ownership concedes that they had inquiry notice on January 24, 2022. (Reply, p. 4.)  Plaintiffs argue that later conduct that Defendants claim forms the basis of their claims flows from the same allegedly fraudulent transfer, and do not constitute separate, actionable wrongs. (Id. at p. 6.)

Governing Law

In a demurrer proceeding, the defects in the complaint must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

Before filing a demurrer, parties must meet and confer in person or by phone. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41.)

Discussion

a.     Meet and Confer

The parties met and conferred on this motion on June 19, 2026. (Rognlien Decl., ¶ 4.) The meet and confer requirement is satisfied.

b.     Statute of Limitations

The statute of limitations on an elder abuse claim is four years. (Welf. & Inst. Code, § 15657.7.) The statute of limitations for fraud is three years. (Code Civ. Proc. § 338(d).) The statute of limitations for a corporate director’s breach of fiduciary duty is four years. (Code Civ. Proc. § 343.) The statute of limitations for a cancellation of instrument claim based on fraud is three years. (Robertson v. Superior Court (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 1319, 1326.) The statute of limitations for conversion is three years. (Code Civ. Proc., § 338(c)(1); Hillhouse v. Wolf (1958) 166 Cal.App.2d Supp. 833, 835.)

Defendants allege that Shannon Bankosh gave Sharon and Terry Crystal a certificate of transfer of ownership of the company to sign on January 24, 2022, representing that it would transfer ownership to her as trustee, not as an individual, and fraudulently obtained their signature on that certificate. (Cross-Complaint, ¶ 37.) The Cross-Complaint then alleges that Bankosh presented her parents with a Declaration and Save Harmless Agreement Re: Lost Certificate on February 1, 2022 for signature because they could not find Stock Certificate 2, which proved the Crystal Family Trust’s ownership of Sharon Crystal, Inc. (Id. at ¶ 33.) The Cross-Complaint then alleges that Bankosh forged Sharon’s signature on an August 24, 2022 stock transfer. (Id. at ¶ 39.) Exhibit 1, the transfer certificate, is dated January 24, 2022, and states that it transfers 5,341.59 shares of Sharon Crystal, Inc. to “Shannon C. Bankosh.” (Cross-Complaint, Exhibit 1.) Exhibit 3 is Sharon and Terry’s declaration re: lost certificate dated February 1, 2022. (Cross-Complaint, Exhibit 3) Exhibit 4 is a stock assignment to Shannon C. Bankosh dated August 27, 2022.

The Cross-Complaint was filed on April 24, 2026.

Here, the key question is when the statute of limitations began to run. Defendants argue that the statute of limitations did not begin to run until August 27, 2022, the date Bankosh is alleged to have forged signatures on the stock assignment. (Opposition, p. 4.)  Plaintiffs argue that the statute began to run on January 24, 2022, the date Bankosh allegedly misrepresented to Sharon and Terry Crystal that the transfer certificate they were signing transferred ownership of the company to Bankosh as a trustee rather than in her individual capacity – even though the plain language of the certificate said the opposite.

The Court agrees with Bankosh. In an action for fraud, the wrongdoing is the misrepresentation or concealment of a material fact. (Jolly v. Eli Lily & Co. (1988) 44 Cal.3d 1103, 1111 (discussing Miller v. Bechtel Corp. (1983) 33 Cal.3d 868.)) In Jolly v. Eli Lilly & Co., a DES case, the Supreme Court of California found that “the limitations period begins once the plaintiff ‘has notice or information of circumstances to put a reasonable person on inquiry . . . So long as a suspicion exists, it is clear that the plaintiff must go find the facts; she cannot wait for the facts to find her.” (Jolly v. Eli Lilly & Co., supra, 44 Cal.3d, 1110-1111.) The Court found that the statute of limitations on the plaintiff’s claim ran one year after Plaintiff expressed interest in obtaining more information about DES because she felt someone had done something wrong to her concerning DES. (Id. at p. 1112.)

Here, Sharon and Terry signed the stock transfer certificate on January 24, 2022, putting them on inquiry notice that the transfer would be to Bankosh as an individual. The allegedly forged signature in August of 2022 is not an entirely new wrongdoing but rather an additional step in the same allegedly fraudulent scheme to steal the company from Sharon and Terry. As such, the Court concludes that Defendants were on inquiry notice of the fraud when they reviewed and signed the January 24, 2022 stock transfer transferring the company to Shannon Bankosh as an individual and when they had subsequent communications with an attorney, Mr. Diamond, regarding the transfers. The stock certificate clearly identifies the recipient as Bankosh as an individual and Defendants have never contended that Sharon and Terry’s signature on the January 24, 2022 transfer certificate is forged.

The Cross-Complaint’s claim for fraud, cancellation of instruments and conversion are all based entirely on the allegedly fraudulent transfer. (Cross-Complaint, ¶¶ 100, 121-122.) In addition, the Cross-complaint’s financial elder abuse claim is based on the allegation that Shannon Bankosh “intentionally deceived and manipulated Sharon into signing Stock in the Corporation to Cross-Defendant for Bankosh’s personal benefit, forged Sharon’s signature on the Stock Assignment, and took money from the Corporation.” (Cross-Complaint, ¶ 91.) The allegation that Shannon took money from the corporation cannot form the basis of Sharon’s claim for elder abuse because the corporation is not an elder and because Sharon had not owned any part of the corporation in her personal capacity since 2004. Therefore, the claim is also based on the allegedly fraudulent stock transfer. 

Accordingly, Defendants’ first, second, fourth, and fifth cross-claims are barred by the statute of limitations.

“In order to rely on the discovery rule for delayed accrual of a cause of action ‘a plaintiff whose complaint shows on its face that his claim would be barred without the benefit of the discovery rule must specifically plead facts to show (1) the time and manner of discovery and (2) the inability to have made earlier discovery despite reasonable diligence.’ [citation] In assessing the sufficiency of the allegations of delayed discovery the court places the burden on the plaintiff to ‘show diligence’; ‘conclusory allegations will not withstand demurrer.’” (Fox v. Ethicon Endo-Surgery, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 797, 808, citing McKelvey v. Boeing North American, Inc. (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 151, 160.)

Paragraph 37 of the cross-complaint alleges that: “On information and belief, Bankosh presented that Declaration to Sharon and Terry for their signature as a further action to conceal from Sharon, Terry, and James Diamond that Bankosh had already acquired her parent’s signature on a Stock Certificate number three that purported to transfer ownership of the Corporation to Bankosh as an individual rather than trustee.” (Cross-Complaint, ¶ 37.)

This allegation is insufficient to plead the requirements of the discovery rule. The complaint does not allege a specific time that Defendants discovered Shannon Bankosh’s alleged fraud, only that Sharon became aware of fraudulent bank transfers sometime in 2025. The Complaint also does not allege any other facts showing that Defendants could not have discovered Shannon’s fraud earlier. In fact, the exhibits to the complaints, specifically Sharon and Terry’s signatures on the January 24, 2022 stock transfer, show that Sharon and Terry reviewed the proposed transfer and signed it.

 

The Cross-complaint’s financial elder abuse claim is based on the allegation that Shannon Bankosh “intentionally deceived and manipulated Sharon into signing Stock in the Corporation to Cross-Defendant for Bankosh’s personal benefit, forged Sharon’s signature on the Stock Assignment, and took money from the Corporation.” (Cross-Complaint, ¶ 91.) The allegation that Shannon took money from the corporation cannot form the basis of Sharon’s claim for elder abuse because the corporation is not an elder and because Sharon had not owned any part of the corporation in her personal capacity since 2004. Therefore, the claim is based on the allegedly fraudulent stock transfer.

                           i.          Leave to amend

The legal standard for inquiry notice of fraud is if the “facts [are] sufficient to make a reasonably prudent person suspicious of fraud, thus putting him on inquiry.” (Hobart v. Hobart Estate Co. (1945) 26 Cal.2d 415, 437.) Exhibit 1 to the Cross-Complaint, stating explicitly that the stocks are being transferred to Shannon Bankosh alone, not as a trustee of any trust, would raise suspicion in a reasonably prudent person who believed the stocks were being transferred to a trust. The Court does not believe that Defendants will be able to amend the cross-complaint to state a claim under the discovery rule.

c.      Defendants’ claim for breach of fiduciary duty

Defendants argue that Sharon has standing to sue for breach of fiduciary duty because Sharon rightfully has an interest in Sharon Crystal, Inc.

A breach of fiduciary duty occurs when there is (1) a fiduciary duty, (2) breach of the duty, and (3) damage caused by the breach. (Charnay v. Cobert¿(2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 170, 182; Stanley v. Richmond (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1070, 1086.) A real party in interest with the right to sue is the person who owns or holds title to the claim or property involved, as opposed to others who may be interested or benefitted by the litigation. (Gantman v. United Pac. Ins. Co. (1991) 232 Cal.App.3d 1560, 1566.) Where a corporation is the real party in interest, a corporate shareholder may also have standing to bring a claim on their own behalf to enforce shareholder voting rights, to enjoin an ultra vires act, or for damages arising out of insider trading, but cannot sue for corporate mismanagement. (Schuster v. Gardner (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 305, 312.) “An action is derivative if ‘the gravamen of the complaint is injury to the corporation, or to the whole body of its stock or property without any severance of distribution among individual holders, or if it seeks to recover assets for the corporation or prevent the dissipation of its assets.’” (Id. at p. 313.)

Here, Defendants primarily allege corporate misconduct and damage to the Company. Defendants allege that Shannon Bankosh replaced the Company’s credit card terminals with her Eden Naturals terminals, facilitated large transfers to Eden Naturals, and made false claims on the Company’s taxes. (Cross-Complaint, ¶¶ 51, 54, 59.) These are not allegations regarding shareholder voting rights, ultra vires acts, or insider trading—they are allegations of corporate mismanagement. The Cross-Complaint alleges that as a result of Shannon Bankosh’s actions, the Company lost revenue and fell behind on its tax obligations. (Id. at ¶ 55, 60-61.) These injuries are to the company, not to Sharon. Even if the cross-complaint alleged a violation that was directly actionable by a shareholder, Defendants do not allege that Sharon is a shareholder. Defendants do not have standing to claim breach of fiduciary duty based on Shannon Bankosh’s breach of fiduciary duty because Defendants are not the injured corporation, nor are they shareholders in that corporation.

                           i.          Leave to amend

Since Defendants lack standing to bring a claim on behalf of the corporation, leave to amend would be futile.

Conclusion

Plaintiffs’ demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend. Since the demurrer is sustained without leave to amend, the motion to strike is moot.

Defendants’ cross-complaint is dismissed.

(Id. at p. 16.) (ID. at p. 17.) (Id. at p. 18.) IJW argues that the requested relief is narrowly tailored to the trade secrets and breach of contract issues to preserve the status quo and prevent further harm. (Id. at p. 20.)

In opposition, Morduchowitz argues that IJW’s application should be denied because there is no emergency, and because the issues it seeks relief on are problems of its own creation. Second, Morduchowitz argues that IJW does not identify any trade secrets that Morduchowitz allegedly misappropriated because IJW’s general identification of sales strategies, data, strategic plans, and research and development are not specific enough to sustain a claim under the DTSA. (Id. at p. 7.) Morduchowitz also argues that IJW does not own some of the affiliate materials it asks the Court to enjoin and that the record shows that Morduchowitz did not access the material by improper means, nor do they produce evidence of misappropriation beyond mere possession. (Id. at p. 9.) Next, Morduchowitz argues that IJW’s breach of contract claims do not seek the damages sought on this motion, and that IJW does not seek enforcement of the inspection protocol’s terms. (Ibid.) Morduchowitz argues that IJW’s alleged injuries are compensable in damages, and that IJW’s forensics do not show a threat. Finally, Morduchowitz argues that IJW’s proposed order would place Morduchowitz under contempt exposure attached to undefined confidential information and submit his personal and family materials to forensic examination. (Id. at p. 12.) Morduchowitz argues that the proposed order’s terms independently fail as vague and operate as a judicially imposed non-compete agreement, and that the order sought is a discovery motion dressed up as a preliminary injunction. (Id. at p. 13.) Finally Morduchowitz argues that Defendants propose a mutual preservation order that is more fair. (Id. at p. 15.)

Evidentiary Objections

Morduchowitz makes 21 objections to the Frisch Declaration. All of the objections are overruled.

IJW makes 18 objections to the Morduchowitz Declaration. The Court overrules objections 1-5, 7 (except the first sentence), and objections 8-17. The Court sustains objection 6 (offer to compromise), objection 7 as to the first sentence of paragraph 19 only (offer to compromise); and Objection 18 (offer to compromise).

Legal Standard- Preliminary Injunction

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending a decision on the merits.¿ (Major v. Miraverde Homeowners Ass’n. (1992) 7 Cal. App. 4th 618, 623.)¿ In deciding whether or not to grant a preliminary injunction, the court looks to two factors, “(1) the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits, and (2) the relative balance of harms that is likely to result from the granting or denial of interim injunctive relief.”¿ (White v. Davis (2003) 30 Cal.4th 528, 553-54.) The factors are interrelated, with a greater showing on one permitting a lesser showing on the other.¿ (Dodge, Warren & Peters Ins. Services, Inc. v. Riley (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 1414, 1420.)¿ However, the party seeking an injunction must demonstrate at least a reasonable probability of success on the merits.¿ (IT Corp. v. County of Imperial (1983) 35 Cal.3d 63, 73-74.)¿ The party seeking the injunction bears the burden of demonstrating both a likelihood of success on the merits and the occurrence of irreparable harm.¿ (Savage v. Trammell Crow Co. (1990) 223 Cal.App.3d 1562, 1571.) Irreparable harm may exist if the plaintiff can show an inadequate remedy at law.¿ (Code Civ. Pro. § 526 subd. (a).) 

Analysis

IJW seeks an order requiring Defendants not to disclose trade secrets; enjoining Defendants from representing that they are affiliated with IJW; requiring that Defendants preserve trade secrets or confidential information; and “requiring that Defendants submit for forensic inspection all personal electronic devices and storage media, including but not limited to any laptops, mobile phones, tablets, external hard drives, USB drives, and cloud storage accounts, that were used to access, store, or transfer IJW’s trade secrets or confidential information, including any device(s) that accessed Morduchowitz’s personal USB drive after September 8, 2025.” (Proposed Order, p.2.) The majority of IJW’s brief is dedicated to these last two requests. The fourth request, in particular, seems more like the relief a Court might award in a discovery dispute or on a motion to compel response to request for production than the relief the Court would grant on a motion for a preliminary injunction because the request does not correlate directly to the relief sought in IJW’s compliant.

Likelihood of success on the merits

a.      Trade Secret Misappropriation under 15 U.S.C. 1836

To prevail on a claim for misappropriation of trade secrets under the DTSA, a plaintiff must prove “(1) that the plaintiff possessed a trade secret, (2) that the defendant misappropriated the trade secret; and (3) that the misappropriation caused or threatened damage to the plaintiff.” (InteliClear, LLC v. ETC Glob. Holdings, Inc. (9th Cir. 2020) 978 F.3d 653, 657-658.) To prove ownership of a trade secret, the plaintiffs must “describe the subject matter of the trade secret with sufficient particularity to separate it from matters of general knowledge in the trade, clearly refer to tangible trade secret material instead of a system that potentially qualifies for protection, must not rely on ‘catchall’ phrases or identify categories of trade secrets; and must not merely cite and incorporate by reference hundreds of documents containing purported trade secrets. (Id. at 358.)

Here, IJW describes four categories of trade secrets: (1) Marketing, sales, and operations strategies; (2) Data analysis concerning products and services; (3) Long- and short- term strategic plans; and (4) Research and development.

Marketing, sales, and operations strategies are described as including “investor materials providing detailed information on IJW’s methods and processes for conducting its business operations and monetizing business opportunities and ventures.” (Frisch Decl., ¶3(a).) This description does not provide the Court with sufficient information to identify what might qualify as a trade secret. Instead, it describes a category of trade secrets.

Similarly, IJW describes its data analysis as “data and analysis thereof concerning its business operations, including its development and provision of products and services to clients.” (Frisch Decl., ¶ 3(b).)

IJW’s description of its long – and short – term strategic plans as “goals and strategies set forth in confidential and proprietary internal documents,” is insufficient because it obliquely references documents containing the trade secrets without describing them. (Id. At ¶ 3(c).)

IJW’s description of its research and development trade secrets is more specific: these secretes “include, for instance, the specifications of IJW’s product formulations and corresponding production techniques.” (Id. at ¶ 3(d).) However, this description is still of a system that might qualify for protection and does not identify any specific secrets.

The supplemental Frisch declaration submitted in reply provides additional detail:

a. Non-public details of IJW’s actual and prospective business and/or investor relationships, including the identities of the actual and/or prospective counterparties, precise dollar figures associated with such relationships, and the contents of communications between IJW and the actual and/or prospective counterparties;

b. Non-public details regarding the ownership and structure of various IJW affiliated entities, as well as changes thereto and associated documentation;

c. Non-public details regarding the valuation and fundraising efforts of IJW and its affiliates, including precise dollar figures and timing;

d. Non-public details regarding what the Draft Complaint refers to as “IJW’s entire value-realization strategy,” and specific revenue figures and rates of return associated with different aspects of said “strategy”; and

e. Non-public details regarding IJW’s construction of a distillery, including the budget for such project.

(Supplemental Frisch Decl., ¶ 9.)

In InteliClear, LLC v. ETC Global Holdings, Inc., the ninth circuit found that the description “the InteliClear System's unique design and concepts and the unique software, formulas, processes, programs, tools, techniques, tables, fields, functionality, and logic by which its components interrelate and process data,” was insufficient alone. However, supplemental declarations describing specific features of the InteliClear system, such as specific tables, columns, account identifiers, codes, and methodologies were sufficient to show a triable issue of fact on Plaintiff’s claim for misappropriation of trade secrets and thus defeat the defendant’s motion for summary judgment. (InteliClear, LLC v. ETC Global Holdings, Inc., supra, 978 F.3d at 658-659.)

Here, the supplemental Frisch declaration provides sufficient detail describing what trade secrets IJW alleges Morduchowitz misappropriated.

IJW submits evidence that Morduchowitz misappropriated the trade secrets at issue in two specific pitches he made on behalf of Samah Capital, one for a burbon brand and another for a tequila company. (Frisch Decl., ¶ 18-20.) Morduchowitz submits evidence that he did not use trade secrets in those pitches and that IJW’s founder authorized him to pursue opportunities outside of IJW in 2024. (Morduchowitz Decl., ¶ 8.) Morduchowitz also submits evidence that IJW passed on both opportunities before he made the pitches on behalf of Samah Capital. (Id. at ¶ 19.) In reply, IJW identifies what confidential material the pitches contained, but does not provide evidence showing that Mr. Elishis did not authorize the pitches. As such, IJW has not met its burden of showing misappropriation.

b.      Breach of Contract

IJW alleges that Morduchowitz breached his employment agreement and the Forensic Inspection Protocol Agreement, and seeks monetary damages, specific performance, and injunctive relief. (Complaint, ¶¶ 88, 96.)  

To prove breach of contract, a plaintiff must show (1) the existence of a contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendants’ breach; and (4) resulting damage. (Wall Street Network, Ltd. v. N. Y. Times Co. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1171, 1178.) When a plaintiff seeks specific performance, it must also prove the inadequacy of a legal remedy, that the underlying contract was reasonable and supported by adequate consideration; the existence of a mutuality of remedies, sufficiently definite contractual terms; and substantial similarity between requested performance and promised performance. (Union Oil Co. of Cal. v. Greka Energy Corp. (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 129, 134 [“‘Specific performance … decreed whenever: (1) … terms are sufficiently definite; (2) consideration is adequate; (3) there is substantial similarity of the requested performance to the contractual terms; (4) there is mutuality of remedies; and (5) plaintiff's legal remedy is inadequate.’”].) 

IJW presents evidence that Morduchowitz’s employment agreement contained a confidentiality obligation and required him to return all IJW’s property at termination. (Frisch Decl., ¶ 10-11.) IJW also brings evidence that Morduchowitz breached this agreement by using IJW’s confidential information to develop Samah Capital and by copying 50,000 documents onto his personal hard drive. (Id. at ¶ 31.) IJW attests that it was harmed by Morduchowitz’s retention of this information. As such, IJW has stated a claim for breach of contract based on the employment contract.

Here, Morduchowitz agreed to turn over all devices containing confidential information after he was terminated, and, with the advice and assistance of counsel, entered into a contract with IJW providing for that forensic inspection – the Forensic Inspection Protocol Agreement. (Morduchowitz Decl., Exhibit 10.) The contract provides for a neutral third party to inspect the devices, identifies which devices are covered by the agreement, and provides Morduchowitz and his counsel the opportunity to remove any privileged or personal information from the devices. (Ibid.)  IJW has presented evidence that Morduchowitz did not turn over the devices or comply with the terms of that contract, and that it has been harmed. As such, IJW has met its burden of showing that it is likely to succeed on its claim for breach of contract.

Balance of the Harms

a.      Use of Confidential Information in violation of the Employment Agreement

IJW alleges that Morduchowitz breached the employment agreement in two ways: by using IJW confidential information to develop Samah Capital and by copying that information onto his personal hard drives and not returning that information. (Frisch Decl., ¶¶ 10-11.) The Court considers the harm done by using confidential information in violation of the employment agreement here.

IJW argues that the balance of the harms favors granting a preliminary injunction barring Morduchowitz from using IJW’s confidential information. IJW argues that Morduchowitz’s pitch decks for a tequila and bourbon brand used this confidential information. (Supplemental Frisch Decl., ¶ 14.) These pitches, however, occurred in 2024 and early 2025, before Morduchowitz left IJW. (Frisch Decl., ¶¶ 18-25.) While there may be a likelihood that those disclosures are actionable breaches of the employment contract, IJW has presented no evidence of imminent harm. Following a careful review of the evidence, the Court cannot find concrete evidence that Morduchowitz is currently using or preparing to use IJW’s confidential information in any future business ventures.  As such, IJW has not satisfied its burden of showing imminent harm.

Additionally, IJW has failed to show that any potential breaches of the confidentiality provision of the employment agreement could not be adequately compensated with monetary damages. IJW references the harm to their business and reputation, but those injuries are compensable with monetary damages.

The Court also believes that granting the preliminary injunction as to the forensic inspection agreement (see below) significantly minimizes the risk of potential harm to IJW. IJW describes the confidential material Morduchowitz copied in significant detail, and the Court does not believe that such information could be used after it is removed from all Morduchowitz’s devices, as is called for in the Forensic Inspection Agreement. (See Supplemental Frisch Decl., ¶ 4.) In that sense, a less restrictive injunction will fulfill the same non-monetary objective as an injunction directing Morduchowitz not to use confidential material, an obligation already contained in the employment agreement with remedies in this litigation.

b.      Retention of Confidential Information in violation of the Employment Agreement and Forensic Inspection Agreement

Morduchowitz contends no harm is done by Morduchowitz’s retention of IJW’s information that cannot be remedied by money damages and that enforcement of the Forensic Inspection Agreement is inappropriate because the issue raised is properly addressed as part of the discovery process. (Opposition, p. 14.) While Morduchowitz is correct that these issues most often arise in the context of discovery disputes, IJW is explicitly seeking to enforce the Forensic Inspection Agreement as part of its complaint against Morduchowitz. The Court is not ordering the forensic inspection under the Discovery Act. Rather, the Court is simply enforcing agreements the parties reached with the benefit of counsel on both sides.

Morduchowitz also argues that IJW’s delay in filing this case and bringing this motion shows that there is no imminent harm. IJW, however, presents compelling evidence that the confidential information on Morduchowitz’s devices includes sensitive financial information that could harm IJW if Morduchowitz elects to distribute it publicly. Just because that has not yet happened does not mean that the harm is not imminent. Were Morduchowitz to disclose the information, IJW’s harm would be substantial.

The type of harm posed by Morduchowitz’s use of confidential information is different from the harm posed by his retention of that information. The Court finds that the difference between using general business strategies as a competitor and returning specific documents is determinative. There is no evidence that harm caused by the former type of breach is imminent because there is no evidence that Morduchowitz has pitched opportunities using IJW information since he was terminated. Were he to enter into a deal using IJW information, the harm would also take time to accrue. The latter category, however, would inflict imminent harm on IJW if the information were disclosed because it would enable competitors to use IJW’s information directly. Morduchowitz and his actions are subject to the jurisdiction of this Court and harm done by his use of confidential information is compensable by monetary damages in this action. On the other hand, if IJW’s confidential information in Morduchowitz’s possession became public, non-party competitors could harm IJW and might not be subject to the Court’s jurisdiction in this matter. Accordingly, as to damages arising from Morduchowitz’s failure to return IJW’s confidential information, IJW has met its burden of showing imminent harm.

Morduchowitz’s fairness objections to the Forensic Inspection Agreement are not well taken. The forensic inspection agreement, as written, contains privacy and privilege provisions that address Morduchowitz’s concerns. The forensic inspection agreement appoints a neutral party to inspect Morduchowitz’s devices and gives Morduchowitz and his attorneys the opportunity to remove privileged and personal information from those devices. The fact that Morduchowitz’s new counsel believe a different inspection protocol should have been negotiated is not grounds to disregard the agreement the parties negotiated. Importantly, Morduchwitz entered into this agreement with the advice and assistance of counsel two months after IJW fired him. At that time, both parties had been in negotiations regarding this agreement and related termination issues for weeks.

Finally, the harm done by retention of confidential information in breach of the employment agreement and forensic inspection agreement is not the type easily compensable with monetary damages. Both agreements required Morduchowitz to turn over confidential information. Specific performance is required in this context to make IJW whole.

Conclusion

IJW’s motion for preliminary injunction is GRANTED in part and DENIED in part.

The Court GRANTS the preliminary injunction and orders Morduchowitz to comply with his obligations under the Forensic Inspection Agreement.

The Court DENIES IJW’s motion for a preliminary injunction as to its other requests.
