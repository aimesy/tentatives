[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING IN PART PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES, COSTS, AND EXPENSES

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

JULY 21, 2026

 

On January 4, 2022, Plaintiffs filed a complaint alleging claims under the Song Beverly Act.  Plaintiffs alleged (1) violation of subdivision (d) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (2) violation of subdivision (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (3) violation of subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code section 1793.2; and (4) breach of implied warranty of merchantability (Civil Code §§1791.1, 1794 and 1795.5). 

Plaintiffs’ request for judicial notice of other trial court orders is denied.  The federal orders are unpublished and nonbinding, and the trial court orders are unpublished and nonprecedential.  (See Santa Ana Hospital Medical Center v. Belshe (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 819, 831 [“a written trial court ruling has no precedential value”].)

Plaintiffs’ evidentiary objections are overruled.

DISCUSSION

As the prevailing party, Plaintiffs are entitled to an award of reasonable attorney fees and expenses.  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)  Plaintiffs request a total of $59,186.45, consisting of (1) $38,224.50 attorney fees, (2) $13,378.58 as a 1.35 lodestar enhancement, (3) $3,583.47 in costs and expenses; and (4) an additional $4,000 for Plaintiffs’ counsel to review Defendants’ opposition and to draft a reply.  (Motion at p. i.)  Defendant does not dispute Plaintiffs’ entitlement to fees and only asks that Plaintiffs’ fees be reduced to $10,498.05

A.        Attorney Fees

California courts apply the “lodestar” approach to determine what attorney fees are reasonable.  (See, e.g., Holguin v. DISH Network LLC (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 1310, 1332.)  This inquiry “begins with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.”  (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.)  From there, the “lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided.”  (Ibid.)  Relevant factors include “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.”  (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.)  The party seeking fees has the burden of documenting the appropriate hours expended and hourly rates.  (City of Colton v. Singletary (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 751, 784.)

Plaintiffs provide a copy of counsel’s billing records, which show 74.5 hours billed over three years of litigation.  (Shahian Decl., ¶26, Ex. 1.)  Plaintiff’s counsel charges various hourly rates from $360 to $875.  (Shahian Decl. ¶26.)  These rates are generally reasonable for experienced attorneys in Los Angeles specializing in these types of cases. (The rates above $600 are doubtful in the Court’s view.)  But because they are experienced with Song-Beverly litigation, the higher rate attorneys should be more efficient in these types of cases than less experienced attorneys.

The number of staff on the case indicates inefficiencies.  Plaintiffs acknowledge that 19 attorneys staffed the case, but “only 6 attorneys. . . accounted for 43.7 hours out of the 74.5 total hours billed (60%).”  (Motion at p. 6, fn. 8.)  With so many attorneys working on the case, there were some inefficiencies when each new attorney needed to review the case file to learn the case.  The billing records also reflect inefficiencies in the number of hours for certain tasks, such as drafting boilerplate discovery requests and motions.  The Court has seen many similar actions brought by Plaintiffs’ counsel Strategic Legal Practices, APC.  The complaints, discovery issues, and fee motions are similar across the cases brought by Plaintiffs’ counsel.  Therefore, less time is needed to draft these documents, and some deduction is warranted.  (See Opposition at pp. 5-8.)  Defendant also identifies numerous entries that required an unreasonably excessive amount of time given the nature of the task and the ultimate outcome of the work. (Id. at pp. 7-8.)

Plaintiffs request an additional lodestar multiplier of 1.35.  “[A] trial court should award a multiplier for exceptional representation only when the quality of representation far exceeds the quality of representation that would have been provided by an attorney of comparable skill and experience billing at the hourly rate used in the lodestar calculation.”  (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 1139.)  This matter was not noticeably different from other lemon law cases, did not involve complex or novel legal issues warranting a multiplier, and Plaintiffs’ counsel has extensive experience litigating similar matters.  Despite the length of time from case inception to settlement, there are no indications that Plaintiffs’ counsel engaged in any actions different from a typical strategy to achieve this result.

The Court declines to add a positive multiplier.  Instead, the Court will apply a negative multiplier of 0.8 (20% reduction) due to the inefficiencies caused by overstaffing and excessive billing for boilerplate items.  (Cash v. County of Los Angeles (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 741, 749.)

Considering the type of case, complexity of the case, length of litigation, and the record as a whole, the Court concludes that a reasonable amount of attorney fees is $33,779.60 ($42,224.50 minus $8,444.90).

C.        Costs and Expenses

Defendant argues that all costs should be stricken because Plaintiffs did not file a memorandum of costs and it is not possible to determine what was reasonable.  (Opposition at pp. 12-13.)

A prevailing plaintiff in a Song-Beverly action may recover all “costs and expenses . . . determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1794, subd. (d).)  “[I]t is clear the Legislature intended the word ‘expenses’ to cover items not included in the detailed statutory definition of ‘costs.’”  (Jensen v. BMW of North America, Inc. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112, 137.)

Plaintiffs’ costs and expenses are reasonable, and Defendant’s opposition does not provide any grounds to strike the costs and expenses.

CONCLUSION

The motion for attorney fees and costs is GRANTED IN PART.  The Court awards Plaintiffs $33,779.60 in attorney fees and $3,583.17 in costs and expenses.

On May 30, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a complaint alleging (1) breach of the warranty of habitability; (2) negligence; (3) breach of quiet enjoyment; (4) nuisance; (5) Business and Professions Code section 17200; (6) intentional infliction of emotional distress; (7) violation of Los Angeles Municipal Code Section 45.30, et seq.; (8) violation of Civil Code section 1942.4; (9) violation of Civil Code section 1942.5. 

On October 13, 2025, Plaintiffs filed the instant Motion to Substitute Plaintiffs’ Successor in Interest in place of Decedent Plaintiff Juan Ramon Padilla.  No opposition or reply was filed as of July 16, 2026.

DISCUSSION

            “A pending action or proceeding does not abate by the death of a party if the cause of action survives.”  (CCP §377.21.)          

            “On motion after the death of a person who commenced an action or proceeding, the court shall allow a pending action or proceeding that does not abate to be continued by the decedent's personal representative or, if none, by the decedent's successor in interest.”  (CCP §377.31.)

            “The person who seeks to commence an action or proceeding or to continue a pending action or proceeding as the decedent's successor in interest under this article, shall execute and file an affidavit or a declaration under penalty of perjury under the laws of this state stating all of the following:

(1) The decedent's name.

(2) The date and place of the decedent's death.

(3) ‘No proceeding is now pending in California for administration of the decedent's estate.’

(4) If the decedent's estate was administered, a copy of the final order showing the distribution of the decedent's cause of action to the successor in interest.

(5) Either of the following, as appropriate, with facts in support thereof:

(A) ‘The affiant or declarant is the decedent's successor in interest (as defined in Section 377.11 of the California Code of Civil Procedure) and succeeds to the decedent's interest in the action or proceeding.’

(B) ‘The affiant or declarant is authorized to act on behalf of the decedent's successor in interest (as defined in Section 377.11 of the California Code of Civil Procedure) with respect to the decedent's interest in the action or proceeding.’

(6) ‘No other person has a superior right to commence the action or proceeding or to be substituted for the decedent in the pending action or proceeding.’

(7) ‘The affiant or declarant affirms or declares under penalty of perjury under the laws of the State of California that the foregoing is true and correct.’”  (CCP §377.32.)

Plaintiffs’ submit the declaration of Plaintiff Martina Gonzalez Maldonado, wife of decedent Plaintiff Juan Ramon Padilla.  (Motion, M. Maldonado Dec., ¶1.)  Maldonado testifies that she is Padilla’s successor-in-interest based on her status as surviving wife.  (Id. at ¶¶2, 4.)  Maldonado testifies Padilla died on August 24, 2025.  (Id. at ¶3, Ex. B.)  Maldonado confirms that there is no proceeding pending in California for administration of Padilla’s estate.  (Id. at ¶4.)  Maldonado testifies that no other person has a superior right to continue this action or to be substituted in place of the decedents in the pending action.  (Id. at ¶7.)  Maldonado testifies Padilla died without any other person who has priority over me under Probate Code section 6402, subdivisions (a)-(c).  (Id. at ¶8.)  Maldonado signed the declaration under penalty of perjury under the laws of the State of California.  (Id. at p. 3.)  Plaintiffs submit a declaration satisfying the requirements to substitute Maldonado as Padilla’s successor-in-interest.

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ Motion to Substitute Plaintiffs’ Successor In Interest in Place of Decedent Plaintiff Juan Ramon Padilla is GRANTED.  Plaintiff Martins Gonzalez Maldonado is substituted in place of Plaintiff Juan Ramon Padilla as Padilla’s successor-in-interest.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING MOTION TO COMPEL INITIAL DISCLOSURES PURSUANT TO CALIFORNIA CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE § 871.26 AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS  

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

July 21, 2026

 

BACKGROUND

On June 26, 2025, Plaintiff Denai Blackshire (“Plaintiff”) filed this Song-Beverly action against Defendants FCA US LLC (“FCA”), Santa Monica Chrysler Jeep Dodge Ram, and Does 1 through 10, inclusive (“Defendants”). On or about September 6, 2020, Plaintiff entered into a warranty contract with FCA regarding a 2020 Jeep Wrangler (“Vehicle”) which was manufactured or distributed by FCA. (Compl., ¶ 7.) Defects and nonconformities manifested themselves within the applicable express warranty period, substantially impairing the use, value, or safety of the Vehicle and rendering the value of the Vehicle worthless and/or de minimis. (Id. at ¶¶ 12-14.) Plaintiff’s Complaint alleges six causes of action for (1) Violation of Subdivision (D) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (2) Violation of Subdivision (B) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (3) Violation of Subdivision (A)(3) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (4) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability; (5) Negligent Repair; and (6) Fraudulent Inducement Concealment.

On June 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed this Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures and Request for Monetary Sanction against FCA.

On July 8, 2026, FCA filed an opposition.

No reply has been filed.

 

            LEGAL STANDARD

“Within 60 days after the filing of the answer or other responsive pleading, all parties shall, without awaiting a discovery request, provide to all other parties an initial disclosure and documents pursuant to subdivisions (f), (g), and (h).” (Civ. Proc. Code, § 871.26, subd. (b).) “The defendant or manufacturer shall provide the following documents to all other parties pursuant to the timelines prescribed in subdivision (b): … (6) Motor vehicle information reports, including build documentation, component information, and delivery details. (7) Entire warranty transaction history for the motor vehicle. (8) Listing of required field actions applicable to the motor vehicle. (9) Published technical service bulletins (”TSBs”)1 for the same make, model, and year reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle. (10) Published information service bulletins (“ISBs”) for the same make, model, and year reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle… (12) Warranty policies and procedure manuals. (13) Service manuals reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle….” (Id. at § 871.26, subd. (h).)

“Unless the party failing to comply with this section shows good cause, notwithstanding any other law and in addition to any other sanctions imposed pursuant to this chapter, a court shall impose sanctions as follows:

(1) A one-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($1,500) sanction against the plaintiff's attorney or two-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($2,500) sanction against the defense attorney respectively, paid within 15 business days for failure to comply with the document production requirements as prescribed in subdivision (b).

(2) A one-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($1,500) sanction against the plaintiff's attorney or two-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($2,500) sanction against the defense attorney respectively, paid within 15 business days for failure to comply with the provisions relating to depositions as prescribed in subdivision (c).”

(Id. at § 871.26, subd. (j).)

 

DISCUSSION

Plaintiff moves for an order compelling FCA to produce all documents required under section 871.26(h)(6)-(10) and (12)-(13) and imposing mandatory sanctions against FCA and its counsel in the amount of $2,500.

A.    Initial Disclosures

Plaintiff argues that FCA has not provided written responses accompanied by verifications of defense counsel as required by Civil Code of Procedure section 016.090(a)(5). Plaintiff contends that complying with section 871.26(h) does not require a complex or burdensome search as FCA need only search the VIN number in its own warranty database and dealer-facing systems. Plaintiff argues that FCA has not produced the Complete DealerCONNECT report for the Vehicle and has only produced selected repair orders and some warranty information. Plaintiff also argues that unverified complaint claims and AutoPay claims must be produced. Furthermore, Plaintiff argues that FCA must conduct a separate, independent search of all warranty claims paid under both the 85-41-XX-XX prefix and the 08-19-XX-40 series for the subject VIN. Additionally, while FCA produced some Technical Service Bulletins (TSBs), it has produced no Informational Service Bulletins (ISBs). Moreover, without verification, Plaintiff contends Plaintiff cannot independently confirm that FCA has produced all TSBs applicable to the Vehicle. Next, Plaintiff contends that FCA has not produced service manuals for all systems reasonably related to the alleged nonconformities. Plaintiff argues that the information FCA has not produced from its Warranty System/DealerCONNECT is directly relevant to the reasonable number of repair attempts and willfulness for civil penalties.

In opposition, FCA argues that nothing in section 871.26 requires a manufacturer to serve written responses, verifications, sworn statements, or a certification of search completeness. Rather, the statute requires production of enumerated categories of documents and information. FCA argues that Plaintiff incorrectly attempts to import a verification requirement from Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.090(a)(5) when section 871.26 is a distinct statutory scheme. Furthermore, FCA contends that section 871.26 establishes limited early disclosure procedures for Song-Beverly cases to occur prior to the expense of full discovery. FCA argues that the disclosures do not require a statement of compliance, search terms, or to produce every document that may be searchable by VIN in every internal system. FCA argues that Plaintiff seeks to expand section 871.26 beyond its text to authorize broad discovery. Plaintiff may pursue discovery at the appropriate time. Moreover, FCA already produced the documents required by 871.26. FCA contends Plaintiff has not shown that a required statutory category was not produced. Instead, Plaintiff has claimed that production is incomplete. Plaintiff’s request expands beyond the purpose and text of section 871.26.

Here, the Court agrees with FCA that Plaintiff has incorrectly claimed that section 871.26 requires a manufacturer to serve written responses, verifications, sworn statements, or a certification of search completeness. Nowhere in section 871.26 requires written responses or verifications. Instead, it states: “Within 60 days after the filing of the answer or other responsive pleading, all parties shall, without awaiting a discovery request, provide to all other parties an initial disclosure and documents pursuant to subdivisions (f), (g), and (h).” (Civ. Proc. Code, § 871.26, subd. (b).) Plaintiff has incorrectly conflated section 871.26 disclosures with initial disclosures under section 2016.090(a)(5). Thus, Plaintiff’s argument that FCA has failed to provide verified disclosures is inapplicable.

Moreover, Plaintiff does not contend that FCA failed to serve initial disclosure production in its entirety. Instead, Plaintiff argues that the initial disclosures are incomplete. FCA has served its initial disclosures which included repair records, warranty claims records, the repair order detail report, vehicle information records sales documents, service contract reports, Customer Assistance Inquiry records, the Monroney label, TSBs, the service manual, and recall-related materials, among other documents, as required by section 871.26(h). (Opposition, p. 4.) Plaintiff admits to having received document disclosures of the service manual and TSBs. (Motion, p. 12.) As section 871.26 does not require verification, FCA was not required to have verified that all records have been produced. Thus, the Court finds that FCA complied with its initial disclosure obligations for these categories. To the extent that Plaintiff seeks further or additional information, Plaintiff should do so through other discovery methods such as interrogatories or requests for production.

Accordingly, the motion to compel is denied.

B.     Sanctions

Plaintiff requests sanctions for $2,500. In opposition, FCA argues that sanctions should be denied because FCA served a timely initial production, produced categories of documents required by section 871.26, and responded to Plaintiff’s meet and confer. Plaintiff’s motion rests on disputed legal interpretations and demand beyond section 871.26.

Since the Court finds FCA to have complied with section 871.26 by serving the required documents in their initial disclosures, the Court denies Plaintiff’s request for sanctions.

 

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, the motion to compel initial disclosures and request for monetary sanctions are DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING DEFENDANT FCA US LLC’S DEMURRER TO PLAINTIFF’S COMPLAINT

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

July 21, 2026

 

On June 30, 2025, Plaintiff Jennifer Post (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants FCA US LLC (“Defendant”), Stead Motors Inc. dba Walnut Creek Chrysler Jeep Dodger Ram, and Does 1 through 10 arising from Plaintiff’s purchase of an allegedly defective vehicle (“Vehicle”).

On October 14, 2025, Defendant filed this Demurrer to the fourth cause of action for breach of implied warranty and the sixth cause of action for fraudulent inducement – concealment.

On July 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.

No reply has been filed.

 

 

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true.  (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.)

 

DISCUSSION

A.    The Fourth Cause of Action is Time-Barred

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action for breach of implied warranty is time-barred by the four-year statute of limitations.

An implied warranty claim is governed by the four-year statute of limitations period under California’s Uniform Commercial Code section 2725.  (Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1297, 1305-1306 (Mexia).)  “A cause of action accrues when the breach occurs, regardless of the aggrieved party’s lack of knowledge of the breach.  A breach of warranty occurs when tender of delivery is made, except that where a warranty explicitly extends to future performance of the goods and discovery of the breach must await the time of such performance the cause of action accrues when the breach is or should have been discovered.”  (Comm. Code, § 2725, subd. (2).)  In contrast to an express warranty, “an implied warranty . . . is not a warranty that ‘explicitly extends to future performance of the goods’  [Citations.]”  (Cardinal Health 301, Inc. v. Tyco Electronics Corp. (2008) 169 Cal.App.4th 116, 134 (Cardinal Health 301).)  “The implied warranty of merchantability may be breached by a latent defect undiscoverable at the time of sale.”  (Mexia, supra, 174 Cal.App.4th at p. 1304.)  “In the case of a latent defect, a product is rendered unmerchantable, and the warranty of merchantability is breached, by the existence of the unseen defect, not by its subsequent discovery. . . . Thus, although a defect may not be discovered for months or years after a sale, merchantability is evaluated as if the defect were known.”  (Id. at p. 1305.)

Defendant contends that Plaintiff purchased the Vehicle on September 23, 2020. Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s breach of implied warranty cause of action accrued on that date and expired four years later on September 23, 2024. Plaintiff did not file suit until June 30, 2025, which is nearly a year after the four-year limitations period expired.

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Plaintiff had no way of knowing about the breach of implied warranty until the defect manifested itself. Plaintiff contends that Plaintiff’s implied warranty claim was thus tolled by the delayed discovery rule and is timely. Plaintiff argues that the time accrues where the consumer discovers the defect and argues that Cardinal Health 301 is inapplicable.

The Court is not persuaded by Plaintiff’s argument. Cardinal Health 301 expressly states that: “Because an implied warranty is one that arises by operation of law rather than by an express agreement of the parties, courts have consistently held it is not a warranty that “explicitly extends to future performance of the goods.” (Cardinal Health 301, supra, 169 Cal.App.4th at p. 134.) Thus, Plaintiff’s argument that statute of limitations does not accrue until the defect should have been discovered is inapplicable.

Moreover, Plaintiff does not dispute that Plaintiff purchased on September 23, 2020. In fact, Plaintiff’s Complaint states that Plaintiff entered a warranty contract with Defendant on September 23, 2020. (Compl., ¶ 7.) Plaintiff filed the Complaint on June 30, 2025, which is beyond four years. (See Compl.) Thus, the statute of limitations has run.

Accordingly, the demurrer to the fourth cause of action is sustained without leave to amend.

B.     The Sixth Cause of Action is Not Time-Barred

Defendant argues that the sixth cause of action for fraudulent inducement – concealment is time-barred by a three-year statute of limitations.

To sustain a demurrer on the statute of limitations, the running of the statute must appear clearly and affirmatively on the face of the complaint.  (Geneva Towers Ltd. Partnership v. City of San Francisco (2003) 29 Cal.4th 769, 781.)  “‘[I]t is not enough that the complaint might be time-barred.  [Citation.]’”  (Ibid.)

Plaintiff alleges that: “the running of the limitation periods have been tolled by, inter alia, the following doctrines or rules: equitable tolling, the discovery rule, the fraudulent concealment rules, equitable estoppel, the repair rule, and/or class action tolling (e.g., the American Pipe rule).” (Compl., ¶ 37.)

“The general rule is that the statute of limitations begins to run when the wrongful act is done, even though the plaintiff is ignorant of his or her cause of action or of the identity of the wrongdoer.”  (Snow v. A. H. Robins Co. (1985) 165 Cal.App.3d 120, 127.)  The running of the statute of limitations may be postponed on certain causes of action until the time of discovery of the facts.  (Ibid.)  “With respect to actions based on fraud, the statute of limitations is tolled whenever plaintiff is able to show the defendant fraudulently concealed facts which would have led him to discover his potential cause of action.”  (Id. at pp. 127-128.)

Here, Plaintiff alleges that the parties entered into a warranty contract on September 23, 2020. (Compl., ¶ 7.) Before purchasing the Vehicle, Plaintiff alleges FCA knew that the Vehicle was defective. (Id. at ¶ 18.) “Plaintiff discovered Defendants' wrongful conduct alleged herein shortly before the filing of the complaint” on June 30, 2025. (Id. at ¶ 38.)

The Complaint does not facially show that the statute of limitations bars this cause of action. It is not clear from the face of the Complaint when Plaintiff should have discovered that Defendant concealed information about the vehicle’s defects.

Accordingly, the demurrer is overruled on this ground.

 

C.    The Sixth Cause of Action Fails to Plead Fraud with Specificity

Defendant argues that the sixth cause of action for fraudulent inducement – concealment does not allege facts with the requisite specificity.

Fraud based on concealment requires that “(1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.”  (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 310-311 (Bigler-Engler).)  “Even under the strict rules of common law pleading, one of the canons was that less particularity is required when the facts lie more in the knowledge of the opposite party.”  (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn., Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1256, 1384.)

An essential element of intentional concealment includes the duty to disclose, which must be based upon a transaction, or a special relationship, between plaintiff and defendant.  (Id. at p. 314.)  “There are ‘four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.’”  (Id. at p. 311.)  “[O]ther than the first instance, in which there must be a fiduciary relationship between the parties, ‘the other three circumstances in which nondisclosure may be actionable presuppose[ ] the existence of some other relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise. . . . “[W]here material facts are known to one party and not to the other, failure to disclose them is not actionable fraud unless there is some relationship between the parties which gives rise to a duty to disclose such known facts.”  [Citation.]’  [Citation.]”  (Hoffman v. 162 North Wolfe LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 1178, 1187 (Hoffman).)  “Such a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.”  (Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at p. 312.)

Here, Plaintiff alleges Defendant knew about the Vehicle and Engine defects prior to the sale of the Vehicle. (Compl., ¶ 66.) Plaintiff did not know about the defects. (Id. at ¶  71.) Defendant, who had superior knowledge of the defects, did not disclose the defects to Plaintiff. (Id. at ¶ 67.)  These allegations are specific enough to allege the information that was concealed and the danger posed.  (See Jones v. ConocoPhillips Co. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1187, 1199-1200.)

However, Plaintiff does not allege facts showing the existence of a relationship between the parties that gives rise to a duty to disclose facts.  (See Hoffman, supra, 228 Cal.App.4th at p. 1187.)  Plaintiff alleges that Defendant had knowledge through sources not available to consumers such as Plaintiff including “pre-production testing data, early consumer complaints about the Engine Defect made directly to FCA and its network of dealers, aggregate warranty data compiled from FCA's network of dealers, testing conducted by FCA in response to these complaints, as well as warranty repair and part replacements data received by FCA from FCA's network of dealers, amongst other sources of internal information.” (Compl., ¶ 67.) There are no facts showing that Defendant’s knowledge was exclusive or that Defendant intended to defraud.  Indeed, Plaintiff alleges that there were prior customer complaints, suggesting that the knowledge was in fact public. (Ibid.)

Moreover, in a transaction that does not involve fiduciary or confidential relations, the “transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.”  (Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at pp. 311-312.)  Plaintiff alleges no such direct transaction with and direct concealment by Defendant.

Accordingly, the demurrer to the sixth cause of action is sustained on this ground.

 

D.    The Sixth Cause of Action is Barred by the Economic Loss Rule

Defendant argues that the sixth cause of action is barred by the Economic Loss Rule.

            Under the economic loss rule, “[w]here a purchaser’s expectations in a sale are frustrated because the product he bought is not working properly, his remedy is said to be in contract alone, for he has suffered only ‘economic’ losses.”  (Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 988 (Robinson), quotation marks omitted.)  However, tort damages may be permitted when the breach of contract is accompanied by a tort such as fraud.  (Id. at pp. 989-990.)  To plead around the economic loss rule, a party must plead the existence of a duty that arises independent of any contractual duty and independent injury, other than economic loss, that arises from the breach of that duty.  (Id. at pp. 988-991.)

“[U]nder California law, the economic loss rule does not bar [a claim] for fraudulent inducement by concealment.  Fraudulent inducement claims fall within an exception to the economic loss rule recognized by our Supreme Court” in Robinson, and such claims allege fraudulent conduct that is independent of the alleged warranty breaches.  (Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 843.)  The Supreme Court has confirmed that “the economic loss rule does not apply to limit recovery for intentional tort claims like fraud.”  (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 38.)  “A plaintiff may assert a tort claim for fraudulent concealment based on conduct occurring in the course of a contractual relationship, if the elements of the cause of action can be established independently of the parties’ contractual rights and obligations and the tortious conduct exposes the plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond the reasonable contemplation of the parties when they entered into the agreement.”  (Ibid.)

Accordingly, a properly pleaded claim for fraudulent inducement is not barred by the economic loss rule.  Here, however, Plaintiff does not establish all elements of fraudulent concealment independently of the parties’ contractual rights and obligations.

Additionally, Plaintiff has not pled an independent harm beyond the contractual right and obligations. Plaintiff alleges: “Plaintiff was harmed by purchasing a vehicle that Plaintiff would not have leased and/or purchased had Plaintiff known the true facts about the Engine Defect.  Furthermore, Plaintiff was unknowingly exposed to the risk of liability, accident and injury as a result of Defendant's fraudulent concealment of the Engine Defect.” (Compl., ¶ 71.) However, this allegation fails to state that Plaintiff was in fact damaged. Unknown risk that does not result in liability or injury by definition is not actual damage.  (Small v. Fritz Companies, Inc. (2003) 30 Cal.4th 167, 202 [“Misrepresentation, even maliciously committed, does not support a cause of action unless the plaintiff suffered consequential damages,” concurring opinion]; Boschma v. Home Loan Center, Inc. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 230, 248 [“as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage”].)  Moreover, the alleged harm of purchasing the Vehicle is an economic harm which was within the reasonable contemplation of the parties when they entered into the agreement.  (See Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 38.) 

Accordingly, this cause of action is barred by the economic loss rule.

 

CONCLUSION

Accordingly, the demurrer is SUSTAINED. The demurrer to the fourth cause of action is sustained without leave to amend and the demurrer to the sixth cause of action is sustained on the grounds that is insufficiently plead and barred by the economic loss rule.

At the hearing, Plaintiff should be prepared to explain what additional facts will be pleaded and exactly how any amendments can remedy the deficiencies of the sixth cause of action without making it a sham pleading.  The Court will decide at the hearing whether to grant leave to file a First Amended Complaint.

(a), (b) [demands for inspection].)  Motions to compel initial responses are not subject to meet and confer requirements or filing deadlines.

A court is required to impose monetary sanctions against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes motions to compel initial responses to interrogatories and demands unless the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, subd. (c) and 2031.300, subd. (c).)  For requests for admissions: “It is mandatory that the court impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) on the party or attorney, or both, whose failure to serve a timely response to requests for admission necessitated this motion.”  (Code Civ. GRANTING DEFENDANT PUBLIC STORAGE’S MOTION FOR SANCTIONS

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

JULY 21, 2026

 

On February 5, 2025, Plaintiff Jose Paz filed a complaint against Defendants Public Storage, Storage Equities, Inc. and DOE 1 (Manager) alleging (1) general negligence and (2) premises liability.  Plaintiff slipped and fell on water and/or an unknown substance at the premises of Defendant Public Storage.

On June 22, 2026, Defendant Public Storage Operating Company filed the instant Motion for Sanctions.  On July 8, 2026, Plaintiff Jose Paz filed an opposition.  On July 14, 2026, Defendant filed a reply. 

Trial is set for August 10, 2026.

I.  Legal Standard

            “To the extent authorized by the chapter governing any particular discovery method or any other provision of this title, the court, after notice to any affected party, person, or attorney, and after opportunity for hearing, may impose the following sanctions against anyone engaging in conduct that is a misuse of the discovery process:…(b) The court may impose an issue sanction ordering that designated facts shall be taken as established in the action in accordance with the claim of the party adversely affected by the misuse of the discovery process. The court may also impose an issue sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from supporting or opposing designated claims or defenses.  (c) The court may impose an evidence sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from introducing designated matters in evidence….”  (Code of Civ. Proc., §2023.030, subd. (b) and (c).)

Discovery sanctions are not intended to punish but to accomplish discovery.  (Newland v. Supr. Ct. (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 608, 613)  Discovery sanctions are not intended to punish but to accomplish discovery.  (Id. at 613.)  CCP §2023.010 does not require that any heightened showing be made in connection with a request for terminating sanctions.  The Court is also not required to make any findings or detail with particularity the basis for its decision to impose sanctions.  (Ghanooni v. Super Shuttle (1993) 20 Cal.App.4th 256, 261.) 

“Spoliation of evidence means the destruction or significant alteration of evidence or the failure to preserve evidence for another's use in pending or future litigation.  Such conduct is condemned because it can destroy fairness and justice, for it increases the risk of an erroneous decision on the merits of the underlying cause of action. Destroying evidence can also increase the costs of litigation as parties attempt to reconstruct the destroyed evidence or to develop other evidence, which may be less accessible, less persuasive, or both.  While there is no tort cause of action for the intentional destruction of evidence after litigation has commenced, it is a misuse of the discovery process that is subject to a broad range of punishment, including monetary, issue, evidentiary, and terminating sanctions.  A terminating sanction is appropriate in the first instance without a violation of prior court orders in egregious cases of intentional spoliation of evidence.”  (Williams v. Russ (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1223.)  A party “moving for discovery sanctions based on the spoliation of evidence must make an initial prima facie showing that the responding party in fact destroyed evidence that had a substantial probability of damaging the moving party's ability to establish an essential element of his claim or defense.”  (Id. at 1227.)

            II.  Defendant Public Storage is Entitled to Issue Sanctions

Plaintiff intentionally destroyed or abandoned the shoes he was wearing during the accident in April 2024.  (Morgan Dec., Ex. E, 36:21-37:9.)  Plaintiff did so because he was moving and could not take everything with him because he did not have enough room.  (Id. at 37:4-6.)  Plaintiff retained counsel within two to three weeks after the incident, which occurred on March 14, 2023.  (Id. at 118:25-119:7.) 

Based on these facts, Plaintiff intentionally destroyed or spoliated evidence, although not in bad faith.  Plaintiff argues this does not qualify as discovery abuse warranting sanctions because it was “unintentional.”  However, Plaintiff’s destruction or abandonment of his shoes was intentional, although there is no evidence that he did it in bad faith or in order to damage Defendant’s case.  The applicable standard does not require bad faith.  “Spoliation of evidence means the destruction or significant alteration of evidence or the failure to preserve evidence for another's use in pending or future litigation.”  (William, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at 1223.)  In “egregious” cases of spoliation, which would include cases of bad faith, terminating sanctions may be impose in the first instance without prior discovery abuses.  (Id.)

Defendant only asks for imposition of issue or evidentiary sanctions.  Defendant need only “make an initial prima facie showing” that Plaintiff’s destruction of the shoes he was wearing on the day of the accident had “a substantial probability of damaging the moving party's ability to establish an essential element of his claim or defense.”  (Id. at 1227.)  Defendant is therefore not required to establish that Plaintiff’s shoes would have necessarily established comparative fault or negligence.  Defendant need only establish that loss of the shoes damaged its “ability to establish an element” of comparative fault. 

Defendant makes a prima facie case that loss of the shoes damages its ability to demonstrate that Plaintiff’s comparative fault for the accident.  The footwear a plaintiff is wearing when he or she slips and falls can be the basis for finding comparative fault.  (Scott v. Alpha Beta Co. (1980) 104 Cal.App.3d 305, 310 (plaintiff’s choice to wear pink furry house slippers at time of fall was reasonable basis for jury to conclude that plaintiff was comparatively at fault for the accident).)  Plaintiff’s destruction of his shoes deprived the Defendant of the ability to physically examine the condition of the shoes and formulate a defense based on comparative fault.  Plaintiff’s deposition testimony is insufficient to cure Defendant’s inability of Defendant to examine the physical shoes Plaintiff wore during the incident. 

Defendant requests the following sanctions:

1. issue sanctions establishing that the condition of Plaintiff’s footwear, including its tread, wear, contamination and slip resistance, was a substantial factor in causing the incident and precluding Plaintiff from disputing those issues; and additionally or in the alternative;

2. evidentiary sanctions precluding Plaintiff from offering any evidence, testimony, or argument concerning the condition, design, traction, tread or slip resistance of the footwear worn at the time of the incident, and permitting Defendant to present evidence and argument regarding spoliation and to request an adverse inference at trial that the lost footwear would have been unfavorable to Plaintiff’s claims.

The Court grants Defendant’s request for an evidentiary sanction precluding Plaintiff from offering evidence, testimony, or argument concerning the condition, design, traction, tread or slip resistance of the footwear worn at the time of the incident, and permitting Defendant to present evidence and argument regarding spoliation and to request an adverse inference at trial that the lost footwear would have been unfavorable to Plaintiff’s claim.  The request for issue sanctions is denied as moot.

            Accordingly, the motion for evidentiary sanctions is GRANTED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING DEMURRER AND DENYING MOTION TO STRIKE AS MOOT

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

JULY 21, 2026

 

On February 24, 2026, Plaintiff Lewis Brians filed a complaint against Defendants The Estate of Frank Anthony Sinatra, Deceased (the “Estate”) and Sean Umina as Trustee of the Frank Sinatra Living Trust (the “Trustee”)(collectively “Defendants” alleging (1) breach of an oral agreement; (2) failure to pay minimum wage; (3) failure to pay overtime wages; (4) failure to keep proper payroll records or to classify employee accurately; (5) waiting time penalties; (6) quantum meruit. 

On May 6, 2026, Defendants filed the instant demurrer and motion to strike.  On July 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed oppositions to the demurrer and motion to strike.  On July 13, 2026, Defendants filed replies to the oppositions. 

DISCUSSION

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true.  (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.)  “Because a demurrer challenges defects on the face of the complaint, it can only refer to matters outside the pleading that are subject to judicial notice.”  (Arce ex rel. Arce v. Kaiser Found. Health Plan, Inc. (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 471, 556.)

            A demurrer based on an affirmative defense “cannot be properly sustained where the action might be barred by the defense but is not necessarily barred.”  (CrossTalk Productions, Inc. v. Jacobson (1998) 65 Cal.App.4th 631, 635.)  Demurrer based on statute of limitations can only be sustained where the dates on the face of the complaint clearly and affirmatively establish that the action is time-barred.  (Roman v. County of Los Angeles (2000) 85 Cal.App.4th 316, 324-325 (“If the dates establishing the running of the statute of limitations do not clearly appear in the complaint, there is no ground for general demurrer”); (“demurrer based on an affirmative defense cannot properly be sustained where the action might be barred by the defense, but is not necessarily barred.”) 

            “The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: (a) Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (b) Strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.” (Code of Civ. Proc.,  §436.)

            Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 436, the Court may strike out “any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading” and/or “all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.” (Code of Civ. Proc., §436.) In ruling on a motion to strike, the allegations in the complaint are considered in context and presumed to be true: “[J]udges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth.” (Clauson v. Sup.Ct. (Pedus Services, Inc.)(1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)

A.    Plaintiff’s First Cause of Action for Breach of Oral Contract is Not Necessarily Barred by the Statute of Frauds

Where the complaint seeks to enforce an agreement required to be in writing under the statute of frauds, but nonetheless alleges the agreement was oral, a general demurrer lies. The complaint on its face discloses a bar to recovery.  (Rossberg v. Bank of America, N.A. (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1481, 1503; Parker v. Solomon (1959) 171 Cal.App.2d 125, 136.) 

“The following contracts are invalid, unless they, or some note or memorandum thereof, are in writing and subscribed by the party to be charged or by the party’s agent:…(5) An agreement that by its terms is not be performed during the lifetime of the promisor.”  (Civ. Code, §1624, subd. (a)(5).) 

“A mere possibility that a contract will not be performed within the lifetime of the promisor is insufficient” to support application of Civil Code section 1624 subdivision (a)(5).  (Spears v. Spears (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th 1294, 1298, 1307 (decedent’s oral promises to repay his debts to his son in periodic payments were not subject to statute of frauds merely because decedent stated that “if he died before paying off the debt, the debt would become due and payable in full”; plaintiff did not allege that decedent was prohibited from repaying debt before his death or that agreements otherwise foreclosed possibility that decedent could have fully performed during his lifetime.)  “If the terms of a contract are such that it admits of performance during the lifetime of the promisor, it is not within the statutory provision here involved.”  (Roy v. Salisbury (1942) 21 Cal.2d 176, 182 (contract that required plaintiff to care for decedent’s dog if he “ever became unable to care for said dog, or if he should die” was not subject to statute of frauds because contract could be performed within lifetime of decedent; if decedent had become unable to care for the dog during his lifetime and dog could have potentially died before decedent died thereby completing performance within the decedent’s lifetime).) 

In Hagan v. McNary (1915) 170 Cal. 141, the Supreme Court applied Civil Code section 1624 subdivision (a)(5) to an agreement that required the plaintiff to provide a “regular, permanent, and established home” “for and during the term of his [decedent’s] natural life.”  (Hagan, supra, 170 Cal. at 142-143.)  “The agreement, as alleged and proven, states no time for the payment of the $7,000 which Kupper agreed to pay to plaintiff for the services to be rendered. The services consisted of furnishing Kupper a regular, permanent, and established home during the term of his natural life. This, of course, includes his whole life, and the service was not and could not be completed so long as breath remained in his body. The plaintiff's part of the agreement became completely executed upon his death, and not until then.”  (Hagan, supra, 170 Cal. at 143.)  Because the parties’ agreement did not specify a time for payment, “the obligation to pay does not become matured, and payment is not due until the services are completely performed.  Complete performance of the service did not take place until the moment of [decedent’s] death.  No obligation for immediate payment arose, therefore, until the moment after; that is, after the death of [decedent].”  (Id. at 143.)

In Parker v. Solomon (1959) 171 Cal.App.2d 125, plaintiff agreed not to press her claims as decedent’s unknown child, not to move to Los Angeles where decedent was residing and to stay out of decedent’s life in exchange for decedent’s promise that he would transfer half of his estate to her either during his lifetime or at his death.  (Parker, supra, 171 Cal.App.2d at 129.)  The court found the agreement was subject to the statute of frauds, affirming the trial court’s order sustaining demurrer without leave to amend.  The court explained, “[i]t is clear that the agreement pleaded could not have been fully performed on plaintiff's part prior to her father's death, since her promise was to refrain from asserting any claim against him ‘and to remain out of his life’. And it is equally clear that the amount which plaintiff claims under the agreement (one-half the value of his estate) could not be ascertained until after his death.”  (Id. at 131.) 

According to Plaintiff, prior to August 1, 2023, he worked part time for Frank Sinatra (“Sinatra”) as a personal attendant or health care worker who was not live in.  (Complaint, ¶5.)  From August 2022 to August 1, 2023, Sinatra paid Plaintiff a rate of $25 per hour and he paid by personal check.  (Id.) 

After August 1, 2023, Plaintiff moved into Sinatra’s house to serve as a round-the-clock, daily caretaker.  (Id.)  Shortly after moving in, Sinatra announced he would no longer pay Brian in cash but would be opening up a certificate of deposit (“CD”) or other similar financial instrument.  (Id. at ¶6.)  Plaintiff took this to mean that his wages would be deposited into the CD that would mature later, and he did not wish to dispute it at the time when he just moved in.  (Id.)  Sinatra extracted a promise from Plaintiff that he would stay with him “till the end,”  which Plaintiff did until February 2026, when Sinatra died.  (Id.)

Plaintiff alleges he attempted to discuss when and how much he would be paid, but Sinatra would only become agitated and shout that he would be left to die alone.  (Id. at ¶8.)  Sinatra offered to make a couple of payments to Brian of $5,000 to catch up with back wages.  (Id.)  Plaintiff indicated that would not be enough and Sinatra responded, “then you’ll have to get it after I’m gone.”  (Id.)  Sinatra died in February 2026 without making any payment of $5000 or any wages at all to Plaintiff.  (Id.) 

Plaintiff alleges Sinatra funded a CD as promised but only deposited $15,000.  (Id. at ¶6.)  Plaintiff alleges in the complaint filed on February 24, 2026 that the CD does not mature for several more months.  (Id.) 

In Plaintiff’s first cause of action, Plaintiff alleges Sinatra and Plaintiff entered into an oral agreement whereby “Brians would care for Sinatra ‘till the end’ and in his own house, so he would not die alone, and Brians stated that he would.”  (Id. at ¶10.)  Plaintiff alleges that “in consideration of Brians’ work, Sinatra would pay Brians an amount to be calculated in the future, or that Brians would be compensated out of Sinatra’s estate when Sinatra was gone.”  (Id.)

Based on Plaintiff’s allegations, the statute of frauds does not necessarily bar his oral contract claim.  Plaintiff fails to allege any obligation of Sinatra’s that could not have been performed within his lifetime.  Plaintiff does not allege that parties arrived at any agreement regarding when Sinatra’s was obligated to pay Plaintiff, and Sinatra only mentioned the possibility that Plaintiff would have to be paid out of the estate.  Unlike the decedent in Parker, Sinatra did not agree to give Plaintiff a portion of his “estate” in compensation for his services.  “Estate” necessarily refers to a person’s assets after death and payment of half of that could only be determined after death.  (Parker, supra, 171 Cal.App.2d at 131.) 

The mere possibility that Sinatra would satisfy his payment obligations after death is insufficient to establish that the statute of frauds applies to the parties’ agreement.  Unlike the decedent in Parker, Sinatra did not agree to give Plaintiff a portion of his “estate” in compensation for his services.  “Estate” necessarily refers to a person’s assets after death and payment of half of that could only be determined after death.  (Parker, supra, 171 Cal.App.2d at 131.)  Under the oral agreement alleged, Sinatra could have fulfilled his obligations by paying Plaintiff prior to his death, as services were rendered.  Like the decedent in Spears, Plaintiff has not alleged an agreement with Sinatra prohibiting him from paying Plaintiff before his death or that “otherwise foreclosed the possibility that decedent could have fully performed during his lifetime.”  (Spears, supra, 97 Cal.App.5th at 1207.) 

For the same reasons, Plaintiff’s alleged oral contract is distinguishable from that alleged in Smith v. Bliss (1941) 44 Cal.App.2d 171.  In Smith, the promisor made a verbal promise to pay plaintiff for employment as a companion and caretaker for promisor’s lifetime.  (Smith, supra, 44 Cal.App.2d at 173-174.)  In compensation for plaintiff’s employment as a lifetime companion and caretaker, the promisor promised to pay plaintiff the same amounts she paid to a third party, who was also promisor’s friend and companion.  (Id. at 174.)  The court found plaintiff alleged an agreement that could not be performed within the promisor’s lifetime, because “the amount of compensation depended on the amount of the accounts in favor of Irene Martin,” and “[u]ntil the date of Mrs. Coffin’s death that amount could not be known.”  (Id.)  Here, Plaintiff’s fails clearly allege agreed upon compensation in any amount, much less compensation tied to any measure that could only be determined upon Sinatra’s death.    

Plaintiff’s alleged oral agreement is also factually distinguishable from Hagan.  Unlike the plaintiff in Hagan, Plaintiff does not allege that Sinatra agreed to pay him a lump sum.  Plaintiff “assumed” the parties’ agreed upon hourly wage of $25 per hour applied but he fails to allege mutual consent to any specific payment terms.  Plaintiff’s allegation that Sinatra “offered” to pay $5,000 does not allege an agreement but merely an offer.  (Complaint, ¶8.)  Hagan stated the rule that, where a time for payment is not specified, “the obligation to pay does not become matured, and payment is not due until the services are completely performed.”  (Hagan, supra, 170 Cal. at 143.)  Sinatra’s promise to pay the wages into a CD also did not necessarily foreclose his performance prior to death.  Plaintiff does not allege any agreement as to a specific type of CD, e.g. fixed rate, 1-year, 5-year.

The alleged oral agreement included terms that would clearly be barred by Civil Code section 1624 subdivision (a)(5).  Plaintiff alleges Sinatra agreed as part of the parties’ oral employment agreement that Plaintiff “could continue to live in the house for six months, rent-free, after Sinatra’s death, and mentioned this agreement to third parties before his death.”  (Complaint, ¶12.)  Sinatra was foreclosed from fulfilling this promise within his lifetime because the agreement specifically allowed for Plaintiff to reside at Sinatra’s home for six months “after Sinatra’s death.”  However, Sinatra’s promise to allow Defendant to reside at his home after his death would not be grounds to sustain demurrer to the entire first cause of action.  “If a claimant alleges two or more promises of performance ‘that can easily be distinguished and separated by the court by reference to the agreement itself’ (2 Corbin on Contracts, s 133, at pp. 127—128), only that promise of performance which falls clearly within the statute of frauds cannot be enforced.”  (White Lighting Co. v. Wolfson (1968) 68 Cal.2d 336, 345.)

Defendants fail to establish that Plaintiff’s alleged oral employment contract is “necessarily barred” by the statute of frauds set forth in Civil Code section 1624 subdivision (a)(5) based on the complaint allegations.  (CrossTalk Productions, Inc., supra, 65 Cal.App.4th at 635.)  At best, Defendants establish that Plaintiff’s alleged oral agreement “might be barred” by the statute of frauds.  Defendants’ demurrer based on statute of frauds is overruled. 

B.     The First Cause of Action for Breach of Contract does not Implicate Probate Code Section 21700

“A contract to make a will or devise or other instrument, or not to revoke a will or devise or other instrument, or to die intestate, if made after the effective date of this statute, can be established only by one of the following:  (1) provisions of a will or other instrument stating the material provisions of the contract.  (2) An express reference in a will or other instrument to a contract and extrinsic evidence proving the terms of the contract.  (3) A writing signed by the decedent evidencing the contract.  (4) Clear and convincing evidence of an agreement between the decedent and the claimant or a promise by the decedent to the claimant that is enforceable in equity.  (5) Clear and convincing evidence of an agreement between the decedent and another person for the benefit of the claimant or a promise by the decedent and another person for the benefit of the claimant or a promise by the decedent to another person for the benefit of the claimant that is enforceable in equity.”  (Prob. Code, §21700, subd. (a).)

            Defendants argue Plaintiff’s complaint “implicates” Probate Code section 21700 subdivision (a) based on Sinatra’s alleged statement that Plaintiff would have to obtain payment from his estate if his offer to make two payments of $5,000 was unacceptable.  Sinatra’s statements in response to Plaintiff’s rejection of the $5,000 offer does not transform the underlying agreement into a “contract to make a will or devise or other instrument.”  Defendants’ demurrer to the first cause of action based on Probate Code section 21700 subdivision (a) is overruled.

C.     The First Cause of Action for Breach of Contract Fails to Allege a Sufficiently Definite Oral Employment Agreement

Under California law the basic requirements for an enforceable contract are (1) parties capable of contracting, (2) the consent of those parties, (3) a lawful object, and (4) adequate consideration. (Civ. Code, § 1550.)  “The consent of the parties to a contract must be free, mutual, and communicated by each to the other.  The absence of mutual consent, i.e., the failure to reach a meeting of the minds on all material points, prevents the formation of a contract…Where a contract is so uncertain and indefinite that the intention of the parties in material particulars cannot be ascertained, the contract is void and unenforceable.  Although the terms of a contract need not be stated in the minutest detail, it must evidence a meeting of the minds upon the essential features of the agreement.  Whether a term is material or essential depends on its relative importance to the parties and whether its absence would make enforcing the remainder of the contract unfair to either party.”  (Santana v. Studebaker Health Care Center, LLC (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 1, 11–12.) 

“Under California law, a contract will be enforced if it is sufficiently definite (and this is a question of law) for the court to ascertain the parties' obligations and to determine whether those obligations have been performed or breached.  To be enforceable, a promise must be definite enough that a court can determine the scope of the duty, and the limits of performance must be sufficiently defined to provide a rational basis for the assessment of damages.  Where a contract is so uncertain and indefinite that the intention of the parties in material particulars cannot be ascertained, the contract is void and unenforceable.  The terms of a contract are reasonably certain if they provide a basis for determining the existence of a breach and for giving an appropriate remedy.  But if a supposed ‘contract’ does not provide a basis for determining what obligations the parties have agreed to, and hence does not make possible a determination of whether those agreed obligations have been breached, there is no contract.”  (Bustamante v. Intuit, Inc. (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 199, 209.) 

“A ‘material term’ is defined as a ‘contractual provision dealing with a significant issue such as subject matter, price, payment, quantity, quality, duration, or the work to be done.’”  (Westlands Water Dist. v. All Persons Interested (2023) 95 Cal.App.5th 98, 127 (quoting Black’s Law Dict. (11th ed. 2019), p. 1170, col. 2)(emphasis in the original).)  Thus, the failure to include an amount of repayment in a draft agreement to convert the parties’ water service contract into a repayment contract rendered the draft agreement too indefinite to be an enforceable agreement.  (Id. at 127-129.)  The amount of repayment was an essential and material term based on the statute governing such conversion agreements and the parties’ own testimony.  (Id.)  The draft agreement’s inclusion of the formula according to which repayment amount would be calculated did not resolve the uncertainty of the agreement, because the draft did not include an exhibit with information necessary to apply the formula.  (Id. at 129.)

Here, Plaintiff alleges that the parties were operating under a part-time employment agreement whereby Sinatra would pay Plaintiff $25 per hour and Plaintiff was not a live-in employee.  (Complaint, ¶6.)  Once Plaintiff agreed to become a live-in employee, Sinatra changed the method of payment—deposit into an unspecified CD rather than cash payment—but made no mention of the hourly wage.  (Id.)  Apart from Plaintiff’s live-in status, Plaintiff’s “round the clock” schedule and the method of payment, Plaintiff does not allege re-negotiation of any other term of the parties’ initial employment agreement.  On that basis, Plaintiff “assumed” he would be compensated at the same rate going forward.  (Complaint, ¶11.)

Plaintiff alleges multiple other new terms of the parties’ oral agreement as a result of Plaintiff’s live-in status.  Plaintiff alleges Sinatra allowed Plaintiff to live in his home, “rent free,” with all utilities, groceries and other household items paid for by Sinatra.  (Id. at ¶12.)  Plaintiff alleges Sinatra also agreed to allow Plaintiff to live in the house rent-free for six months after Sinatra’s death.  (Id. at ¶12.)

Defendants argue the alleged employment agreement fails because the parties never agreed on the amount of compensation.  Defendants argue parties never mutually agreed upon a payment term for Plaintiff’s live-in employment.  Defendants argue Plaintiff alleges the cessation of the existing $25/hr part-time employment agreement and the failure to replace that agreement’s payment terms. 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues he is entitled to plead in the alternative.  Plaintiff argues he alleges “he was paid $25 per hour, paid by Sinatra’s personal check, at the beginning, however, Sinatra apparently had a different idea about how much to pay him after Plaintiff had already moved all belongings into Sinatra’s house.”  (Opposition, 7:1-4.)  Plaintiff argues this does not render the agreement uncertain or unenforceable, because the payment terms can be supplied by the State and County’s minimum wage. 

Plaintiff fails to allege a sufficiently definite oral employment agreement.  Plaintiff fails to allege mutual agreement on an essential term of the employment agreement, the rate or amount of compensation.  Plaintiff alleges that he “assumed” $25/hr would continue to be his rate of payment for live-in services.  Plaintiff’s assumption does not establish mutual agreement, nor are there any factual allegations indicating that Sinatra agreed to $25/hr for Plaintiff’s live-in services.  The amount of compensation was never agreed upon and the factual allegations indicate that was a material term of Plaintiff and Sinatra.  Plaintiff does not claim the amount of compensation was merely an incidental or minor matter.  Without an agreed upon amount of compensation, it is impossible to determine whether Sinatra breached his obligations to Plaintiff.  A specific amount of compensation, such as an hourly wage or a lump sum, is particularly crucial given the existence of other substantial forms of compensation that did not exist before the parties’ live-in arrangement, including free room, board and utilities. 

Plaintiff’s opposition also fails to establish that he can cure the defect.  Plaintiff’s arguments that the Court can read-in an hourly rate based on minimum wage or other evidence would be pertinent to a quantum meruit claim.  The Court cannot cure the parties’ failure to mutually consent to a material contract term by unilaterally imposing a term on the parties.  At that point, the Court would be exercising its equitable power to provide an equitable remedy, which would be quantum meruit. 

Defendants’ demurrer to the first cause of action for breach of contract based on failure to plead a sufficiently definite, enforceable oral agreement is sustained without leave to amend.

D.    The Second and Third Causes of Action For Failure to Pay Minimum Wage and Failure to Pay Overtime are Sufficiently Stated and Not Uncertain

Defendants demur to the second and third causes of action for failure to pay minimum wage and failure to pay overtime on grounds.  Defendants ask that the Court disregard paragraphs 18 and 19 of the second cause of action, because they contain unsupported legal conclusions.  Defendants argue that without these allegations based on an inapplicable wage order, Plaintiff’s allegations of failure to pay minimum wage and failure to pay overtime are conclusory. 

Plaintiff’s allegations at paragraphs 18 and 19 are not subject to being stricken merely because they contain legal conclusions or allegations regarding California law.  Even if the Court were to disregard paragraphs 18 and 19, Plaintiff’s allegation that Sinatra failed to pay him minimum wage and overtime under California law is an allegation of fact that must be accepted as true on demurrer.  Plaintiff alleges he worked seven days a week for more than 9 hours a day, triggering the overtime rates.  (Complaint, ¶22.)  This is also a factual allegation that must be accepted as true.  The Second and Third causes of action are not so poorly drafted that Defendants cannot reasonably respond.  To the extent Defendants believe Plaintiff did not satisfy the requirements for overtime, Defendants are disputing the truth of the factual allegations, not their sufficiency.

Defendants’ Demurrer on grounds that the second and third causes of action fail to allege the elements of a failure to pay minimum wage claim and a failure to pay overtime claim is overruled. 

E.     The Fourth Cause of Action is Uncertain and Fails to State a Cause of Action

Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action is for “Failure to Keep Proper Payroll Records or to Classify Plaintiff Accurately as a Household Employee.”  Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action fails to allege any facts supporting a duty to “keep proper payroll records” or to “classify Plaintiff accurately as a Household Employee.”  As Defendants argue in opposition, this renders the fourth cause of action uncertain.  To the extent Plaintiff is seeking to assert a cause of action under the Labor Code, Plaintiff must allege the specific code section in order for Defendants to be able to reasonably respond. 

Defendants’ demurrer to the fourth cause of action for “Failure to Keep Proper Payroll Records or to Classify Plaintiff Accurately as a Household Employee” is sustained with 30 days leave to amend on grounds of uncertainty. 

F.      The Fifth Cause of Action for Waiting Time Penalties is Sufficiently Alleged

Defendants demur to the fifth cause of action on grounds that it relies on an alleged personal, oral, post death arrangement with Sinatra and causes of action that are insufficiently alleged.  Plaintiff alleges entitlement to waiting time penalties due to Defendants’ failure to pay minimum wage, overtime or any wages at all to him.  Plaintiff relies on Labor Code section 203 to calculate the penalties on his claim for unpaid minimum wage and overtime.  The second and third causes of action for failure to pay minimum wage and overtime are sufficiently alleged.  The derivative claim for waiting time penalties is also sufficiently alleged.

Defendants’ demurrer to the fifth cause of action for failure allege the elements of a waiting time penalties claim is overruled.

G.    The Sixth Cause of Action for Quantum Meruit is Sufficiently Alleged

“Quantum meruit refers to the well-established principle that the law implies a promise to pay for services performed under circumstances disclosing that they were not gratuitously rendered. To recover in quantum meruit, a party need not prove the existence of a contract, but it must show the circumstances were such that the services were rendered under some understanding or expectation of both parties that compensation therefor was to be made.”  (Chodos v. Borman (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 76, 96, citations and quotation marks omitted.) “[I]n order to recover under a quantum meruit theory, a plaintiff must establish both that he or she was acting pursuant to either an express or implied request for such services from the defendant and that the services rendered were intended to and did benefit the defendant.” (Day v. Alta Bates Medical Center (2002) 98 Cal.App.4th 243, 248.)

Defendants argue the sixth cause of action relies on the oral, post-death arrangement alleged in the first cause of action for breach of contract and must fail for that reason.  Plaintiff’s sixth cause of action does not rely on the contract alleged in the first cause of action.  Plaintiff’s sixth cause of action is based on Sinatra’s request that Plaintiff serve as his live-in caretaker until his death, which services Plaintiff rendered and which benefitted Sinatra.  (Complaint, ¶¶ 5, 33, 34.)  Plaintiff sufficiently alleges an equitable, quantum meruit claim arising from the parties’ agreement, which was insufficiently specific to be enforced as a contract.  (Parker, supra, 171 Cal.App.2d at 134 (plaintiff’s remedy was in quantum meruit for services rendered pursuant to agreement that was barred by statute of frauds).) 

Defendants’ demurrer to the sixth cause of action for quantum meruit based on failure to allege the elements of a quantum meruit claim is overruled.

H.    Plaintiff Fails to Allege Grounds to Assert Any of His Claims against Defendants for Sinatra’s Failure to Pay for Plaintiff’s Services

Defendants demur to the entire complaint on grounds that Plaintiff fails to allege any facts making them liable for services rendered to Sinatra pursuant to Sinatra’s request.  Defendants also argue Plaintiff improperly makes allegations as to “all Defendants,” which is improper given that each Defendant stands in a different position relative to Plaintiff and Sinatra.

In response, Plaintiff relies on Code of Civil Procedure section 377.40 to establish the Estate’s liability for Sinatra’s obligations to him.  “Subject to Part 4 (commencing with Section 9000) of Division 7 of the Probate Code governing creditor claims, a cause of action against a decedent that survives may be asserted against the decedent's personal representative or, to the extent provided by statute, against the decedent's successor in interest.”  (Code of Civ. Proc., §377.40.)  Plaintiff does not name Sinatra’s personal representative or successor-in-interest.  Plaintiff’s naming of “The Estate of Frank Anthony Sinatra, Deceased” is therefore improper under Code of Civil Procedure section 377.40 and insufficient to state a cause of action against decedent that survives his death.  Plaintiff also does not allege that the Trust is Sinatra’s successor-in-interest, nor does he claim that he can do so.  Plaintiff states in opposition that he has initiated a probate proceeding but this does not cure the failure to name a personal representative in the original complaint. 

Plaintiff also concedes that the Trust is, at best, a possible source from which to satisfy Sinatra’s obligations to him if the probate estate is inadequate.  (Objection to Demurrer, 13:5-8.)  Plaintiff relies on Probate Code section 19400, which provides “Subject to Section 366.2 of the Code of Civil Procedure, if there is no proceeding to administer the probate estate of the deceased settlor, and if the trustee does not file a proposed notice to creditors pursuant to Section 19003 and does not publish notice to creditors pursuant to Chapter 3 (commencing with Section 19040), then a beneficiary of the trust to whom payment, delivery, or transfer of the deceased settlor's property is made pursuant to the terms of the trust is personally liable, to the extent provided in Section 19402, for the unsecured claims of the creditors of the deceased settlor's probate estate.”  As Plaintiff states in his opposition, he has initiated probate and there is a probate proceeding pending at this time. 

Plaintiff fails to allege facts that would make the Trust liable for the Sinatra’s debt to Plaintiff.  Plaintiff also fails to allege that the Trust is the successor-in-interest of Sinatra, nor does he name Sinatra’s personal representative pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 377.40.  Plaintiff also concedes that he has initiated a probate proceeding to administer Sinatra’s estate.  Where a probate estate is opened after a decedent’s death, “a creditor of the deceased must first file a claim in the probate proceeding in order to recover.”  (Spears, supra, 97 Cal.App.5th at 1302.)  Plaintiff does not maintain that he can name the Trust as successor-in-interest given the current circumstances. 

Defendants’ demurrer based on failure to allege facts establishing Defendants’ liability for Sinatra’s obligations is SUSTAINED without leave to amend as to Defendant Trust, SUSTAINED WITH 30 DAYS LEAVE TO AMEND as to the Estate on the Second, Third, Fourth, Fifth and Sixth Causes of Action and SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND as to the First Cause of Action as to the Estate. 

CONCLUSION

Defendants’ demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend as to Defendant Trust, SUSTAINED WITH 30 DAYS LEAVE TO AMEND as to the Estate on the Second, Third, Fourth, Fifth and Sixth Causes of Action and SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND as to the First Cause of Action as to the Estate.  Defendants’ Motion to Strike is MOOT in light of the ruling on demurrer.

On February 22, 2022, Plaintiff Richard Niver filed this action against Defendants Knight Sunrise Hollywood LLC, Jimmy Ochoa, and US OPCO NOHO F Inc.

On June 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion for trial preference.  Defendants’ evidentiary objections are overruled.

A party who is over 70 years old may petition the court for a preference, which the court shall grant if the court makes both of the following findings: (1) the party has a substantial interest in the action as a whole; and (2) the health of the party is such that a preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing the party’s interest in the litigation.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 36, subd. (a).)  An affidavit submitted in support of a motion for preference under subdivision (a) of Section 36 may be signed by the attorney for the party seeking preference based upon information and belief as to the medical diagnosis and prognosis of any party.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 36.5.)  “The issue under subdivision (a) is not whether an elderly litigant might die before trial or become so disabled that she might as well be absent when trial is called.  Provided there is evidence that the party involved is over 70, all subdivision (a) requires is a showing that the party’s “health . . . is such that a preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing [her] interest in the litigation.  (Italics added.)”  (Fox v. Superior Court (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 529, 534 (Fox).)  “Subdivision (a) requires the granting of calendar preference to ‘prevent’ prejudice to a stricken litigant’s interests.  (Italics added.)  The idea that [plaintiff] should be made to wait to file a preference motion until she is clearly in her final days—when attendance at a trial is hardly what she should be doing—makes no sense at all.”  (Id. at p. 536.)

“Where a party meets the requisite standard for calendar preference under subdivision (a), preference must be granted.  No weighing of interests is involved.”  (Fox, supra, 21 Cal.App.5th at p. 535.)  Any inconvenience to the court or to other litigants is irrelevant and “[f]ailure to complete discovery or other pretrial matters does not affect the absolute substantive right to trial preference for those litigants who qualify for preference.”  (Swaithes v. Superior Court (1989) 212 Cal.App.3d 1082, 1085 (Swaithes).)

Plaintiff is 77 years old.  (Niver Decl. ¶ 2.)  Plaintiff suffered a stroke and was hospitalized from November 2-5, 2025, while still employed by Defendants.  (Niver Decl. ¶ 5; see Niver Decl. ¶ 8.)  Since then, Plaintiff “ha[s] been treated for and continue[s] to live with serious medical conditions, including the right internal carotid artery occlusion and carotid stenosis.”  (Niver Decl. ¶ 6.)  He has “persistent weakness on the left side of [his] body” and “difficulty with [his] memory, concentration, and processing things.”  (Niver Decl. ¶ 7.)  Plaintiff “often struggle[s] to find the right words,” “tire[s] easily and become[s] fatigued,” “ha[s] difficulty reliably using [his] cell phone,” “need[s] to take breaks,” and is “most reliable and clearheaded in the morning.”  (Niver Decl. ¶ 7.)  Plaintiff is “frightened that [he] will suffer another stroke.”  (Niver Decl. ¶ 9.)  He is concerned that a delay in the trial “creates a real risk that [his] health and ability to think clearly will decline to the point that [he] cannot effectively remember the events of [his] case, testify at trial, sit for a deposition, assist [his] attorney, or get through lengthy court proceedings.”  (Niver Decl. ¶ 10.)

Plaintiff’s counsel also declares that, based on Plaintiff’s medical records and clinical notes, Plaintiff’s “prognosis includes a substantially elevated risk of recurrent stroke and of cognitive and functional decline.”  (Bearman Decl. ¶ 7; see Bearman Decl., Exs. A-C.)

Defendants contend Plaintiff has not met his burden of proving a medical condition that makes trial preference necessary.  Section 36, subdivision (a) requires a determination that Plaintiff’s health is in such condition that a preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing the party’s interest in the litigation, not that there is some serious medical condition.  Plaintiff’s and counsel’s declarations are sufficient to prove Plaintiff’s medical diagnosis and prognosis.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 36.5.)  Additionally, the motion is accompanied by Plaintiff’s medical records.

Defendants also argue that they will be prejudiced by granting trial preference, and they “respectfully request that any order granting preference include: (a) a structured discovery schedule; (b) streamlined procedures to resolve discovery disputes; (c) an expedited briefing schedule for dispositive motions; and (d) regular case management conferences.”  (Opposition at p. 4.)

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s motion for trial preference.  Trial shall be set for no later than November 12, 2026.  This date is calculated as 120 days from July 14, 2026, plus one day for landing on the November 11, 2026 holiday.  (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 12, 12a, 12b.)
