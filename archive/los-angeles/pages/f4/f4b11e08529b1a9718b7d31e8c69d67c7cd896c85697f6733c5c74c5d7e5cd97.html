[TENTATIVE] RULING ON PETITION FOR WRIT OF MANDATE

 

MLT II, a Limited Liability Company (“Petitioner”) petitions for a writ of administrative mandate against respondent City (“Respondent”) directing it to set aside its decision to place the property at 3949 W. Los Feliz Blvd., Los Angeles, CA 90027 (“Property”) into the Rent Escrow Account Program (“REAP”).

The petition for writ of mandate is denied.

I.                Factual Background

In March 2023, Respondent’s Housing Department (“LAHD”) inspected the Property and found eleven violations, including illegal construction, exposed electrical wiring, inoperable fire doors, and debris accumulation. (AR 2425, 5228.) LAHD then issued an Order to Comply. (AR 12.) LAHD reinspected the Property on July 31, 2023, and found seven violations uncorrected. (AR 12.)

On June 17, 2024, LAHD issued Petitioner a Notice of Failure to Comply listing the remaining seven violations and advising Petitioner the Property may be referred to REAP. (AR 66-68.)

On April 3, 2025, LAHD issued a written decision to accept the Property into REAP and a 50% rent reduction applied to all units on the Property. (AR 81-85.)

On May 7, 2025, LAHD conducted an automatic General Manager (“GM”) Hearing for the Property’s entry into REAP. (AR 74, 5228.) At the hearing, LAHD Inspector Lee Miller (“Miller”) testified that four violations remained uncorrected. (GM Hearing Transcript at 5-6.)

On May 12, 2025, GM Hearing Officer Andre Brown (“Hearing Officer”) issued a written decision rejecting the appeal, affirming acceptance of the Property into REAP, finding that violations remained uncorrected. (AR 115-21.)

On May 27, 2025, Petitioner appealed the GM Hearing decision to the Rent Adjustment Commission (“RAC”) Appeals Board (“Board”). (AR  4094, 5232-33.) The Board modified the GM Hearing decision to reflect that only four violations still remained as testified by Miller, but otherwise upheld the decision. [1] (AR 5633)

II.             Procedural History

On October 14, 2025, Petitioner filed its petition. A proof of service filed October 23, 2025 shows Petitioner served the summons and petition on Respondent by personal service on October 17, 2025. On December 19, 2025, Petitioner filed its first amended petition. Proofs of service filed January 28, 2026 show that Petitioner served Real Parties in Interest (“RPIs”) [2] each with the petition by personal service, posting on the RPIs’ unit door, or substituted service on December 29, 2025. On January 13, 2026, Respondent filed its answer to the petition.

At the February 5, 2026 trial setting conference, Petitioner’s counsel indicated his intention to move for augmentation of the administrative record. The Court, apart from staying Petitioner’s 1983 cause of action, ordered the matter set for trial and provided a hearing date and briefing schedule.

On March 24, 2026, the Court signed the parties’ joint stipulation to adjust the briefing schedule, making the opening brief due on or before May 8, 2026; the opposition, on or before June 9, 2026; and the reply on or before June 18, 2026.

In keeping with the Court-approved joint stipulation, on May 8, 2026, Petitioner filed its opening brief.

Respondent served its opposition on Petitioner on June 9, 2026. However, due to a series of clerical errors, Respondent did not file its opposition until June 16, 2026, and that filing was not the finalized version. (Walford Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.) Respondent subsequently filed a corrected opposition on June 30, 2026. (Walford Decl., ¶ 7.) The corrected filing is identical to the version served on Petitioner on June 9, 2026, except for a corrected case number in the caption. (Walford Decl., ¶ 7.)

The Court exercises its discretion to consider both the opposition and the reply. The Court denies Petitioner’s request to strike Respondent’s opposition brief due to the June 16, 2026 filing irregularities, as the corrected opposition filed June 30, 2026 is substantively identical to the version served on Petitioner and presents no prejudice.

III.           Standard of Review

Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 1094.5 is the administrative mandamus provision that structures the procedure for judicial review of adjudicatory decisions rendered by administrative agencies. (Topanga Ass’n for a Scenic Community v. County of Los Angeles (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506, 514-15 [“Topanga”]).) Section 1094.5 does not specify which cases are subject to independent review, leaving that issue to the courts. (Fukuda v. City of Angels (1999) 20 Cal.4th 805, 811 [“Fukuda”]). In cases reviewing decisions that affect a vested, fundamental right, the trial court exercises independent judgment on the evidence. (Bixby v. Pierno (1971) 4 Cal.3d 130, 143 [“Bixby”]; see CCP § 1094.5(c).)

An agency is presumed to have regularly performed its official duties (Evid. Code § 664), and the petitioner therefore has the burden of proof on mandamus. (Steele v. Los Angeles County Civil Service Commission (1958) 166 Cal.App.2d 129, 137.) “[T]he burden of proof falls upon the party attacking the administrative decision to demonstrate wherein the proceedings were unfair, in excess of jurisdiction or showed prejudicial abuse of discretion.” (Alford v. Pierno, (1972) 27 Cal.App.3d 682, 691 [“Alford”].)

IV.           Evidentiary Matters

1.               Request for Judicial Notice

Respondent seeks judicial notice of (1) Los Angeles Municipal Code (“LAMC”) section 161.1004 and 162.00-162.13 (RJN Ex. 1); and (2) Rent Adjustment Commission Regulations (“RAC Regs.”) sections 1200.00-1200.16 (RJN Ex. 2). The requests are granted. (Evid. Code § 452(c).)

2.               Evidentiary Objections

Respondent objects to the Declaration of May Ling Yu (styled “Declaration of MLT II”) and an LAHD worklog pertaining to the Property (“Worklog”). Respondent asserts both are extra-record evidence not appropriate in an administrative mandamus action under CCP section 1094.5 The objections are sustained. [3]  

V.              Analysis

Petitioner seeks an order setting aside Respondent’s decision to place the Property into REAP. Petitioner argues that (1) Petitioner did not receive a fair hearing; (2) the findings were not supported by the evidence; and (3) the decision constituted an excessive fund under the Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution.

1.               Petitioner Received a Fair Hearing

The Court reviews fairness of the administrative proceeding de novo. (Nasha v. City of Los Angeles (2004) 125 Cal.App.4th 470, 482, 22 Cal.Rptr.3d 772.) “The statute’s requirement of a ‘“fair”’ trial means that there must have been ‘a fair administrative hearing.’¿” (Gonzalez v. Santa Clara County Dept. of Social Services (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 72, 96, 167 Cal.Rptr.3d 148.) The question whether the trial was “fair” encompasses pre-hearing and post-hearing events or procedures affecting the due process requirements of adequate notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard. (See Vollstedt v City of Stockton (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 265, 273.) Under Government Code section 11513(b), each party has the right to call and examine witnesses, cross-examine opposing witnesses, and impeach any witness regardless of which party called the witness.

The trial court’s independent evaluation of the fair trial issue is not a trial de novo; the court renders its independent judgment based on the record. (City of Fairfield v Superior Court, (1975) 14 Cal.3d 768, 776.) Here, the burden is on Petitioner to show that the administrative hearing was unfair.

a.                The GM Appropriately Closed the Record

Petitioner argues it did not receive a fair hearing because the GM closed the record prematurely, therefore denying Petitioner sufficient opportunity to demonstrate a wall it built around the mailbox area (“Security Wall”) was code compliant. Petitioner contends this action was despite the GM’s representation that the record would remain open for ten days after the GM Hearing (Pet. Op. Br. at 4-5, citing GM Hearing Transcript at 48:1-4) and a violation of the law (Pet. Op. Br. at 10).

In opposition, Respondent argues that the GM did not prematurely close the record. Specifically, Respondent argues that the Hearing Officer never promised to keep the record open, but rather the LAHD advocate proposed keeping the record open. (Opp. at 11, citing GM Hearing Transcript at 48:1-4.) Respondent further argues that RAC Regulations section 1200.09 required the GM to issue a written decision within ten days. (RAC Regs. § 1200.09.) Respondent contends that, even had the GM held the record open for ten more days, Respondent had not resolved the violations within that timeframe. (Opp. at 11, citing AR 4096-106.) Finally, Respondent argues Petitioner waived the issue by failing to raise the matter before the Board.

Petitioner does not address the matter on reply.

Respondent accurately characterizes the events of the GM Hearing. The transcript reflects that it was the LAHD advocate, not the Hearing Officer, who proposed that the record remain open for ten days. (GM Hearing Transcript at 47:25-48:4 [“[Miller:] The Department will recommend holding the record open ten days…”].) Nothing in the transcript or the remainder of the administrative record indicates that the Hearing Officer adopted that recommendation. Moreover, as Respondent correctly notes, holding the record open for an additional ten days would have contravened RAC Regulations section 1200.09, which requires issuance of a written decision within that same period. (RAC Regs. § 1200.09.) These points are dispositive, and the Court therefore need not reach Respondent’s remaining arguments.

Accordingly, Petitioner has not met its burden to show it did not receive a fair hearing due to the closure of the administrative record.

b.               Petitioner Had an Opportunity to Cross-Examine the Inspectors

Petitioner contends that it was denied an opportunity to confront the “unidentified individual” who determined the severity level of the violations. (Pet. Op. Br. at 13.) The administrative record, however, reflects which inspectors assigned the severity levels for the violations they identified. (See, e.g., AR 60.) Petitioner therefore could have called those inspectors to testify. Petitioner has not shown any resulting due process deficiency concerning its ability to confront witnesses.

2.               The Findings Were Supported by the Evidence

Petitioner argues that the findings regarding the Security Wall—namely, that it was an unpermitted construction, a fire code violation, and that the violations had high severity—were not supported by the evidence.[4]

“Substantial evidence” is relevant evidence that a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion (California Youth Authority v. State Personnel Board (2002) 104 Cal.App.4th 575, 585 [“California Youth Authority”]) or evidence of ponderable legal significance, which is reasonable in nature, credible and of solid value (Mohilef v. Janovici (1996) 51 Cal.App.4th 267, 305, n. 28). Substantial evidence can be the opinion of a single expert (Coastal Southwest Dev. Corp. v. Coastal Zone Conservation Comm’n (1976) 55 Cal.App.3d 525, 532), or opinions in a staff report (Griffin Dev. Co. v. City of Oxnard (1985) 39 Cal.3d 256, 261).

The Court considers all evidence in the administrative record, including evidence that detracts from evidence supporting the agency’s decision. (California Youth Authority at 585.) The Court must uphold the decision unless it concludes, based on the evidence before the agency, a reasonable person could not reach the conclusion reached by the administrative agency.¿(Harris v. City of Costa Mesa¿(1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 963, 969 [“Harris”].) Where “reasonable persons may differ,” the courts will not disturb the judgment of the administrative agency. (Breakzone Billiards v. City of Torrance (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1246.) The Court does not weigh evidence or decide who has the better argument and must resolve reasonable doubts in favor of the findings and decision. (Topanga, supra, 11 Cal.3d at 514.)

a.                The Unpermitted Construction Finding Was Supported by the Evidence

Petitioner contends the record does not support a finding that the Security Wall was an unpermitted construction, emphasizing that a permit application was submitted on November 5, 2024 and later finalized “at the earliest date available under LADBS’s schedule.” (Pet. Op. Br. at 8, citing AR 4098-99.)

Petitioner’s own evidence, however, establishes that the Security Wall was unpermitted at the time of the GM Hearing. Under LAMC section 91.103.1, “[n]o person shall…alter any building or structure…until after a permit therefor has been obtained.” A permit application is not a permit, and construction undertaken prior to permit issuance is unpermitted as a matter of law. The undisputed record shows the permit had not issued by the May 7, 2025 GM Hearing. (Pet. Op. Br. at 8; AR 4098-4099.) Contrary to Petitioner’s assertion (Reply at 5), the pendency of a permit application does not render existing work “permitted.” (LAMC § 91.103.1.)

Accordingly, substantial evidence supports the finding that the Security Wall constituted unpermitted construction.

b.               The Fire Code Violation Finding Was Supported by the Evidence

Petitioner next argues that the record does not support a finding that the Security Wall violated the Fire Code. Petitioner relies on LADBS’s notation “Permit Flag – Not a Fire Life Safety Project” on its permit application and asserts this reflects an LADBS determination that “no fire code issue was implicated.” (Pet. Op. Br. at 8-9, citing AR 4098.)

The record supports a different conclusion. Inspector Zepeda’s April 27, 2023 reinspection documented multiple violations, including unpermitted construction (LAMC §§ 91.103.1, 91.106.1.1, 91.8105, 161.902), exposed electrical wiring (LAMC §§ 93.0104, 93.0201, 93.0304, 93.0314), and fire door deficiencies (LAMC §§ 91.8104, 91.906.1). (AR 65.) Inspector Miller testified that, as of May 2, 2025, these violations had not been corrected. (GM Hearing Transcript at 6-7.)

Petitioner’s reply—that its permit was pending and “thus [the Security Wall] was not a violation of any code, including the fire code” (Reply at 7)—rests on a misinterpretation of both the permit application and the timing. The LADBS form Petitioner relies on is dated May 21, 2025, two weeks after the GM Hearing. (AR 4098, 5228.) And the “Permit Flag” merely reflects whether a fire/life safety plan review is required; it does not constitute an LADBS determination that the construction complies with fire safety requirements. (LAMC § 57.118.1.)

By contrast, the administrative record includes inspection reports (e.g., AR 14-25) and sworn testimony (GM Hearing Transcript at 6-8) supporting LAHD’s finding that the Security Wall violated the cited LAMC provisions. Petitioner identifies no evidence undermining those findings.

c.                The High Severity Finding Was Supported by the Evidence

Petitioner contends there was “no evidence whatsoever” supporting the “high” severity rating assigned to the violations. (Pet. Op. Br. at 10.) Respondent, however, identifies inspection materials, including the documents titled Severity Level Basis, that set forth the reasoning underlying each assigned severity level. (Opp. at 9-10, citing AR 60-61.)

Those inspection materials provide specific explanations for the high severity ratings. For example, the inspector noted that exposed wiring constitutes a “potentially life-threatening condition that may cause an electrical fire, electrocution, or electric shock,” that defective fire doors “increase[] the potential for fire and smoke to spread throughout the building,” and that unpermitted construction “could include structural hazards with life-threatening conditions that compromise the integrity of the building.” (AR 61.)

Petitioner’s assertion that these explanations represent a post hoc justification (Reply at 3-4) is unpersuasive. The statements appear in the administrative record as part of the contemporaneous inspection documentation and therefore constitute evidence properly considered under the substantial evidence standard.

On this record, a reasonable decisionmaker could conclude the violations warranted high severity ratings. Thus, substantial evidence supports the severity findings.

3.               The Decision Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment

Petitioner argues that REAP rent cancellations functionally impose an $800,000 fine for the Security Wall violation, rendering the REAP determination an excessive fine under the Eightl Amendment. Petitioner relies on Austin v. United States (1993) 509 U.S. 602 (“Austin”) for the proposition that when a forfeiture serves a punitive purpose, even if it also has remedial features, the forfeiture is subject to the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on excessive fines. (Pet. Op. Br. at 14, citing Austin at 608-10, 613.) Petitioner contends that “it should be undisputed that rent cancellations pursuant to REAP are penalties imposed for not resolving code violations, and are thus subject to the Eighth Amendment.” (Pet. Op. Br. at 15.)

Respondent counters that REAP rent reductions are not punitive but instead constitute a regulatory mechanism adjusting rent to reflect a reduction in habitability. (Opp. at 15.) [5] Petitioner does not address Respondent’s Eighth Amendment arguments in reply. (See Reply at p. 6.)

In Austin, the Supreme Court considered whether civil forfeiture of a mobile home and auto body shop used to facilitate drug transactions was subject to the Excessive Fines Clause. (Austin, supra, 509 U.S. at 604.) The Court held that a forfeiture is subject to Eighth Amendment scrutiny where it “can only be explained as serving in part to punish,” even if it also serves remedial purposes. (Austin, supra, at pp. 611-612.) In surveying traditional forfeitures, the Court observed that they have historically been justified either on the theory that the property itself is “guilty” or that owners may be properly punished for allowing unlawful use of their property. (Id. at 615.) The Court also noted that forfeiture historically was not imposed where the owner had done all that reasonably could be expected to prevent unlawful use. (Id. at 616.)

Applying those principles, the Court in Austin concluded that the statutory forfeitures at issue were punitive. The provisions included an “innocent owner” defense, a feature the Court found indicative of a focus on culpability rather than remediation. (Id. at 619.) The Court further emphasized legislative history expressly identifying deterrent and punitive aims. (Ibid.) Finally, the Court rejected the government’s characterization of the forfeiture as remedial, noting the forfeited property’s value had “absolutely no correlation to any damages sustained by society or to the cost of enforcing the law.” (Id. at 621, quoting United States v. Ward (1980) 558 U.S. 242, 254.)

Considering the reasoning of Austin, the Court finds Respondent’s view more persuasive. REAP applies only where LAHD determines that rental units are “untenantable.” (RAC Regs., § 1200.03.) The rent reduction is calculated based on the number and severity of violations affecting habitability. (RAC Regs., § 1200.07.) It is limited both by a minimum post-reduction rent and by a maximum percentage reduction. (Ibid.) Landlords may avoid rent reductions entirely by demonstrating that reductions would jeopardize tenant health and safety. (RAC Regs., § 1200.05.) REAP entry itself is avoided if the owner corrects all violations before the GM Hearing (RAC Regs., § 1200.09(F)), and removal from REAP is achieved by resolving outstanding orders. (RAC Regs., § 1200.13.)

This framework addresses conditions that impair habitability and encourages timely correction of violations. It ties the reduction to the actual effects of violations on tenants, limits the amount of any reduction, and provides a safe harbor when compliance is reached before the GM Hearing. These aspects demonstrate a regulatory scheme measured to compensate tenants for diminished habitability and to incentivize compliance, not to punish owners irrespective of culpability.

Accordingly, the Court concludes that REAP rent cancellation is not a punishment as understood in Austin, and is not subject to the Eighth Amendment limitation on excessive fines.

VI.           Conclusion

The Petitioner received a fair trial, Respondent’s decision is supported by substantial evidence, and the decision did not violate the Eighth Amendment. The petition for administrative mandamus is DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: APPLICATIONS FOR WRIT OF ATTACHMENT

 

Plaintiff Rahim Investments, LLC (“Plaintiff”) seeks writs of attachment against defendants Oro Extraction and Distro Services, L.L.C. (“Oro”), Distillation Ventures, LLC (“Distillation”) and Michael A. Hermosillo (“Hermosillo”) (collectively, “Defendants”).

The applications are granted.

I.                Background

1.     Factual Background

On or about February 10, 2022, Plaintiff loaned Defendants $500,000 (“Loan”) as evidenced by a promissory note (“Note”) dated the same. (Rahim Decl., ¶¶ 2-3, Ex. 1; Hermosillo Decl., ¶ 3) Plaintiff extended the Loan to provide “initial working capital needs” for the entity Defendants. (Rahim Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 1) Plaintiff delivered the Loan funds to Defendants by cashier’s check to Distillation. (Rahim Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 2.)

Hermosillo is the managing member of both Oro and Distillation. (Hermosillo Decl., ¶ 2.)

Plaintiff contends Defendants have not made any required payments under the Loan and Note, including the annual payments due February 28 of 2023, 2024, and 2025. (Rahim Decl., ¶ 5.) Defendants contend they made one $2,500 payment toward the Loan balance in October 2023, but agree they have not paid any annual true-up payments. (Hermosillo Decl., ¶¶ 4-5, Ex. 2.)

As a result, Plaintiff demanded late charges and opted to accelerate the loan. (Hermosillo Decl., ¶¶ 6-8.)

2.     Procedural History

On July 8, 2025, Plaintiff filed this action against Defendants. Proofs of service filed August 8 and 27, 2025 show Plaintiff served the summons and complaint on Defendants by personal service, Oro on August 5, 2025, Distillation on August 13, 2025, and Hermosillo on August 18, 2025. On November 3, 2025, Defendants filed their answer.

On May 19, 2026, Plaintiff filed two of the instant applications for writs of attachment, one against Distillation and one against Hermosillo. On May 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed the third instant application for a writ of attachment against Oro. On June 25, 2026, Defendants filed their opposition. On July 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed its reply.

II.             Applicable Law

“Upon the filing of the complaint or at any time thereafter, the plaintiff may apply pursuant to this article for a right to attach order and a writ of attachment by filing an application for the order and writ with the court in which the action is brought.” (CCP § 484.010.)

The application shall be executed under oath and must include: (1) a statement showing that the attachment is sought to secure the recovery on a claim upon which an attachment may be issued; (2) a statement of the amount to be secured by the attachment; (3) a statement that the attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based; (4) a statement that the applicant has no information or belief that the claim is discharged or that the prosecution of the action is stayed in a proceeding under the Bankruptcy Act (11 U.S.C. section 101 et seq.); and (5) a description of the property to be attached under the writ of attachment and a statement that the plaintiff is informed and believes that such property is subject to attachment. (CCP § 484.020.)

The application for a writ of attachment must be supported “by an affidavit showing that the plaintiff on the facts presented would be entitled to a judgment on the claim upon which the attachment is based.” (CCP § 484.030.)

The court shall consider the showing made by the parties, as well as the pleadings and other papers in the record. (CCP § 484.090(a), (d).) The Court shall issue a right to attach order if it finds all of the following:

“(1) The claim upon which the attachment is based is one upon which an attachment may be issued.

(2) The plaintiff has established the probable validity of the claim upon which the attachment is based.

(3) The attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based.

(4) The amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.”

(CCP § 484.090(a)(1-4).)

At the times prescribed by CCP section 1005, subdivision (b), the defendant must be served with a copy of the summons and complaint, notice of application and hearing, and a copy of the application and supporting affidavits. (CCP § 484.040.)

“The Attachment Law statutes are subject to strict construction….” (Epstein v. Abrams (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1168.)

III.           Analysis

1.     Claim on Which Attachment Can Be Based

“[A]n attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney’s fees.” (CCP § 483.010(a).) “An attachment may not be issued on a claim which is secured by any interest in real property arising from agreement ….” (CCP § 483.010(b).)

Here, Plaintiff’s claims against Defendants are premised on its cause of action for breach of contract. (Mem. at 3.) Defendants do not oppose on this ground. Plaintiff may seek attachment based on its breach claim.

2.     Contract Based on a Commercial Claim

If the action is against a defendant who is a natural person, an attachment may be issued only on a commercial claim which arises out of the defendant’s conduct of a trade, business, or profession. (CCP §483.010(c).) Consumer transactions cannot form a basis for attachment. (CCP §483.010(c); Kadison, Pfaelzer, Woodard, Quinn & Rossi v. Wilson (“Kadison”) (1987) 197 Cal.App.3d 1, 4 [action involving trust property was a commercial, not a consumer, transaction].)

Hermosillo is the principal of Oro and Distillation. (Hermosillo Decl., ¶ 2.) The purpose of the Loan was “to fund [Oro and Distillation’s] initial working capital needs”. (Rahim Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. 1; Hermosillo Decl., Ex. 1.) Hermosillo does not oppose on this ground. Accordingly, the claim against Hermosillo for breach of contract is a commercial claim arising out of Hermosillo’s business.

3.     Fixed or Readily Ascertainable Amount

A claim is “readily ascertainable” where the damages may be readily ascertained by reference to the contract and the basis of the calculation appears to be reasonable and definite. (CIT Group/Equipment Financing, Inc. v. Super DVD, Inc. (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 537, 540-41.) The fact that the damages are unliquidated is not determinative. (Ibid.) But the contract must furnish a standard by which the amount may be ascertained and there must be a basis by which the damages can be determined by proof. (Ibid.)

Here, Plaintiff and Defendants’ evidence are in alignment. Plaintiff extended the $500,000 Loan to Defendants. (Rahim Decl., ¶¶ 2-4, Exs. 1-2; Hermosillo Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 1.) Plaintiff contends in its moving papers that Defendants made no payments. (Rahim Decl., ¶ 5.) In opposition, Defendants contend they made one $2,500 payment toward the Loan balance in October 2023. (Hermosillo Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 2.) In reply, Plaintiff does not address this point beyond acknowledging Defendants claim to have made a $2,500 payment. (Reply at 2.) However, Plaintiff’s reply evidence appears to acknowledge that Defendants indeed made one $2,500 payment in October 2023 as they claim. (Zain Decl., Ex. 1.)

Plaintiff’s application seeks to attach only the $500,000 principal amount for the Loan and $50,000 for estimated fees. The estimated fees are supported by an attorney declaration and are reasonable. (Robinson Decl., ¶ 8.) Reducing that amount by $2,500 [1] for the documented (Hermosillo Decl., Ex. 2.) and acknowledged (Zain Decl., Ex. 1) payment, the readily ascertainable amount is $547,500.

Defendants do not oppose on this ground. The readily ascertainable amount is $547,500.

4.     Probable Validity of Plaintiff’s Claims

“A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.” (CCP § 481.190.) “If the defendant opposes the application, ‘the court must then consider the relative merits of the positions of the respective parties and make a determination of the probable outcome of the litigation.’ [Citations.]” (Pech v. Morgan (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 841, 855.)

In evaluating probable validity, the Court does not adjudicate the ultimate merits of the parties’ legal theories. The Court instead weighs the parties’ competing factual showings to determine, on balance, whether the applicant is more likely than not to obtain judgment. (See CCP §§ 481.190, 484.090.) The Court therefore assesses the credibility, internal consistency, and relative weight of the evidence, while avoiding any determination that would amount to a final merits ruling. The analysis below is made solely for the limited purpose of determining the “probable validity” of the applicant’s claim at this preliminary stage.

Here, the application is based on claims for breach of contract. To establish a claim for breach of contract, a plaintiff must prove: (1) existence of a contract; (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendant’s breach of the contract; and (4) damages incurred by plaintiff as a result of the breach. (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1367.)

Plaintiff and Defendants’ evidence shows that Plaintiff extended Defendants the $500,000 Loan as evidenced by the Note. (Rahim Decl., ¶¶ 2-4, Exs. 1-2; Hermosillo Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 1.) Defendants indisputably did not make the required annual $116,000 true-up payments. (Rahim Decl., ¶ 5; Hermosillo Decl., ¶ 5.)

In opposition, Defendant’s raise a usury defense, claiming the annual true-up payments constitute interest, and, including these true-up payments, the total interest rate is approximately 29% annually. (Opp. at 7-9.) In support of this interpretation of the Note, Defendants supply Plaintiff’s demand letter, which, inter alia, calculates that Defendants then owed Plaintiff $1,182,600.

In reply, Plaintiff argues the true-up payments are installment payments on the principal and interest, not additional interest payments.

Plaintiff’s arguments and evidence are more persuasive. While Zain Rahim’s AI-generated demand letter grossly miscalculated the amount due on the Loan (Zain Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. 1), the terms of the Note are unambiguous. Section 4 of the Note provides for Defendants’ repayment obligations. (Rahim Decl., Ex. 1; Hermosillo Decl., Ex. 1.) Section 4.1 provides for monthly profit installments, whereby Defendants agreed to pay 20% of their Adjusted Net Profit, defined elsewhere in the Note, each month until the balance of the Loan was paid. (Rahim Decl., Ex. 1; Hermosillo Decl., Ex. 1.) Section 4.2 provided for a minimum annual obligation of $116,000, and stated that Defendants must at the end of each year make a payment sufficient to make up the difference, if any between the minimum annual obligation and the actual amount paid in monthly profit installments. (Rahim Decl., Ex. 1; Hermosillo Decl., Ex. 1.) Section 4.3 provided that Defendants must pay the entire outstanding Loan balance, if any, on the Loan maturity date. (Rahim Decl., Ex. 1; Hermosillo Decl., Ex. 1.) Section 4.4 allowed that Defendants could prepay all or any portion of the Loan without penalty. (Rahim Decl., Ex. 1; Hermosillo Decl., Ex. 1.) Finally, Section 4.5 provided that payments would first apply to accrued unpaid late fees, second to accrued interest on the principal, and third to unpaid principal. (Rahim Decl., Ex. 1; Hermosillo Decl., Ex. 1.)

These are all the provisions in Section 4 and all provisions defining Defendant’s repayment obligations. Defendants’ contention that the true-up payments are interest payments beyond other interest described in the Note is not founded in the terms of the Note. Consequently, Plaintiff’s usury defense, at this stage and based on the evidence provided, fails. [2]

Accordingly, Plaintiff has shown the probable validity of breach of contract claim.

5.     Purpose of Attachment

The other required finding under CCP section 484.090 is that the “attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based.” (CCP § 484.090(a)(3).)

Plaintiff declares that “[a]ttachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on a claim upon which the attachment is based.” (App. ¶ 4.) Defendants do not oppose on this ground. Attachment is sought for proper purpose.

6.     Bankruptcy

CCP section 484.020(d) requires a “statement that the applicant has no information or belief that the claim is discharged in a proceeding under Title 11 of the United States Code (Bankruptcy) or that the prosecution of the action is stayed in a proceeding under Title 11 of the United States Code (Bankruptcy).”

Plaintiff provides this statement. (App. ¶ 5.) Defendants do not oppose on this ground. This requirement has been satisfied.

7.     Property Subject to Attachment

CCP section 487.010(a) states that, where the defendant is a corporation, all corporate property for which a method of levy is provided in CCP section 488.300 et seq. is subject to attachment.

Where the defendant is a natural person, the property to which attachment can apply are limited per CCP section 487.010(c).

As to Hermosillo, Plaintiff seeks property described as follows:

“Real property interest (except leasehold interests with unexpired terms of less than one year); accounts receivable, chattel paper and general intangibles arising out of Defendant’s trade, business, or profession (provided any has a minimum $150 principal balance); equipment and inventory; final money judgments arising out of Defendant’s conduct of a trade, business, or profession; cash on hand or in deposit accounts (but for $1,000 in the aggregate); negotiable documents of title, instruments and securities held by Defendant” (Apps. ¶ 9.)

Hermosillo does not oppose on this ground. This description is sufficient.

8.     Claims of Exemption

No claims of exemption are sought.

9.     Undertaking

CCP section 489.210 requires the plaintiff to file an undertaking before issuance of a writ of attachment. CCP section 489.220 provides, with exceptions, for an undertaking in the amount of $10,000. Defendants do not object to the amount of the undertaking. Accordingly, the Court will order an undertaking in the amount of $10,000.

IV.           Conclusion

Plaintiff’s applications for attachment against Defendants are GRANTED. The undertaking will be $10,000. See CCP §489.220. No writ shall issue against any defendant until Plaintiff posts the $10,000 undertaking for that defendant.

Within 3 court days of this order, Plaintiff is to file updated Right to Attach Orders reflecting the correct bench officer and amount of attachment, consistent with this ruling.
