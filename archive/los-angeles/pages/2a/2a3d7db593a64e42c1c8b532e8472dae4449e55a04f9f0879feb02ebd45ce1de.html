Case Name: Debreu, et al. v. The TCW Group, Inc., et al.

Case No.: 21STCV13868

Matter: Demurrer

Moving Party: Defendants Nippon Life Insurance Company and NLI US Investments,

Inc.

Responding Party: Plaintiffs Stanislas Debreu, individually and as co-trustee of The Debreu 

Family Living Trust Dated April 3, 2013, and Elizabeth Byrne Debreu, as Co-trustee of The Debreu Family Living Trust Dated April 3, 2013

Notice: OK

Ruling: The Demurrer is sustained, without leave to amend.


Moving party to give notice.


The Court encourages all parties to appear remotely via LA CourtConnect.  If submitting on the Court's tentative ruling, please follow the instructions provided above.


On April 28, 2022, Plaintiffs Stanislas Debreu, individually and as co-trustee of The Debreu Family Living Trust Dated April 3, 2013, and Elizabeth Byrne Debreu, as Co-trustee of The Debreu Family Living Trust Dated April 3, 2013, filed the operative Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) against Defendants The Carlyle Group, L.P., Carlyle Partners V, L.P., Carlyle Financial Services Harbor, L.P., Carlyle Partners V-A, L.P., CP V Coinvestment A, L.P., CP V Coinvestment B, L.P. and Carlyle-Clipper Coinvestment, L.P., David Lippman, Laird Landmann, Tad Rivelle, Meredith Jackson, TCW Group, Inc., TCW Owners, LLC, Clipper Holding, L.P., Clipper G.P., LLC, Nippon Life Insurance Company, NLI US Investments, Inc., and Jess M. Ravich for (1) constructive fraud, (2) conversion, (3) conspiracy to commit conversion, (4) unjust enrichment, (5) breach of written contract, (6) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and (7) declaratory relief.  

Plaintiffs “seek redress . . . for the theft and conversion of approximately $2.5 million, accomplished through a fraudulent scheme to strip Plaintiffs of contractual ‘Tag Along Rights’ that protected the value and liquidity of their minority partnership interests. The Tag Along Rights provided that whenever certain limited partners comprising a majority sold their partnership interests to a third-party, Plaintiffs had the option of selling their minority interests on the same terms, at the same price, and in the same proportions.”  (SAC ¶ 1.)

This matter returns to the Superior Court following a reversal by the Court of Appeal in appellate case number B324314. 

Defendants Nippon Life Insurance Company and NLI US Investments, Inc. now demur to the second and third causes of action for conversion and conspiracy to convert for failure to state sufficient facts. 

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, and “treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Serrano v. Priest (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591.)  “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.”  (Hahn v. Mirda¿(2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  It is error “to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if the plaintiff shows there is a reasonable possibility any defect identified by the defendant can be cured by amendment.”  (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist.¿(1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

As a threshold matter, the Court addresses the applicable law. The Court of Appeal's remand order is clear and binding: this Court "shall determine whether New York or California law, or a combination of the two, applies to the operative complaint" and "then determine whether each cause of action is sufficiently pleaded."

The disposition explicitly limits the choice to California and New York law. Delaware law may not be applied at the demurrer stage. Nippon Defendants' argument that Delaware law controls is foreclosed by the appellate mandate. When an appellate court remands with specific directions, those directions are binding on the trial court and must be followed. Any material variance from the directions is unauthorized and void. The trial court is empowered to act only in accordance with the direction of the reviewing court.

As between California and New York law, the Court finds California law governs Plaintiffs' tort claims against the Nippon Defendants. The Nippon Defendants are not parties to the 2013 Management Security Holders Agreement ("2013 MSA"), which contains a New York choice-of-law provision. A contractual choice-of-law clause does not typically bind non-signatories. The Court of Appeal noted that "not all the defendants are parties to the 2013 MSA, and it is unclear whether they would be subject to the New York choice of law provision."

California applies the governmental interest analysis to tort claims. The relevant contacts point decisively to California: Plaintiffs are California residents residing in Los Angeles County; the alleged wrongful conduct was conceived and executed at TCW's Los Angeles headquarters; the Individual Defendants lived and worked in Los Angeles County; and the injury was suffered in California. California has a strong interest in applying its conversion and conspiracy law to protect its residents from tortious conduct committed within its borders. New York has no meaningful connection to these tort claims—no challenged conduct occurred there and no injury was felt there. Accordingly, the Court applies California law to analyze the tort claims against the Nippon Defendants.

The elements of a conversion claim are: (1) the plaintiff's ownership or right to possession of the property; (2) the defendant's conversion by a wrongful act or disposition of property rights; and (3) damages.  (Burlesci v. Petersen (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 1062, 1066.)

Plaintiffs argue that their Original Clipper partnership units were "forcibly converted" into TCW Owners LLC units of inherently less value via a "sham merger" designed to bypass Tag-Along rights.

This claim fails as a matter of law. When an entity merges or restructures, equity interests are modified, exchanged, or diluted by operation of corporate law. Conversion requires the wrongful taking or total expropriation of a specific, identifiable property interest (See Voris v. Lampert (2019) 7 Cal.5th 1141, 1151.) Where the core grievance is that a restructuring left plaintiffs holding equity that is diluted, constrained, or of inherently less value because key contractual privileges (Tag-Along rights) were removed, the law categorizes the injury as an economic loss governed by breach of contract or breach of fiduciary duty against the proper defendants. Tag-Along Rights are contractual incidents of ownership—they do not constitute the equity interest itself but rather contractual protections regarding how that interest may be sold in certain circumstances. The 2013 MSA granted Significant Management Holders the right to participate pro rata in third-party sales by majority investors. Debreu's claim is not that he was prevented from selling his units altogether. Rather, he alleges that his Class A-2 limited partnership units were converted into membership units in TCW Owners LLC through the merger; the new membership units lacked the tag-along protections previously attached to his Class A-2 units; and as a result, when the 2013 Carlyle Investors sold their interests, he could not participate on the same terms.

This sort of diminution in the value of equity interests through corporate transactions does not state a conversion claim. Conversion is the wrongful exercise of dominion over specific, identifiable property—not a remedy for loss of asset value or component benefit. Indeed, “a mere contractual right of payment, without more, will not suffice.” (Farmers Ins. Exch. v. Zerin (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 445, 452.)

The Court finds that the conversion claim fails because Plaintiff failed to allege a valid property interest that was interfered with for the purposes of this tort. 

Because the underlying conversion claim fails, so too does the derivative third cause of action for conspiracy to convert. 

In sum, the Demurrer is sustained, without leave to amend.

The RJN is granted as to Exhibits 1 and 7; the RJN is otherwise denied.

Case Name: Perez, et al.  v. Vernon Rock Investments LLC, et al.

Case No.: 23STCV15614

Matter: Motion to Enforce Settlement

Moving Party: Plaintiffs Maria Perez, et al.

Responding Party: Defendant Vernon Rock Investments LLC

Notice: OK

Ruling: The Motion is denied.


Moving party to give notice.


The Court encourages all parties to appear remotely via LA CourtConnect.  If submitting on the Court's tentative ruling, please follow the instructions provided above.


Plaintiffs Maria Perez, et al. seek to enforce their settlement with Defendant Vernon Rock Investments, LLC in which Defendant is required to tender $40,000 to Plaintiffs. 

The Court is authorized to enter judgment pursuant to a stipulated settlement.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 664.6.)  In reviewing a motion to enforce a settlement, the Court determines “whether the parties entered into a valid and binding settlement.”  (Hines v. Lukes (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1174, 1182.)  “A settlement is enforceable under section 664.6 only if the parties agreed to all material settlement terms.  [Citations.]  The court ruling on the motion may consider the parties’ declarations and other evidence in deciding what terms the parties agreed to, and the court’s factual findings in this regard are reviewed under the substantial evidence standard.  [Citations.]  If the court determines that the parties entered into an enforceable settlement, it should grant the motion and enter a formal judgment pursuant to the terms of the settlement.”  (Id. at pp. 1182-1183; see also Osumi v. Sutton (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 1355, 1357 [“Strong public policy in favor of the settlement of civil cases gives the trial court, which approves the settlement, the power to enforce it”].)

Plaintiffs contend that 6 weeks after there was an initial agreement, Defense counsel stated he did not have authority to settle the case.

The singular issue is whether Defendant authorized the settlement. “[A]bsent express authority, it is established that an attorney does not have implied plenary authority to enter into contracts on behalf of his client.” (Wilson v. Eddy (1969) 2 Cal.App.3d 613, 618; Blanton v. Womancare, Inc. (1985) 38 Cal. 3d 396, 407.)

Defendant contends that “During our telephone discussions, we discussed a wide range of numbers to resolve the matter. . . . I stated only that an amount in the range of $40,000 would be considered as part of a settlement. On the other hand, we also discussed offers of less than $10,000. A wide range of numbers were discussed. 6. I did not intend any statement to authorize my counsel to transmit a firm, unconditional $40,000 settlement offer. 7. I understood that any settlement offer would need to be further discussed with me before my counsel sent it out in final form. 8. I did not learn of the firm offer until May 18, 2026.” (Hasan Decl. ¶¶ 5-8.) 

Defense counsel states, “Based on those conversations, I mistakenly understood that I had authority to communicate a settlement offer at $40,000 on the terms reflected in my May 11, 2026 email.”

After the May 11, 2026, offer, Defense counsel stated on May 18, 2026, that he was “working” on a long-form agreement. There was then apparently silence until June 26, 2026, when defense counsel stated to Plaintiff’s counsel that: “My client alleges that I did not have authority from him to make the settlement offer. He is not going to sign. I have been trying for the last few weeks to deal with the client on this issue but the client will not budge. It makes for a very tenuous situation. We will need to re-sent the trial date.”

Plaintiffs contend there was an agreement in principle and that the 6-week delay in communications tends to show a lack of credibility.

Based on the evidence, the Court believes there was never any settlement agreement. Attorneys require express consent to settle a case. The timeline appears consistent in that on May 18, 2026, Defense counsel states he is “working on it”, then later that same day the client learns of the unauthorized offer, and then Defense counsel spends several weeks trying to deal with the client, leading to counsel breaking his silence on June 26, 2026, that there was never any authorization. Had defense counsel continued to send emails after May 18, 2026, indicating that a draft was forthcoming that would have indicated a lack of credibility; however, such emails do not exist.

Because there was never any settlement agreement, the Motion is denied.

This is a personal injury action.

Defendant Andranik Margaryan seeks to compel further responses to his special interrogatories, form interrogatories, and request for production from Plaintiff.

The Motions as to special and form interrogatories are granted. These interrogatories seek basic information about Plaintiff’s damages and injuries. The objections lack merit, and the responses are evasive and incomplete.

Plaintiff is sanctioned $1,000, payable within 30 days.

Case Number: 26STCV01649    Hearing Date: July 31, 2026    Dept: 310

Tentative Ruling

Judge Kevin C. Brazile

Department 310

Hearing Date: July 31, 2026

Case Name: Yu v. Chung, et al.

Case No.: 26STCV01649

Matter: Demurrer

Moving party: Defendants Scott Youngu Chung, Sara Hansol Chung, SCJY, Inc., and 

CHJJ, Inc

Responding party: Plaintiff Tim Yu

Ruling: The Demurrer is sustained in part, with 20 days leave to amend.


The Motion to Strike is granted, without leave to amend.


Moving party to give notice.


The Court encourages all parties to appear remotely via LA CourtConnect.  If submitting on the Court's tentative ruling, please follow the instructions provided above.


On May 20, 2026, Plaintiff Tim Yu filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) for (1) breach of contract, (2) conversion, (3) breach of fiduciary duty, (4) fraud, (5) concealment, and (6) negligent misrepresentation. Plaintiff alleges that he invested $260,000 in a restaurant business but received no benefit—Defendants actually used the funds for their own personal gain and then reneged on a promise to repurchase Plaintiff’s shares.

Defendants Scott Youngu Chung, Sara Hansol Chung, SCJY, Inc., and CHJJ, Inc. now demur to the FAC for uncertainty and failure to state sufficient facts. 

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, and “treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Serrano v. Priest (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591.)  “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.”  (Hahn v. Mirda¿(2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  It is error “to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if the plaintiff shows there is a reasonable possibility any defect identified by the defendant can be cured by amendment.”  (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist.¿(1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

As an initial matter, the Demurrer of CHJJ, Inc. is sustained in its entirety, with 20 days leave to amend. The FAC fails to allege any facts with respect to CHJJ.

The first cause of action for breach of contract fails because the FAC fails to plead all material terms for a contract. The contract at issue is supposedly the promise to repurchase the $260,000 in shares, but this alone is not a contract and, among other things, lacks consideration. The Demurrer is sustained as to the first cause of action, with 20 days leave to amend.

A cause of action for conversion of money requires an allegation of a specific, identifiable sum belonging to the plaintiff; a generalized claim for unliquidated profits or money owed under an accounting is insufficient (Voris v. Lampert (2019) 7 Cal.5th 1141, 1159; Kim v. Westmoore Partners, Inc. (2011) 201 Cal.App.4th 267, 284). Plaintiff alleges converted property consists generally of "profits, shares, commission, and other cash owed" (FAC ¶ 55). Because Plaintiff has not alleged a specific sum, the conversion claim fails. Leave to amend is granted.

Next, corporate officers and directors owe fiduciary duties to shareholders. Plaintiff adequately alleges an ongoing corporate relationship and fiduciary capacity as to individual Defendants Scott Youngu Chung and Sara Hansol Chung (FAC ¶¶ 19–22, 38, 46–47, 58). Thus, the demurrer is overruled as to Defendants Scott Chung and Sara Chung. However, a corporation does not owe a fiduciary duty to its own shareholders; therefore, the demurrer is sustained, with 20 days leave to amend, as to corporate Defendants SCJY, Inc. and CHJJ, Inc.

Fraud and negligent misrepresentation must be pleaded with heightened particularity, establishing who made what misrepresentation, when, where, and how (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.) The FAC lacks this particularity. The Demurrer is sustained as to the fourth and sixth causes of action, with 20 days leave to amend.

Finally, as to concealment, Plaintiff adequately alleges that Defendants solicited and accepted Plaintiff's investment funds while intentionally concealing their true intent to divert those funds for their own personal gain. Taking these factual allegations as true for purposes of demurrer, Plaintiff has stated a viable claim for concealment.

In sum, the Demurrer of CHJJ is sustained, with 20 days leave to amend. The Demurrer of the remaining Defendants is sustained in part as set forth herein, with 20 days leave to amend.

Lastly, Defendants’ Motion to Strike the reference to “Bernie Madoff” is granted as this reference is irrelevant and prejudicial. Leave to amend is denied.

Moving party to give notice.
