DEPARTMENT 415 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV07815    Hearing Date: August 5, 2026    Dept: 415

    TENTATIVE RULING

 

DEPARTMENT

415

HEARING DATE

August 5, 2026

CASE NUMBER

25STCV07815

MOTION

Demurrer to First Amended Complaint

MOVING PARTY

Defendant General Motors LLC

OPPOSING PARTY

Plaintiff Eleazar Martinez Mora

 

MOTION

Plaintiff Eleazar Martinez Mora (“Plaintiff”) brough the instant action against Defendant General Motors LLC (“Defendant”), alleging he bought a defective motor vehicle Defendant manufactured.  Defendant demurs to the fourth and fifth causes of action in Plaintiff’s first amended complaint.  Plaintiff opposes the demurrer.

ANALYSIS

“It is black letter law that a demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.”  (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.)  In ruling on a demurrer, the court must “liberally construe[]” the allegations of the complaint.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)  “This rule of liberal construction means that the reviewing court draws inferences favorable to the plaintiff, not the defendant.”  (Perez v. Golden Empire Transit Dist. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1228, 1238.)

Defendant relies extensively on trial court orders. “Neither federal district court procedures nor decisions are binding on this court.” (Thomas G. Ferruzzo, Inc. v. Superior Court (1980) 104 Cal.App.3d 501, 503.)  Likewise, “a written trial court ruling has no precedential value.”  (Santa Ana Hospital Medical Center v. Belshe (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 819, 831.)  The Court disregards these citations.

Fourth Cause of Action: Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability

Defendant argues the statute of limitations bars Plaintiff’s claim for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability.  The statute of limitations on a breach of warranty claim for consumer goods is four years.  (Com. Code, § 2725, subd. (1).)  The breach of an implied warranty accrues upon delivery of the goods, regardless of when the buyer discovers the breach.  (Cardinal Health 301, Inc. v. Tyco Electronics Corp. (2008) 169 Cal.App.4th 116, 129 (Cardinal Health).) “[U]nder [Commercial Code]  section 2725, the general limitations rule for a breach of warranty cause of action is four years from the date the goods are delivered (regardless of the date the buyer discovers the breach), unless the ‘warranty explicitly extends to future performance of the goods and discovery of the breach must await the time of such performance.’”  (Ibid.; see also Carrau v. Marvin Lumber & Cedar Co. (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 281, 291 [“[t]he vast majority of the courts ... have concluded that the ‘future performance’ exception to the four-year limitations period applies only when the seller explicitly has agreed to warrant its product for a defined period of time. These courts reason that California Uniform Commercial Code section 2725 reflects the drafters' intention to establish a reasonable period of time—four years—beyond which sellers need not worry about stale warranty claims”].)  

The Court notes, in his amended complaint, Plaintiff alleges he did not suspect his vehicle was defective beyond repair until shortly before filing this action.  (First Amended Complaint, ¶ 33.)  However, the breach of an implied warranty accrues upon delivery of the goods, regardless of when the buyer discovers the breach.  (See Cardinal Health, supra, 169 Cal.App.4th at p. 134 [“Because an implied warranty is one that arises by operation of law rather than by an express agreement of the parties, courts have consistently held it is not a warranty that ‘explicitly extends to future performance of the goods’”].)

Plaintiff alleges he received the vehicle at issue on March 14, 2020.  (First Amended Complaint, ¶ 6.)  As concerning his implied warranty claim, Plaintiff thus had until March 14, 2024 to file the complaint in this action.  But Plaintiff did not do so until March 19, 2025. 

In its December 3, 2025 order, the Court allowed Plaintiff to amend this cause of action.  But Plaintiff has not appreciably amended the complaint to cure the deficiencies in the pleading with respect to the fourth cause of action.  Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the fourth cause of action for being time-barred and concludes it is futile to permit Plaintiff to further amend the claim. 

Fifth Cause of Action: Fraud By Concealment  

On the other hand, the Court cannot resolve Defendant’s argument the fifth cause of action is time-barred on demurrer.  

In this regard, as noted above, Plaintiff alleges he did not suspect the vehicle was defective beyond repair until shortly before filing this action.  Under the delayed discovery rule, a claim does not accrue until “a plaintiff knew or should have known of the wrongful conduct at issue.”  (April Enterprises, Inc. v. KTTV (1983) 147 Cal.App.3d 805, 832.)  Further, with respect to the fraud claim, the statute of limitations did not run while Defendant represented it could repair the defects with Plaintiff’s vehicle.  (See Cardinal Health, supra, 169 Cal.App.4th at pp. 133–134.)  The Court cannot resolve the evidentiary issue of when Plaintiff knew or should have known of the claims against Defendant on demurrer.

However, as Defendant points out, Plaintiff has not adequately alleged the claim for fraud because Plaintiff must allege fraud with particularity, which, as explained below, Plaintiff has not yet done.  (Wilhelm v. Pray, Price, Williams & Russell (1986) 186 Cal.App.3d 1324, 1331.)  “There are four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.” (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336, internal quotations and citations omitted.) 

In this regard, Plaintiff alleges Defendant knew of the defect in the engine of the vehicle because it had exclusive knowledge of information not known to the public, and Defendant, nonetheless, concealed this defect from Plaintiff. (First Amended Complaint, ¶¶ 77, 82-83.)  However, a claim for fraud must result from “direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.”  (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 312.)  Plaintiff has yet to allege specific facts to show he directly purchased the vehicle from Defendant, such that Defendant concealed these facts from him.  Accordingly, the demurrer to the fifth cause of action is sustained, but Plaintiff will be given the opportunity to amend it.

 The Court notes, in opposing the motion, Plaintiff cites OCM Principal Opportunities Fund, L.P. v. CIBC World Markets Corp. (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 835 (OCM Principal), which is not on point.  In that case, an investment bank made statements in an offering memorandum for a company’s promissory notes, which were misleading due to the bank’s failure to disclose other pertinent facts.  The Court concluded the bank had a duty to disclose the company’s “circumstances to potential purchasers.”  (Id. at p. 860.)  In this case, however, Plaintiff does not allege Defendant “ma[de] partial representations but also suppress[ed] some material facts.”  (Id. at p. 859, internal quotations and citations omitted.)  Instead, Plaintiff asserts Defendant had superior and exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to Plaintiffs. (Complaint, ¶¶ 77, 82-83.)   Therefore, Plaintiff’s’ claims are premised on a distinct theory of liability, and OCM Principal is not relevant.

Likewise, Plaintiff’s reliance on Bader v. Johnson & Johnson (2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 1094 (Bader) is misplaced.  As the Court of Appeal noted in Bjoin v. J-M Manufacturing Co., Inc. (2025) 113 Cal.App.5th 884 (Bjoin), the dicta in Bader “is too broad and vague to constitute a standard which is applicable in other cases.” (Bjoin, supra, 113 Cal.App.5th at p. 904.)

As discussed above, in his fifth cause of action, Plaintiff has not established through his allegations the threshold facts regarding Plaintiffs’ interactions with Defendant, such that Defendant concealed material facts from him.  Therefore, the demurrer to this cause of action is sustained.  However, Plaintiff will be permitted one more opportunity to amend this claim because the first amended complaint “does not on its face foreclose any reasonable possibility of amendment.” (City of Stockton v. Superior Court (2007) 42 Cal.4th 730, 747.)

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant’s demurrer to the fourth cause of action is sustained without leave to amend.  The demurrer as to the fifth cause of action is sustained, but Plaintiff will have a further opportunity to amend it.  Plaintiff is to file an amended complaint within 20 days of notice of this order.

Defendant is ordered to provide notice of the Court’s ruling and to file proof of service of same.

 


Case Number: 25STCV36742    Hearing Date: August 5, 2026    Dept: 415

    TENTATIVE RULING

 

DEPARTMENT

415

HEARING DATE

August 5, 2026

CASE NUMBER

25STCV36742

MOTION

Motion to Compel Arbitration

MOVING PARTIES

Defendants Downtown LA Law Group LLP, Farid Yaghoubtil, Daniel Azizi, and Salar Hendizadeh

OPPOSING PARTY

Plaintiff Sereen Banna

 

MOTION

              Defendants Downtown LA Law Group LLP (“Downtown LA Law Group”), Farid Yaghoubtil, Daniel Azizi, and Salar Hendizadeh (collectively, “Defendants”) move to compel arbitration of the claims Plaintiff Sereen Banna (“Plaintiff”) asserts against Defendants.  Plaintiff opposes the motion. 

Plaintiff also filed an amended complaint.  Plaintiff had the right to amend the complaint without leave of court before Defendants responded to the complaint.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 472.)  But Plaintiff waited until after Defendants filed this motion to compel arbitration on February 26, 2026 to file the amended complaint on July 22, 2026, two weeks before the scheduled hearing concerning the instant motion.  Plaintiff was not required to seek leave of court to file the amended complaint because Defendant had not yet filed an answer, demurrer, or motion to strike.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 472.)    

ANALYSIS

The moving party on a petition to compel arbitration “bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence, while a party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense.  The trial court sits as the trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, and any oral testimony the court may receive at its discretion, to reach a final determination.”  (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 842, internal quotations and citations omitted.) 

In support of their motion, Defendants have submitted an arbitration agreement Plaintiff signed when Plaintiff worked for Downtown LA Law Group.  The arbitration agreement provides, in relevant part, Plaintiff and Downtown LA Law Group and any of Downtown LA Law Group’s “related entities, holding companies, parents, subsidiaries, divisions, officers, shareholders, directors, employees, agents, venders, contractors, doctors, patients, insurers, predecessors, successors, and assigns” agree to “submit[] to final and binding arbitration” “any controversy, claim or dispute” which “relat[es] to or aris[es] out of [Plaintiff’s] employment, or the cessation of that employment . . . .”  (Declaration of Amirah Rezkallah in Support of Motion to Compel Arbitration, Exhibit 1, p. 1.)  Accordingly, Defendant has met its burden to show an agreement to arbitrate covers Plaintiff’s claims. 

The arbitration agreement Plaintiff signed specifies the Federal Arbitration Act governs.  (Rezkallah Decl., Exhibit 1, p. 2.)   A choice of law provision in an arbitration agreement is enforceable.  (Coopers & Lybrand v. Superior Court (1989) 212 Cal.App.3d 524, 538.)  As such, the Federal Arbitration Act applies. 

Plaintiff argues the Ending Forced Arbitration of Sexual Assault and Sexual Harassment Act of 2021 (EFAA), which became law on March 3, 2022, bars arbitration of Plaintiff’s claims.  This law amends the FAA to provide, “at the election of the person alleging conduct constituting a sexual harassment dispute or sexual assault dispute, or the named representative of a class or in a collective action alleging such conduct, no predispute arbitration agreement . . . shall be valid or enforceable with respect to a case which is filed under Federal, Tribal, or State law and relates to the sexual assault dispute or the sexual harassment dispute.”    (9 U.S.C. § 402(a).) 

For purposes of this law, a sexual assault dispute is “a dispute involving a nonconsensual sexual act or sexual contact . . . , including when the victim lacks capacity to consent . . . ,” and a sexual harassment dispute is “a dispute relating to conduct that is         alleged to constitute sexual harassment under applicable Federal, Tribal, or State law.  (9 U.S.C. § 401(3)-(4).)  The law applies to “any dispute or claim that arises or accrues on or after the date of enactment of this Act.”  (9 U.S.C. § 401 note.)  Plaintiff’s claims arose after her employment with Downtown LA Law Group commenced in December 2023.  (First Amended Complaint, ¶ 22.)  Accordingly, if appropriate, the EFAA may apply to this case. 

However, in the first amended complaint, Plaintiff does not assert a claim for sexual harassment.  Instead, Plaintiff asserts a claim for harassment based on national origin and religion.  (See First Amended Complaint, ¶ 100.)  To state a claim for sexual harassment, Plaintiff must allege Defendants subjected Plaintiff to sexual propositions, unwarranted graphic discussion of sexual acts, commentary on the employee's body and the sexual uses to which it could be put, expressly or impliedly conditioning employment upon acceptance of a supervisor’s unwelcome sexual advances, or the creation of a hostile environment for an employee because of that employee's sex.  (Mogilefsky v. Superior Court (1993) 20 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1414-1415.) 

In her first amended complaint, Plaintiff tersely alleges Defendants terminated Plaintiff’s employment because of Plaintiff’s sex or gender, which was a substantial motivating factor for her termination.  (First Amended Complaint, ¶¶ 89, 90.)  That may constitute discrimination against Plaintiff “in compensation or in terms, conditions, or privileges of employment” (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (a)) but not harassment.  Accordingly, the Court concludes EFAA does not bar arbitration of Plaintiff’s claims.

Plaintiff argues the arbitration agreement is not enforceable pursuant to California’s requirements for arbitration of employment claims.  As set forth above, the arbitration agreement Plaintiff signed specifies the FAA governs, which is an enforceable choice of law provision.   Under the FAA, defenses which derive their meaning from the fact an agreement to arbitrate is at issue cannot invalidate agreements to arbitrate.  (AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion (2011) 563 U.S. 333, 339 (AT&T Mobility).)  The rules for arbitration of employment claims under California law are specific to arbitration agreements.  A court may invalidate an arbitration agreement based on “generally applicable contract defenses” like fraud or unconscionability, but not on legal rules that “apply only to arbitration or that derive their meaning from the fact that an agreement to arbitrate is at issue.”’” (Viking River Cruises, Inc. v. Moriana (2022) 596 U.S. 639, 650.)  These rules cannot invalidate the subject arbitration agreement.  Therefore, it is enforceable.

Plaintiff also argues the arbitration agreement does not apply to all Defendants.  As set forth above, the arbitration agreement applies to Downtown LA Law Group and its “related entities, holding companies, parents, subsidiaries, divisions, officers, shareholders, directors, employees, agents, venders, contractors, doctors, patients, insurers, predecessors, successors, and assigns . . . .”  (Rezkallah Decl., Exhibit 1, p. 1.)   It, therefore, applies to all Defendants in this case.  (Dryer v. Los Angeles Rams (1985) 40 Cal.3d 406, 418.)

Unconscionability

Plaintiff contends the arbitration agreement is unconscionable.  Pursuant to Civil Code section 1670.5, subdivision (a), “If the court as a matter of law finds the contract or any clause of the contract to have been unconscionable at the time it was made the court may refuse to enforce the contract, or it may enforce the remainder of the contract without the unconscionable clause, or it may so limit the application of any unconscionable clause as to avoid any unconscionable result.”  Unconscionability is defined as “an absence of meaningful choice on the part of one of the parties together with contract terms which are unreasonably favorable to the other party.”  (A & M Produce Co. v. FMC Corp. (1982) 135 Cal.App.3d 473, 486 (A & M Produce).)  Unconscionability thus has a procedural and a substantive element.  (Ibid.)  

The Court considers the factors of oppression and surprise in determining whether a contract is procedurally unconscionable.  (A & M Produce, supra, 135 Cal.App.3d at p. 486.)  Oppression refers to inequality of bargaining power, which cuts off meaningful negotiation.  (Ibid.)  Surprise refers to the concealment of the agreed-upon terms in a lengthy form drafted by the party seeking to enforce them.  (Ibid.)  Substantive unconscionability refers to the extent to which a contractual term reallocates the risks of a transaction “in an objectively unreasonable or unexpected or unexpected manner.”  (Id. at 487.)  Both substantive and procedural unconscionability must be present for a contract to be unenforceable, and “the greater the unfair surprise or inequality of bargaining power, the less unreasonable the risk allocation which will be tolerated.”  (Ibid.)

The evidence before the Court indicates Downtown LA Law Group prepared the arbitration agreement, and Plaintiff had no opportunity to negotiate or discuss the terms of the arbitration agreement.  (See Declaration of Sereen Banna, ¶¶ 4-8.)  It is, therefore, a contract of adhesion.  (Neal v. State Farm Ins. Companies (1961) 188 Cal.App.2d 690, 694.)  This is some evidence of procedural unconscionability.  (Flores v. Transamerica HomeFirst, Inc. (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 846, 853.)   While Plaintiff argues the incorporation by reference of rules for arbitration into the agreement is some evidence of surprise, it is not, because the arbitration agreement specifically identifies the applicable rules and the source thereof. 

Furthermore, while Plaintiff has demonstrated some procedural unconscionability, Plaintiff has not demonstrated the arbitration clause is substantively unconscionable.  Plaintiff’s contention in this regard is based on rules which apply specifically to arbitration agreements.  As discussed above, because the FAA applies in this instance, the Court cannot invalidate the arbitration agreement at issue based on rules which apply specifically to arbitration agreements.  (AT&T Mobility, supra, 563 U.S. at p. 339.)  Accordingly, Plaintiff fails to show the arbitration clause is substantively unconscionable.  Thus, the arbitration clause is not unenforceable due to unconscionability. 

Plaintiff further argues Defendants failed to require him to sign a new arbitration agreement when Downtown LA Law Group re-hired her.  That is immaterial.  By its terms, the agreement Plaintiff signed applies to claims which “relat[e] to or aris[e] out of [Plaintiff’s] employment, or the cessation of that employment . . . .”  (Rezkallah Decl., Exhibit 1, p. 1.)  This clause did not expire when Downtown LA Law Group re-hired Plaintiff. 

Plaintiff also contends the date on the arbitration agreement does not match the declaration Defendants filed in support of the motion.  However, Plaintiff admits to signing the arbitration agreement.  (See Banna Decl., ¶ 7.)  As the authenticity of the agreement is not in dispute, any mismatch of the dates is irrelevant. 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendants’ motion to compel Plaintiff to arbitrate is granted.  This case is stayed pending arbitration.  The Court sets an order to show case regarding status of arbitration to be heard on May 5, 2027.

Defendants are ordered to provide notice of the Court’s ruling and to file proof of service of same.
