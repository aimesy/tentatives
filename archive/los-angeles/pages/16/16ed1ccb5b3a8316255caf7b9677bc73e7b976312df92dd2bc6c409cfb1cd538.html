DEPARTMENT 516 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 22STCV12527    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 516

Hearing Date:             July 27, 2026 

Case Name:                 Gerry Burk, Trustee of the 5721 Trust, et al. v. Triple Images, LLC, et al.

Case No.:                    22STCV12527

Matter:                        Motion for Leave to Allow Plaintiff Gerry Burk to Represent 5721 Trust

Moving Party:             Plaintiff Gerry Burk

Responding Party:       None

 

Tentative Ruling:      The hearing on Plaintiff Gerry Burk’s motion for leave is continued until

                                    August 26, 2026.

 

 

            Plaintiff Gerry Burk, as individual and trustee for 5721 Trust (“Plaintiffs”), filed this action against Defendants Triple Images, LLC also known as Triple Image Cosmetic, LLC, Bezad Kahoolyzadeh also known as Bezad Cohen Aka David Cohen, Tessfaye Kinfe, also known as Tess Kinfe, Michael Goland, all persons unknown, claiming any legal or equitable right, title, estate, or interest in the property adverse to plaintiff’s interest, or any cloud on Plaintiff’s title to that property and Does 1-100.

 

            Plaintiff Gerry Burk, in his individual capacity, moves for leave to file a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”). No opposition was filed.

 

Legal Standard

 

            The court may, in furtherance of justice, allow a party to amend any pleading upon any terms as may be proper. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 473, subd. (a), 576.) Courts liberally grant leave to amend based on a strong policy favoring the resolution of all disputes between parties in the same case. (Nestle v. Santa Monica (1972) 6 Cal.3d 920, 939; Morgan v. Superior Court (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 527, 530.) Thus, requests for leave to amend will generally be granted unless the party seeking to amend has been dilatory in bringing the proposed amendment, and the delay will cause prejudice to the opposing party if leave to amend is granted. (Hirsa v. Superior Court (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 486, 490; see also Armenta ex rel. City of Burbank (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 636, 642 [“instances justifying the court’s denial of leave to amend are rare.”].) Absent prejudice, delay alone is insufficient to deny leave to amend. (Higgins v. Del Faro (1981) 123 Cal.App.3d 558, 564-565.) 

 

            A party requesting leave to amend must state what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted and added, as well as specify where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the changes are located.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a)(1)-(3).) The moving party must also attach the proposed amended pleading with a declaration by counsel, describing (1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) why the request was not made earlier.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(b)(1)-(4).)   

 

Analysis

 

            Improper Service

 

            Code of Civil Procedure section 1005, subdivision (b) states that moving papers “shall be served and filed at least 16 court days before the hearing.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1005, subd. (b).)

 

            Plaintiff Gerry Burk filed his motion on July 6, 2026. In accordance with Code of Civil Procedure section 1005, subdivision (b), Plaintiff was required to file and serve his motion by July 2, 2026. No opposition was filed by any Defendant; thus, it is also likely that the motion was not timely served on Defendants.

 

            Thus, Plaintiff Gerry Burk ’s motion is improperly served.

 

            Plaintiff Gerry Burk ’s motion will be continued to allow Plaintiff to properly serve his motion on Defendants according to the rules of California Civil Procedure.

           

Conclusion

 

            Plaintiff Gerry Burk’s motion for leave to file an amended Complaint is continued until August 26, 2026.

 


Case Number: 24STCV30845    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 516

Hearing Date:             July 27, 2026

Case Name:                 Park v. Kia America, Inc., et al.

Case No.:                    24STCV30845

Matter:                        Motion for Attorney Fees

Moving Party:             Plaintiff Jongsul Park

Responding Party:       Defendant Kia America, Inc.

 

Tentative Ruling:      Plaintiff Jongsul Park’s motion is granted in part and denied in part.

 

 

            Plaintiff Jongsul Park (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendant Kia America, Inc. (“Defendant”) and Does 1 through 10. The Complaint alleged five causes of action against Defendant for: (1) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (d); (2) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b); (3) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3); and (4) breach of express written warranty under Civil Code sections 1791.1, and 1794; and (5) breach of implied  warranty under Civil Code sections 1791.1, and 1794. The Complaint alleges that on December 30, 2023, Plaintiff purchased the Subject Vehicle, a 2023 Kia Carnival vehicle identification number KNDNB4H38P6243956.

 

            Plaintiff filed a motion for attorneys' fees. Kia opposes.

 

Legal Standard

 

            Where the buyer of a vehicle prevails in an action under the Song-Beverly Act, he “shall be allowed by the court to recover . . . a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney's fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred . . . .” (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).) 

 

            “[T]he starting point of every fee award . . . must be a calculation of the attorney's services in terms of the time he has expended on the case. Anchoring the analysis to this concept is the only way of approaching the problem that can claim objectivity, a claim which is obviously vital to the prestige of the bar and the courts.”¿(In re Vitamin Cases (2003) 110 Cal. App. 4th 1041, 1058, [citation omitted].) “[T]he primary method for establishing the amount of ‘reasonable’ attorney fees is the lodestar method. The lodestar (or touchstone) is produced by multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended by counsel by a reasonable hourly rate.”¿(Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2001) 92 Cal. App. 4th 819, 833 (Thayer).) Under this approach, a base amount is calculated from a compilation of time reasonably spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 48; Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 639 (Serrano).) A fee request that appears unreasonably inflated is a special circumstance that permits the trial court to reduce the award or deny it altogether. (Serrano, supra, 32 Cal. 3d at p. 635.)

 

Request for Judicial Notice

            Plaintiff requests that the Court take judicial notice of nine court records in the following cases: (1) Fuller v. FCA US LLC (L.A. Super. Ct. Case No. BC556964); (2) Caplan v. FCA US LLC (L.A. Super. Ct. Case No. BC580793); (3) Vanwaus v. FCA US, LLC (L.A. Super. Ct. Case No. BC591282); (4) Noormohammad et al. v. BMW of North America, Inc. (San Bernardino Super. Ct. Case No. CIVDS1717263); (5) Zayas v. FCA US LLC (Orange County Super. Ct. Case No. 30-2019-01053549-CU-BC-CJC); (6) Galligan v. General Motors, LLC (Orange County Superior Ct. Case No. 30-2019-01045846-CU-BC-CJC); (7) Cechvala, et al. v. FCA US, LLC (San Diego Superior Ct. Case No. 37-2018-00010099-CU-BC-CTL); (8) Markarian v. FCA US LLC (Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 23CHCV00247); and(9) Prasad v. General Motors, LLC (Sacramento Superior Court Case No. 34-2023-00333314-CU-BC-GDS).

 

            The Court grants Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice pursuant to Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (d).

 

Analysis

 

            Plaintiff requests fees in the amount of $31,342.50 in attorney’s fees, plus $5,000 for Plaintiff to read Defendant’s opposition and file a reply.  

 

            Reasonableness of Plaintiff’s Counsel’s Rates

 

            “A prevailing party has the burden of showing that the fees incurred were reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation, and were reasonable in amount.” (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 817–818 (Robertson).) “[T]he lodestar is the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community; it may be adjusted by the court based on factors including, as relevant herein, (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132 (Ketchum).)

 

             Plaintiff requests that the Court accept Counsel Benjeman Beck’s (“Beck”) hourly rates from $550 to $600. (Beck Decl., ¶ 31.) Plaintiff also requests that the Court accept Counsel Misoo Choi’s (“Choi”) hourly rate from 550 to $575. (Choi Decl., ¶ 4.) The Court finds that Plaintiff has presented sufficient evidence to support the hourly rates requested for her attorneys Beck and Choi. However, as to legal assistant Kimberly Santos's (“Santos”) hourly rate, Plaintiff provides no evidence or argument that her $150 hourly rate is reasonable. To the Court’s knowledge, the hourly rate is unreasonable and excessive. The Court will impose a reduction to Santos’ hourly rate from $150 to $100.

 

           Thus, the Court accepts Beck and Choi’s Hourly rate, but imposes a reduction of Santos hourly rate from $150 to $100. The Court imposes a total reduction of $1,725.00.

           

            Reasonableness of Plaintiff’s Counsel’s Hours        

 

            The Court must base the attorneys’ fees on the “actual time expended on the case

 as long as such fees are reasonably incurred—both from the standpoint of time spent and the amount charged.” (Robertson, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at p. 817.) The Court must ascertain “whether under all the circumstances of the case the amount of actual time expended and the monetary charge being made for the time expended are reasonable.” (Ibid.)

 

            Plaintiff contends that her attorneys and staff spent a total of 71.5 hours to litigate her case. The breakdown for each attorney is as follows:

 

·         Beck 32.3

·         Choi: 13.7

·         Santos: 34.5

 

            In opposition, Defendant argues that the amount of hours requested is unreasonable and excessive because the need for two attorneys was unnecessary, Plaintiff’s counsel double billed, and Plaintiff seeks fees that have not been earned without supporting the request as to the present motion.

 

           As an initial matter, Plaintiff does not present the Court with any argument that her case was novel or complex that would justify the amount of hours requested. The case also lasted just under a year, and no substantial motions were filed in this action, or vehicle inspection. While Plaintiff is not limited to the use of only one attorney, Plaintiff is still required to meet her burden to establish that two attorneys were reasonable and necessary to litigate her case. Here, Plaintiff contends that both attorneys were necessary to communicate with her because she is a native Korean speaker, so Choi handled the client relationship while Beck litigated the case. (Beck Supp. Decl., ¶ 3.) However, correspondence between Beck and Choi is not a recoverable fee; thus, the Court will strike $1,615.00 from the total amount.

 

           As for Santos, Plaintiff has not presented sufficient basis to support the need for the 34.5 hours spent by Beck’s legal assistant. Plaintiff argues that the Court should accept the hours requested because a lower rate was used to perform clerical tasks instead of her attorney’s higher rates. However, this does not meet Plaintiff’s burden. The Court finds that a $1,293.75 reduction is warranted and reasonable for the hours worked.

 

           In opposition, Defendant also argues that Plaintiff conducted discovery after Plaintiff received the settlement agreement on August 11, 2025. Plaintiff argues that she had an ongoing obligation to comply with discovery. However, it is unreasonable for Plaintiff to request the Court grant fees for an unreasonable expenditure of hours to conduct discovery, and not serve responses. Thus, the Court will impose a $2,687.00 reduction.

 

           In reply, Plaintiff contends that her attorney spent $3,180 of the $5,000 anticipated fees on this motion. Thus, the Court will impose a $1,820 reduction.

 

           Therefore, the Court will impose a total reduction of $7,415.75.

 

Conclusion

 

            Plaintiff’s motion for attorneys' fees is granted in part and denied in part. Plaintiff is

awarded fees in the total amount of $29,021.75.

 

 


Case Number: 25STCV09944    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 516

Hearing Date:             July 27, 2026 

Case Name:                 Crist, et al. v. LA Colima 2010 LLC, et al.

Case No.:                    25STCV09944

Matter:                        Demurrer with Motion to Strike  

Moving Party:             Defendants LA Colima 2010 LLC and Apartment Management Consultants, LLC

Responding Party:       Plaintiffs Wesley Crist and Marlena Whisner

 

Tentative Ruling:      Defendants LA Colima 2010 LLC and Apartment Management Consultants, LLC’s demurrer is sustained.

 

                                    Defendants LA Colima 2010 LLC and Apartment Management Consultants, LLC’s motion to strike is granted.

 

 

            Plaintiffs Wesley Crist and Marlena Whisner (“Plaintiffs”) filed this action against Defendants LA Colima 2010 LLC and Apartment Management Consultants, LLC (“Defendants”) and Does 1 through 100. The First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleges five causes of action for: (1) breach of contract; (2) breach of implied warranty of habitability; (3) nuisance; (4) intentional infliction of emotional distress; and (5) negligence.

 

            Defendants demur to Plaintiffs’ fourth cause of action and moves to strike punitive damages and prayer for attorney’s fees . Plaintiffs oppose.

 

Legal Standard

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice.¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)¿The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading by raising questions of law.¿(Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.)¿“In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)¿The court “ ‘ “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law . . . .” ’ ”¿ (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.)¿In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated.¿(Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.) 

            “The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: (a) Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (b) Strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.”¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) “Immaterial” or “irrelevant” matters include allegations not essential to the claim, allegations neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint. (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subds. (b)(1)-(3).) 

Analysis

 

            Meet and Confer

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, “a demurring party is required to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or video conference with the party that filed the pleading. . . .” (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41, subd. (a).) A moving party in a motion to strike must meet and confer before filing the motion. (Code Civ. Proc. §435.5, subd. (a).)

 

            Defendants contend that the parties met and conferred in accordance with statute. (Frankel Decl., ¶ 2.) However, the parties did not fulfill their meet and confer obligations. Defendants declare that on June 3, 2026, they sent written correspondence to Plaintiffs. (Id. at ¶ 3.) Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that the parties meet and confer “in person, by telephone, or video conference.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41, subd. (a).) Thus, the parties did not fulfill their meet-and-confer obligations. In the interest of judicial efficiency, the Court will address the merits of Defendants’ demurrer and motion to strike.

 

            Demurrer

 

            Defendants demur to Plaintiffs’ fourth cause of action for failure to allege sufficient facts to constitute a cause of action.

 

                        Fourth Cause of Action-Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

 

            “The elements of a prima facie case of intentional infliction of mental distress are (1) outrageous conduct by the defendant, (2) intention to cause or reckless disregard of the probability of causing emotional distress, (3) severe emotional suffering and (4) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress.” (Newby v. Alto Riviera Apartments (1976) 60 Cal.App.3d 288, 295.) “Behavior may be considered outrageous if a defendant (1) abuses a relation or position which gives him power to damage the plaintiff's interest; (2) knows the plaintiff is susceptible to injuries through mental distress; or (3) acts intentionally or unreasonably with the recognition that the acts are likely to result in illness through mental distress.”) (Ibid.) “A defendant's conduct is “outrageous” when it is so “‘“extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.”’”” (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1050–1051.)

 

            Defendants argue that Plaintiffs fail to allege any conduct by Defendants that would constitute extreme and outrageous. (Mot., 6:8-9.) The FAC alleges that:

 

45. The conduct of defendants as herein alleged was egregious, extreme and outrageous and done with reckless disregard of the likelihood to inflict severe emotional distress. Specifically, the defendants have completely failed in their duty as a landlord to maintain the SUBJECT PROPERTY and have allowed slum living conditions to persist which spread infection, sickness and disease.

 

46. The defendants’ conduct was, reckless, and or intentional and malicious, and done for the purpose of causing Plaintiff to suffer humiliation, mental anguish and emotional distress.

 

(FAC, ¶¶ 45-46.)

 

Defendants cite to Hughes to argue that Plaintiffs’ allegations do not meet the high bar to allege extreme and outrageous conduct. However, the Court finds Defendants' reliance on Hughes inapplicable because in  Hughes, the Court addressed intentional infliction of emotional distress in the context of sexual harassment. (Hughes, supra, 46 Cal.4th at p. 1051.) The present dispute between the parties concerns allegations regarding condition of the “Subject Property” owned by Defendants and leased by Plaintiffs.

 

            In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that they have alleged sufficient facts for a viable cause of action per Stoiber. In Stoiber, the Court did address whether a tenant may state a cause of action in tort against a landlord for failure to keep the premises in a lawful state of habitability, which the court found that a tenant could “by pleading proper facts”.  In Stoiber, the Court reversed thetrial court ruling on the demurrers and judgments on the pleadings. (Stoiber v. Honeychuck (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 903, 932.)

 

            However, the Court is persuaded by Defendants’ argument that the FAC lacks allegations of sufficient facts including extreme and outrageous conduct by Defendants. Plaintiffs have not presented the Court with any legal authority that the allegations of the FAC are sufficient to survive demurrer as to the fourth cause of action, nor does the Court find that Plaintiffs’ allegations are sufficient.

 

            Thus, the demurrer as to the fourth cause of action is sustained.

 

            Motion to Strike

 

            Defendants move to strike the following portions of Plaintiffs’ FAC: (1) ¶ 21; (2) ¶ 32; (3) ¶ 42; (4) ¶ 46; (5) ¶ 48; (6) prayer for punitive damages; and (7) prayer for attorney’s fees.

 

                        Punitive Damages

 

            To state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a complaint must set forth the elements as stated in the general punitive damage statute, Civil Code section 3294. (College Hospital, Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 721.) These statutory elements include allegations that the defendant has engaged in oppression, fraud, or malice. (Civ. Code § 3294, subd. (a).) “‘Malice’ means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Civ. Code, § 3294 subd. (c)(1).) Punitive damages thus require more than the mere commission of a tort. (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166 (Grieves).)

 

            The claim for punitive damages may not rest on conclusory allegations of oppression, fraud, or malice; instead, it must be supported by factual allegations that warrant such a conclusion. (Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal. App. 4th 1033, 1041-1042. (Court of Appeal issued peremptory writ directing trial court to issue order striking plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages because “[t]he sole basis for seeking punitive damages are … conclusory allegations” which were “devoid of any factual assertions supporting a conclusion [defendants] acted with oppression, fraud or malice.”)

 

            Defendants argue that Plaintiffs’ FAC does not contain any allegations that warrant punitive damages because Plaintiffs’ rely on conclusory allegations. Defendants also argue that even taking the allegations as true, lack of timely maintenance does not constitute despicable behavior or conscious disregard.

 

            In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that punitive damages are warranted because Plaintiffs’ counsel has tried similar cases where the jury has awarded punitive damages. (Opp., 3:27-28.) However, this is not a legal argument to prevail in Defendants’ motion to strike. Plaintiffs cite to thirteen cases, but these cases do not support Plaintiffs argument that the FAC alleges sufficient facts in the case before the Court to prevail on the motion to strike. As addressed above, the question is not whether in some cases, a tenant can pursue punitive damages against their landlord. The question before the Court is whether sufficient allegations have been made to support a claim for punitive damages. The FAC does not meet that requirement. Plaintiff makes conclusory statements, and these conclusory statements are not sufficient to support a request for punitive damages.

 

            Thus, the Court grants Defendants’ motion to strike punitive damages as to each paragraph and prayer.

 

                        Attorney’s Fees

 

            “California follows the “American rule,” under which each party to a lawsuit ordinarily must pay his or her own attorney fees.” (Musaelian v. Adams (2009) 45 Cal.4th 512, 516.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 1021 codifies the rule, providing that the measure and mode of attorney compensation are left to the agreement of the parties “[e]xcept as attorney's fees are specifically provided for by statute.” (Ibid.)

           Defendants argue that Plaintiffs do not allege any statutory or contractual basis to support a claim for attorney’s fees. Defendants argue that Exhibit A, attached to the FAC, is from a different unit. However, no exhibit is attached to the FAC.

 

            In opposition, Plaintiffs do not address Defendants' argument.

 

            In the FAC presented before the Court, there is no basis for a claim for attorney’s fees.

 

            Thus, the Court grants Defendants’ motion to strike attorney’s fees.

 

Leave to Amend

 

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that the pleading can be successfully amended. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

 

            In the opposition to the demurrer, Plaintiffs state, “[I]F the court finds any defects in the Complaint for Plaintiffs respectfully request leave to amend.”  The burden is on the Plaintiffs to show the Court that the pleading can be successfully amended. The opposition to the demurrer and motion to strike do not support granting leave to amend. The Court will give Plaintiffs an opportunity to argue whether to grant leave to amend.

 

Conclusion

 

            Defendants LA Colima 2010 LLC and Apartment Management Consultants, LLC’s demurrer is sustained as to the fourth cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress.

 

            Defendants LA Colima 2010 LLC and Apartment Management Consultants, LLC’s motion to strike is granted in its entirety.

 

 

 

 


Case Number: 25STCV12550    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 516

Hearing Date:             July 27, 2026

Case Name:                 Douroux v. The Pep Boys Manny Moe & Jack of California LLC

Case No.:                    25STCV12550           

Motion:                       Motion to be Relieved as Counsel

Moving Party:             Counsel for Plaintiff

Opposing Party:          None

 

Tentative Ruling:      The Motion to be Relieved as Counsel is denied.     

 

            Plaintiff’s counsel –Molly E. Hoot- seeks to be relieved as counsel of record for Plaintiff John Douroux. No opposition was filed.      

 

Discussion

 

            An attorney is entitled to withdraw upon the consent of the client, or without that consent if approved by the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 284; Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 904, 915.) In the latter case, counsel must make a motion to be relieved as attorney of record.  The motion must be made using mandatory forms: Notice of Motion and Motion to be Relieved as Counsel – Civil (MC-051), Declaration (MC-052), and Proposed Order (MC-053). (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (a), (b), (e).)

 

            The declaration accompanying the motion to be relieved as counsel must state “in general terms and without compromising the confidentiality of the attorney-client relationship why a motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 284(2) is brought instead of filing a consent under Code of Civil Procedure section 284(1).” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (b).)

 

            The notice to the client must be delivered by personal service, electronic means, or mail. If it is done via mail under Code of Civil Procedure section 1013, it must be accompanied by a declaration confirming the service address to be the most current residence or that it is the client’s last known address, and the attorney has been unable to locate a more current address. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (d)(1)(A)-(B).) If the notice is served on the client by electronic service under Code of Civil Procedure section 1010.6 and rule 2.251, it must be accompanied by a declaration stating that the electronic service address is the client's current electronic service address. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (d)(2).)

 

            Here, Counsel Molly E. Hoot (“Counsel”) has complied with the Rules of Court by filing forms MC-051, MC-052, and MC-053.  

 

Counsel states that there has been a breakdown in the attorney-client relationship because Plaintiff has failed to respond to Counsel’s communication.

 

However, there is also ambiguity in relation to whether Counsel has confirmed Plaintiff’s address to be served. Counsel declares that she confirmed Plaintiff’s address through online research and also that she is unable to confirm Plaintiff’s address. (MC-052) Thus, service is not in accordance with California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362(d).

 

              Thus, the Court denies Counsel’s motion to be relieved as counsel.
