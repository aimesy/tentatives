DEPARTMENT 515 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 23STCV31247    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion for Summary Judgment

The Motion for Summary Judgment is denied. The alternative Motion for Summary Adjudication is granted as to the third, fourth, fifth, sixth, tenth, and eleventh causes of action and is otherwise denied.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Joseph Palladino (Plaintiff) filed this action against RTX Corporation (RTX), Raytheon Company (Raytheon), and Pablo Gibu (Gibu) (collectively, Defendants), alleging that Defendants discriminated against him because of his age and a shoulder condition, harassed him, and retaliated against him during his final years as an opto-mechanical technician at Raytheon’s El Segundo facility.

The causes of action are: (1) Age Discrimination; (2) Age Harassment; (3) Disability Discrimination; (4) Disability Harassment; (5) Failure to Provide Reasonable Accommodation; (6) Failure to Engage in the Interactive Process; (7) Failure to Prevent Discrimination, Harassment, and Retaliation; (8) Retaliation in Violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act; (9) Whistleblower Retaliation; (10) Retaliation for Reporting Unsafe Conditions; (11) Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy; and (12) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress. The Complaint also prays for punitive damages.

Defendants filed a Motion for Summary Judgment. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

On August 4, 2026, the Court continued the hearing and ordered supplemental briefing on the timeliness of the second, third, fourth, fifth, and sixth causes of action. Plaintiff filed a Supplemental Opposition.

LEGAL STANDARD

A defendant moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication bears the burden of persuasion that one or more elements of the cause of action cannot be established, or that a complete defense to it exists. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.) The moving defendant bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing that no triable issue of material fact exists. Once the defendant carries that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to make a prima facie showing that a triable issue exists. (Id. at pp. 850-851.) The Court considers all of the evidence and all reasonable inferences from it in the light most favorable to the opposing party. (Id. at p. 843; Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for punitive damages, or an issue of duty. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

Where the Court identifies a ground of law that is dispositive when applied to an undisputed material fact in the separate statements, it may reach that ground even though the moving party did not explicitly tender it, provided the opposing party is given an opportunity to respond and to show a triable issue of fact material to that ground. (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 70.)

ANALYSIS

I. Timeliness

Defendants contend that conduct before December 12, 2020 is time-barred and that the third through sixth causes of action are barred in their entirety because the disability-related conduct ended on June 23, 2020. In his Supplemental Opposition, Plaintiff argues that the disability discrimination claim accrued only when an adverse action followed December 12, 2020, that the continuing violation doctrine reaches the earlier conduct on all five claims, that a constructive discharge claim accrues at resignation, and that conduct outside the period is admissible as evidence of motive.

The Court rejects Defendants’ forfeiture argument because Plaintiff responded, at the Court’s invitation, to a ground the Court raised after his Opposition was filed. Accrual is also within the scope of the order, because a limitations defense first asks when the cause of action accrued.

A complaint alleging a violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act “shall not be filed after the expiration of three years from the date upon which the unlawful practice or refusal to cooperate occurred.” (Gov. Code, § 12960, subd. (e)(5).) Here, Plaintiff filed his charge with the Civil Rights Department on December 12, 2023. (Moore Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. D.) Therefore, conduct before December 12, 2020 is actionable only if some doctrine brings it within the period. The age discrimination and retaliation claims rest on the February through May 2022 suspension, transfer, and pay reduction, and they are timely. Plaintiff’s argument that the period runs from his November 2022 resignation depends on a constructive discharge. For the reasons stated below, the Court concludes that the resignation was not a constructive discharge.

A. Disability Claims

A cause of action ordinarily accrues when it is complete with all of its elements, which are wrongdoing, harm, and causation. (Aryeh v. Canon Business Solutions, Inc. (2013) 55 Cal.4th 1185, 1191.) A disability discrimination claim is complete when the employer takes an adverse employment action because of the disability, and an accommodation or interactive process claim is complete when the employer fails to accommodate or to engage. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subds. (a), (m), (n).) The continuing violation doctrine allows liability for conduct outside the period “if it is sufficiently connected to unlawful conduct within the limitations period.” (Richards v. CH2M Hill, Inc. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 798, 802 (Richards).) It applies where the employer’s actions are “(1) sufficiently similar in kind ...; (2) have occurred with reasonable frequency; (3) and have not acquired a degree of permanence.” (Id. at p. 823.) The doctrine requires at least one act of the same kind within the limitations period. (Trovato v. Beckman Coulter, Inc. (2011) 192 Cal.App.4th 319, 326-327 (Trovato).)

Here, the third through sixth causes of action accrued no later than July 2020, and no disability-related act falls within the period. Plaintiff reported the aggravated shoulder condition in May 2020, worked under modified duty from May 22 to June 22, 2020, and returned to full duty without restrictions on June 23, 2020. (Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 44, 49; Pl.’s Exs. 46, 142.) He submitted no further medical records and requested no further accommodation after that date. (12/5 Dep. 98:4-10.) The Court does not treat anything before the May 2020 request as disability-related, because Defendants learned of the condition only then. The last disability-related act Plaintiff identifies is Gibu’s denial of overtime on July 10 and 11, 2020, attributed to a restriction that had lifted the month before. (Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 44, 48, 53; Pl.’s Ex. 143.) Plaintiff adds that Gibu continued to withhold opportunities “thereafter” because of the accommodation request, but he gives no date and no instance. (Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 44, 47.)

Plaintiff argues that the claim accrued after December 12, 2020 because the pay cut, the loss of overtime, and the 2022 transfer were adverse actions that a grievance did not undo. However, an adverse action begins the period for a disability claim only if it was taken because of the disability. Plaintiff offers no evidence connecting any post-2020 action to the shoulder, and he testified that no one at Raytheon made a negative comment about the injury. (9/23 Dep. 177:24-178:1.) His supplemental declaration describes the 2021 and 2022 treatment as reductions in hours, loss of responsibilities, and a transfer “while younger employees” received overtime, and it attributes that treatment to his accommodation request only as something he “felt.” (Supp. Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 134, 137.) Plaintiff’s belief about Defendants’ motive is not evidence of that motive. Thus, the disability discrimination claim accrued with the July 2020 overtime denials, and the accommodation and interactive process claims accrued no later than June 23, 2020.

Nor does the continuing violation doctrine make these claims timely. The 2021 overtime grievances and the 2022 suspension, transfer, and pay reduction are not acts of the same kind as the July 2020 denials, because nothing in the record ties them to the disability. (Trovato, supra, 192 Cal.App.4th at pp. 326-327.) Plaintiff’s reliance on Richards is misplaced. There, the employer’s failures to accommodate continued into the limitations period. (Richards, supra, 26 Cal.4th at pp. 812, 823-824.) Here, the accommodation succeeded, the process ended in June 2020, and Plaintiff sought nothing further. Accordingly, the third, fourth, fifth, and sixth causes of action are time-barred, and summary adjudication of those causes of action is granted.

B. Age Harassment

Defendants contend that the harassing conduct before December 12, 2020 was neither similar to nor recurring with the later conduct, that it consisted of personnel decisions unconnected to age, and that it acquired permanence no later than 2018, when Plaintiff grieved the written warning and Gibu remained.

Plaintiff argues that Gibu’s treatment of the lab’s older technicians was one course of conduct from 2019 through his departure, and that he did not learn that informal resolution was futile until 2022.

Here, the second cause of action is timely, because acts contributing to the hostile environment fall within the period. Plaintiff’s overtime earnings continued to fall through 2021 and 2022 while younger technicians received more, and he grieved the distribution repeatedly in 2021. (Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 36, 61-66, 99; Garcia Decl., ¶ 4.) The work station Gibu placed at the cell entryway in 2020 stayed there until Plaintiff left the E3 Lab in February 2022. (Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 45-46.) From November 2021, Plaintiff watched Gibu give Garcia, another technician in his late fifties, a demeaning assignment and withhold his overtime. (Palladino Decl., ¶ 46; Garcia Decl., ¶ 5.) In 2022, Defendants suspended Plaintiff, transferred him to a basement lab where he worked alone, and cut his pay. (Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 97, 108.) McAlpine places Gibu’s favoritism toward younger employees and his exclusion of Plaintiff in 2021 and 2022. (Supp. McAlpine Decl., ¶¶ 4-5.)

Whether the 2018 warning, the 2019 and 2020 overtime cuts, the 2020 reprimands, and the October 2020 revocation of the door code join that course of conduct depends on the three Richards factors, and Defendants have not established as a matter of law that any of them is missing. First, the acts are of a kind. Before and after December 12, 2020, the same manager denied overtime, excluded Plaintiff from the lab’s common space and access, and disciplined him on grounds later withdrawn. (Richards, supra, 26 Cal.4th at p. 823.) Defendants’ contention that the earlier acts were personnel decisions unconnected to age fails for the reasons stated in the harassment analysis below.

Second, the frequency is reasonable. Defendants count only the 2018 warning and the February 2020 reprimand and find 18 months between them. However, the overtime denials ran continuously from 2019, four incidents fell between January and May 2020, and the door code revocation followed in October. (Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 34-36, 38-41, 57.)

Third, permanence means “that an employer’s statements and actions make clear to a reasonable employee that any further efforts at informal conciliation to obtain reasonable accommodation or end harassment will be futile.” (Richards, supra, 26 Cal.4th at p. 823.) Plaintiff grieved the 2018 warning and Defendants rescinded it, and the May 2020 accusation of unauthorized overtime was overturned. (Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 31-32, 57.) Because his grievances through 2020 succeeded, Plaintiff had reason to keep grieving. Defendants read Plaintiff’s statement that no continuous pattern was established until years later as a concession of permanence. (Opp., at p. 10:14-17.) Plaintiff there describes the 2018 warning as an isolated and remedied event, which is not a concession that the pattern had become permanent. A reasonable trier of fact could find that Plaintiff was not on notice that conciliation was futile until his 2022 complaints were closed without investigation. (Yanowitz v. L’Oreal USA, Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1059-1060 (Yanowitz).) Accordingly, the Court cannot determine as a matter of law that the continuing violation doctrine does not apply to the second cause of action.

C. Earlier Conduct

Whatever the scope of the harassment claim, conduct outside the period is admissible as background evidence of the motive behind timely acts. (Richards, supra, 26 Cal.4th at p. 812; National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Morgan (2002) 536 U.S. 101, 113.) The Court considers the 2018 through 2020 evidence for that purpose, and not as an independent basis for the discrimination or retaliation claims.

II. Age Discrimination

To defeat a discrimination claim under the Fair Employment and Housing Act on summary judgment, the employer must show either that the plaintiff cannot establish a prima facie case or that legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons explain the challenged actions. In this context, “legitimate” reasons are “reasons that are facially unrelated to prohibited bias, and which, if true, would thus preclude a finding of discrimination.” (Guz v. Bechtel National, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 354-356, 358 (Guz).) If the employer makes that showing, the plaintiff must demonstrate “such weaknesses, implausibilities, inconsistencies, incoherencies, or contradictions in the employer’s proffered legitimate reasons for its action that a reasonable factfinder could rationally find them ‘unworthy of credence.’” (Hersant v. Department of Social Services (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 997, 1005.)

Defendants identify legitimate reasons for the February 2022 suspension, the transfer to the ASIC Lab, the March 2022 written warning, and the pay reduction. In written statements, 14 coworkers complained of Plaintiff’s conduct, Labor Relations investigator Buchanan investigated and reported, and management determined that Plaintiff’s hourly rate exceeded the cap for his classification. (Pl.’s Ex. 73, RAYTHEON-PALLADINO 000497-499; Palladino Decl., ¶ 108.) The burden therefore shifts to Plaintiff.

Here, Plaintiff’s evidence raises triable issues of pretext and discriminatory animus. First, Gibu was the E3 Lab operations manager who initiated or participated in the challenged actions. He told McAlpine that he wanted to “get rid of the old guys in his lab” and wanted “a new, young, fresh group,” and he said in Garcia’s presence that he wanted to “get rid of the older guys.” (McAlpine Decl., ¶ 4; Garcia Decl., ¶ 4.) Defendants characterize these statements as stray remarks. A discriminatory comment made outside the context of an employment decision may still be relevant circumstantial evidence of discrimination. (Reid v. Google, Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 512, 538-539; Gov. Code, § 12923, subd. (c).) Gibu spoke as the manager of the lab, and he cut Plaintiff’s overtime, issued the discipline Plaintiff challenges, and revoked the open-and-close access Plaintiff had held for 15 years. (Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 31, 36, 57.)

Second, Plaintiff’s overtime earnings fell from $52,593 in 2018 to $32,933 in 2019 and continued to fall through his final years, while younger technicians received more overtime. (Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 36, 99; Garcia Decl., ¶ 4; Pl.’s Exs. 36-41.) Defendants respond that Plaintiff declined to present the aggregate time records of 28 union employees. Raw age comparisons will not by themselves support an inference of discrimination. (Guz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at pp. 367-368.) However, the overtime evidence here corroborates Garcia’s account of how Gibu distributed hours in the same lab during the same period.

Third, Plaintiff’s supervisor Xiong testified that from 2018 onward he could not recall Plaintiff doing anything that warranted discipline, and that he never observed Plaintiff violating the company rule invoked against him. (Xiong Depo. 63:2-9, 119:10-14.)

Fourth, the investigation underlying the 2022 discipline did not substantiate the hostile work environment allegations. The investigator instead described a “strong personality that can be difficult to work with” and found that Plaintiff’s behavior fell short of company values and made some employees uncomfortable. (Pl.’s Ex. 73, RAYTHEON-PALLADINO 000499.) Defendants may argue those findings to a jury as their honest belief. However, a factfinder could also read the report as undermining the discipline, because the investigator declined to sustain the allegation that prompted the suspension. Littleton, who attended every employee interview in the October 2021 human resources investigation, states that it concluded Gibu had created a hostile working environment, and that Brost said the following month that she wanted to get rid of Palladino rather than Gibu. (Littleton Decl., ¶¶ 20, 22.) A trier of fact could find Defendants’ justifications unworthy of credence. (Guz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 363.)

Defendants contend that Plaintiff’s replacement, Randy Okamoto, was older than Plaintiff or close to his age. Plaintiff disputes that Okamoto replaced him. Where the manager stated an intent to remove older technicians, the age of one successor does not resolve whether the manager acted because of age. Defendants further contend that Gibu, at 51, was in the same protected class. That fact weakens the inference of bias, but a manager over 40 can still act on age.

Defendants also contend that a written reprimand and a paid administrative leave are not adverse employment actions, citing Akers v. County of San Diego (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 1441, 1457. In Akers, the court held that an adverse employment action extends beyond ultimate acts such as hiring, firing, demotion, and refusal to promote. (Id. at pp. 1455-1457.) In any event, Plaintiff also identifies the transfer, the loss of lead and incentive pay, and the loss of overtime opportunity. Those actions materially affected the terms and conditions of his employment. Accordingly, summary adjudication of the first cause of action is denied.

III. Age Harassment

The Legislature has declared that a single incident of harassing conduct suffices to create a triable issue where it unreasonably interferes with work performance or creates an intimidating, hostile, or offensive working environment, that the existence of a hostile environment depends on the totality of the circumstances, and that harassment cases are rarely appropriate for disposition on summary judgment. (Gov. Code, § 12923, subds. (b), (c), (e).) Official employment actions can themselves contribute to the hostile message when they establish a widespread pattern of bias. (Roby v. McKesson Corp. (2009) 47 Cal.4th 686, 709 (Roby).)

Here, Plaintiff’s evidence permits a finding of a hostile environment based on age. Gibu stated his intent to replace the lab’s older technicians. (McAlpine Decl., ¶ 4.) He then excluded Plaintiff alone from the door code, leaving him locked out until a coworker admitted him, and he moved Plaintiff’s work station to the cell entryway, where coworkers laughed at its placement. (McAlpine Decl., ¶ 5; Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 45, 57.) McAlpine, an operations manager with four decades at the facility, had never seen a Raytheon employee treated so negatively, and she never saw Gibu revoke a younger employee’s lab access or move a younger employee’s work station out of the main lab area. (McAlpine Decl., ¶ 5.) After Garcia reported Gibu in November 2021, Gibu relocated Garcia’s work station, assigned him work far below his skill level for six months, and withheld his overtime while roughly 300 hours went to younger employees. (Garcia Decl., ¶ 5.) The five employees moved out of or terminated from the E3 Lab under Gibu were all over 50. (McAlpine Decl., ¶ 7.)

Defendants contend that Plaintiff cannot rely on Gibu’s statement because he did not hear it. (Lyle v. Warner Bros. Television Productions (2006) 38 Cal.4th 264, 285.) The rule in Lyle concerns harassment aimed at third parties that a plaintiff seeks to add to his own environment. Plaintiff offers Gibu’s statement as evidence of the motive behind conduct he experienced daily, and McAlpine reported the statement to him while he was still in the lab. (Palladino Decl., ¶ 30.)

Defendants further rely on Reno v. Baird (1998) 18 Cal.4th 640, 646-647, for the rule that personnel management decisions belong to discrimination claims. The Court applies Roby instead, because the personnel actions Plaintiff identifies accompany biased statements and socially exclusionary conduct from which a jury could find a hostile message. (Roby, supra, 47 Cal.4th at pp. 708-709.) Defendants also cite Beltran v. Hard Rock Hotel Licensing, Inc. (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th 865, 879, but the court there applied Government Code section 12923 to a different record. Because Gibu personally engaged in the alleged harassing conduct, the claim survives against him individually as well. Accordingly, summary adjudication of the second cause of action is denied as to all Defendants.

IV. Retaliation

To establish retaliation under the Fair Employment and Housing Act, a plaintiff must show protected activity, an adverse employment action, and a causal link between the two. (Yanowitz, supra, 36 Cal.4th at p. 1042.) A series of subtle yet damaging injuries, considered collectively, can constitute an adverse employment action. (Id. at pp. 1052, 1055-1056.)

Here, Plaintiff engaged in protected activity on January 31, 2022, when he complained to Defendants’ ethics function of harassment and retaliation by Gibu, and on February 3, 2022, when he told ethics investigator Forbes that Gibu wanted to get rid of the older technicians and named McAlpine as a witness. (Defs.’ Ex. 128, RAYTHEON-PALLADINO 000525; Palladino Decl., ¶ 85.) Two days after the January 31 complaint, Defendants suspended Plaintiff. (Palladino Decl., ¶ 87; Littleton Decl., ¶ 26.) In the weeks that followed, management collected written statements about Plaintiff from his coworkers. (Xiong Depo. 61:20-62:9.) Defendants then issued discipline on allegations their investigator declined to substantiate, transferred Plaintiff to the ASIC Lab in the basement, and cut his pay. (Pl.’s Ex. 73, RAYTHEON-PALLADINO 000499; Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 97, 108.) Brost testified that the findings of Beck’s investigation were “what caused the movement, transfer.” (Brost Depo. 59:5-9.)

Defendants respond that temporal proximity alone cannot establish pretext once the employer offers legitimate reasons, citing Arteaga v. Brink’s, Inc. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 327, 353-354. Plaintiff offers more than timing. No one contacted McAlpine until Raytheon’s counsel called her in August 2024, and no one contacted Garcia, who had reported the same conduct. (McAlpine Decl., ¶ 8; Garcia Decl., ¶ 8.) Defendants investigated the complainant rather than the accused and, as Littleton heard, declined to remove Gibu because “that move would satisfy Joe.” (Littleton Decl., ¶¶ 8, 23; Pl.’s Ex. 156.)

Defendants also contend that Plaintiff’s cat’s paw theory fails because he cannot show the decision makers relied on Gibu. Brost testified that she acted on an investigation whose subject was Plaintiff, and Buchanan reported that Gibu and Xiong, when interviewed, described their difficulties managing Plaintiff. (Pl.’s Ex. 73, RAYTHEON-PALLADINO 000498.) Whether Gibu’s stated animus entered the decision through those channels is a question of fact. Accordingly, summary adjudication of the eighth cause of action is denied.

V. Whistleblower Retaliation

Labor Code section 1102.6 governs this claim. The employee must show by a preponderance of the evidence that protected activity was a contributing factor in the adverse action. The employer must then demonstrate by clear and convincing evidence that it would have taken the action for legitimate, independent reasons in any event. The McDonnell Douglas framework does not apply. (Lawson v. PPG Architectural Finishes, Inc. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 703, 716, 718.)

Defendants contend that Plaintiff engaged in no protected activity before his employment ended.

Here, Plaintiff disclosed to supervisors, human resources, and ethics personnel what he reasonably believed were violations of law, including age harassment and sustained failures to post and distribute overtime, both before and throughout 2022. (Defs.’ Ex. 128, RAYTHEON-PALLADINO 000525; Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 61-66, 85, 105, 123; Littleton Decl., ¶ 46; Pl.’s Ex. 91.) The suspension two days after the January 31, 2022 complaint, followed by the transfer and the pay cut, permits a finding that the disclosures were a contributing factor. Littleton also states that on February 8, 2022, the union business agent asked him not to file a grievance on Plaintiff’s behalf and told the stewards that Plaintiff would be terminated if one were filed. (Littleton Decl., ¶ 29.) Received only for the fact that it was made, the statement shows that the stewards were warned off a grievance days after the suspension and that no grievance followed. That sequence is consistent with the inference the timing supports. On this record, Defendants have not established their same-decision defense by clear and convincing evidence as a matter of law. Accordingly, summary adjudication of the ninth cause of action is denied.

VI. Safety Complaints

Labor Code section 6310 protects employees who complain about workplace safety or health conditions. Here, Defendants presented evidence that Plaintiff made no safety or health complaint, including his testimony that he could identify none beyond the interpersonal hostility at issue in his other claims. (9/23 Dep. 164:1-18.) Plaintiff did not respond to Defendants’ argument on this claim and states that Defendants’ facts on it are undisputed. Defendants carried their initial burden, and Plaintiff failed to raise a triable issue. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) Accordingly, summary adjudication of the tenth cause of action is granted.

VII. Constructive Discharge

Because Plaintiff resigned, the eleventh cause of action requires a constructive discharge. Plaintiff must show that Defendants intentionally created or knowingly permitted working conditions so intolerable or aggravated at the time of his resignation that a reasonable employer would realize a reasonable person in his position would be compelled to resign. (Turner v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1238, 1251 (Turner).) The conditions must be “sufficiently extraordinary and egregious to overcome the normal motivation of a competent, diligent, and reasonable employee to remain on the job.” (Id. at p. 1246.) A demotion with a pay cut does not by itself amount to constructive discharge, and the standard is objective. (Id. at pp. 1247-1248.)

Here, the undisputed evidence of how Plaintiff left defeats that showing. The suspension, the discipline, the transfer, and the pay reduction all occurred between February and May 2022, six months before he resigned. Plaintiff began running pension calculations on April 17, 2022, and requested a retirement application packet pre-populated with the dates he selected. (Giancaterino Decl., ¶¶ 11-12, Exs. A, D.) Less than three months before he left, he told his ASIC Lab manager that he had decided to retire. (Manser Decl., ¶ 8.) He could recall nothing else that factored into his decision and nothing offensive or upsetting occurring in September, October, or November 2022 beyond what he had already described. (9/23 Dep. 163:11-24.) On or about November 17, 2022, after meeting with a retirement specialist about the deadline to lock in a favorable interest rate, he elected a five-year annuity paying $22,614.69 per month. The same election would have paid $17,456.40 per month had his benefit commenced a month later. (Giancaterino Decl., ¶¶ 13-15, Ex. E.)

In October 2022, Plaintiff submitted a written request to return to his position in the E3 Lab, and he confirmed at deposition that he wanted to return even though Gibu was still there. (9/23 Dep. 160:4-9; Pl.’s Ex. 93.) By seeking a position in the environment he describes as intolerable, Plaintiff provided evidence that a reasonable person would not have felt compelled to resign. The months between the last aggravating acts and the resignation support the same inference, because the standard measures the conditions at the time of resignation. (Turner, supra, 7 Cal.4th at p. 1251.)

Plaintiff presents evidence that Defendants never adequately investigated his complaints and that his mental health had suffered for years. The jury will consider those grievances through the surviving claims. However, that evidence does not establish that quitting was Plaintiff’s only reasonable alternative when he sought reinstatement to the E3 Lab and timed his departure to a pension deadline he selected. Thus, Plaintiff has not raised a triable issue of constructive discharge, and without an actual or constructive discharge the wrongful termination claim fails. Accordingly, summary adjudication of the eleventh cause of action is granted.

VIII. Failure to Prevent

Defendants contend that Plaintiff never complained.

A claim for failure to prevent discrimination, harassment, or retaliation under Government Code section 12940, subdivision (k) depends on the underlying claims under the Fair Employment and Housing Act. (Trujillo v. North County Transit Dist. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 280, 289.)

Here, the age discrimination, age harassment, and retaliation claims survive, and the January 31, 2022 ethics complaint refutes Defendants’ contention. (Defs.’ Ex. 128, RAYTHEON-PALLADINO 000525.) No one who handled the complaints contacted McAlpine, whom Plaintiff named as a witness, or Garcia, who had reported the same conduct. (McAlpine Decl., ¶ 8; Garcia Decl., ¶ 8.) Littleton states that the October 2021 investigation identified Gibu as the source of the hostile environment and that Defendants disciplined no one. (Littleton Decl., ¶¶ 20-21.) A factfinder could find that Defendants failed to take reasonable steps to prevent and correct the conduct. Accordingly, summary adjudication of the seventh cause of action is denied.

IX. Emotional Distress

Defendants contend that workers’ compensation exclusivity bars the twelfth cause of action and that the conduct Plaintiff describes was not outrageous.

Conduct that violates the Fair Employment and Housing Act falls outside the compensation bargain. Thus, the exclusivity doctrine does not bar a claim resting on that conduct. (Light v. Department of Parks & Recreation (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 75, 96-97, 101.)

Here, a jury must decide whether the conduct was outrageous, for the same reasons the harassment claim survives. A factfinder could conclude that a multi-year campaign of age-based exclusion and retaliatory discipline against a 36-year employee, ending with his isolation in a basement lab, exceeds all bounds tolerated in a civilized community. Plaintiff presents evidence of a resulting diagnosis of severe depression and anxiety. (Palladino Decl., ¶ 130; Pl.’s Ex. 184.) Accordingly, summary adjudication of the twelfth cause of action is denied as to all Defendants.

X. Punitive Damages

Summary adjudication of a punitive damages claim requires Defendants to establish as a matter of law that no officer, director, or managing agent committed, authorized, or ratified malicious or oppressive conduct. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (b).) Gibu’s statements of intent and the discipline imposed on unsubstantiated allegations would permit a finding of malice or oppression as to Gibu individually, and the managing agent requirement does not limit his personal exposure.

As to the entity Defendants, a managing agent is an employee who exercises substantial discretionary authority over decisions that ultimately determine corporate policy, and the scope of that authority is ordinarily a question of fact. (White v. Ultramar, Inc. (1999) 21 Cal.4th 563, 567, 576-577.) Defendants invoke Roby, which limited corporate policy to “formal policies that affect a substantial portion of the company and that are the type likely to come to the attention of corporate leadership.” (Roby, supra, 47 Cal.4th at pp. 714-715.)

Here, the individuals Plaintiff identifies are not situated as the local supervisor in Roby was. Schenkenberger imposed the suspension, convened the Step 2 grievance hearing, and held the Employee and Labor Relations directorship covering the bargaining unit at the El Segundo site. Weiss and Rips sat at the ethics function that closed Plaintiff’s complaints. (Littleton Decl., ¶¶ 8, 26, 36; Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 115-116; Pl.’s Exs. 111, 112.) Plaintiff offers evidence that these individuals applied the policies governing discrimination complaints and union discipline across the workforce, that none contacted the witnesses Plaintiff named, and that the discipline, transfer, and pay cut remained in place. (McAlpine Decl., ¶ 8; Garcia Decl., ¶ 8.) On this record, the Court cannot determine as a matter of law whether their authority extends to formal policy of the kind Roby describes, or whether their conduct ratified the underlying acts. Accordingly, summary adjudication of the punitive damages claim is denied as to all Defendants.

XI. RTX as Employer

Defendants contend that RTX never employed Plaintiff because Raytheon, a wholly owned RTX subsidiary, was his employer. With Exhibit A to the Moore Declaration excluded, Defendants rely principally on the Green Declaration.

Centralized control of labor relations is the most important of the factors the integrated enterprise test weighs. (Laird v. Capital Cities/ABC, Inc. (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 727, 737-738.)

Here, Plaintiff’s evidence creates a triable issue of an integrated employment relationship. Human resources business partner Beck testified that she uses the name “Raytheon” to refer to RTX Corporation. (Beck Depo. 13:22-14:3.) The Total Rewards department that administered Plaintiff’s compensation and pension serves RTX and its subsidiaries, including Raytheon. (Giancaterino Decl., ¶ 2.) RTX issued the Non-Retaliation Policy governing Plaintiff’s workplace, and RTX-level ethics personnel handled and closed his complaints. (Palladino Decl., ¶¶ 71, 115-116; Pl.’s Exs. 31, 111, 112.) The Court does not reach Plaintiff’s separate argument that RTX is liable as an agent of the employer under Government Code section 12926, subdivision (d). Accordingly, summary adjudication of the issue whether RTX employed Plaintiff is denied.

XII. Compensatory Damages

Defendants separately seek adjudication that Plaintiff cannot establish compensatory damages because his pension payments exceed his former salary. Summary adjudication is available only as to a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages under Civil Code section 3294, or an issue of duty. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1); DeCastro West Chodorow & Burns, Inc. v. Superior Court (1996) 47 Cal.App.4th 410, 419, 422.) The request also fails on its terms. The annuity pays for five years from December 1, 2022, but Plaintiff declares that he intended to work another decade, and he presents evidence of diagnosed emotional distress. (Giancaterino Decl., ¶¶ 14-15, Ex. E; Palladino Decl., ¶ 130; Pl.’s Ex. 184.)

Defendants also request attorney fees and costs under Government Code section 12965 based on Plaintiff’s abandonment of one cause of action. A prevailing defendant recovers fees under that section only on a showing that the action was frivolous, unreasonable, or groundless, and Defendants have made no such showing. Accordingly, summary adjudication of the issue of compensatory damages is denied, and the request for attorney fees is denied.

 

CONCLUSION

The Motion for Summary Judgment is denied. The alternative Motion for Summary Adjudication is granted as to the third, fourth, fifth, sixth, tenth, and eleventh causes of action and is otherwise denied.


Case Number: 25STCV05429    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion for Summary Judgment

The Motion for Summary Judgment is granted as to Defendant Christian McCormick and denied as to Defendant SF Markets, LLC dba Sprouts Farmers Market. The alternative Motion for Summary Adjudication is denied as to Sprouts and denied as moot as to McCormick.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Cordeny Kelly (Plaintiff) filed this action against SF Markets, LLC dba Sprouts Farmers Market, sued as Sprouts Farmers Market, LLC (Sprouts), and Christian McCormick (McCormick) (collectively, Defendants), alleging that he was injured when he stepped on a rack lying on the floor of a Sprouts grocery store.

The causes of action are: (1) General Negligence; and (2) Premises Liability.

Defendants filed a Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the alternative, Summary Adjudication. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

 

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

The Court relies on the transcript, not on counsel’s summaries of it, and rules on the objections as directed to the transcript. The Court rules only on the objections material to the disposition of the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (q).)

Gomez was with Plaintiff when the incident occurred, and his testimony that Plaintiff tripped on the grille is percipient testimony, not opinion. (Evid. Code, § 702, subd. (a); Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at pp. 42, 69:8-10.)

Objection 3. Overruled as to Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at pp. 61:19-25 and 62:1-7. After Gomez qualified his first answer, counsel re-asked whether Sprouts inspected the area while Coolsys was working, and Gomez answered “No.” (Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at p. 62:3-5.) The qualification goes to the weight of the testimony, not its admissibility.

Objection 4. Overruled as to Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at pp. 73:11-25 and 74:1-23. The testimony that no employee was assigned to the area at any interval and the qualification that deli team members could go to the case depending on their tasks are both in the transcript, and the Court reads them together. (Evid. Code, § 356.)

Objection 6. Overruled as to Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at pp. 81:23-25 and 82:1-12. The Court takes from the testimony only what it says: that Gomez did not recall a documented sweep or inspection in the interval, and that no video shows an employee inspecting the floor around the rack. Whether the absence of a record supports an inference that no inspection occurred goes to weight, not admissibility.

Objection 7. Overruled as to Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at pp. 107:11-23, 108:7-23, and 110:1-10. (Evid. Code, § 356.)

 

LEGAL STANDARD

“The motion for summary judgment shall be granted if all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) “[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.) “There is a triable issue of material fact if, and only if, the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.” (Ibid.)

A defendant meets its initial burden by showing “that one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) “Once the defendant or cross-defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff or cross-complainant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Ibid.) The Court views “the evidence in the light most favorable to plaintiff, as the nonmoving party, liberally construing her evidence while strictly scrutinizing defendant’s.” (Martinez v. Chippewa Enterprises, Inc. (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 1179, 1184 (Martinez).)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Separate Statement

Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s separate statement does not comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (b)(3), because Additional Material Facts 1 through 16 cite an incident report, discovery responses, a sweep policy, a sweep report, and a store layout that Plaintiff never filed, because the statement is not in two-column format, and because Additional Material Facts 44 through 50 state legal propositions. Defendants ask the Court to disregard the separate statement in its entirety.

“Failure to comply with this requirement of a separate statement may constitute a sufficient ground, in the court’s discretion, for granting the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (b)(3).) A deficient opposing statement does not relieve the moving party of its burden of persuasion. (See Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 850.)

Here, the Court disregards Additional Material Facts 1 through 16 to the extent they rest on the unfiled documents, none of which is in the record. (Gerami Decl., ¶¶ 6-9, 13.) Additional Material Facts 44 through 50 state legal propositions and are not treated as facts. However, the Court declines to disregard the separate statement as a whole. Plaintiff responded to each of Defendants’ 11 facts, and Plaintiff filed and served the deposition transcript of Gomez, Sprouts’s person most qualified (Gomez Depo.), which Defendants used at length on reply. (Reply, at pp. 5:8-10, 5:27 - 6:1, 6:15-16, 7:7-11, 7:18, 8:6-7.)

II. Unpleaded Theories

Defendants argue that the Opposition introduces theories of negligent contractor selection, qualification, and licensing that the Complaint does not plead.

A “‘defendant moving for summary judgment need address only the issues raised by the complaint; the plaintiff cannot bring up new, unpleaded issues in his or her opposing papers.’” (Laabs v. City of Victorville (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1253.) However, “new factual issues presented in opposition to a motion for summary judgment should be considered if the controlling pleading, construed broadly, encompasses them.” (Id. at p. 1257.)

Here, the Complaint alleges that Defendants were responsible for the operation and maintenance of the premises, negligently maintained the walkways and aisles, and failed to warn. (Compl., GN-1; Prem.L-1.) The Court agrees with Defendants that contractor vetting is a different theory and does not consider it. Whether Sprouts supervised the work, inspected the aisle, or cleared it goes to the method of performing the pleaded maintenance duty, as Defendants concede, and the Court considers those questions on the merits. (Reply, at p. 4:18-24.)

III. Open and Obvious

Defendants contend that the rack was open and obvious, that Plaintiff saw it and walked around it twice before stepping on it, and that the condition’s obviousness discharged any duty to warn of it. Defendants further contend that the factors in Rowland v. Christian (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108 negate any duty to remedy the condition, because injury to a customer who has already avoided a visible rack is not foreseeable, Sprouts did not create the condition, and liability would require instantaneous detection of contractor activity.

Whether a duty exists is a question of law. (Krongos v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 387, 392.) “[A]lthough the obviousness of a danger may obviate the duty to warn of its existence, if it is foreseeable that the danger may cause injury despite the fact that it is obvious (e.g., when necessity requires persons to encounter it), there may be a duty to remedy the danger, and the breach of that duty may in turn form the basis for liability . . . .” (Osborn v. Mission Ready Mix (1990) 224 Cal.App.3d 104, 122 (Osborn).) Necessity is the example the Osborn court gave, and the wet pavement in Martinez, which “appears to have provided a principal if not sole access way from the street to defendant’s building,” was an instance of it. (Martinez, supra, 121 Cal.App.4th at p. 1185.) Later cases state the exception more broadly: obviousness “will not negate a duty of care when it is foreseeable that, because of necessity or other circumstances, a person may choose to encounter the condition.” (Jacobs v. Coldwell Banker Residential Brokerage Co. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 438, 447 (Jacobs).) Whether a duty to remedy an obvious condition exists depends on the Rowland factors, “such as the foreseeability of harm, defendant’s advance knowledge vel non of the dangerous condition, and the burden of discharging the duty.” (Martinez, supra, 121 Cal.App.4th at p. 1185.) Those factors “are evaluated at a relatively broad level of factual generality.” (Cabral v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2011) 51 Cal.4th 764, 772 (Cabral).) The court’s task “‘is not to decide whether a particular plaintiff’s injury was reasonably foreseeable in light of a particular defendant’s conduct, but rather to evaluate more generally whether the category of negligent conduct at issue is sufficiently likely to result in the kind of harm experienced that liability may appropriately be imposed . . . .’” (Ibid.)

Here, the open and obvious doctrine does not negate Sprouts’s duty as a matter of law. First, Plaintiff’s claims are not limited to a failure to warn. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants negligently maintained the store’s walkways and aisles and failed to keep the premises free of hazards. (Compl., GN-1; Prem.L-1.) The Court assumes for purposes of the Motion that the rack’s visibility discharged any duty to warn of it. Whether Sprouts owed a duty to remove or block off a component of its own refrigerator case lying in a customer aisle is a different question. Defendants’ reliance on Obrien v. Fong Wan (1960) 185 Cal.App.2d 112, 116 for a categorical rule of no liability for an obvious danger is misplaced. Obrien predates Rowland, and the Court of Appeal identifies the Osborn formulation as “[t]he modern and controlling law on this subject.” (Martinez, supra, 121 Cal.App.4th at p. 1184.)

Second, the record shows a foreseeable encounter despite the rack’s visibility. The cases ask whether the plaintiff was required or invited to encounter the danger. (Jacobs, supra, 14 Cal.App.5th at pp. 447-448.) In Jacobs, the plaintiff stood on a diving board over an empty pool to look over a fence, and the court agreed with the trial court that he “‘was neither required nor invited to do so.’” (Id. at p. 448.) The court asked the question at the level of the invited class: “potential buyers did not have to approach the dangerous condition (i.e., the empty pool) in order to inspect the backyard.” (Ibid.) It contrasted a golf club patron who slipped on the club’s walkway: “‘in view of the [club’s] invitation to use the walkway, she was entitled to consider it reasonably safe.’” (Ibid.) The invitation that matters is an invitation into the area of the hazard.

Here, Sprouts invited its customers into the aisle where the rack lay, and its assistant manager directed Plaintiff within it. The rack lay at one end of the cheese island in an aisle that remained open to customers during store hours, with no barrier and no sign that service was under way. (Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at pp. 30 - 31:19, 32, 99:5-8.) Sprouts expected customers to look at products displayed at varying heights while walking through that area. (Id. at pp. 32, 54 - 55:2, 118.) Moments before the incident, Plaintiff asked Gomez where an item was, Gomez pointed him toward the general area where the item could be found, that area was also where the rack lay, and Plaintiff was moving in the direction Gomez guided him when he stepped on the rack. (Id. at pp. 105:18-23, 107:15-16, 108, 110; Kawakami Wong Decl., Ex. F, Kelly Depo., at pp. 19:25 - 20:2.) Unlike the buyers in Jacobs, who could inspect the backyard without approaching the pool’s edge, Sprouts invited this customer to the spot where the object lay, to look at products displayed above it. The foreseeable consequence of that invitation is a customer whose eyes are on the merchandise and whose foot meets the object. Those are the “other circumstances” Jacobs describes.

Defendants reply that Plaintiff had already walked around the rack twice, and that Gomez pointed only to a general product area and denied leading Plaintiff to a hazardous condition. (Reply, at pp. 5:25 - 6:3; Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at p. 118.) The two earlier passes show that a route around the rack existed, not that the invitation to look at the merchandise beside it was withdrawn. The general area to which Gomez pointed was, by his account, the area where the rack lay. (Id. at p. 108.) Thus, leaving a component of a store’s display case in an open customer aisle during business hours is a category of conduct likely to result in a customer stepping on it, whether or not that customer noticed the object on an earlier pass. (Cabral, supra, 51 Cal.4th at p. 772; Jacobs, supra, 14 Cal.App.5th at pp. 446, 448.)

Nor did Plaintiff “‘knowingly embrace an entirely obvious risk’” in the sense Jacobs describes. (Jacobs, supra, 14 Cal.App.5th at pp. 448-449.) The plaintiff in Jacobs sought out the danger for a purpose unconnected to the invitation. Plaintiff here stepped on an object he had passed earlier while doing what the store expected and its manager directed. Whether that lapse was reasonable is a question of comparative fault. In Martinez, the court rejected the same argument Defendants make, holding that how the plaintiff navigated a visible hazard “may pose an issue of comparative negligence” but “does not now warrant relieving defendant of all the legal burden of the situation.” (Martinez, supra, 121 Cal.App.4th at p. 1186.)

Third, the remaining Rowland factors do not support a categorical exception. The connection between the conduct and the injury is direct, because the rack Plaintiff stepped on is the one Sprouts’s contractor left on the floor. (Gomez Decl., ¶ 4.) Sprouts knew before the incident that its contractor was working on the case in the open aisle, because its assistant store manager led the technician there. (Gomez Decl., ¶ 3.) Defendants reply that awareness of the work is not awareness of the rack. (Reply, at p. 6:12-13.) At the level of generality Cabral requires, a possessor that has its contractor disassembling a display case in an open customer aisle during business hours knows in advance that a floor-level obstruction is a likely product of that work. (Cabral, supra, 51 Cal.4th at p. 772.) The burden of discharging the duty is not the instantaneous detection Defendants describe. Sprouts already required its refrigeration contractor, Coolsys to use cones, barriers, or warning signs, and it keeps cones for floor-level hazards. (Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at pp. 44:14-25, 60.) The burden is to enforce that requirement, to check the aisle when the contractor walks away, or to cone or remove the rack, as Gomez did after the incident. (Id. at p. 69:19-24.)

That Plaintiff saw the rack twice and later stepped on it may matter to breach, causation, and comparative fault. However, it does not eliminate the duty. (Cabral, supra, 51 Cal.4th at p. 772; Martinez, supra, 121 Cal.App.4th at p. 1186.) Accordingly, the Court denies summary adjudication of the negligence and premises liability causes of action on the ground that the condition was open and obvious.

IV. Notice

Defendants contend that Sprouts had no actual notice of the rack, because no employee saw or handled it before the incident, and no constructive notice, because a third-party technician placed it and left it unattended for less than 20 minutes, too short an interval to charge Sprouts with discovering it.

“A store owner exercises ordinary care by making reasonable inspections of the portions of the premises open to customers, and the care required is commensurate with the risks involved.” (Ortega v. Kmart Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1205.) “[W]here the plaintiff relies on the failure to correct a dangerous condition to prove the owner’s negligence, the plaintiff has the burden of showing that the owner had notice of the defect in sufficient time to correct it.” (Id. at p. 1206.) “Whether a dangerous condition has existed long enough for a reasonably prudent person to have discovered it is a question of fact for the jury, and the cases do not impose exact time limitations.” (Id. at p. 1207.) Plaintiffs “may demonstrate the storekeeper had constructive notice of the dangerous condition if they can show that the site had not been inspected within a reasonable period of time so that a person exercising due care would have discovered and corrected the hazard.” (Id. at p. 1212.)

The notice requirement does not apply to a condition the owner itself created. “Where the dangerous or defective condition of the property which causes the injury has been created by reason of the negligence of the owner of the property or his employee acting within the scope of the employment, the owner of the property cannot be permitted to assert that he had no notice or knowledge of the defective or dangerous condition . . . . Under such circumstances knowledge thereof is imputed to him.” (Hatfield v. Levy Brothers (1941) 18 Cal.2d 798, 806 (Hatfield).) Where the condition is instead “brought about by natural wear and tear, or third persons, or acts of God or by other causes which are not due to the negligence of the owner, or his employees, then to impose liability the owner must have either actual or constructive knowledge of the dangerous condition or have been able by the exercise of ordinary care to discover the condition . . . .” (Id. at p. 806.) The nondelegable duty doctrine treats a maintenance contractor’s negligence as the possessor’s own. “‘[T]he duty which a possessor of land owes to others to put and maintain it in reasonably safe condition is nondelegable. If an independent contractor, no matter how carefully selected, is employed to perform it, the possessor is answerable for harm caused by the negligent failure of his contractor to put or maintain the buildings and structures in reasonably safe condition[.]’” (Srithong v. Total Investment Co. (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 721, 726 (Srithong), quoting Brown v. George Pepperdine Foundation (1943) 23 Cal.2d 256, 260 (Brown).) The rule “is a form of vicarious liability because it is not based on the personal fault of the landowner who hired the independent contractor.” (Srithong, supra, 23 Cal.App.4th at p. 727.)

Here, a triable issue of material fact exists on notice. Plaintiff identifies no employee who saw the rack before the incident, and Gomez’s testimony that an employee exercising reasonable care would have seen it was conditioned on a team member being in the area. (Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at p. 80:3-12; Reply, at p. 6:14-16.) The question is therefore constructive notice. First, under Ortega, the reasonableness of Sprouts’s inspection is a question for the jury on this record. Defendants’ own evidence fixes the chronology. The technician removed the black rack, placed it on the floor at 12:16:18 p.m., worked beside it until 12:18:43 p.m., and then left the area without returning the rack to its place. (Gomez Decl., ¶ 4.) At approximately 12:38 p.m., Plaintiff stepped on the rack. (Gomez Decl., ¶ 5; Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at p. 76.) By Defendants’ count, the rack lay unattended for 19 minutes and 37 seconds. (Reply, at p. 7:3-8.)

Before the incident, Sprouts knew that Coolsys was servicing a refrigerator case in a customer-accessible aisle during store hours, because Gomez had led the technician there at 12:13:05 p.m. (Gomez Decl., ¶ 3; Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at pp. 31:5-8, 56, 58.) Sprouts required Coolsys to use cones, barriers, or warning signs, and Coolsys used none. (Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at pp. 60, 61:1-3.) Sprouts did not close the aisle, did not block off the work area, assigned no employee to monitor the contractor or to make sure customers could pass safely, and did not inspect the area while the work proceeded. (Id. at pp. 58, 59:7-16, 60, 61:19-21, 62:3-5, 84.) Store sweeps were to be conducted hourly, and a courtesy clerk was responsible for inspecting the area between noon and 1:00 p.m. (Id. at pp. 64, 72.) However, Gomez could not recall the last documented inspection before the incident or any documented sweep between 12:16 p.m. and 12:38 p.m., no one was assigned to pass through the area at any interval of 20 minutes or less, Gomez did not know whether any employee inspected the area after the technician removed the rack, and no video shows an employee inspecting the floor around the rack in that interval. (Id. at pp. 72, 73:11-25, 74, 82, 85:2-8.)

Defendants reply that the hourly store sweep set the measure of reasonable care, that no sweep came due in the unattended interval, and that Plaintiff offers no admissible evidence that a reasonable inspection required a shorter interval. (Reply, at p. 7:9-20.) Defendants assume the answer to the question Ortega assigns to the jury. The care required “is commensurate with the risks involved,” and the cases “do not impose exact time limitations.” (Ortega, supra, 26 Cal.4th at pp. 1205, 1207.) The Supreme Court in Ortega described a case in which “the Court of Appeal affirmed the plaintiff’s judgment, based on evidence that the clerk responsible for the area in which the plaintiff slipped on a spool of thread took a 20-minute coffee break without notifying anyone to cover her area during that period,” and it quoted that court’s holding: “‘Was a 20-minute interval between inspections of the aisles commensurate with the exercise of ordinary care by defendant? This is a question that was properly left with the jury to decide.’” (Id. at p. 1208, quoting Sapp v. W. T. Grant Co. (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 89, 94 (Sapp).) Here, unlike in Sapp, the source and duration of the condition are known, and the evidence of the risk itself supports a finding that a shorter interval was called for: Sprouts knew of the work, required Coolsys to cone the area, and assigned no one to the aisle while the work proceeded or after the technician left. The jury decides whether an hourly sweep was reasonable care for an aisle in which the store’s contractor was removing a component from a refrigerator case. (Ortega, supra, 26 Cal.4th at pp. 1207, 1212-1213.) No evidence shows that anyone inspected this aisle after the technician walked away from the rack. Therefore, Defendants’ showing that the unattended interval was under 20 minutes does not establish as a matter of law that Sprouts lacked constructive notice. Moreover, even if that showing shifted the burden, Plaintiff’s evidence raises a triable issue.

V. Causation

Defendants contend that Plaintiff’s inattention while navigating a rack he had already seen and avoided, rather than any act or omission by Sprouts, was the substantial factor in causing the incident.

“A plaintiff meets the causation element by showing that (1) the defendant’s breach of its duty to exercise ordinary care was a substantial factor in bringing about plaintiff’s harm, and (2) there is no rule of law relieving the defendant of liability.” (Ortega, supra, 26 Cal.4th at p. 1205.) “These are factual questions for the jury to decide, except in cases in which the facts as to causation are undisputed.” (Ibid.)

Here, the mechanism of injury is undisputed. Defendants state in their own separate statement that Plaintiff “stepped onto a black rack on the floor.” (Separate Statement, at p. 2:14-16 [UMF No. 4]; Kawakami Wong Decl., Ex. B, Kelly Depo., at pp. 20:1-2, 20:23-24.) Gomez agreed that Plaintiff tripped on the grille and, after viewing the video, testified that “[t]he customer stepped onto the rack, and I helped him.” (Gerami Decl., Ex. 5, Gomez Depo., at pp. 42, 99:13-15.) The Court does not rely on Gomez’s later answer that he picked up the grille because he realized it had caused the loss of footing. Gomez’s “I don’t know” answers withhold a label for the event and do not dispute that Plaintiff’s foot met the rack. (Id. at p. 105:6-7.) Defendants’ remaining argument, that Sprouts committed no negligent act, is the duty and notice question, which is triable for the reasons stated above. Plaintiff’s inattention is a matter of comparative fault, not a bar, and Defendants cite no authority holding that a plaintiff’s momentary inattention to a known hazard defeats causation as a matter of law. Defendants’ reliance on Constance B. v. State of California (1986) 178 Cal.App.3d 200, 207 is misplaced. There, the assailant at a highway rest stop stood in the light, so the alleged lighting deficiency could not have caused the attack. (Id. at pp. 211-212.) Here, the rack is the undisputed cause of the loss of footing. Accordingly, the Court denies summary adjudication of the negligence and premises liability causes of action on the ground that Plaintiff cannot establish causation.

VI. McCormick

Defendants contend that McCormick was named solely by virtue of his employment as store manager, that he was not on duty or on the premises on the date of the incident, and that any liability for his conduct is Sprouts’s under respondeat superior. Plaintiff does not oppose summary adjudication as to McCormick. (Opp., at p. 3:12-13; Gerami Decl., ¶ 50; Response to Separate Statement, at p. 1:21-23.) The Court grants summary judgment in favor of McCormick, and the alternative request for summary adjudication as to McCormick is denied as moot.

 

CONCLUSION

The Motion for Summary Judgment is granted as to Defendant Christian McCormick and denied as to Defendant SF Markets, LLC dba Sprouts Farmers Market. The alternative Motion for Summary Adjudication is denied as to Sprouts and denied as moot as to McCormick.
