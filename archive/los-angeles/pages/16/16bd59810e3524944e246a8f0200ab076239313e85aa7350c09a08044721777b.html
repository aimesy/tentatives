DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

Demurrer Standard

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly's of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616; Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139 [“[U]nder our liberal pleading rules, where the complaint contains substantive factual allegations sufficiently apprising defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet, a demurrer for uncertainty should be overruled or plaintiff given leave to amend.]

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

 

Meet and Confer

A party is required to meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) The meet and confer effort must occur at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(2).) 

 

Motion to Strike Standard

Motions to strike are used to challenge defects in the pleadings not subject to demurrer.  Any party may move to strike the whole or any part of a pleading within the time allotted to respond to the pleading.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1).) The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the Court is required to take judicial notice. (Id. § 437, subd. (a).) The Court may strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading, and strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id. § 436.)  An “irrelevant” matter includes any “demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint or cross-complaint.”  (Id. § 431.10, subds. (b)(3), (c); see also Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1036-1042.)

 

Judgment on the Pleadings Standard

“A motion for judgment on the pleadings serves the function of a demurrer, challenging only defects on the face of the complaint… [¶] The grounds for a motion for judgment on the pleadings must appear on the face of the complaint or from a matter of which the court may take judicial notice.” (Richardson-Tunnell v. School Ins. Program for Employees (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1056, 1061.) In considering a motion for judgment on the pleadings, courts consider whether the factual allegations, assumed true, are sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Fire Insurance Exchange v. Superior Court (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 446, 452-453.)

 

Analysis

The order lacks any reference to the previously ordered meet and confer declarations. The court file shows no supplemental declaration(s) filed. While a new judicial officer now oversees the subject action, nothing in the underlying circumstances leading to the continuance of the demurrers and motion to dismiss alters the prior order for supplemental briefing demonstrating an effort to narrow the issues presented to the court and otherwise cease efforts to force the court to make arguments for the parties. (See Badie v. Bank of America (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 779, 784–785 [“When [a party] fails to raise a point or asserts it but fails to support it with reasoned argument and citations to authority, we treat the point as waived”].)

The court sustained the demurrer of  Digital Realities to the first amended complaint after all other defendants answered and filed their motion for summary adjudication on the second cause of action. The court therefore also reiterates the prior concerns raised over the restatement of redundant issues conceded by the answer to the first amended complaint, and potential new positions not previously raised. “A party demurring to a pleading that has been amended after a demurrer to an earlier version of the pleading was sustained shall not demur to any portion of the amended complaint, cross-complaint, or answer on grounds that could have been raised by demurrer to the earlier version of the complaint, cross-complaint, or answer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (b).) While an amended pleading can be a basis for renewal, Defendants elected to NOT raise any challenges to the first amended complaint, and instead answered the first amended complaint. (Goncharov v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2018) 19 Cal.App.5th 1157, 1166-1167.) The supplemental briefing statement must therefore address and justify any and all positions on previously challenged claims, including the basis for the demurrer after the answer (except for Digital Realities), and the right to bring new challenges, if applicable, including an explanation for not previously raising the position.

The demurrers and motion to strike are continued. The parties are ordered to file a joint statement 14 calendar days before the next hearing date. If a joint statement remains unachievable, Defendants, except for Digital Realities, must file a statement addressing the validity of each and every challenged cause of action in the demurrer.

All defendants may alternatively answer the operative complaint and file a motion for judgment on the pleadings. (Code Civ. Proc., 438, subd. (g).) If Defendants elect to proceed with this alternative path, the parties are ordered to notify the court and proceed with the required pleadings.

 

CONCLUSION

The demurrers and motion to strike are CONTINUED.

Case Number: 23LBCV00750    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: S27

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Kinecta Federal Credit Union alleges Defendant Hector Reyna defaulted on a loan, thereby leaving an outstanding balance of $17,558.29, and on a revolving credit account (credit card), with an outstanding balance of $10,39518. On April 25, 2023, Plaintiff filed a complaint for 1. Breach of Unsecured Written Agreement 2. Common Count 3. Breach of Agreement 4. Open Book Account, and 5. Account Stated.

On December 7, 2023, the clerk entered a default. On December 8, 2023, the court entered a default judgment for $32,375.60. On April 22, 2026, the clerk issued a writ of execution, which led to judgment debtor filing a claim of exemption, and the setting of the hearing by Judgment Creditor.

 

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

(a) The claim of exemption and notice of opposition to the claim of exemption constitute the pleadings, subject to the power of the court to permit amendments in the interest of justice.

(b) At a hearing under this section, the exemption claimant has the burden of proof.

(c) The claim of exemption is deemed controverted by the notice of opposition to the claim of exemption and both shall be received in evidence. If no other evidence is offered, the court, if satisfied that sufficient facts are shown by the claim of exemption (including the financial statement if one is required) and the notice of opposition, may make its determination thereon. If not satisfied, the court shall order the hearing continued for the production of other evidence, oral or documentary.

(Code Civ. Proc., § 703.580.)

“Money in a judgment debtor's deposit account that is not otherwise exempt under this chapter is exempt to the extent necessary for the support of the judgment debtor and the spouse and dependents of the judgment debtor.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 704.225)

 

Analysis

Judgment Creditor represents the exemption is based on necessities and family support. (Code Civ. Proc., § 706.051, subd. (b).) Judgment Debtor submitted no application or information to the court. The opposition states that judgment debtor lacks documentation in support.

Judgment Creditor filed the notice of exemption hearing and challenges the lack of documentation. Judgment Creditor represents Judgment Debtor presented a financial statement showing gross earnings of “$16,000 per month,” tax and withholdings and deductions of $5,041,” and “monthly expenses of $500.00 for utility and telephone, $1,500.00 for food and household supplies, $5,000.00 for house payment and maintenance and $800.00 for transportation and auto expenses.... [The list also includes] $500.00 for clothing, $25.00 for medical and dental and $150.00 for laundry and cleaning ... $200.00 for entertainment and $190.00 for insurance.” Judgment Creditor summarizes the financial sheet as take home pay of $10,959, with $8,865 in expenses. Judgment Creditor requests 20% allowable by law per pay period for payment on account of this debt.

Again, the application lacks any presented information, thereby allowing the court to determine the validity of the claim. The court therefore continues the hearing in order to allow Judgment Debtor to submit documentation to the court for review. (Code Civ. Proc., § 703.580, subd. (c).)

 

CONCLUSION

Reyna’s Claim of Exemption is CONTINUED. 

Judgment Creditor to give notice.

Case Number: 24LBCV02664    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: S27

 

1.     Background Facts

On December 2, 2024, Plaintiff Charles Richey (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendant Stephen Christopher Bello (“Defendant”), alleging six causes of action: (1) Assault; (2) Battery; (3) Conversion; (4) False Imprisonment; (5) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress; and (6) Injunctive Relief.   Plaintiff alleges that, on November 26, 2024, Defendant aggressively approached Plaintiff, threatening him, and attempted to wrestle Plaintiff’s phone out of his hand.

This matter came on for trial on February 4, 2026. The Court found in favor of Plaintiff and against Defendant.  The Court awarded $5,000.00 to Plaintiff for pain and suffering, and $2,500.00 for emotional distress.  

Judgment was entered on February 19, 2026, in favor of Plaintiff and against Defendant.

Plaintiff filed a memorandum of costs on February 26, 2026.

2.     Motion to Tax Costs

On March 17, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to tax costs.  Defendant seeks to deny Plaintiff’s costs in their entirety pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1033, subdivision (a), because Plaintiff recovered a judgment that could have been rendered in a limited civil case. (Mot., pp. 3-4.)  In the alternative, Defendant argues Plaintiff’s claimed costs should be taxed because they are unsupported and/or not reasonably necessary. (Mot., pp. 5-8.) 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues section 1033, subdivision (a), considers whether a civil case was objectively reasonable when the action was filed, not whether the eventual recovery was justified in hindsight. (Opp., p. 2.)   Plaintiff further argues the challenged items were reasonably necessary. (Opp., pp. 4-8.)

 

A.    Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 1032(b) provides that a prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs. Section 1033.5 itemizes costs that are recoverable. Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5(c) states, in relevant part: “Any award for costs shall be subject to the following: (1) Costs are allowable if incurred, whether or not paid. (2) Allowable costs shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation. (3) Allowable costs shall be reasonable in amount. (4) Items not mentioned in this section . . . may be allowed . . . in the Court’s discretion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c).)

Code of Civil Procedure section 1032(a)(4) allows for other costs and expenses to be recoverable when expressly authorized by law. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032(a)(4).) 

“If the items in a cost memorandum appear proper, the verified memorandum is prima facie evidence the expenses were necessarily incurred by the’ prevailing party. To controvert this evidence, the burden is on the objecting party to present evidence showing the contrary.” (Whatley-Miller v. Cooper (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 1103, 1115.) “[A] party’s ‘mere statements in the points and authorities accompanying its notice of motion to strike cost bill and the declaration of its counsel are insufficient to rebut the prima facie showing [that the costs were necessarily incurred].” (Jones v. Dumrichob (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1266.) 

B.    Whether Plaintiff’s costs should be stricken entirely under Code of Civil Procedure § 1033(a)

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033, subdivision (a), states: “Costs or any portion of claimed costs shall be as determined by the court in its discretion in a case other than a limited civil case in accordance with Section 1034 where the prevailing party recovers a judgment that could have been rendered in a limited civil case.”

“[S]ection 1033(a) applies when a plaintiff has obtained a judgment for money damages in an amount (now $25,000[1] or less) that could have been recovered in a limited civil case, but the plaintiff did not bring the action as a limited civil case and thus did not take advantage of the cost- and time-saving advantages of limited civil case procedures. In this situation, even though a plaintiff who obtains a money judgment would otherwise be entitled to recover litigation costs as a matter of right, section 1033(a) gives the trial court discretion to deny, in whole or in part, the plaintiff's recovery of litigation costs.” (Chavez v. City of Los Angeles (2010) 47 Cal.4th 970, 982–983.)

Defendant argues the Court should disallow all of Plaintiff’s costs because he filed the case as an unlimited civil case with no reasonable basis for achieving the jurisdictional minimum and only recovered $7,500.00. (Mot., p. 3.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues his forum choice was reasonable, as his complaint supported a punitive damages claim that could exceed the $35,000 limited civil cap at the time of filing. (Opp., p. 2.)

Here, the Court declines to deny Plaintiff’s recovery of litigation costs pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1033, subdivision (a).  While Plaintiff was awarded damages well below the unlimited jurisdiction minimum, it cannot be determined that Plaintiff wasted judicial resources.  (Valentino v. Elliott Sav-On Gas, Inc. (1988) 201 Cal.App.3d 692, 701 [reasoning that section 1033(a) “is to discourage plaintiffs from ‘over filing’ their cases”].) This is primarily because Plaintiff held a reasonable belief that his evidence would support damages exceeding the jurisdictional minimum. 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion is denied on this ground.  Plaintiff is not precluded from recovering costs when his judgment was less than the jurisdictional minimum.

C.    Whether Plaintiff’s claimed costs should be taxed

Defendant argues Plaintiff’s claimed costs are unsupported and/or not reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation. (Mot., pp. 5-8.) Defendant further argues Plaintiff’s claimed costs should be evaluated against what would have been reasonably incurred in a small-claims matter. (Mot., pp. 4-5.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues his memorandum of costs was verified and timely filed on February 26, 2026; therefore, the memorandum is prima facie evidence that each item was properly incurred. (Opp., p. 3.)  Plaintiff further argues Defendant failed to submit evidence showing each claim was not properly incurred. (Opp., p. 3.)  Moreover, Plaintiff contends the challenged items were reasonably necessary. (Opp., pp. 4-8.) 

As a preliminary matter, the Court is not persuaded by Defendant’s assertion Plaintiff’s claimed costs should be evaluated against a small-claims matter.  As discussed above, Plaintiff held a reasonable belief his evidence would support damages exceeding the jurisdictional minimum. Defendant provides no case law suggesting the Court should evaluate Plaintiff’s claimed costs against a small-claims matter.  The Court therefore evaluates each claimed cost on whether it is reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (c)(2).

i.              Filing and Motion Fees, Fees for electronic filing or service

Plaintiff seeks $504.10 in filing and motion fees, and $323.60 in fees for electronic filing or service. (Memo. of Costs, p. 1, ¶¶ 1, 14.)

Defendant argues Plaintiff’s filing-related charges are not shown to have been reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation; for example, Plaintiff filed a preemptory challenge to the assigned judicial officer, a notice of posting of jury fees only to waive jury trial, and a notice of change of address. (Mot., p. 6.)  Defendant argues these filings were not necessary to resolve the factually bare issues and should therefore not be shifted to Defendant as recoverable costs. (Mot., p. 6.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues he does not claim a charge for a peremptory challenge or a separate charge for a notice posting jury fees. (Opp., p. 8.)  Plaintiff further argues that the claim for a jury deposit fee is allowed expressly by section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(1), and that a filing charge for a notice of change of address is necessary to keep the address of record current with the Court. (Opp., p. 8.) Finally, Plaintiff concedes he cannot find documentation for $43.00 claimed for a certified copy of the restraining order judgment and thus withdraws it. (Opp., p. 8.)

The memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of reasonableness regarding costs and expenses. (Whatley-Miller, supra, 212 Cal.App.4th at 1115.)  Filing fees, motion fees, and fees for electronic filing or service are recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivisions (a)(1) and (a)(14). Here, Defendant does not present evidence to the contrary. Defendant only challenges the costs sought by Plaintiff by alleging they are not reasonably necessary.  Nonetheless, in opposition, Plaintiff provides invoices for all documents filed in this action. (Herron Decl., Exhs. A-L.)  Defendant has not overcome Plaintiff’s showing of reasonableness. 

As a result of Plaintiff’s concession regarding the $43.00 fee for a certified copy of the restraining order, the Court will tax Item 1 by $43.00 to reflect $461.10 in filing and motion fees actually incurred.  The Court will not tax Item 14 fees, as Defendant has not overcome Plaintiff’s prima facie showing of reasonableness.

ii.             Deposition Costs

Plaintiff seeks $2,354.00 in deposition costs. (Memo. of Costs, p. 1, ¶ 4.) 

Defendant argues Plaintiff’s deposition costs were not reasonably necessary, as the relevant information could have been obtained through written discovery, stipulations, or other less expensive means. (Mot., pp. 6-7.)  Defendant argues these costs should be taxed in their entirety because Plaintiff cannot demonstrate the depositions were reasonably necessary and reasonable in amount where the damages award was within the jurisdictional limit of small claims. (Mot., p. 7.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues Defendant contended he was acting to protect another person, Sheila O’Flynn, during the incident.  (Opp., p. 4.)  Plaintiff served requests for admission to narrow the issues before taking any depositions, but Defendant denied that he was acting in self-defense or in defense of others. (Opp., p. 5.)  Plaintiff therefore contends depositions were necessary and reasonable to address Defendant’s justification. (Opp., p. 5.) Plaintiff further argues both deponents were called to testify at trial. (Opp., p. 5.)

Deposition costs are recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(3).  Here, Defendant provides no substantive evidence to challenge Plaintiff’s prima facie showing of reasonableness.  Defendant only argues that the deposition costs are unreasonable because Plaintiff could have obtained the information through written discovery and Plaintiff’s award was within the jurisdictional limit of small claims.  Nonetheless, Plaintiff provides an invoice for both the deposition of Sheila O’Flynn and Defendant. (Herron Decl., Ex. D.)  Defendant has not overcome Plaintiff’s showing of reasonableness.  The Court does not tax the deposition costs.

iii.            Service of Process

Plaintiff seeks $748.50 in service of process fees. (Memo. of Costs, p. 1, ¶ 5.)

Defendant argues Defendant’s counsel accepted service by Notice and Acknowledgment of Receipt, filed on February 18, 2025, after Plaintiff mailed the papers. (Mot., p. 8.)  Defendant therefore contends third-party process server charges are not reasonably necessary. (Mot., p. 8.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues he conducted service in the order contemplated by the statute, by first engaging a registered process server, and then mailing the complaint package when service was unsuccessful. (Opp., pp. 6-7.) Plaintiff contends the registered process server attempted service at Defendant’s residence five times, and at Defendant’s business four times. (Opp., p. 7.)  Plaintiff further argues a large portion of the service fees were not spent serving Defendant, but rather on serving subpoenas on a third parties. (Opp., p. 7.)

Service of process costs are recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(4).  Here, Defendant provides no substantive evidence to challenge Plaintiff’s prima facie showing of reasonableness.  Defendant argues the service costs are unreasonable because Defendant’s counsel accepted service.  However, Plaintiff supplies invoices for service of a deposition or trial subpoenas on a third party.  (See Herron Decl., ¶ 12; Ex. F.)  Plaintiff also provides status reports of the registered process server’s attempted service on Defendant but does not provide invoices. (Herron Decl., ¶ 12; Ex. L.)   Defendant has not overcome Plaintiff’s showing of reasonableness.  Nonetheless, because Plaintiff does not provide an invoice for service attempts on Defendant, the Court taxes Item 5 by $70.00 to reflect $678.50 in service of process fees actually incurred.

iv.            Court reporter fees

Plaintiff seeks $2,180.00 in court reporter fees. (Memo. of Costs, p. 1, ¶ 11.)

Defendant argues Plaintiff’s court reporter fees are unreasonably high for a short bench trial that involved limited factual and legal complexity. (Mot., p. 7.) 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues the court reporter was the appointed official court reporter pro tempore, on the stipulation of both parties. (Opp., pp. 5-6.) Plaintiff further argues Defendant offers no evidence the costs in this matter are different from prevailing Los Angeles County rates. (Opp., p. 6.)

Court reporter fees are recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(11).  Here, Defendant provides no substantive evidence to challenge Plaintiff’s prima facie showing of reasonableness. Defendant only challenges the costs by alleging they are disproportionate to the nature and apparent length of the proceedings but fails to provide evidence in support.  Nonetheless, Plaintiff provides invoices for the court reporter. (See Herron Decl., Ex. E.)  Defendant has not overcome Plaintiff’s showing of reasonableness and these costs will not be taxed.

v.             Other

Plaintiff seeks $2.59 in “other” costs. (Memo. of Costs, p. 1, ¶ 15.)

Defendant argues Plaintiff claims $2.59 in “other” costs, without explanation or supporting documentation, thus the cost should be taxed in its entirety. (Mot., p. 8.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues the $2.59 cost is the postage to mail the complaint package to Defendant. (Opp., p. 6.)

Here, Defendant provides no substantive evidence to challenge Plaintiff’s prima facie showing of reasonableness. Nonetheless, Plaintiff claims $2.59 as postage on the memorandum of costs worksheet. (See Herron Decl., Ex. C.)  Plaintiff’s counsel also declares the cost as postage related to serving Defendant via mail. (Herron Decl., ¶ 12.)  Postage is not an allowable cost except for exhibits, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (b)(3).  Since the postage here was related to serving Defendant, it is not allowable. Thus, the Court taxes “other” costs in its entirety.

 

3.     Conclusion

Defendant Stephen Christopher Bello’s Motion to Tax Costs is GRANTED IN PART.  Filing and motion fees are taxed in the amount of $43.00.  Service of process costs are taxed in the amount of $70.00. “Other” costs are taxed by $2.59.

Moving Defendant is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 24LBCV02847    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: S27

 

1.     Background Facts

On December 20, 2024, Plaintiff Jose Villa (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants, Port of Los Angeles, City of Los Angeles (the “City”), County of Los Angeles, SSA Marine, a Carrix Enterprise, SSA Marine, Inc., SSA Pacific of America (“SSA”), Carrix, Inc., Stevedoring Services of America, Inc., Berth 55 Warehouse, and AltaSea at the Port of Los Angeles, and Does 1 to 100, inclusive, alleging two causes of action: (1) Premises Liability, and (2) Negligence.  Plaintiff alleges he tripped on the side of a step, which was in a dangerous condition, and it caused him to fall into a glass door and/or window.

On March 27, 2025, SSA and the City filed a cross-complaint against Cross-Defendants U.S. Expeditors, Jose Villa, Villa Transportation, Inc., Inner Valley Transport, inc., and IVT Logistics Worldwide, Inc. (“Cross-Defendant”), alleging four causes of action: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Equitable Indemnity; (3) Declaratory Relief; and (4) Apportionment of Fault.

On January 29, 2026, the City filed its dismissal with prejudice as to the three equitable causes of action, Equitable Indemnity, Declaratory Relief, and Apportionment of Fault.

On February 10, 2026, the City filed its dismissal as to the Breach of Contract cause of action, without prejudice.

Cross-Defendant filed a memorandum of costs on February 25, 2026.

2.     Motion to Tax Costs

 

On March 16, 2026, the City filed the instant motion to strike or tax costs.  The City seeks to strike or tax $1,381.70 for the deposition of SSA’s PMK Kevin Nielsen on the grounds it was not reasonably necessary to litigate. (Mot., p. 3.)  The City further seeks to strike or tax $202.44 in “runner fees,” arguing they are not expenses recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5. (Mot., p. 3.)  Finally, the City argues that, as a threshold matter, Cross-Defendant may not be entitled to recover any costs at all, as costs may only be awarded to a “prevailing party” under Code of Civil Procedure section 1032. (Mot., p. 3.)

 

A.    Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 1032(b) provides that a prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs. Section 1033.5 itemizes costs that are recoverable. Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5(c) states, in relevant part: “Any award for costs shall be subject to the following: (1) Costs are allowable if incurred, whether or not paid. (2) Allowable costs shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation. (3) Allowable costs shall be reasonable in amount. (4) Items not mentioned in this section . . . may be allowed . . . in the Court’s discretion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c).)

“If the items in a cost memorandum appear proper, the verified memorandum is prima facie evidence the expenses were necessarily incurred by the prevailing party. To controvert this evidence, the burden is on the objecting party to present evidence showing the contrary.” (Whatley-Miller v. Cooper (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 1103, 1115.) “[A] party’s ‘mere statements in the points and authorities accompanying its notice of motion to strike cost bill and the declaration of its counsel are insufficient to rebut the prima facie showing [that the costs were necessarily incurred].” (Jones v. Dumrichob (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1266.) 

B.     Prevailing Party

The City argues Cross-Defendant may not be entitled to recover any costs at all, because the City voluntarily dismissed its cross-complaint before any adjudication.  (Mot., p. 3.)  Therefore, the City argues, it is within the Court’s discretion whether Cross-Defendant is the prevailing party. (Mot., p. 3.)

In opposition, Cross-Defendant argues a party in whose favor a dismissal is entered is considered a prevailing party. (Opp., p. 3.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 1032 defines prevailing party as “a defendant in whose favor a dismissal is entered…” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (a)(4).)  The City’s request for dismissal of the three equitable causes of action of the City’s cross-complaint was entered on January 29, 2026. (See Request for Dismissal 1/29/26.) The City’s request for dismissal of the Breach of Contract cause of action of the City’s cross-complaint was entered on February 10, 2026. (See Request for Dismissal 2/10/26.)   Therefore, dismissal was entered in favor of Cross-Defendant.  The Court finds Cross-Defendant is the prevailing party under section 1032 and is therefore entitled as a matter of right to recovery of costs.

C.    Deposition Costs

Cross-Defendant seeks to recover $1,381.70 for the deposition of SSA’s PMK, Kevin Nielsen. (Memo. of Costs Worksheet, p. 1, ¶ 4.)

The City argues the deposition of Kevin Nielsen took place on January 29, 2026, and focused entirely on issues pertaining to SSA’s claims. (Mot., p. 4.)  The City argues Cross-Defendant’s participation in the deposition was independent of the City’s cross-complaint because the deposition was noticed by Plaintiff and concerned Plaintiff’s claims. (Mot., p. 5.)

In opposition, Cross-Defendant argues that the City has not met their burden to challenge the propriety of costs. (Opp., p. 4.)  Cross-Defendant argues both the City and SSA were seeking defense and indemnity from Cross-Defendant, therefore the costs of deposition of SSA’s PMK is necessary. (Opp., p. 5.)  Cross-Defendant further argues the deposition concerned issues relating to the City’s ownership of the property, SSA’s tenancy, and the rights and obligations of the City and SSA pursuant to the City’s tenancy permit issued to SSA. (Opp., p. 5.)

Deposition costs are recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(3).  Here, the City provides no substantive evidence to challenge Cross-Defendant's prima facie showing of reasonableness.  The City only argues that the deposition costs are unreasonable because the deposition was independent of the City’s cross-complaint.  Nonetheless, Cross-Defendant provides relevant portions of the transcript of the deposition of Kevin Nielsen that show the deposition concerned issues relating to the City’s ownership of the property and the rights and obligations of the City pursuant to the City’s tenancy permit issued to SSA. (See Arshansky Decl., ¶ 9; Ex. C.) The City has not overcome Cross-Defendant's showing of reasonableness.  The Court does not tax the deposition costs. 

 

D.    Runner Fees

Cross-Defendant seeks to recover “runner fees” in the total amount of $202.44.

The City seeks to strike or tax $202.44 in “runner fees” associated with filing documents in the action, arguing they are not expenses recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5. (Mot., p. 5.)   

In opposition, Cross-Defendant argues messenger fees may be allowed in the Court’s discretion. (Opp., p. 5.) Cross-Defendant argues the runner fees were incurred for filing the demurrer, summary judgment motion, and an ex-parte application and were thus reasonably necessary to the litigation. (Opp., p. 5.)

Messenger costs are not expressly permitted or prohibited by statute and are therefore discretionary.  Here, Cross-Defendant submits invoices of the runner fee for each filing.  (See Arshansky Decl., ¶ 10; Ex. D.)  However, the Court finds the “runner fees” are unnecessary on their face given cheaper alternatives available to Cross-Defendant.  Therefore, the Court taxes the runner fees in their entirety.

 

3.     Conclusion

Defendant City of Los Angeles’s Motion to Strike or Tax Costs is GRANTED IN PART.  Runner fees are taxed in the amount of $202.44.

Case Number: 25LBCV00347    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: S27

 

1.     Background Facts

This is a negligence action arising out of a slip and fall incident that occurred on or about September 2, 2023, wherein Plaintiff Maria Franco (“Plaintiff”) sustained injuries and damages. On February 11, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative Complaint against Defendants 7-Eleven Store #20679; 7-Eleven, Inc.; Asha Investments, Inc. d/b/a 7-Eleven; and DOES 1 through 100 (collectively, “Defendants”), asserting a sole cause of action for Premises Liability – Negligence. 

Plaintiff Maria Franco died on June 22, 2026.  Now, Plaintiff’s daughter, Blanca Estela Perez moves to substitute into this action in Plaintiff’s place, and to be appointed Plaintiff’s successor-in-interest.

2.     Legal Standard

“On motion after the death of a person who commenced an action or proceeding, the court shall allow a pending action or proceeding that does not abate to be continued by the decedent's personal representative or, if none, by the decedent's successor in interest.” Code Civ. Proc., § 377.31. The person seeking to continue the action in the decedent’s name must file an affidavit that complies with Code of Civil Procedure Section 377.32(a)(1)-(7). “Except as otherwise provided by statute, a cause of action for or against a person is not lost by reason of the person's death, but survives subject to the applicable limitations period.” Code Civ. Proc., § 377.20. 

 

3.     Motion to Substitute Personal Representative and to Appoint Blanca Estela Perez as Successor-In-Interest

Blanca Estela Perez (“Ms. Perez”) moves the Court for an order appointing her as the successor-in-interest of Plaintiff, who is now deceased. (Mot., p. 3.)  Ms. Perez contends Plaintiff commenced this action during her lifetime and that her death does not automatically terminate Plaintiff’s claims. (Mot., p. 3.)  Ms. Perez contends she is the proper beneficiary and successor-in-interest as she is Plaintiff’s surviving child and Plaintiff died without leaving a will. (Mot., p. 5.)

Here, Plaintiff’s cause of action survives her death. 

As to the declaration under Code of Civil Procedure section 377.32, the declaration must state all of the following:

(1) The decedent's name.

(2) The date and place of the decedent's death.

(3) “No proceeding is now pending in California for administration of the decedent's estate.”

(4) If the decedent's estate was administered, a copy of the final order showing the distribution of the decedent's cause of action to the successor in interest.

(5) Either of the following, as appropriate, with facts in support thereof:

(A) “The affiant or declarant is the decedent's successor in interest (as defined in Section 377.11 of the California Code of Civil Procedure) and succeeds to the decedent's interest in the action or proceeding.”

(B) “The affiant or declarant is authorized to act on behalf of the decedent's successor in interest (as defined in Section 377.11 of the California Code of Civil Procedure) with respect to the decedent's interest in the action or proceeding.”

(6) “No other person has a superior right to commence the action or proceeding or to be substituted for the decedent in the pending action or proceeding.”

(7) “The affiant or declarant affirms or declares under penalty of perjury under the laws of the State of California that the foregoing is true and correct.”

(Code of Civ. Proc. § 377.32, subd. (a)(1)-(7).)

While Ms. Perez submits a declaration and certified copy of Plaintiff’s death certificate, the Court notes some issues.  First, Ms. Perez attaches a certified copy of Plaintiff’s death certificate to her declaration as Exhibit A.  Ms. Perez identifies Maria Franco as the decedent, stating she died on June 22, 2026, in Long Beach, California.  (Perez Decl., ¶¶ 2, 3.)  However, the death certificate identifies Plaintiff’s date of death as February 22, 2026. (See Perez Decl., Ex. A.) 

Second, Ms. Perez declares she is the surviving child and issue of Plaintiff, and therefore is Plaintiff’s successor-in-interest. (Perez Decl., ¶ 9.) Ms. Perez confirms “No other person has a superior right to be substituted for Maria Franco in this pending action.” (Perez Decl., ¶ 11.)  However, the death certificate identifies “Paola Perez” as Plaintiff’s daughter and “Pablo Martin Perez” as Plaintiff’s husband. (See Perez Decl., Ex. A.)   Ms. Perez does not address whether these two individuals have superior right to be substituted for Plaintiff in this action.

Ultimately, the Court finds Blanca Estela Perez has not successfully met the requirements of section 377.32.  It is unclear whether there is a person with a superior right to be substituted for Plaintiff, and the date of death on the death certificate conflicts with the date of death Ms. Perez declares.  

4.     Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, Ms. Perez’s motion to substitute the personal representative for Plaintiff and appoint Blanca Estela Perez as successor-in-interest is DENIED without prejudice.

Defendant moves for summary adjudication on Plaintiff’s fourth and fifth causes of action.  Plaintiff failed to timely file opposition papers.   

Recently, in Mandell-Brown v. Novo Nordisk Inc. The Court has reviewed the moving papers and finds they are not deficient on their face.  In light of the lack of opposition and an opposing separate statement, the Court grants the motion.

The Court reminds counsel of its obligations under Rule of Professional Conduct 3.1(a)(2):

“A lawyer shall not present a claim or defense in litigation that is not warranted under existing law, unless it can be supported by a good faith argument for an extension, modification, or reversal of the existing law.”

If counsel does not intend to argue the validity of its clients’ causes of action in good faith, the proper course of action is to dismiss those claims, not to waste the Court and the opposing party’s resources by failing to oppose dispositive motions.

Case Number: 25LBCV02171    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: S27

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff filed this negligence and premises liability action against a number of private and public entities. He filed his operative first amended complaint (FAC) on January 27, 2026.

Two defendants answered the FAC on March 3, 2026; Plaintiff failed to prove service of the complaint or FAC on a number of other defendants.

On March 18, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel failed to appear for a Case Management Conference and OSC Re: Failure to File Proof of Service. The Court continued the Conference and OSC to April 15, 2026 and set a concurrent OSC Re: Plaintiff’s counsel failure to appear, prosecute, dismissal, and sanctions.

On April 15, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel again failed to appear. The Court noted that all parties still had not been served and ordered the complaint dismissed without prejudice.

On May 15, 2026, Plaintiff applied ex parte for relief from dismissal based on his counsel’s mistake, inadvertence, or neglect. Defendant Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority (LAHSA) opposed the issuance relief on an ex parte basis, but did not oppose the motion on its substance. On May 18, 2026, the Court set the application for hearing on ordinary notice on this date.

Plaintiff’s application for relief from dismissal is now before the Court. No party has filed an opposition to the motion on its substance.

 

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

“The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Application for this relief . . . shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473(b).)

“Notwithstanding any other requirements of [section 473], the court shall, whenever an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney's sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, vacate any . . . (2) resulting default judgment or dismissal entered against his or her client, unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney's mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.” (Id., italics added.)

The latter mandatory provision of section 473(b) was added to the statute “to provide relief to the client even when the attorney's mistake or neglect was not excusable." (Garcia v. Hejmadi (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 674, 682.)

/ / /

Analysis

Plaintiff’s unopposed motion is granted.

The declaration in support of Plaintiff’s motion indicates this case “was dismissed without prejudice due to non-service of Defendants County of Los Angeles Health Department and Los Angeles Department of Health and Environmental Services”, and proofs of service have now been filed. (Buckholtz Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.) Counsel states that the lapse occurred “because [he] experienced a medical emergency, a stroke, from which [he has] recovered.” (Id., ¶ 3.)

The Court notes that the complaint was not dismissed solely because of failure to serve all defendants; it was also dismissed for Counsel’s failure to appear at multiple case management conferences. Nonetheless, Counsel moved promptly to have the dismissal set aside. In the absence of a substantive opposition, the Court is willing to infer that Counsel’s health issues also caused his failures to appear as required.

Plaintiff has demonstrated the dismissal was caused by his counsel’s neglect. Relief is mandatory. The motion is granted.

 

CONCLUSION

Plaintiff Josepff Smith’s Motion to Set Aside/Vacate Dismissal is GRANTED.

Case Number: 26LBCP00016    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: S27

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Travelle Malone (Petitioner) petitions for relief from the deadline to submit a government claim arising from an incident where he was allegedly struck by a vehicle driven by one of respondent City of Irvine’s (Respondent’s) employees in the course of the employee’s work.

Petitioner filed his petition on January 15, 2026, accompanied by his declaration and various exhibits. Respondent filed its opposition on April 13, 2026.

On July 14, 2026, Petitioner filed an Errata to his petition, supplementing his declaration and exhibits. The Court considers the errata, as it was filed and served more than 16 court days before the instant hearing and Respondent lodged no objection.

The undisputed timeline of relevant events is as follows:

January 10, 2025

Accident occurs. (Malone Decl., ¶ 2.) Petitioner is unaware that the driver who struck him is employed by Respondent. (Labib Decl., ¶ 5.)

May 28, 2025

Petitioner retains counsel. (Labib Decl., ¶ 2.)

June 2, 2025

Petitioner’s counsel submits a request to law enforcement for the Traffic Collision Report (TCR) prepared in connection with the accident. (Id., ¶ 3.)

June 10, 2025

Petitioner’s deadline to submit his government claim expires.

July 25, 2026

Petitioner’s counsel receives the TCR from law enforcement, which identifies the driver of the vehicle as Respondent’s employee for the first time. (Id., ¶ 4 and Exh. C.)

July 28, 2025

Petitioner’s counsel submits a government claim. (Id., ¶ 6 and Exh. D.)

August 1, 2025

A County government office affiliated with Respondent (the County of Orange – County Executive Office of Risk) rejects Petitioner’s late-filed claim. (Id., ¶ 7.)

October 31, 2025

Petitioner’s counsel applies to Respondent to submit a late government claim.

December 15, 2025

Petitioner’s application to submit a late claim is denied by operation of law for Respondent’s failure to respond to the request. (Malone Decl., ¶ 5.)

On January 15, 2026, Petitioner filed his instant petition for relief under Government Code section 916.6.

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

Before a suit for damages may be filed against a public entity, a claimant must present a timely written claim to the public entity, and the claim must have been acted upon by the board or deemed rejected.¿(Gov. Code § 945.4;¿Munoz v. State of California¿(1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1767, 1777.)¿¿A claim relating to a cause of action for injury to person or personal property must be presented to the public entity no later than six months after the accrual of the cause of action.¿ (Gov. Code § 911.2;¿Munoz, supra,¿33 Cal.App.4th, at p. 1776.)¿¿ 

If a claimant fails to present a claim within the statutory period, the claimant may apply “within a reasonable time not to exceed one year after the accrual of the cause of action” to the public entity for leave¿to present a late claim.¿¿(Gov. Code § 911.4, subd. (b).)¿ If the public entity denies leave to present a late claim, a claimant may petition the Court under Government Code¿§ 946.6 for relief from the requirements of Government Code § 945.4.¿¿(Munoz, supra,¿33 Cal.App.4th at p. 1777.)¿¿ 

A petition¿pursuant to¿Government Code¿§¿946.6 must be “filed within six months after the application to the board is denied or deemed to be denied pursuant to Section 911.6.”¿ (Gov. Code § 946.6, subd.¿(b);¿see also¿City of Los Angeles v. Superior Court¿(1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 621, 627.)¿ This requirement is mandatory¿and¿operates¿as a statute of limitations.¿¿(Lineaweaver v. S. Cal. Rapid Transit Dist.¿(1983) 139 Cal.App.3d 738, 741;¿see also¿J.M. v. Huntington Beach Union High School Dist.¿(2017) 2 Cal.5th 648, 653.)

Analysis

Petitioner has demonstrated excusable neglect for purposes of section 915.6.

“The showing required of a party seeking relief under section 946.6 is the same as that required under section 473 of the Code of Civil Procedure, which provides for relief from default. [Citation.]” (Bettencourt v. Los Rios Community College Dist. (1986) 42 Cal.3d 270, 275, fn. 5; see also Renteria v. Juvenile Justice, Department of Corrections & Rehabilitation (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 903, 911 [citing same].) “Section 946.6 is a remedial statute intended to provide relief from technical rules which otherwise provide a trap for the unwary claimant. [Citation.] The remedial policies underlying the statute are ‘that wherever possible cases be heard on their merits and any doubts which may exist should be resolved in favor of the application.’ [Citation.]” (Ebersol v. Cowan (1983) 35 Cal.3d 427, 435, citing Viles v. State (1967) 66 Cal.2d 24, 29-31.)

That said, “[t]o obtain relief under section 946.6, subdivision (c)(1), ‘[t]he mere recital of mistake, inadvertence, surprise or excusable neglect is not sufficient to warrant relief. Relief on grounds of mistake, inadvertence, surprise or excusable neglect is available only on a showing that the claimant's failure to timely present a claim was reasonable when tested by the objective “reasonably prudent person” standard.’ ... Under the reasonably prudent person standard, ‘[e]xcusable neglect is that neglect which might have been the act of a reasonably prudent person under the circumstances.’ ... When relief is sought based on mistake, because of the reasonably prudent person standard ‘it is not every mistake that will excuse a default, the determining factor being the reasonableness of the misconception.’ ... .” (N.G. v. County of San Diego (2020) 59 Cal.App.5th 63, 74, internal citations and footnotes omitted, italics in original.)

Petitioner acted as a reasonably prudent person might in this case.

Respondent relies on Department of Water & Power v. Superior Court (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 1288 (DWP) to argue Petitioner was not reasonably diligent in investigating the circumstances of the accident and discovering Respondent’s involvement.

DWP is distinguishable. In DWP, work by the public entity respondent contributed to a dangerous condition on the road at the site of the petitioner’s accident. (Id., at pp. 1291-1292.) The public entity’s involvement was apparent from the moment of the accident. The petitioner retained counsel long before submitting his claim and even filed a lawsuit against the driver of the other vehicle, yet failed explore the entity’s liability until after the claims deadline had passed. (Id., at p. 1292.) Here, there were no such clear indications a public entity was involved, Petitioner was not represented by counsel until shortly before Respondent’s involvement was discovered, and a claim was promptly submitted afterward.

Where a plaintiff has no reason to believe a public entity is involved with an underlying incident, it is reasonably prudent to treat it as though it is a claim against a private citizen. (See DeVore v. Department of California Highway Patrol (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 454, 462-463.)   A party may struggle to demonstrate mistake, etc. where he “fails to do anything” to pursue his claim until more than six months after an incident requiring a government claim. (See Barragan v. County of Los Angeles (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 1373, 1383-1384.) But that is not the case here. Petitioner did not do nothing; he sought counsel well within the statute of limitations for a claim against a private citizen. Declarations in support of the motion also indicate that nothing about the vehicle that struck Petitioner indicated it was affiliated with Respondent. (Labib Decl., ¶ 5.) His counsel investigated the incident with reasonable diligence, given that counsel also had no reason to believe the defendant might be a public entity and the statute of limitations for a case against a private litigant would not imminently expire. Upon receiving the TCR indicating Respondent was involved, Petitioner’s counsel submitted a government claim almost immediately, and only a few weeks after the deadline passed.

Respondent otherwise relies on what it characterizes as undue delay between its initial rejection of Petitioner’s claim and his application to submit a late claim. The Court finds Petitioner or his counsel’s neglect excusable in this regard; after rejection of the initial claim, Respondent was on notice of Petitioner’s cause of action. The delay of less than three months was not patently unreasonable, and it was reasonable for Petitioner’s counsel to believe the need for urgency had passed. The relevance of Petitioner’s purported neglect after his claim had already been rejected is unclear, and the Court errs toward hearing Petitioner’s claim on its merits. (The Court notes that Respondent itself did not feel Petitioner’s request for late submission was important enough to require a response; it allowed the request to expire by operation of law rather than rejecting it. (See Labib Decl., ¶ 9.))

 

CONCLUSION

Petitioner Travelle Malone’s Petition for Relief from Claims Requirement is GRANTED.
