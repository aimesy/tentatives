MOVING PARTY:                 Plaintiff Jane Un        

 

RESPONDING PARTY:       Defendant Petra Fuhriman

Motion to Compel Further Responses to Form Interrogatories and for the Imposition of Sanctions

The court considered the moving and opposition papers filed in connection with this motion.

DISCUSSION

Plaintiff Jane Un, individually and derivatively on behalf of GPS Monitoring Solutions, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) moves the court for an order (1) compelling defendant Petra Fuhriman (“Defendant”) to serve further responses to set one of Plaintiff’s Form Interrogatories and (2) to impose monetary sanctions in the sum of $3,210 against Defendant and her counsel of record Shirley Chiu.

In her opposition, Defendant argues that the motion is untimely and must be denied.  The court denies Plaintiff’s motion because the motion is untimely since Plaintiff served the motion on November 28, 2025, and Defendant served a supplemental response to Form Interrogatory 4.1 on September 29, 2025, and served the verification to Defendant’s initial responses and Defendant’s supplemental response to Form Interrogatory 4.1 on September 29, 2025.  (Declaration of Mario Iskander, ¶¶ 5, 7; Ex. B, D; Declaration of Shirley Chiu, ¶ 2, Ex. A.)  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (c); Sexton v. Superior Court (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1410 [“the time within which to make a motion to compel production of documents is mandatory and jurisdictional just as it is for motions to compel further answers to interrogatories.”].)  Thus, the deadline to compel further responses was November 17, 2025, 45 days plus two court days for electronic service from the date the verifications were served, September 29, 2025.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1010.6, subd. (a)(3)(B).)  Plaintiff has not shown that the parties extended the deadline to compel further responses in writing past November 17, 2025.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (c).)

The court denies Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (d).)

The court grants Defendant’s request for monetary sanctions in the amount of $2,500.00 ($500/hour x 5 hours) against Plaintiff and her counsel of record.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (d).)

ORDER

            The court denies plaintiff Jane Un’s motion to compel further responses to Form Interrogatories and for the imposition of sanctions.

            The court orders plaintiff Jane Un to pay monetary sanctions to defendant Petra Fuhriman in the amount of $2,500.00 within 30 days of the date of service of this order.

 

 

 

 

            The court orders defendant Petra Fuhriman to give notice of this ruling.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

[tentative] Order RE:

(3)   Plaintiff’s motion to compel Deposition; Compel further responses to requests for production of documents (Set Three), special interrogatories (set two), and admission (Set Three); and Request for MONETARY sanctions

MOVING PARTY:                 (1) & (2) Defendant City of Los Angeles

The court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers filed in connection with these motions.

MOTION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER

            Defendant City of Los Angeles (“Defendant”) moves for a protective order prohibiting plaintiff LA Forward Institute (“Plaintiff”) from (1) pursuing a deposition of Dr. Cris Liban (“Dr. Liban”), the President of the Board of Transportation Commissioners, or of any other board member and (2) pursuing extensive written discovery beyond the public records concerning the Board of Transportation Commissioners’ December 2024 vote or, in the alternative, that the court stay discovery regarding the Board of Transportation Commissioners until Judge Kin has resolved the writ action challenging the Board of Transportation Commissioners’ December 2024 vote and the parties have met and conferred after to discuss what discovery remains.

            Defendant moves on the grounds that (1) the request to depose Dr. Liban seeks testimony that is not relevant, (2) the deposition of Dr. Liban would invade the deliberate process privilege, (3) the burden, expense, or intrusiveness of the discovery clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to discovery of admissible evidence, and (4) a protective order would prevent unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, and/or undue burden and expense. In addition, Defendant argues that the protective order is necessary to protect Defendant from unwarranted annoyance, oppression, undue burden, and expense in responding to interrogatories, requests for admission, and requests for production concerning the Board of Transportation Commissioners.

            First, the court finds that the subject matter of Dr. Liban’s deposition is relevant.  “ ‘[T]he scope of discovery extends to any information that reasonably might lead to other evidence that would be admissible at trial. ‘Thus, the scope of permissible discovery is one of reason, logic, and common sense.’ [Citation.] These rules are applied liberally in favor of discovery.”  (Lipton v. Superior Court (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 1599, 1612.)  As Plaintiff notes, the circumstances surrounding the December 10, 2024, Board of Transportation Commissioners’ special meeting, such as who convened the meeting and why it was scheduled, are relevant to Plaintiff’s theory that Defendant violated the Fair Employment and Housing Act, state housing law, and the California Equal Protection clause by improperly blocking the project from moving forward.

Second, the court finds that the apex doctrine does not apply because Dr. Liban is not the kind of “highly placed public officer” to whom the doctrine applies, such as attorneys general, the Director of California Employment Development Department, the former Director of State Department of Health Services, state governors, and members of state licensing boards. (Contractors' State License Bd. v. Superior Court (2018) 23 Cal.App.5th 125, 131 [noting that the doctrine has been invoked to protect individuals including attorneys general, the Director of California Employment Development Department, the former Director of State Department of Health Services, state governors, and members of state licensing boards].)   Furthermore, Defendant does not provide any evidence that the Board of Transportation Commissioners is an independent city agency or that it issues administrative decisions independent of Defendant which would make Dr. Liban, as President of the Board of Transportation Commissioners, a highly placed public officer of such an agency.  Accordingly, Dr. Liban is not the kind of “highly placed public officer” to whom the rule applies. 

Even if the apex doctrine were to apply to Dr. Liban, the court finds that Plaintiff has met its burden to show (1) good cause that Dr. Liban has unique or superior personal knowledge of discoverable information in this action and (2) that less intrusive methods of discovery have been exhausted.  (Contractors' State License Bd., supra, 23 Cal.App.5th at p. 132 [An exception will be made to the general rule that agency heads and other top government executives are not subject to deposition “only when the deposing party makes two showings.  First, the deposing party must show that the government official has direct personal factual information pertaining to material issues in the action. . . . Second, the deposing party must also show the information to be gained from the deposition is not available through any other source.”] [internal quotations and citation omitted].)

            Dr. Liban states that (1) he is the President of Defendant’s Board of Transportation Commissioners, (2) he had no personal involvement in the scheduling or planning of the Board of Transportation Commissioners special meeting that took place on December 10, 2024, and (3) he had no involvement or personal knowledge of Defendant’s handling of issues concerning the proposed project prior to or after the December 10, 2024 meeting and vote.  (Liban Decl., ¶¶ 2, 5, 6.)  However, the declaration does not address Plaintiff’s concerns, such as who proposed the December 10, 2024 meeting and whether Dr. Liban has personal knowledge regarding the history of hearings and votes in connection with affordable housing projects.  Dr. Liban may have personal factual information pertaining to those material issues. 

            The court also finds that Plaintiff has exhausted less intrusive discovery methods because the parties have met and conferred regarding information related to the December 10, 2024 Board of Transportation Commissioners’ special meeting and Plaintiff has sought to depose lower-level employees who have relevant knowledge, such as Ken Husting, the Principal Transportation Engineer with the Bureau of Parking Management.  (Opp. to Motion to Compel Deposition, p. 8, lines 24-28.)  In addition, Plaintiff has served requests for production of documents, special interrogatories, and requests for admission concerning the circumstances surrounding the December 10, 2024 Board of Transportation Commissioners’ special meeting, but Defendant has objected to those discovery requests.  (Scott Decl., Exhs. I, J, K.)  Thus, less intrusive discovery methods have been exhausted.

Third, the court finds that Defendant has not shown good cause for an order preventing the deposition of Dr. Liban because Defendant fails to meet its burden to show that (1) the public has a specific interest in nondisclosure of Dr. Liban’s testimony and (2) why that interest clearly outweighs the public interest in disclosure as Defendant fails to address its burden on this issue.  (Citizens for Open Government v. City of Lodi (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 296, 307 [explaining the policy behind the privilege was not sufficient to explain the public’s specific interest in nondisclosure of the documents at issue, and thus the city “failed to carry its burden to explain what the public's specific interest in nondisclosure was in this case. . . . The city also failed to carry its burden to explain why the public’s interest in nondisclosure in this case clearly outweigh[ed] the public interest in disclosure”] [internal quotations and citation omitted].)  Defendant merely argues that Plaintiffs cannot depose Board of Transportation Commission members regarding information that is covered by the deliberative process privilege.  (Motion, p. 12:23-24.)

In addition, Plaintiff has identified factual areas of inquiry that fall outside the deliberative process privilege.  In County of Los Angeles v. Superior Court (1975) 13 Cal.3d 721, the plaintiff sought to depose each supervisor regarding discussions that occurred between the Board and the County’s labor negotiators during closed-session meetings preceding negotiations with the employee representative.  (Id. at p. 724.)  The plaintiff conceded that the purpose of the questioning was to probe the supervisors’ reasoning behind their subsequent adoption of the salary ordinance and to uncover evidence showing that the strike was a substantial factor in the ultimate legislative wage increases.  (Ibid.)  Thus, the trial court in that case erred in ordering the deponents to answer questions directed at the legislators’ reasons for voting for the ordinance.  (Id. at p. 732.)  However, the circumstances in this case are different.  Plaintiff seeks to focus on who convened the Special Meeting, why the Special Meeting was scheduled when it was, and the Board’s typical practices in approving affordable housing projects on City-owned land. (Opp. p. 5:3-8.)  These areas of inquiry do not probe into Dr. Liban’s reasons for voting or the considerations underlying how he voted in the December 10, 2024 Board of Transportation Commissioners’ special meeting.  Thus, Plaintiff identifies factual areas of inquiry outside the scope of the deliberative process privilege.  Plaintiff is therefore not barred from deposing Dr. Liban or any other board member.  

            The court grants Defendant’s request for a protective order regarding Requests for Admission nos. 59 and 60 because Defendant has shown good cause to make an order that those requests need not be answered since the needs of the case do not warrant these requests as they are not relevant to the Board of Transportation Commissioners special meeting or the matter as a whole.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.080, subd. (b); People ex rel. Harris v. Sarpas (2014) 225 Cal.App.4th 1539, 1552 [“In considering whether the discovery is unduly burdensome or expensive, the court takes into account ‘the needs of the case, the amount in controversy, and the importance of the issues at stake in the litigation’”].)

The court denies Defendant’s request for a protective order regarding Interrogatory nos. 20 through 33 because Defendant has not shown good cause to make an order those interrogatories need not be answered since Defendant fails to show that justice requires an order  to protect Defendant from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.090, subd. (b).)  Defendant has not presented any evidence to support a finding of annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden and expense and, as discussed above, information regarding the Board of Transportation Commissioners special meeting is relevant to the subject matter of this action. 

The court denies Defendant’s request for a protective order regarding Plaintiff’s Requests for Production of Documents because Defendant has not shown good cause to make an order that Requests for Production of Documents nos. 31 through 46 need not be answered because Defendant fails to meet its burden to show that (1) the public has a specific interest in nondisclosure and (2) why that interest clearly outweighs the public interest in disclosure as Defendant fails to address its burden entirely.  (Citizens for Open Government, supra, 205 Cal.App.4th at p. 307.)

MOTION TO STAY DEPOSITION AND QUASH NOTICE OF DEPOSITION

            Defendant moves the court for an order staying the deposition of Dr. Liban and quashing the Notice of Deposition issued by Plaintiff on the grounds that (1) it is not relevant, (2) the “apex” doctrine applies, and (3) the deliberative process privilege applies.

            Code of Civil Procedure, section 2025.410, subdivision (c), provides: “In addition to serving [a] written objection, a party may also move for an order staying the taking of the deposition and quashing the deposition notice.  This motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.  The taking of the deposition is stayed pending the determination of this motion.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.410, subd. (c).)

             For the same reasons as discussed above in connection with Defendant’s motion for a protective order, the court denies Defendant’s motion to stay the deposition of Dr. Liban and to quash the Notice of Deposition.

MOTION TO COMPEL DEPOSITION, PROVIDE FURTHER RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS, SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES, AND ADMISSION, AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS

            Plaintiff moves the court for an order to (1) produce Dr. Liban to be deposed by Plaintiff, (2) provide further responses to Plaintiff’s Third Set of Requests for Production of Documents, numbers 31-44, (3) provide further responses to Plaintiff’s Second Set of Special Interrogatories, numbers 20-33, (4) provide further responses to Plaintiff's Third Set of Requests for Admission, numbers 59-60, and (5) award monetary sanctions.

            If a party to the action fails to appear for or proceed with an examination without making a valid objection under Section 2025.410, the party noticing the deposition may move to compel the deponent’s attendance and testimony.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (a).)

            For the same reasons discussed above in connection with Defendant’s motion for a protective order, the court grants Plaintiff’s motion to compel deposition, ordering Defendant to produce Dr. Liban to appear for and testify at a deposition, but prohibiting Plaintiff from inquiring into Dr. Liban or any other board members’ state of mind leading to their vote in the December 10, 2024, Board of Transportation Commissioners’ special meeting.

            The court notes that Plaintiff also requested orders compelling further responses to requests for production of documents, special interrogatories, and admission.  As the court previously pointed out in the March 18, 2026 minute order, Plaintiff improperly combined one motion to compel deposition and three motions to compel further discovery responses into one motion.  Combining multiple motions into one motion is not in furtherance of judicial efficiency and economy, and circumvents the court’s reservation system.  Thus, the court denies Plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses to requests for production of documents, special interrogatories, and admission. 

            The court denies Plaintiff's request for monetary sanctions because Plaintiff has not identified every person, party, and attorney against whom the sanctions are sought in the notice of motion as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.040.

 

ORDER

            The court grants in part, and denies in part, defendant City of Los Angeles’ motion for protective order prohibiting plaintiff LA Forward Institute from (1) pursuing a deposition of Dr. Cris Liban or of any other board member and (2) pursuing extensive written discovery beyond the public records concerning the Board of Transportation Commissioners’ December 2024 vote.

            The court issues a protective order that defendant City of Los Angeles need not answer Requests for Admission nos. 59 and 60.

            The court denies all other relief requested by defendant City of Los Angeles in its motion for protective order without prejudice to its asserting appropriate objections to questions at deposition or to written discovery requests that inquire into the state of mind that may have led to Dr. Liban’s or other board members’ votes in the December 10, 2024 Board of Transportation Commissioners’ special meeting.

            The court denies defendant City of Los Angeles’ motion to stay the deposition and quash the notice of Dr. Liban’s deposition.  

            The court grants plaintiff LA Forward Institute’s motion to compel deposition as follows.

             The court orders defendant City of Los Angeles to produce Dr. Liban to appear for and testify at a deposition, to be taken by counsel for plaintiff LA Forward Institute, on a date to be agreed upon by counsel, within 30 days of the date of this order.

            The court denies plaintiff LA Forward Institute’s motion to compel further responses to requests for production of documents, special interrogatories, and admission.

            The court denies plaintiff LA Forward Institute’s request for sanctions.

            The court orders Plaintiff LA Forward Institute to give notice of this ruling.

            IT IS SO ORDERED.

DATED:  June 26, 2026

[t]he party propounding the interrogatories may move for an order compelling response to the interrogatories”]; Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. [tentative] Order RE:

 

defendant’s demurrer to plaintiff’s first amended complaint

 

 

MOVING PARTY:                 Defendant General Motors LLC

 

RESPONDING PARTY:       Plaintiff Giancarlo Ricci

Demurrer to Complaint

The court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers filed in connection with the demurrer.

DISCUSSION

Defendant General Motors LLC (“Defendant”) moves the court for an order sustaining its demurrer to each cause of action alleged by plaintiff Giancarlo Ricci (“Plaintiff”) in his First Amended Complaint (“FAC”).

The court overrules Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s first through third causes of action because Defendant has not shown that Plaintiff does not state facts sufficient to constitute causes of action on the ground that they are barred by the statute of limitations and statute of repose set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 871.21.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).)

“An action covered by Section 871.20 shall be commenced within one year after the expiration of the applicable express warranty.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.21, subd. (a).)  Notwithstanding that deadline, “an action covered by Section 871.20 shall not be brought later than six years after the date of original delivery of the motor vehicle.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.21, subd. (b).)  Section 871.20 states, in relevant part, that “this chapter applies to an action, brought against a manufacturer who has elected under Section 871.29 to proceed under this chapter, seeking restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle pursuant to subdivision (b) or (d) of Section 1793.2, 1793.22, or Section 1794 of the Civil Code, or for civil penalties pursuant to subdivision (c) of Section 1794 of the Civil Code, where the request for restitution or replacement is based on noncompliance with the applicable express warranty.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.20, subd. (a).)

As a threshold matter, the court notes that Plaintiff has argued that section 871.21 “does not apply to inadequate literature/parts claims under Civil Code section 1793.2(a)(3).”  (Opp., p. 6:7-11.)  But (1) Plaintiff’s third cause of action under that statute alleges that (i) Defendant’s failure to comply with section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3) was willful and, (ii) “[a]ccordingly, Plaintiff is entitled to a civil penalty of two times Plaintiff’s actual damages, pursuant to Civil Code section 1794(c)[,]” such that (2) it is encompassed by the statute of repose set forth in section 871.21 since section 871.20 states that it is applicable to an action against a manufacturer “for civil penalties pursuant to subdivision (c) of Section 1794 of the Civil Code . . . .”  (FAC, ¶ 45.)  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.20, subd. (a).)  

The court also finds that Plaintiff has not shown that section 871.21 does not apply to this action on the ground that it applies to actions filed after July 1, 2025.  Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.30, if a manufacturer elects to be governed by the subject chapter with respect to all motor vehicles sold in the year 2025 and in all prior years, sections 871.20 to 871.28 apply to actions already filed after January 1, 2025.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.30, subds. (a) [manufacturer may elect to be governed by the chapter within 30 days of April 2, 2025, i.e., “the effective date of the act adding this section”], (c) [“Unless a manufacturer has made the election described in subdivision (a), Sections 871.20 to 871.28, inclusive, shall not apply to an action described in subdivision (a) of Section 871.20, including actions already filed between January 1, 2025 and the effective date of the act adding this section, with respect to all of its vehicles sold new in the year 2025 and in all prior years”].)

The court, however, finds that retroactive application of the statute of repose set forth in section 871.21 is unconstitutional as applied to Plaintiff.

“[I]t has been specifically held that the Legislature may shorten or extend the period of the statute of limitations, or similar time statutes relating to procedure, and that the changed period may be made applicable to pending proceedings.  [Citations.]  There is, of course, one important qualification to the rule; where the change in remedy, as, for example, the shortening of a time limit provision, is made retroactive, there must be a reasonable time permitted for the party affected to avail himself of his remedy before the statute takes effect.  If the statute operates immediately to cut off the existing remedy, or within so short a time as to give the party no reasonable opportunity to exercise his remedy, then the retroactive application of it is unconstitutional as to such party.”  (Rosefield Packing Co. v. Superior Court in and for City and County of San Francisco (1935) 4 Cal.2d 120, 122-123; Niagara Fire Ins. Co. v. Cole (1965) 235 Cal.App.2d 40, 43 [“a statute shortening the period of limitations cannot be applied retroactively to wipe out an accrued cause of action that is not barred by the then applicable statute of limitations”].)

Plaintiff has alleged that they entered into a warranty contract with Defendant regarding the subject vehicle on October 21, 2016.  (FAC, ¶ 6.)  Thus, pursuant to section 871.21, Plaintiff was required to file an action seeking restitution, replacement, or civil penalties against Defendant based on an alleged breach of its express warranty no later than six years after October 21, 2016, i.e., October 21, 2022, which is before the effective date of section 871.21.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.21, subd. (b).)  Because retroactive application of section 871.21 would “wipe out” Plaintiff’s causes of action that were not otherwise barred as of October 21, 2022,[1] it cannot be applied here.  (Rosefield Packing Co., supra, 4 Cal.2d at pp. 122-123; Niagara Fire Ins. Co., supra, 235 Cal.App.2d at p. 43.)

The court sustains Defendant’s demurrer to the fourth cause of action for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability because it does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action as barred by the statute of limitations since (1) “[t]he statute of limitations for breaches of the implied warranty of merchantability is four years” after the accrual of the cause of action; (2) a cause of action accrues when the breach occurs, regardless of the lack of knowledge of the breach, which “occurs when tender of delivery is made, except that where a warranty explicitly extends to future performance of the goods and discovery of the breach must await the time of such performance the cause of action accrues when the breach is or should have been discovered[;]” (3) an implied warranty “is not a warranty that explicitly extends to future performance of the goods[,]” such that the statute of limitations occurs upon tender of delivery and not “when the breach is or should have been discovered[;]” and (4) Plaintiff filed this action on March 19, 2025, i.e., more than four years after the date of delivery of the subject vehicle on October 21, 2016.  (FAC, ¶ 6.)  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e); Com. Code, § 2725, subds. (1), (2); Cardinal Health 301, Inc. v. Tyco Electronics Corp. (2008) 169 Cal.App.4th 116, 143.)

The court overrules Defendant’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action for fraudulent inducement – concealment because Defendant has not shown that it does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action since Plaintiff has alleged that he did not discover the facts constituting the fraud or mistake until shortly before filing this action (FAC, ¶ 30), such that Defendant has not shown that it is necessarily barred by the statute of limitations;  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.10, subd. (e), 338, subd. (d) [“The cause of action [for fraud] is not deemed to have accrued until the discovery, by the aggrieved party, of the facts constituting the fraud or mistake”].)

The burden is on the plaintiff “to articulate how it could amend its pleading to render it sufficient.”¿ (Palm Springs Villas II Homeowners Assn., Inc. v. Parth (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 268, 290.)¿ To satisfy that burden, a plaintiff “must show in what manner he can amend his complaint and how that amendment will change the legal effect of his pleading.”¿ (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.)¿ The court finds that Plaintiff has not met his burden to show how he can amend the fourth cause of action to render it sufficient against Defendant.  The court therefore sustains Defendant’s demurrer to that cause of action without leave to amend.

ORDER

            The court overrules defendant General Motors LLC’s demurrer to plaintiff Giancarlo Ricci’s first, second, third, and fifth causes of action.

            The court sustains defendant General Motors LLC’s demurrer to plaintiff Giancarlo Ricci’s fourth cause of action for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability without leave to amend.

            The court orders defendant General Motors LLC to file an answer to plaintiff Giancarlo Ricci’s First Amended Complaint within 10 days of the date of this order.

            The court orders defendant General Motors LLC to give notice of this ruling.

IT IS SO ORDERED.
