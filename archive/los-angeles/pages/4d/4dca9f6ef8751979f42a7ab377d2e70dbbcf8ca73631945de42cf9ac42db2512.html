PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION FOR SANCTIONS AND/OR EVIDENTIARY RELIEF RE DEFENSE EXPERTS JASON ARST AND DANIEL VOSS

 

Motion filed on April 29, 2026.
MOVING PARTY: Plaintiffs Kimberly Gallegos and Alex Gallegos.
RESPONDING PARTY: Defendants Consolidated Disposal Service, LLC and Logan Jose Lizares.
NOTICE: OK.
RELIEF REQUESTED: Plaintiffs seek evidentiary sanctions and related relief concerning defense experts Jason Arst and Daniel Voss.
RULING: Denied.

 

BACKGROUND

 

On April 29, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an ex parte application seeking to specially set an OSC regarding evidentiary sanctions against defense experts Jason Arst and Daniel Voss. On April 30, 2026, the Court denied ex parte relief but treated the application as a noticed discovery motion for sanctions and/or evidentiary relief. Defendants filed an opposition on May 20, 2026. Plaintiffs filed a reply on May 27, 2026. Defendants filed supplemental declarations on May 28, 2026 and June 2, 2026. On June 3, 2026, the Court continued the sanctions motion, ordered Defendants to produce Arst for a full-day deposition on June 5, 2026, and permitted limited supplemental briefing. On June 4, 2026, the Court denied Defendants’ ex parte application for a protective order and left the June 3, 2026 order in effect. Plaintiffs and Defendants filed supplemental briefs on June 18, 2026.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.010, subdivision (g) defines misuse of the discovery process to include disobeying a court order to provide discovery. Section 2023.030 authorizes monetary sanctions and evidence sanctions for misuse of the discovery process. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subds. (a), (c).) In expert discovery, a noticed expert must produce demanded materials no later than three business days before the deposition. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2034.415.) The Court may exclude expert opinion testimony when a party unreasonably fails to produce discoverable expert reports and writings or unreasonably fails to make the expert available for deposition. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2034.300, subds. (c), (d).)

 

The “reasonableness” inquiry asks whether the conduct compromises the purposes of the expert discovery statutes, including “to assist the parties and the trier of fact in ascertaining the truth; to encourage settlement by educating the parties as to the strengths of their claims and defenses; to expedite and facilitate preparation and trial; to prevent delay; and to safeguard against surprise.” (Id. at p. 1447; Boston v. Penny Lane Centers, Inc. (2009) 170 Cal.App.4th 936, 950; Stanchfield v. Hamer Toyota, Inc. (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1495, 1504.) The Court has discretion to overlook excusable delays and trivial defects. (Boston, supra, 170 Cal.App.4th at p. 952.) Discovery sanctions “must be tailored in order to remedy the offending party’s discovery abuse, should not give the aggrieved party more than what it is entitled to, and should not be used to punish the offending party.” (Karlsson v. Ford Motor Co. (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1202, 1217.)

 

Here, the Court’s April 8, 2026 order reopened expert discovery only for the limited purpose of completing the depositions of experts Arst and Voss. The order required Defendants to reproduce the materials demanded in those deposition notices in a reasonably accessible form, including usable renditions of raw or native data files, no later than seven business days before each rescheduled deposition. The order also required both depositions to be completed no later than 30 calendar days from the date of the order. (4/8/26 Minute Order, p. 6.) Defendants did not meet the business-day production deadlines. Stream states he mistakenly read the production deadlines as calendar-day deadlines, relied on the order’s reference to Defendants’ agreement to provide file materials “one week in advance,” and instructed Arst to have his file ready on April 29, 2026 and Voss to have his file ready on April 30, 2026. (Stream Decl. ¶¶ 3-5.) Defendants then reproduced Voss’s file on April 29, 2026 at 2:03 p.m. and produced Arst’s file on April 29, 2026 at 4:08 p.m. (Stream Decl. ¶¶ 8-9.)

 

The depositions also were not completed within the 30 calendar days required by the April 8, 2026 order. The Court addressed that noncompliance on June 3, 2026 by continuing the sanctions hearing, ordering Defendants to produce Arst for a full-day deposition on June 5, 2026, and reserving the sanctions issues. (6/3/26 Minute Order, pp. 6-7.) On June 4, 2026, the Court denied Defendants’ ex parte application for a protective order limiting the Arst deposition and left the June 3, 2026 order in effect. (6/4/26 Minute Order, pp. 2-3.)

 

The remaining issue is whether further sanctions are warranted after those remedial orders. Defendants state that Voss’s deposition began on May 29, 2026 and was completed on May 30, 2026 after approximately 14 hours. (Defendants’ Supplemental Brief, p. 1.) Defendants also state that Arst’s deposition was completed on June 5, 2026 after approximately 8 hours. (Ibid.) In their supplemental brief, Plaintiffs request that the Court strike experts Arst and Voss, exclude all opinions and expert materials dated after or derived from work performed after February 18, 2026, order Voss to produce his Rule 26 testimony list and reconstruction calculations within two court days or be precluded from testifying, and impose monetary sanctions.

 

The Court declines to impose further sanctions. Defendants’ prior noncompliance warranted remedial orders to complete the depositions. Those depositions have now been completed. Striking Voss and Arst, or categorically excluding broad classes of expert opinions and materials, would exceed what is necessary to address the discovery misuse shown on the present record. Plaintiffs also have not submitted a supported amount of fees or costs for the Court to evaluate as a monetary sanction. The motion is denied.

 

CONCLUSION

Plaintiffs’ motion for sanctions and/or evidentiary relief re defense experts Jason Arst and Daniel Voss is denied.

CONSOLIDATED
DISPOSAL SERVICE, LLC AND LOGAN LIZARES’ MOTION TO COMPEL PRODUCTION OF EXPERT
FILE MATERIALS OR, IN THE ALTERNATIVE, EXCLUDE TESTIMONY OF PLAINTIFFS’ EXPERT,
PERRY PONDER

 

Motion
filed April 2, 2026.

MOVING
PARTY: Defendants Defendants Consolidated Disposal Service, LLC and Logan
Jose Lizares

RESPONDING
PARTY: Plaintiffs Kimberly Gallegos and Alex Gallegos

NOTICE:
OK.

RELIEF
REQUESTED

Defendants
move to compel production of expert file materials for Plaintiffs' expert Perry
Ponder or, in the alternative, to exclude his testimony.

RULING

Denied.





BACKGROUND

Defendants Consolidated Disposal Services and Logan Jose
Lizares filed this motion on April 2, 2026, supported by the declaration of Theodore
Stream and exhibits. Plaintiffs filed an opposition on June 9, 2026, supported
by the declaration of Katherine Harvey-Lee and exhibits. Defendants filed a
reply on June 15, 2026, supported by the declaration of Brian Coolidge.
Defendants filed a notice of errata on June 16, 2026.





DISCUSSION

The expert witness discovery statutes are intended to
provide parties with fair notice of the substance of expert testimony so that
the opposing party may prepare for meaningful cross-examination and marshal
rebuttal evidence. (Bonds v. Roy (1999) 20 Cal.4th 140, 146-147; Boston
v. Penny Lane Centers, Inc. (2009) 170 Cal.App.4th 936, 950-951.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 2034.210, subdivision (c),
permits a party demanding the exchange of expert witness information to
simultaneously demand production of all discoverable reports and writings
prepared by designated experts. If such a demand is made, all parties must
produce all discoverable reports and writings on the exchange date. (Code Civ.
Proc., § 2034.270.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 2034.300 provides that, upon
objection by a party who has made complete and timely compliance with section
2034.260, the Court shall exclude expert opinion testimony offered by a party
who has unreasonably failed to produce expert reports or writings required by
section 2034.270.

Expert discovery is governed by the statutory discovery
deadlines. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.030.) A continuance of trial does not
reopen discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.020, subd. (b).) Discovery may
thereafter be reopened only upon noticed motion and a showing of good cause.
(Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.050.)

Here, Defendants served an amended demand for exchange of
expert witness information and reports on May 12, 2025. The demand requested
production of "all discoverable reports and writings, if any, made by each
expert listed in the course of preparing that expert's opinion," with an
exchange date of June 16, 2025. (Stream Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. A.)

Plaintiffs noticed the depositions of their retained experts
in November 2025 without accompanying document requests. Plaintiffs had
previously invited the parties to cross-notice expert depositions. (Harvey-Lee
Decl. ¶¶ 1-3, Exhs. 1-2.) Defendants did not cross-notice Mr. Ponders
deposition, did not serve a deposition notice requesting production of
documents, and did not object to the manner in which the deposition was
noticed. (Plf. Harvey-Lee Decl. ¶¶ 7-10.)

Mr. Ponder was deposed on January 30, 2026. Defendants
attended and cross-examined him. During the deposition, Mr. Ponder testified
concerning calculations involving acceleration, closing speed, and accident
reconstruction issues. (Stream Decl. ¶ 4, Ex. C.)

On February 5, 2026, Plaintiffs' counsel advised that
Plaintiffs would comply with outstanding discovery obligations, including
production of responsive expert materials. (Strean Decl. ¶ 3, Ex. B.) On
February 9, 2026, Plaintiffs produced documents from Mr. Ponder's file that
Plaintiffs believed might be offered into evidence at trial in response to
supplemental discovery propounded by MFG. (Harvey-Lee Decl. ¶ 11.)

On February 19, 2026, Defendants specifically requested
production of Mr. Ponder's calculations. (Def. Counsel TS Decl. ¶ 3, Ex. B.)

Expert discovery closed on February 23, 2026.

By minute order dated February 24, 2026, the Court continued
trial to July 27, 2026, but expressly ordered that all discovery, including
expert discovery, remained closed as of February 23, 2026, absent a noticed
motion demonstrating good cause to reopen discovery. (2/24/26 Minute Order at
pp. 4-5.)

The Court thereafter reopened expert discovery only for the
limited purpose of completing the depositions of Defendants' experts Jason Arst
and Daniel Voss. (4/8/26 Minute Order at p. 5.) The Court expressly stated that
no other expert discovery was reopened. That limitation remained in effect by
the Court's June 3, 2026 order. (6/3/26 Minute Order at p. 7.)

Defendants filed this motion on April 2, 2026.

On May 12, 2026, Plaintiffs produced Mr. Ponder's
calculations in response to Defendants' February 19, 2026 request. (Harvey-Lee
Decl. ¶ 12; Coolidge Decl. ¶ 2.)

Defendants thereafter requested confirmation that the
calculations and trial exhibits constituted Mr. Ponder's complete file.
(Coolidge Decl. ¶¶ 3-5.) Plaintiffs did not provide that confirmation.
(Coolidge Decl. ¶¶ 6-7.)

To the extent Defendants seek an order compelling additional
production or requiring Plaintiffs to provide a verified certification that no
additional expert materials exist, the motion is denied.

The record demonstrates that Defendants were aware of the
alleged production deficiency before expert discovery closed. Mr. Ponder testified
concerning the calculations during his January 30, 2026 deposition, and
Defendants specifically requested those calculations on February 19, 2026, four
days before expert discovery closed. Nevertheless, Defendants did not seek
judicial relief before the discovery cutoff, did not seek an order shortening
time, and did not move to reopen expert discovery pursuant to Code of Civil
Procedure section 2024.050. Nor have Defendants offered an adequate explanation
why such relief could not have been sought while discovery remained open.

Moreover, the specific calculations identified in the motion
were ultimately produced on May 12, 2026. Although Defendants now seek
confirmation that no additional materials exist, they identify no specific
existing report, worksheet, calculation, or other writing that remains
unproduced. The present motion therefore seeks relief extending beyond the
specific production issue identified before the expert discovery cutoff.

The Court likewise declines to exclude Plaintiffs' expert
testimony pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2034.300.

Section 2034.300 authorizes the exclusion of expert opinion
only where its statutory prerequisites have been established. On the present
record, Defendants have not demonstrated an unreasonable failure to produce
discoverable expert writings warranting the extraordinary sanction of excluding
otherwise relevant expert testimony.

Although Defendants contend the calculations should have
been produced earlier, Plaintiffs produced materials from the expert's file on
February 9, 2026, and produced the requested calculations on May 12, 2026,
before trial, before the deposition of Defendants' accident reconstruction
expert, and before hearing of this motion. Defendants have not demonstrated
that the timing of production has materially impaired their ability to prepare
for trial, cross-examine Plaintiffs' expert, or present rebuttal expert
testimony. Under these circumstances, exclusion would be a disproportionate
remedy.

The Court further observes that trial courts have a
substantial interest in enforcing discovery deadlines and orderly pretrial
procedures. Permitting parties to defer discovery disputes until the eve of
trial, or to repackage those disputes as pretrial evidentiary motions,
undermines those objectives and is generally disfavored absent compelling
circumstances. Discovery disputes should ordinarily be addressed during the
discovery process when meaningful relief remains available to the Court.





CONCLUSION

The motion to compel production of expert file materials, or
alternatively to exclude the testimony of Plaintiffs' expert Perry Ponder, is
DENIED.

This ruling is without prejudice to any proper evidentiary
objection that may arise during trial based upon the evidence actually offered.
However, nothing in this ruling should be construed as authorizing a party to
relitigate discovery disputes through a motion in limine where those disputes
reasonably could have been raised and resolved before the applicable discovery
cutoff. The Court will consider evidentiary objections based upon the trial
record as it develops and in accordance with the Evidence Code and applicable
statutory authority.

DEMURRER AND MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Demurrer and motion filed on October 22, 2025.
MOVING PARTY: Defendant Los Angeles Unified School District.
RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff A. Tigranyan, a minor, by and through his guardian ad litem Anush Karapetyan.
NOTICE: OK.
RELIEF REQUESTED: Defendant demurs to the second, third, fifth, seventh, eighth, ninth, and tenth causes of action. Defendant moves to strike portions of the Complaint seeking statutory damages and attorney’s fees under Civil Code sections 52 and 54.3 and Education Code section 262.4.
RULING: Sustained in part.

 

BACKGROUND

 

On May 12, 2025, Plaintiff A. Tigranyan, a minor, by and through his guardian ad litem Anush Karapetyan, filed a Complaint against Defendants Los Angeles Unified School District, Institute for Applied Behavior Analysis, Elizabeth “Lizzie” Campbell, and Does 1 through 100.

 

On October 22, 2025, Defendant Los Angeles Unified School District filed a demurrer and a motion to strike. On June 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed oppositions to the demurrer and motion to strike. On June 15, 2026, Defendant filed replies in support of the demurrer and motion to strike.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

 

A demurrer should be overruled if the complaint states facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action “under any possible legal theory.” (Aubry, supra, 2 Cal.4th at p. 967.) Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

 

The court may, upon motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false, or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.) A motion to strike may be used to challenge a demand for relief not supported by the allegations of the pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subd. (b)(3).)

 

DISCUSSION

 

A. DEMURRER

 

Defendant demurrers to the second, third, fifth, seventh, eighth, ninth, and tenth causes of action on the grounds that each fails to state facts sufficient to constitute each cause of action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).)

 

COA 2: Premises Liability

 

“The elements of a cause of action for premises liability are the same as those for negligence: duty, breach, causation, and damages.” (Castellon v. U.S. Bancorp (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 994, 998.) “Premises liability is grounded in the possession of the premises and the attendant right to control and manage the premises.” (Kesner v. Superior Court (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1132, 1158, quotation marks omitted.)

 

A public entity is not liable for an injury arising out of an act or omission of the public entity or its employees except as provided by statute. (Gov. Code, § 815, subd. (a).) “The sole statutory basis for imposing liability on public entities as property owners is Government Code section 835.” (Cerna v. City of Oakland (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 1340, 1347.) “To state a cause of action against a public entity under Government Code section 835 a plaintiff must plead: (1) a dangerous condition existed on the public property at the time of the injury; (2) the dangerous condition proximately caused the injury; (3) the dangerous condition created a reasonably foreseeable risk of the kind of injury sustained; and (4) the public entity had actual or constructive notice of the dangerous condition of the property in sufficient time to have taken measures to protect against the dangerous condition.” (Vedder v. County of Imperial (1974) 36 Cal.App.3d 654, 659.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges he was a special needs preschool student at San Jose Street Elementary School. (Complaint ¶¶ 2, 10-11.) LAUSD “owned, controlled, maintained, and operated San Jose Street Elementary School, including all buildings, classrooms, and playground equipment therein.” (Complaint ¶ 40.) According to his IEP, Plaintiff required specialized academic instruction, constant supervision, and behavior intervention services, and had “tendencies toward self-injury and impulsive behaviors, such as jumping from heights.” (Complaint ¶¶ 12-13.) Plaintiff alleges LAUSD created a dangerous condition by (1) “[p]lacing classroom furniture, including chairs and bookshelves, in a manner that posed a hazard to a student with known behavioral tendencies like PLAINTIFF,” (2) “[f]ailing to secure or remove items that could foreseeably be used dangerously by PLAINTIFF given his documented behavioral profile,” (3) failing to supervise areas with “potential hazards, such as climbing structures and elevated surfaces,” and (4) failing to implement safety protocols for special needs students. (Complaint ¶ 43.) Plaintiff alleges this condition caused injuries when he: (1) “jumped from a chair and hit his head on a bookshelf,” (2) “climbed and jumped from a cube chair, fracturing his arm,” and (3) “fell on playground equipment and injured his ear.” (Complaint ¶ 44.) Plaintiff alleges LAUSD had actual and constructive notice through Plaintiff’s IEP, prior incidents involving Plaintiff’s behaviors at school, and the knowledge of LAUSD employees and agents. (Complaint ¶ 45.)

 

The Court finds Plaintiff does not sufficiently plead a dangerous condition of public property. “A dangerous condition exists when public property ‘is physically damaged, deteriorated, or defective in such a way as to foreseeably endanger those using the property itself,’ or possesses physical characteristics in its design, location, features or relationship to its surroundings that endanger users.” (Cerna, supra, 161 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1347-1348.) “A lack of human supervision and protection is not a deficiency in the physical characteristics of public property.” (Id. at p. 1352.) Thus, Plaintiff’s theories based on failure to supervise or failure to implement safety protocols do not state a dangerous condition claim. Plaintiff’s remaining theories based on classroom furniture, bookshelves, chairs, climbing structures, elevated surfaces, and playground equipment are also insufficient. Plaintiff does not identify what physical feature or property characteristic made the chair, cube chair, bookshelf, or apparatus dangerous when used with due care. “[A] claim alleging a dangerous condition may not rely on generalized allegations but must specify in what manner the condition constituted a dangerous condition.” (Brenner v. City of El Cajon (2003) 113 Cal.App.4th 434, 439-440.) Plaintiff’s notice allegations likewise concern Plaintiff’s IEP, behavioral tendencies, and need for constant supervision, not LAUSD’s notice of a dangerous condition in the property itself. (Complaint ¶ 45.)

 

The demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the second cause of action, with 20 days leave to amend.

 

COA 3: Negligent Hiring, Training, Supervision, and Retention

 

The elements of a cause of action for negligent hiring, retention, or supervision are: (1) the employer’s hiring, retaining, or supervising an employee; (2) the employee was incompetent or unfit; (3) the employer had reason to believe undue risk of harm would exist because of the employment; and (4) harm occurs. (Evan F. v. Hughson United Methodist Church (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 828, 836-837.)

 

A school district may be directly or “vicariously liable under section 815.2 for the negligence of administrators or supervisors in hiring, supervising and retaining” employees. (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High Sch. Dist. (2012), 53 Cal. 4th 861, 879; D.Z. v. Los Angeles Unified Sch. Dist. (2019) 35 Cal. App. 5th 210, 230 (“appellant had to prove both that Shelburne posed a risk of harm to students and that the risk of harm was reasonably foreseeable, i.e., that LAUSD knew or should have known of the risk.”) “While school districts and their employees have never been considered insurers of the physical safety of students, California law has long imposed on school authorities a duty to ‘supervise at all times the conduct of the children on the school grounds and to enforce those rules and regulations necessary to their protection.’” (Id. at p. 869, quoting Dailey v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (1970) 2 Cal.3d 741, 747.) “Either a total lack of supervision [citation] or ineffective supervision [citation] may constitute a lack of ordinary care on the part of those responsible for student supervision.” (Ibid.) “Under section 815.2, subdivision (a) of the Government Code, a school district is vicariously liable for injuries proximately caused by such negligence.” (Ibid.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges LAUSD and IABA had a duty to exercise reasonable care in hiring, training, supervision, and retention, “particularly those responsible for supervising special needs students like PLAINTIFF.” (Complaint ¶ 49.) Plaintiff alleges LAUSD and IABA “knew or should have known that their employees, including DOES 1-50, lacked the proper training, supervision, and competence to properly care for PLAINTIFF, a student with autism and other special needs who required constant supervision.” (Complaint ¶ 51.) Plaintiff alleges the negligent acts included (1) “[f]ailing to adequately train staff on proper supervision protocols for special needs students,” (2) “[f]ailing to train staff on appropriate responses to the behaviors of autistic students like Plaintiff,” (3) “[f]ailing to properly supervise staff to ensure they were providing the required level of care and supervision,” (4) “[f]ailing to implement appropriate coverage protocols during staff breaks and transitions,” (5) “[f]ailing to ensure that staff understood and complied with PLAINTIFF’s IEP requirements,” and (6) “[f]ailing to discipline or retain staff, including Defendant CAMPBELL, after incidents involving inadequate supervision.” (Complaint ¶ 52.) Plaintiff alleges this negligent hiring, training, supervision, and retention “directly and proximately caused PLAINTIFF’s injuries on the DATES OF INJURY.” (Complaint ¶ 53.)

 

LAUSD argues Plaintiff fails to plead a negligent hiring, training, supervision, and retention claim because William S. Hart is limited to sexual abuse cases and Plaintiff does not allege sexual abuse by an LAUSD employee. The Court disagrees. William S. Hart recognizes liability under Government Code section 815.2 for negligent hiring, supervision, and retention by school administrators and supervisors. Courts have applied William S. Hart and Government Code section 815.2 in cases not involving sexual abuse. (See Regents of University of California v. Superior Court (2018) 4 Cal.5th 607, 620 [university duty to protect students from foreseeable violence during curricular activities]; E.I. v. El Segundo Unified School Dist. (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 1267, 1279 [negligent supervision and training claims arising from student bullying]; Jimenez v. Roseville City School Dist. (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 594, 603 [negligent supervision claim arising from student injury while doing flips at school].)

 

For the first time in reply, LAUSD argues Plaintiff does not allege that a LAUSD supervisory or administrative employee knew or had reason to know of a particular employee’s unfitness or incompetence. Even if considered, Plaintiff alleges Does 1 through 100 included administrators, employees, agents, and representatives of LAUSD tasked with supervising or ensuring Plaintiff’s safety, LAUSD administrators and employees had notice that Plaintiff required assistance at all times, and LAUSD knew or should have known its employees lacked the training, supervision, and competence needed to care for Plaintiff. (Complaint ¶¶ 6, 14, 51-52.) At the pleading stage, these allegations are sufficient.

 

The demurrer is OVERRULED as to the third cause of action.

 

COAs 5 and 7: Negligence Per Se and Violation of the Unruh Civil Rights Act

 

Plaintiff does not oppose the demurrer to the fifth and seventh causes of action and states he has abandoned both causes of action. The demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the fifth and seventh causes of action, without leave to amend.

 

COA 8: Violation of Civil Code Section 54.3

 

Under Civil Code section 54, “[i]ndividuals with disabilities or medical conditions have the same right as the general public to the full and free use of the streets, highways, sidewalks, walkways, public buildings, medical facilities, including hospitals, clinics, and physicians’ offices, public facilities, and other public places.” Section 54.1 provides that “[i]ndividuals with disabilities shall be entitled to full and equal access, as other members of the general public, to accommodations, advantages, facilities... and privileges of... places of public accommodation... and other places to which the general public is invited.” Section 54.3 creates a cause of action against an entity who interferes with these rights. “The purpose of section 54.3, however, is not to provide a cause of action for disabled persons who have suffered physical injury but to persons who have been denied the same access to public facilities as persons without a disability.” (Urhausen v. Longs Drug Stores California, Inc. (2007) 155 Cal.App.4th 254, 264.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges “[p]ublic schools are public facilities and places of public accommodation within the meaning of Civil Code §§54 and 54.1.” (Complaint ¶ 89.) Plaintiff alleges he is “an individual with disabilities within the meaning of Civil Code §§54 and 54.1, as he has autism spectrum disorder, is nonverbal, and requires accommodations to access educational services.” (Complaint ¶ 90.) Plaintiff alleges Defendants denied him “full and equal access to public facilities and accommodations” by (1) “[f]ailing to provide the accommodations required by his IEP,” (2) “[f]ailing to ensure PLAINTIFF had the supervision necessary for his safe use and enjoyment of school facilities,” (3) “[f]ailing to implement appropriate safety protocols that would allow PLAINTIFF to safely access all areas of the school,” and (4) “[f]ailing to provide the services necessary for PLAINTIFF to safely participate in educational activities.” (Complaint ¶ 91.) Plaintiff alleges “[t]his denial of access directly resulted in PLAINTIFF’s injuries on September 25, 2024, September 27, 2024, and November 8, 2024.” (Complaint ¶ 92.)

 

Plaintiff does not oppose the demurrer on this ground. The Court acknowledges that Civil Code section 54.1 applies to policy impediments as well as structural impediments. (Hankins v. El Torito Restaurants, Inc. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 510, 524.) However, Plaintiff must still allege facts showing a denial of, or interference with, admittance to or enjoyment of public facilities, or full and equal access to accommodations, advantages, facilities, or privileges within the meaning of Civil Code sections 54, 54.1, and 54.3.

 

The Court finds the Complaint does not sufficiently plead such a denial or interference. The allegations concern failure to implement IEP-required supervision, failure to provide safety protocols, and resulting physical injuries. (Complaint ¶¶ 91-93.) Plaintiff does not identify how Plaintiff was denied admittance to or enjoyment of a public facility, or how his access to school facilities or accommodations was denied or interfered with, as opposed to alleging that he was injured because required supervision and services were not provided.

 

The demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the eighth cause of action, with 20 days leave to amend.

 

COA 9: Disability Discrimination in Violation of Education Code Section 220

 

California Education Code section 220 provides that “[n]o person shall be subjected to discrimination on the basis of disability…in any program or activity conducted by an educational institution that receives, or benefits from, state financial assistance, or enrolls pupils who receive state student financial aid.” A private right of action for damages under this section attaches when the educational institution has, among other elements, “actual knowledge” of discrimination and has acted with “deliberate indifference” in the face of such knowledge. (Donovan v. Poway Unified School Dist. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 567, 597.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges LAUSD “is an educational institution that receives and benefits from state financial assistance.” (Complaint ¶ 98.) Plaintiff alleges he “is a person with a disability within the meaning of Education Code §220, as he has autism spectrum disorder, is nonverbal, and requires accommodations to access educational services.” (Complaint ¶ 99.) Plaintiff alleges Defendants discriminated against him by (1) “[f]ailing to provide the level of supervision required by his IEP,” (2) “[f]ailing to ensure proper coverage during staff breaks,” (3) “[f]ailing to implement appropriate safety protocols specifically needed for a student with PLAINTIFF’s disabilities,” and (4) “[t]reating PLAINTIFF differently than non-disabled students by not providing the accommodations necessary for his safe access to education.” (Complaint ¶ 100.) Plaintiff alleges “[t]his discrimination denied PLAINTIFF full and equal access to the educational programs and activities provided by Defendants to other students.” (Complaint ¶ 101.) Plaintiff also alleges LAUSD school nurses, administrators, and employees “were instructed to and had notice that PLAINTIFF was to be assisted at all times while attending San Jose Street Elementary.” Plaintiff further alleges Defendants failed to implement supervision coverage protocols “[d]espite being aware of PLAINTIFF’s supervision requirements and the consequences of inadequate supervision.” (Complaint ¶¶ 14, 25.)

 

LAUSD argues the Complaint does not plead facts sufficient to show actual knowledge of discrimination or deliberate indifference. The Court agrees. Plaintiff alleges LAUSD knew of Plaintiff’s disability, IEP, and need for constant supervision. (Complaint ¶¶ 12-14, 25.) However, those allegations do not, without more, plead that LAUSD had actual knowledge of disability discrimination against Plaintiff and acted with deliberate indifference to that discrimination. The pleaded facts support Plaintiff’s theory that LAUSD failed to provide required supervision and safety measures, but they do not sufficiently allege that LAUSD knew Plaintiff was being denied access to educational programs or activities because of his disability and deliberately failed to respond.

 

The demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the ninth cause of action, with 20 days leave to amend.

 

COA 10: Breach of Mandatory Duty

 

“Where a public entity is under a mandatory duty imposed by an enactment that is designed to protect against the risk of a particular kind of injury, the public entity is liable for an injury of that kind proximately caused by its failure to discharge the duty unless the public entity establishes that it exercised reasonable diligence to discharge the duty.” (Gov. Code, § 815.6.)

 

Pursuant to Government Code section 815.6, a public entity “may be liable when (1) a mandatory duty is imposed by enactment, (2) the duty was designed to protect against the kind of injury allegedly suffered, and (3) breach of the duty proximately caused injury.” (State Dept. of State Hospitals v. Superior Court (2015) 61 Cal.4th 339, 348.)

 

Here, Plaintiff identifies Education Code sections 56345, 56363, 56500.2, and 44807, and 34 C.F.R. section 300.323(c)(2). (Complaint ¶ 106.) Plaintiff alleges his IEP “explicitly required Behavior Intervention Implementation (BII) services for 1,350 minutes per week as a related service necessary for PLAINTIFF to benefit from his education,” and that “[t]his requirement created a mandatory duty for LAUSD to ensure that these services were provided as specified.” (Complaint ¶ 107.) Plaintiff alleges LAUSD breached those duties by (1) “[f]ailing to ensure that PLAINTIFF received the BII services required by his IEP,” (2) “[a]llowing PLAINTIFF’s BII provider to take breaks without ensuring proper supervision coverage,” (3) “[f]ailing to implement the behavioral support described in PLAINTIFF’s IEP,” and (4) “[f]ailing to hold staff accountable for properly supervising PLAINTIFF as required by his IEP.” (Complaint ¶ 108.) Plaintiff alleges these “mandatory duties” were designed to protect special needs students from “injuries resulting from inadequate supervision and implementation of required supports,” and that the breach caused physical injuries, pain and suffering, emotional distress, and medical expenses. (Complaint ¶¶ 109-110.)

 

LAUSD argues the Complaint fails to identify an enactment imposing a mandatory duty. The Court agrees. The Court is not persuaded that the cited enactments provide a basis for application of Government Code section 815.6. (Ed. Code, § 56345 [IEP contents and legislative intent regarding services in IEP]; Ed. Code, § 56363 [definitions of designated instruction and services and related services]; Ed. Code, § 56500.2 [department investigation of IDEA complaints]; Hoff v. Vacaville Unified School Dist. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 925, 939 [section 44807 does not impose a mandatory duty more broadly on a public entity]; 34 C.F.R. § 300.323(c)(2) [special education and related services to be made available in accordance with the child’s IEP].) An obligation to act, without a specific mandate on how to implement the obligation, or that failure to implement will result in Plaintiff’s specific injury, is insufficient to impose a mandatory duty. (See O’Toole v. Superior Court (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 488, 510 [“A statute is deemed to impose a mandatory duty on a public official only if the statute affirmatively imposes the duty and provides implementing guidelines.”].)

 

The demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the tenth cause of action, with 20 days leave to amend.

 

B. MOTION TO STRIKE

 

In light of the Court’s ruling sustaining the seventh cause of action without leave to amend and sustaining the eighth and ninth causes of action with leave to amend, the motion to strike is DENIED as moot as to paragraph 84 and prayer for relief paragraphs 3 and 8 to the extent they seek relief under Civil Code section 52, paragraph 94 and prayer for relief paragraphs 4 and 8 to the extent they seek relief under Civil Code section 54.3, and paragraph 103 and prayer for relief paragraph 8 to the extent they seek attorney’s fees under Education Code section 262.4.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the second, eighth, ninth, and tenth causes of action, WITH 20 days leave to amend.

 

The demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the fifth and seventh causes of action, without leave to amend.

 

The demurrer is OVERRULED as to the third cause of action.

 

The motion to strike is DENIED as moot.

PLAINTIFF/CROSS-DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO ENFORCE SETTLEMENT AND DISMISSAL OF CROSS-COMPLAINT PURSUANT TO CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE SECTION 664.6

 

Motion filed on March 27, 2026.

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Saeed Zarei and Cross-Defendant LA Ride & Drive

RESPONDING PARTY: None

NOTICE: OK

RELIEF REQUESTED: Moving parties seek an order enforcing the settlement agreement, dismissing Defendant/Cross-Complainant Leila Soodbakhsh’s cross-complaint with prejudice, requiring Soodbakhsh to withdraw her BBB complaint, restraining further statements or reviews addressing the settled dispute, awarding attorney’s fees and costs, and retaining jurisdiction under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6.

RULING: Granted in part.

 

BACKGROUND

On May 23, 2025, Plaintiff Saeed Zarei filed this action. Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint on June 3, 2025. On July 22, 2025, Soodbakhsh filed a Cross-Complaint against Zarei, LA Ride & Drive, and Roes 1 through 20.

 

Zarei and LA Ride & Drive filed this motion on March 27, 2026. No opposition was filed. After the June 4, 2026 hearing, the Court continued the hearing to permit supplemental briefing. Moving parties thereafter filed a supplemental brief, amended proposed order, and request for dismissal.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

CCP § 664.6 states: “If parties to pending litigation stipulate, in a writing signed by the parties outside the presence of the court or orally before the court, for settlement of the case, or part thereof, the court, upon motion, may enter judgment pursuant to the terms of the settlement. If requested by the parties, the court may retain jurisdiction over the parties to enforce the settlement until performance in full of the terms of the settlement.”   

   

Strict compliance with the statutory requirements is necessary before a court can enforce a settlement agreement under this statute.  (Sully-Miller Contracting Co. v. Gledson/Cashman Construction, Inc. (2002) 103 Cal.App.4th 30, 37.)  To enforce a written settlement agreement under CCP section 664.6, the following three elements must be met: (1) the parties must have come to a meeting of the minds on all material points; (2) there must be a writing that contains the material terms of the agreement; and (3) the writing must be signed by the parties.  (Weddington Productions, Inc. v. Flick (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 793, 797-98.)   

   

CCP § 664.6 “require[s] the signatures of the parties seeking to enforce the agreement under [Code of Civil Procedure] section 664.6 and against whom the agreement is sought to be enforced.”  (J.B.B. Investment Partners, Ltd. v. Fair (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 974, 985.)  CCP § 664.6’s “requirement of a ‘writing signed by the parties’ must be read to apply to all parties bringing the section 664.6 motion and against whom the motion is directed.”  (Harris v. Rudin, Richman & Appel (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 299, 306; see Sully-Miller Contracting Co. v. Gledson/Cashman Const., Inc. (2002) 103 Cal.App.4th 30, 35-37 [“A written settlement agreement is not enforceable under section 664.6 unless it is signed by all of the parties to the agreement, not merely the parties against whom the agreement is sought to be enforced.”].)  “A procedure in which a settlement is evidenced by one writing signed by both sides minimizes the possibility of … dispute[s] and legitimizes the summary nature of the section 664.6 procedure.”  (Robertson v. Chen (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 1290, 1293.) 

 

DISCUSSION

The Settlement Agreement states it is between “SAEED ZAREI individually, LA RIDE N DRIVE, a California Corporation and LA RIDE N DRIVE MASTERS, a California Corporation (‘Claimant’) and LEILA SOODBAKHSH an individual and ALIREZA KALABI. (‘Respondent’).” (Yadidi Decl., Ex. D.) The Agreement bears signatures for Saeed Zarei, LA Ride N Drive, LA Ride N Drive Masters, Leila Soodbakhsh, and Alireza Kalaei. (Ibid.) Yadidi declares Plaintiff’s counsel received the fully executed Settlement Agreement on December 17, 2025. (Yadidi Decl., ¶ 7.)

 

Section 2 provides: “In full, final and complete settlement of all Claims arising out of or related to the case #25CHCV01785 Claimant hereby agrees to deliver to LEILA SOODBAKHSH or her agent the title and possession of 2019 Ford Escape with the VIN 1FMCU0F7XKUA55245 by December 15, 2025 in similar condition as it was at the time Claimant took possession per Exhibit A with 107,526 mileage at the time of delivery to Respondent and transferring the title of the above mentioned vehicle to LEILA SOODBAKHSH.” (Yadidi Decl., Ex. D.)

 

Section 2 further provides: “Claimant shall dismiss Respondents only as to case # 25CHCV01785 with prejudice within five (5) days from the date of execution of this Agreement. Simultaneously with the execution of this Agreement, Respondent LEILA SOODBAKHSH shall dismiss the Cross Complaint against Claimant with prejudice.” (Yadidi Decl., Ex. D.)

 

Zarei declares he executed the Settlement Agreement on December 15, 2025, made the 2019 Ford Escape available for pickup on December 18, 2025, and that the vehicle was retrieved by Soodbakhsh’s authorized agent. (Zarei Decl., ¶¶ 6-7.) Zarei further declares he returned the vehicle “in substantially the same condition as when it was originally received,” the vehicle “already had a defective transmission,” and “[a]t no time did I drive or repair the subject vehicle.” (Zarei Decl., ¶¶ 9-10.) No opposition was filed disputing this showing.

 

On June 18, 2026, Zarei filed a request for dismissal with prejudice as to Soodbakhsh and Ali Kalie. Soodbakhsh has not dismissed the Cross-Complaint.

 

The motion is granted in part. The Settlement Agreement is enforced. Soodbakhsh’s Cross-Complaint against Zarei and LA Ride & Drive is dismissed with prejudice.

 

The requests to retain jurisdiction and for an order concerning the pending Small Claims action are denied. The Court is enforcing the dismissal term now, and moving parties identify no remaining settlement performance requiring future enforcement. The Settlement Agreement does not require the Court to direct how any vehicle-condition damages dispute will proceed. Section 664.6 permits enforcement of the parties’ settlement terms; it does not permit the Court to add terms the parties did not include. (Hernandez v. Board of Education (2004) 126 Cal.App.4th 1161, 1176; J.B.B. Investment Partners, Ltd. v. Fair (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 974, 984.)

 

Attorney’s Fees and Costs

 

Section 14 of the Settlement Agreement provides: “[I]f any legal action or other proceeding is brought for the enforcement of this Agreement, or because of an alleged breach, or default in connection with this Agreement or any provision hereof, the prevailing Party shall be entitled to recover its attorneys’ fees and court costs incurred to enforce this Agreement.” (Yadidi Decl., Ex. D, § 14.)

 

Moving parties request $3,825, based on 7 hours preparing and reviewing the motion, 2 anticipated hours to review opposition and prepare a reply, 1 anticipated hour for the hearing, and $75 in filing and e-filing costs. (Yadidi Decl., ¶ 13.) The Court finds 7 hours excessive for preparing and reviewing this motion. No opposition was filed, and the anticipated 2 hours to review opposition and prepare a reply were not incurred. The supplemental briefing was limited to the issues identified after the June 4, 2026 hearing.

 

The request is granted in the reduced amount of $1,575. This amount consists of 2 hours to prepare the motion, 1 hour to prepare the supplemental brief, and 1 hour to appear at the hearing, at counsel’s hourly rate of $375, plus $75 in filing and e-filing costs.

 

CONCLUSION

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendants’ Motion to Enforce Settlement and Dismissal of Cross-Complaint Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 is granted in part.

 

The Settlement Agreement is enforced. Defendant/Cross-Complainant Leila Soodbakhsh’s Cross-Complaint against Saeed Zarei and LA Ride & Drive is dismissed with prejudice.

 

Moving parties are awarded attorney’s fees and costs in the reduced amount of $1,575.

 

The requests to retain jurisdiction and to order the parties to resolve vehicle-condition damages exclusively through the pending Small Claims action are denied.

 

This order does not affect the First Amended Complaint as to defaulted Defendants Nikoloz Jachvadze and Behzad Kalai.
