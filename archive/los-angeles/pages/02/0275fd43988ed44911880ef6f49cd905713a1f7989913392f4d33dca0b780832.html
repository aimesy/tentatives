DEPARTMENT 515 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV09493    Hearing Date: July 8, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication

The Motion for Summary Judgment filed by BMW of North America, LLC is denied. The alternative motion for summary adjudication of the first and third causes of action is denied.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Vera Nahapetyan (Plaintiff) filed this action against BMW of North America, LLC (BMW) and FAA Beverly Hills, Inc., doing business as Beverly Hills BMW (collectively “Defendants”), arising from her acquisition of a 2024 BMW M4 (the Subject Vehicle) on July 10, 2023, for which BMW issued a written warranty.

Plaintiff alleges that the Subject Vehicle developed defects in its suspension, steering, engine, electrical, emission, and transmission systems, and that Defendants failed to repair those defects after a reasonable number of attempts. (Compl. ¶¶ 11, 28, 34.)

The causes of action are: (1) breach of express warranty under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; (2) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; (3) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b); and (4) negligent repair. The first three causes of action are alleged against BMW, and the fourth is alleged against Beverly Hills BMW.

BMW filed a Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the alternative, Summary Adjudication. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Plaintiff requests judicial notice of an amended order denying a motion in limine in Pate v. BMW of North America, LLC (C.D.Cal., No. 2:21-cv-04915-KS). The Court grants the request as to the existence of the order, but not as to the truth of any factual finding or legal conclusion stated in it. (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (d); Sosinsky v. Grant (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 1548, 1564-1565.) As an order of a federal district court, it does not bind this Court and is considered only for whatever persuasive value it may have. (See Aleman v. Airtouch Cellular (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 556, 576, fn. 8. [“Although not binding, unpublished federal district court cases are citable as persuasive authority”].)

LEGAL STANDARD

A defendant moving for summary judgment must show that one or more elements of a cause of action cannot be established or that there is a complete defense to it. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) The motion must be granted if the papers show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).)

A defendant meets its initial burden by presenting evidence, and not by simply arguing that the plaintiff lacks evidence. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854 (Aguilar).) If the defendant carries that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) The Court strictly construes the moving party’s evidence and liberally construes the opposing party’s evidence, resolving any doubts in favor of the opposing party. (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 843.)

A motion for summary adjudication proceeds under the same standard and may be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

 

ANALYSIS

BMW challenges two of the four causes of action on two grounds: (1) the first cause of action for breach of express warranty fails because every defect presented under warranty was either repaired in a single visit or found to be operating as designed, so no nonconformity was subject to more than one repair opportunity; and (2) the third cause of action for violation of section 1793.2, subdivision (b), fails because BMW promptly commenced all repairs, because a violation of the 30-day provision would not entitle Plaintiff to restitution or replacement, and because Plaintiff has no evidence of damages caused by any violation. BMW does not address the second cause of action for breach of implied warranty.

I. Summary Judgment

BMW is not entitled to summary judgment because its motion does not dispose of the entire action. A motion for summary judgment is directed at the entire action against a party. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(1).) BMW’s motion addresses only the first and third causes of action. It offers no argument or evidence directed at the second cause of action for breach of implied warranty. Because the motion cannot resolve every cause of action against BMW, the Court treats it as a motion for summary adjudication of the first and third causes of action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

Plaintiff also argues that the motion is an improper second motion for summary judgment. (Opp. at pp. 3-4.) A party may not move for summary judgment or for adjudication on issues asserted in a prior motion that the court denied, absent newly discovered facts or a change in the law. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(2).) That bar applies only where the court previously denied a motion on these issues on the merits. The record does not show a merits denial. By Plaintiff’s own account, BMW’s earlier motion, brought on or about January 22, 2026, was challenged as improperly noticed under section 437c, subdivision (a)(2), and was not decided on the merits. On this record, the present motion is not barred, and the Court reaches its merits.

II. First Cause of Action for Breach of Express Warranty

BMW argues that the first cause of action fails because each defect was either repaired in a single visit or found to be operating as designed, so no nonconformity was ever subject to more than one repair attempt.

The Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act requires a manufacturer that is unable to repair a new motor vehicle to conform to the applicable express warranties, after a reasonable number of attempts to replace the vehicle or make restitution. (Civ. Code, § 1793.2, subd. (d)(2).) To establish a breach of the express warranty, a plaintiff must prove that (1) the vehicle had a nonconformity covered by the express warranty that substantially impaired its use, value, or safety; (2) the vehicle was presented to an authorized representative of the manufacturer for repair; and (3) the manufacturer or its representative did not repair the nonconformity after a reasonable number of repair attempts. (Donlen v. Ford Motor Co. (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 138, 152.)

A single repair opportunity does not trigger the remedy. In Silvio v. Ford Motor Co. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1205 (Silvio), the buyer delivered his vehicle for one repair attempt and then filed suit. The Court of Appeal held that the statute’s reference to repair attempts, stated in the plural, requires more than one opportunity, and that a manufacturer is not obligated to replace a vehicle or make restitution when it has had only one opportunity to repair it. (Id. at p. 1208.)

Whether a manufacturer has been afforded a reasonable number of repair attempts is ordinarily a question of fact. (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 799 (Robertson).) A nonconformity is not confined to a single component; it may encompass an entire complex of related conditions, and whether a group of symptoms constitutes one nonconformity or several is itself a question for the trier of fact. (Id. at p. 801, fn. 11.)

Each presentation of the vehicle for repair counts as a repair opportunity, whether or not the manufacturer’s agents perform work or succeed in duplicating the problem. In Oregel v. American Isuzu Motors, Inc. (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 1094 (Oregel), the court explained that the only affirmative step the Act imposes on a consumer is to permit the manufacturer a reasonable opportunity to repair the vehicle, and that whether the manufacturer’s agents take advantage of that opportunity, or are unable to find the defect, are matters for which the consumer is not responsible. (Id. at pp. 1103-1104.) The consumer need not prove the cause of the defect. (Id. at p. 1102, fn. 8.)

Here, BMW’s own separate statement establishes that Plaintiff presented the Subject Vehicle for repair on ten occasions between July 2023 and June 2025. (UMF Nos. 4-13.) That record far exceeds the single repair opportunity at issue in Silvio.

BMW characterizes each visit as a discrete defect that was separately resolved, so that no individual nonconformity received more than one attempt. Whether the recurring symptoms are discrete defects or manifestations of a single complex of related conditions is the factual question that Robertson commits to the trier of fact. Plaintiff’s expert, Darrell Blasjo, reviewed the repair records and opines that the symptoms are not separate, isolated defects but interrelated failures within the vehicle’s integrated electronic and control architecture, because modern BMW vehicles rely on shared sensor inputs for braking, stability, traction, and acceleration management. (Blasjo Decl. ¶¶ 12, 32.) He further opines that the defects were not repaired within a single opportunity or within a reasonable number of opportunities. (Blasjo Decl. ¶ 31.)

That some visits ended with a technician unable to duplicate the concern or reporting that the vehicle operated as designed does not remove those presentations from the count. Under Oregel, a presentation counts as a repair opportunity even when the manufacturer’s agents perform no successful repair. (Oregel, supra, 90 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1103-1104.)

Accordingly, BMW has not shown that the failure to repair element cannot be established, and a triable issue of material fact remains. The motion for summary adjudication of the first cause of action is denied.

III. Third Cause of Action for Violation of Section 1793.2, Subdivision (b)

BMW argues that the third cause of action fails because it promptly commenced every repair, because a violation of the 30-day provision does not entitle Plaintiff to restitution or replacement, and because Plaintiff has no evidence of damages caused by any violation. BMW adds that the out-of-service periods it addresses were partially due to the diagnosis of concerns that its technicians could not verify.

Section 1793.2, subdivision (b), provides that, where warranty service or repair is required and unless the buyer agrees in writing to the contrary, the goods shall be serviced or repaired to conform to the applicable warranties within 30 days. (Civ. Code, § 1793.2, subd. (b).)

A violation of that requirement gives rise to damages under Civil Code section 1794, which include the cost of repairs necessary to make the goods conform, together with incidental and consequential damages. (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (b)(2).) A failure to complete warranty repairs within 30 days is independently actionable and supports recovery of the incidental and consequential damages caused by the delay, even where the underlying defect did not substantially impair the vehicle’s use, value, or safety. (Ramos v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (2020) 55 Cal.App.5th 220.)

BMW has not met its initial burden. A defendant moving on the ground that the plaintiff lacks evidence must present evidence that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, evidence of the element at issue. (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 854.) BMW’s separate statement asserts only that Plaintiff has produced no evidence of damages resulting from a failure to complete repairs within 30 days. (UMF No. 14.) That assertion is argument, not evidence. BMW identifies no discovery response, deposition testimony, or other item in the record showing that Plaintiff lacks and cannot obtain evidence of damages. The burden, therefore, never shifted to Plaintiff.

Even if the burden had shifted, a triable issue would remain. BMW’s own separate statement establishes that the Subject Vehicle was out of service for 36 days in connection with the repair visit beginning January 31, 2024, a fact Plaintiff does not dispute. (UMF No. 6.) A single out-of-service period exceeding 30 days would permit a reasonable jury to find that BMW did not complete warranty repairs within the time period outlined in section 1793.2, subdivision (b). BMW’s contention that the out-of-service time reflected the diagnosis of concerns that its technicians could not verify does not resolve the question of whether conforming repairs were required during that period and whether the delay caused Plaintiff compensable harm, which are matters for the trier of fact. Together with the recurring conditions documented throughout the repair history, the undisputed 36-day period is sufficient to raise a triable issue.

BMW also contends that a violation of the 30-day provision would not entitle Plaintiff to restitution or replacement. Even accepting that a bare violation of section 1793.2, subdivision (b), unaccompanied by a substantial nonconformity, does not support the replacement-or-restitution remedy (see Ramos, supra, 55 Cal.App.5th 220), that limitation defines the available remedy; it does not dispose of the cause of action. Because Plaintiff may still recover the incidental and consequential damages caused by any delay, the third cause of action is not resolved in its entirety, and summary adjudication is unavailable.

Accordingly, the motion for summary adjudication of the third cause of action is denied.

 

CONCLUSION

The Motion for Summary Judgment filed by BMW of North America, LLC is denied. The alternative motion for summary adjudication of the first and third causes of action is denied.


Case Number: 25STCV13461    Hearing Date: July 8, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Defendant’s Motion to Vacate Default and Default Judgment

Defendant’s Motion to Vacate Default and Default Judgment is granted.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Huntington-Cortez Garden Apartments Association (Plaintiff) filed this action against Paul W. De Longe, as Trustee of the John W. De Longe and Mary B. De Longe Trust Established January 9, 1968 (Defendant). The Complaint alleges that the premises at 1025 West Huntington Drive, Arcadia (the Property) are residential real property subject to the Tenant Protection Act of 2019, and that Defendant, as landlord, raised the monthly rent to $19,000 effective January 1, 2024, in excess of the limits imposed by that statute. (Civ. Code, § 1947.12.)

The causes of action are: (1) Violation of Statute (Civil Code section 1947.12); and (2) Declaratory Relief. The Complaint prays for damages according to proof of not less than $100,000, prejudgment interest, a permanent injunction against the collection of rent above the statutory limit, and a declaration that the Property is residential real property subject to Section 1947.12.

Defendant was personally served on June 21, 2025. He did not respond, and the clerk entered his default on August 8, 2025. The Court entered a default judgment on March 6, 2026, awarding Plaintiff $100,000 in damages, $11,574.67 in prejudgment interest, $2,890 in attorney fees, and $544 in costs, together with declaratory and injunctive relief.

Defendant filed this Motion to Vacate Default and Default Judgment. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Defendant requests judicial notice of several exhibits submitted with its Compendium of Exhibits: recorded instruments concerning the Property and the interests within it (Exhibits 1, 3, 4, 13, and 28-1 through 28-3), two Redfin property profiles (Exhibit 29), the Complaint in this action (Exhibit 30), and the Declaration of Olivia Anderson filed in support of the default judgment (Exhibit 31).

The request is granted as to the recorded instruments and the court records in this action. (Evid. Code, § 452, subds. (d), (h).) The Court takes notice of the existence of these documents and of their legally operative language, but not of the truth of any disputed factual matter asserted within them. The request is denied as to the Redfin property profiles (Exhibit 29); the valuations and descriptions on a commercial real estate website are not matters of common knowledge or of such indisputable accuracy as to be a proper subject of judicial notice. (Evid. Code, § 452, subds. (g), (h).)

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A court may relieve a party from a default or default judgment taken through mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect, but the application must be made within six months. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).) After the six-month period has run, statutory relief is unavailable, and a party seeking to set aside a default judgment must invoke the court’s equitable power. (Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 981.)

A court sitting in equity may set aside a default judgment for extrinsic fraud or extrinsic mistake. (Ibid.) An extrinsic mistake occurs when circumstances outside the case have deprived a party of a fair adversary hearing; the doctrine reaches a case in which “a mistake led a court to do what it never intended.” (Kulchar v. Kulchar (1969) 1 Cal.3d 467, 471-472.)

To set aside a default judgment for extrinsic mistake, the moving party must satisfy three elements: “First, the defaulted party must demonstrate that it has a meritorious case. Second[ ], the party seeking to set aside the default must articulate a satisfactory excuse for not presenting a defense to the original action. Last[], the moving party must demonstrate diligence in seeking to set aside the default once … discovered.” (Rappleyea, supra, 8 Cal.4th at p. 982.)

 

ANALYSIS

Defendant seeks relief on the ground of extrinsic mistake, contending that his cognitive incapacity prevented him from responding to the Complaint or alerting his counsel or family to the action, thereby resulting in the judgment being entered without a true adversary hearing. Because more than six months passed between the entry of default on August 8, 2025, and the filing of this Motion, statutory relief is unavailable, and the Motion is addressed to the Court’s equitable power. (Rappleyea, supra, 8 Cal.4th at p. 981.) The three elements are addressed in turn.

I. Meritorious Defense

The first element asks whether the defaulted party has a meritorious defense, meaning one substantial enough to warrant a trial on the merits. It does not require Defendant to prove the defense will succeed, nor does it require a defense that could be resolved on demurrer or other pretrial motion; it is enough that the record discloses a defense Defendant could fairly present if the default were set aside. Defendant has made that showing.

The Complaint pleads an ordinary residential rent-control claim: that the Property is residential real property, that Defendant is the landlord, and that raising the monthly rent to $19,000 effective January 1, 2024, exceeded the cap imposed by the Tenant Protection Act. (Civ. Code, § 1947.12, subd. (a).) The statute bars an “owner of residential real property” from increasing, beyond a set annual percentage, “the gross rental rate for a dwelling or a unit” charged to a tenant. (Civ. Code, § 1947.12, subd. (g) [defining “owner” as a person having the right to offer residential real property for rent, and “residential real property” as a dwelling or unit intended for human habitation].)

The recorded instruments describe something quite different. Defendant is not an owner letting dwellings to tenants. Defendant is the lessor under a 1968 ground lease of the land itself, which fixes the rent by reference to the land's appraised value, exclusive of improvements, and resets it on January 1 of each tenth year. (Compendium of Exhibits, Ex. 1.) The lessee is not a resident but Plaintiff’s predecessor, which, through covenants, conditions, and restrictions recorded in 1968 and amended in 1979, organized the premises as a community apartment project, a common interest development in which an undivided interest in the land is coupled with the right of exclusive occupancy of a particular dwelling. (Civ. Code, §§ 4100, 4105; Compendium of Exhibits, Exs. 3, 4.) The individual unit owners hold and convey those fractional interests by recorded grant deed and encumber them by deed of trust, including an institutional loan secured by a condominium rider. These are dealings characteristic of ownership, not tenancy. (Compendium of Exhibits, Ex. 28.) The 2022 extension carried that structure forward to 2058, and the 2023 correspondence indicates that the $19,000 figure is the decennial reset of the ground rent to the appraised value of the underlying land, which the lessee acknowledged. (Compendium of Exhibits, Exs. 2, 6.)

On this record, the payment the Complaint challenges is not, on its face, a rental rate for a dwelling charged by a residential landlord to a tenant. It is a single ground rent for the land, computed on land value exclusive of improvements and paid to Defendant by the association that operates the development. At the same time, the people who occupy the dwellings hold recorded ownership interests and pay assessments to that association rather than rent to Defendant. Whether the Tenant Protection Act nonetheless governs a payment of this kind presents a substantial question that Defendant is entitled to litigate. The defense does not depend on any of the exemptions in subdivision (d), so it does not turn on the exemption notice the Complaint alleges was never given; nor is the 2023 rent agreement a waiver of statutory rights necessarily void under subdivision (l), because that bar operates only if the statute applies in the first place.

The Court does not resolve that question now, and it need not. What matters at this stage is that the recorded instruments present a genuine and substantial defense: that the Property is a common interest development subject to a negotiated ground lease rather than a residential tenancy subject to Section 1947.12. That the defense might not be susceptible of resolution on the pleadings, because it rests on instruments outside the Complaint and may raise questions of fact, does not detract from it; a defense that must be proved at trial is no less a meritorious defense for that reason. Defendant has satisfied the first element.

II. Satisfactory Excuse

The second element requires a satisfactory excuse for the failure to defend. Defendant’s excuse is his cognitive incapacity at the time the Complaint was served, and the record supports that excuse.

A defendant’s mental incapacity can furnish the extrinsic mistake that supports equitable relief. In Olivera v. Grace (1942) 19 Cal.2d 570, an incompetent defendant was personally served, defaulted, and suffered a default judgment reforming a deed; after her death, her administratrix brought an action to set the judgment aside. (Id. at pp. 572-573.) The Supreme Court held that personal service on a person who had not been adjudicated incompetent and had no guardian conferred jurisdiction, so the judgment was not void. (Id. at p. 576.) But the Court held that equity could still relieve the incompetent defendant, ruling “where there has been no adversary proceeding at all, the right of the incompetent defendant to equitable relief may be established if the plaintiff’s ignorance of defendant’s legal disability prevented a true adversary hearing as well as where the plaintiff’s fraud prevented such a hearing.” (Id. at p. 578.)

Here, the evidence establishes that Defendant was cognitively impaired throughout the relevant period. His UCLA physician assessed him in March 2026 with moderate late-onset Alzheimer’s dementia, and his former primary physician documented progressive memory deficits dating to 2021. Those records are before the Court through a custodian-of-records statement, executed under penalty of perjury by the custodian of the UCLA records, which lays a business-records foundation for them. (Evid. Code, § 1271; Supp. Longe Decl., Ex. 33.) Jean De Longe, Patti Drotter, and Lisa Silbar each describe, from firsthand dealings, Defendant’s memory loss, disorganization, and growing inability to manage his own affairs over the same period. (Supp. Longe Decl. ¶¶ 2-5; Drotter Decl. ¶¶ 2-5.) Defendant’s own conduct corroborates the diagnosis: he lost track of where the rent should be sent, could not recall directing the changes he had made, and never gave the summons and complaint to his counsel, his accountant, or his daughter, who found the served papers months later among a disordered accumulation of his files. (Longe Decl. ¶¶ 12-13; Supp. Longe Decl. ¶ 6.)

Plaintiff resists this showing on two grounds. Neither is persuasive.

First, Plaintiff invokes the presumption of capacity. All persons are presumed to have decision-making capacity, and the party asserting incapacity bears the burden of proof. (Prob. Code, § 810, subd. (a); Evid. Code, § 522; Frates v. Treder (1967) 249 Cal.App.2d 199, 204.) A diagnosis alone does not establish incapacity; the determination must rest on evidence of a deficit in mental function. (Prob. Code, § 810, subd. (c); § 811.) The presumption is rebuttable, and Defendant has rebutted it. The record shows not only a diagnosis but documented deficits in memory, attention, and the handling of finances and business affairs, of the kind Section 811 describes, corroborated by three people who dealt with Defendant directly. That evidence carries Defendant’s burden on this Motion.

Second, Plaintiff argues that Defendant had actual notice of the action. Awareness that a lawsuit exists is not the same as the capacity to defend it. The defendant in Olivera was personally served, yet equitable relief remained available. (Olivera, supra, 19 Cal.2d at pp. 576-578.) The question is not whether Defendant received the papers but whether his condition prevented a true adversary hearing. On this record, it did.

III. Diligence

The third element requires diligence in seeking relief once the default was discovered. Defendant has shown it. The successor trustee learned of the action only when the Notice of Entry of Judgment arrived in mid-March 2026, promptly engaged counsel, sought Plaintiff’s stipulation to vacate within days, and filed this Motion when the stipulation was refused. (Longe Decl. ¶¶ 12, 14; McGarrigle Decl. ¶¶ 6-9.) Measured from the discovery of the judgment, that course reflects diligence. (Rappleyea, supra, 8 Cal.4th at p. 982.)

Plaintiff characterizes the timing of the trustee's transition as suspicious and suggests that the incapacity theory is a recent invention. The record does not bear that out. The Resignation of Trustee was executed in September and October 2025, months before the judgment, and its staggered execution is explained by the need to obtain the consent of a conserved beneficiary and a beneficiary residing abroad. (Longe Decl. ¶¶ 6, 8; Supp. Longe Decl. ¶ 3.)

IV. Unclean Hands

Plaintiff contends that Defendant’s own conduct bars equitable relief under the doctrine of unclean hands. Whether the doctrine applies turns on the analogous case law, the nature of the misconduct, and the requirement that the misconduct relate directly to the transaction for which relief is sought. (Blain v. Doctor’s Co. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1048, 1059-1060.) The conduct Plaintiff identifies, chiefly the delay in disclosing the change of trustee, does not bear that direct relationship to the default, and the record does not show the kind of inequitable conduct the doctrine requires. The argument does not defeat relief.

 

CONCLUSION

Defendant’s Motion to Vacate Default and Default Judgment is granted. The default entered on August 8, 2025, and the default judgment entered on March 6, 2026, are vacated. Defendant has attached a demurrer as his response to the complaint. Defendant shall file and serve the demurrer within twenty days of the date of this order.


Case Number: 25STCV32877    Hearing Date: July 8, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Defendant’s Demurrer to the Complaint and Motion to Strike Portions of the Complaint.

Defendant’s Demurrer to the Complaint is sustained with leave to amend. Defendant’s Motion to Strike is denied as moot.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Justin Goldman (Plaintiff) filed this action against Michael Sherman, p/k/a Mike Sherm (Defendant), over two musical compositions in which Plaintiff claims a partial ownership interest, titled “Asshole” and “Baby” (collectively, “the Works”). Plaintiff alleges that he acquired producer, writer, and publishing interests in the Works, and that Defendant impaired their value and exploited them without authorization by failing to clear a sample used in “Asshole,” concealing and misrepresenting the clearance status, and continuing to monetize “Baby” without licensing Plaintiff’s interest. (Compl. ¶¶ 1-12.)

The causes of action are: (1) Fraud; (2) Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing; (3) Conversion; and (4) Unjust Enrichment.

Defendant filed a Demurrer and a Motion to Strike. Plaintiff did not file an Opposition.

 

DEMURRER LEGAL STANDARD

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Wilson v. Transit Authority of City of Sacramento (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 716, 720-21.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “‘A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or the facts alleged.’ [Citation.]” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Services (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, courts assume the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) However, they do not accept as true deductions, contentions, or conclusions of law or fact. (Stonehouse Homes LLC v. City of Sierra Madre (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 531, 538.)

“The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: [¶] (a) [s]trike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading….” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) “The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (a).)

 

DEMURRER ANALYSIS

I. Copyright Preemption

Defendant argues that the Copyright Act preempts the entire Complaint because every claim rests on the alleged impairment of jointly owned copyrights, which Defendant characterizes as a species of infringement that belongs in federal court. “A state law cause of action is preempted by the Copyright Act if two elements are present. First, the rights that a plaintiff asserts under state law must be ‘rights that are equivalent’ to those protected by the Copyright Act. [Citations.] second, the work involved must fall within the ‘subject matter’ of the Copyright Act as set forth in 17 U.S.C. §§ 102 and 103. [Citation.] ” (Kodadek v. MTV Networks, Inc. (9th Cir. 1998) 152 F.3d 1209, 1212.) Under the first prong, a state claim is preempted when it vindicates rights equivalent to the section 106 bundle (reproduction, derivative works, distribution, public performance, public display), but survives when it is qualitatively different from copyright and requires proof of an extra element that changes the nature of the action. (See Kabehie v. Zoland (2002) 102 Cal.App.4th 513, 521; Computer Assocs. Int’l v. Altai (2d Cir. 1992) 982 F.2d 693, 716.)

Defendant’s argument does not apply the “extra element” test. It rests instead on the fourth statutory fair use factor, the effect of the use on the potential market for the work, and reasons that because impairment of a copyright’s market value bears on fair use, any claim premised on impairment is really a federal copyright claim. (See 17 U.S.C. § 107(4).) That reasoning is incorrect. Fair use is an affirmative defense to a claim of infringement, not a definition of the plaintiff’s cause of action, and it does not convert every state law claim that touches a copyright’s value into a preempted federal claim. Whether a claim is preempted turns on the extra element test, and Defendant does not analyze the elements of any cause of action under it.

Because each cause of action independently fails for the reasons stated below, the Court does not decide in this order whether any particular claim is preempted.

II. Fraud

Defendant argues that the fraud claim is not pleaded with the requisite particularity. The elements of fraud are (1) a misrepresentation; (2) knowledge of its falsity; (3) intent to induce reliance; (4) justifiable reliance; and (5) resulting damage. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638 (Lazar).) Fraud must be pleaded with particularity, alleging facts showing how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were made. (Id. at p. 645.)

Here, the Complaint alleges that Defendant concealed that a sample in “Asshole” was uncleared and represented to “Williams and others” that all clearances were complete. (Compl. ¶ 8.) It does not allege when any representation was made, where, or by what means, nor does it identify the “others” to whom the representations were directed. It further alleges that Defendant made false statements to the effect that the Works were cleared, unencumbered, and properly licensed. However, it does not identify which composition, which statement, or the circumstances of any statement. (Compl. ¶ 13.) The Complaint compounds the uncertainty by referring to a single “Work” in paragraph 10 while defining two “Works” in paragraph 1. Accordingly, the demurrer to the fraud cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.

III. Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

Defendant argues that this cause of action fails because it alleges no contract. The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing supplements an existing contract; it cannot exist independently of a contractual relationship, and a claim for its breach presupposes a contract between the parties. (Guz v. Bechtel National, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 349-350.)

Here, the second cause of action alleges no contract between Plaintiff and Defendant. It alleges that Defendant owed a duty of good faith and fair dealing to co-rights holders and breached it by concealing information, withholding royalties, and continuing to exploit the Works. (Compl. ¶ 15.) A duty said to arise from shared ownership of a copyright is not a contractual duty, and the Complaint identifies no agreement between these parties for the covenant to supplement. The claim also does not incorporate the preceding allegations by reference. (Compl. ¶ 15.) Accordingly, the demurrer to the second cause of action is sustained, with leave to amend.

IV. Unjust Enrichment

Defendant argues that California does not recognize unjust enrichment as a cause of action. There is no cause of action in California for unjust enrichment. It is a general principle underlying various remedies, synonymous with restitution, rather than a claim in itself. (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1370.)

Some courts construe a claim so labeled as a request for restitution on a quasi-contract theory. (See Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 221, 231.) Even construed that way, this claim pleads no facts. It incorporates no prior allegations, and it states only that Defendant retained profits from the Works and that equity requires disgorgement. (Compl. ¶ 17.) Accordingly, the demurrer to the unjust enrichment cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.

V. Conversion

Defendant argues that the conversion claim fails because a co-owner cannot convert a jointly owned copyright and because an unspecified sum of money cannot be converted. The elements of conversion are (1) the plaintiff’s ownership or right to possession of the property; (2) the defendant’s wrongful exercise of dominion over it; and (3) resulting damages. (Farmers Ins. Exchange v. Zerin (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 445, 451 (Farmers).) Money may be converted only when a specific, identifiable sum is involved; a mere obligation to pay money will not support the claim. (Id. at pp. 451-452.)

Here, Plaintiff alleges that he is a co-owner of the composition “Baby” and that Defendant wrongfully exercised control over it and the revenues it generated. (Compl. ¶¶ 11-12, 18-19.) A co-owner of a copyright holds an undivided interest and may independently exploit the work; he may not limit the other co-owner’s independent right to do the same. (Sybersound Records, Inc. v. UAV Corp. (9th Cir. 2008) 517 F.3d 1137, 1146.) On these allegations, Defendant’s exploitation of his own co-owned interest does not deprive Plaintiff of Plaintiff’s separate interest, so the claim does not state a conversion of Plaintiff’s property. To the extent the claim rests on unpaid royalties, the Complaint identifies no specific, identifiable sum, and a mere obligation to pay money will not support conversion. (Farmers, supra, 53 Cal.App.4th at p. 452.) Accordingly, the demurrer to the cause of action for conversion is sustained with leave to amend.

 

MOTION TO STRIKE

Because the Court sustains the demurrer with leave to amend as to every cause of action, the Motion to Strike is denied as moot.

 

CONCLUSION

Defendant’s Demurrer to the Complaint is sustained in its entirety; Defendant’s Motion to Strike is denied. Plaintiff has twenty days leave to amend.
