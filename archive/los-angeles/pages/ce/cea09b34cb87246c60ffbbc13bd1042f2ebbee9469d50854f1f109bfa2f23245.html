[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING IN PART AND OVERRULING IN PART DEFENDANT’S DEMURRER

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

September 22, 2026

 

On March 28, 2025, Plaintiff Everett Maldonado filed this action against Defendants BMW of North America LLC and Irvine BMW Mini.

On October 28, 2025, the Court granted in part Defendants’ motion for judgment on the pleadings, with 30 days’ leave to amend.  On December 9, 2025, Plaintiff filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”).

On February 9, 2026, Defendants filed a demurrer to the second, third, fifth, and sixth causes of action.[1]

 

DISCUSSION

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true.  (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.)

For the second and third causes of action (failure to repair and failure to make literature/parts available), Plaintiff sufficiently alleges that Defendants failed to repair the vehicle to conform to the applicable warranties within 30 days.  (E.g., FAC ¶¶ 61, 66-67; see Opposition at pp. 3-4.)  The demurrer to the second and third causes of action is overruled.

For the fifth cause of action (negligent repair), the Court previously denied Defendants’ motion for judgment on the pleadings.  The Court overrules this demurrer on the same grounds.

For the sixth cause of action (fraudulent inducement – concealment), Plaintiff’s amendments in the FAC do not remedy the problems detailed in the Court’s order granting Defendants’ prior motion for judgment on the pleadings.  For the reasons stated in that order and in Defendants’ current demurrer, the demurrer is sustained.

CONCLUSION

The demurrer is SUSTAINED IN PART. 

The demurrer is sustained as to the sixth cause of action.  Plaintiff did not show how he can amend the FAC to remedy the deficiencies of the sixth cause of action, so no leave to amend is granted.  The demurrer is otherwise overruled.

Defendants are ordered to file their answers to the remainder of the FAC within 10 days.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

September 22, 2026

 

On December 15, 2025, Plaintiff Frank Nava filed this action against Defendant American Hartford Gold LLC, and on February 23, 2026, he filed a first amended complaint.

On March 27, 2026, Defendant filed a motion to compel arbitration.

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

Defendant’s objections to the Declaration of Frank Nava are overruled.

Plaintiff’s untimely September 17, 2026 objections to the Declaration of William Baecker are overruled.

Plaintiff’s September 17, 2026 “Response to Defendant’s Objections” is STRICKEN as an unauthorized attempt at a sur-reply.

DISCUSSION

When seeking to compel arbitration of a plaintiff’s claims, the defendant must allege the existence of an agreement to arbitrate.  (Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 219.)  The burden then shifts to the plaintiff to prove the falsity of the agreement.  (Ibid.)  After the Court determines that an agreement to arbitrate exists, it then considers objections to its enforceability.  (Ibid.)

The Court must grant a petition to compel arbitration unless the defendant has waived the right to compel arbitration or if there are grounds to revoke the arbitration agreement.  (Ibid.; Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.)  Under California law and the Federal Arbitration Act, an arbitration agreement may be invalid based upon grounds applicable to any contract, including unconscionability, fraud, duress, and public policy.  (Sanchez v. Western Pizza Enterprises, Inc. (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 154, 165-166.)

A.        Defendant Has Shown the Existence of an Arbitration Agreement with a Delegation Clause.

On April 12, 2022, Plaintiff signed a Shipping and Transaction Agreement that contained a dispute resolution provision.  (Baecker Decl., Ex. A at p. 4, § 12 [“Arbitration Agreement”].)  The parties agreed to arbitrate “[a]ny dispute, claim, or controversy arising out of this Agreement or otherwise between AHGG and the Customer, including but not limited to the breach, termination, enforcement, interpretation, or validity of this Agreement and the scope and applicability of the agreement to arbitrate contained in this paragraph.”

Under California law, for a delegation clause to be effective, the language of the clause must be clear and unmistakable, and the delegation must not be revocable under state contract defenses such as fraud, duress, or unconscionability.  (Aanderud v. Superior Court (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 880, 892.)  An unconscionability challenge must be specifically directed at the delegation provision, not the entire arbitration agreement.  (Id. at p. 895.)

In the Agreement here, the parties agreed to arbitrate “[a]ny dispute, claim, or controversy,” including disputes about the “enforcement, interpretation, or validity of this Agreement and the scope and applicability of the agreement to arbitrate contained in this paragraph.”  This is a clear and unmistakable delegation of those issues.

Defendant has satisfied its burden of showing the existence of an agreement to arbitrate, and that agreement contains a delegation clause.

Plaintiff argues that another section contains a conflicting provision: “In the event any term, condition or provision of this agreement is found void, voidable or unenforceable for any reason by a tribunal of competent jurisdiction, such term, condition or provision shall, if and to the extent possible, be construed as though more narrowly drafted to make it enforceable.”  (Opposition at p. 4.)

“Where . . . a contract includes a severability clause stating a court of competent jurisdiction may excise an unconscionable provision, there is no clear and unmistakable delegation to the arbitrator to decide if the arbitration agreement is unconscionable.”  (Dennison v. Rosland Capital LLC (2020) 47 Cal.App.5th 204, 209-210.)  To distinguish this agreement from the ones in Plaintiff’s cited cases, where the provisions referred to a “court” or “trier of fact,” Defendant notes the existence of an “arbitral tribunal.”  (Reply at pp. 1-2 [citing Code Civ. Proc., § 1297.21].)

The Court is inclined to grant the motion on the grounds that there is an enforceable delegation clause.  However, for the avoidance of all doubt, the Court will also make a determination on unconscionability.

B.        There Is No Procedural Unconscionability.

For an arbitration agreement to be unenforceable as unconscionable, both procedural and substantive unconscionability must be present.  (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114.)  “[T]he more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.”  (Ibid.)

“The relevant factors in assessing the level of procedural unconscionability are oppression and surprise.”  (Orcilla v. Big Sur, Inc. (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 982, 997.)  “‘The oppression component arises from an inequality of bargaining power of the parties to the contract and an absence of real negotiation or a meaningful choice on the part of the weaker party.’”  (Abramson v. Juniper Networks, Inc. (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 638, 656.)  “The circumstances relevant to establishing oppression include, but are not limited to (1) the amount of time the party is given to consider the proposed contract; (2) the amount and type of pressure exerted on the party to sign the proposed contract; (3) the length of the proposed contract and the length and complexity of the challenged provision; (4) the education and experience of the party; and (5) whether the party’s review of the proposed contract was aided by an attorney.”  (Grand Prospect Partners, L.P. v. Ross Dress for Less, Inc. (2015) 232 Cal.App.4th 1332, 1348, fn. omitted.)  “The component of surprise arises when the challenged terms are ‘hidden in a prolix printed form drafted by the party seeking to enforce them.’”  (Ibid.)

Plaintiff argues that there is “a high level of procedural unconscionability” because he “not have experience in buying precious metals, did not have an attorney assist him in his review, was rushed through his review of the contract by AHG representatives, and was told to invest because ‘the U.S. dollar was going to collapse.’”  (Opposition at p. 5; see id. at pp. 10-11.)  Plaintiff also argues that he “was economically vulnerable having been injured and no longer working at his job,” citing Sanchez v. Superior Court (2025) 108 Cal.App.5th 615.  (Opposition at pp. 5-6.)  The Sanchez court found “a substantial amount of oppression” when the individual “was presented with the preprinted retainer agreement containing the arbitration agreement on a ‘take it or leave it basis’; he was in danger of losing his home and had driven from Bakersfield to Costa Mesa to meet with an attorney for help; he has a 6th-grade education in Mexico, no education in the United States and very limited English language skills; and he was not advised to seek an attorney to review the agreement, nor provided the time to have an attorney review the document.”  (Sanchez, supra, 108 Cal.App.5th at p. 626.)  He also had “limited English skills, but the arbitration agreement was wholly in English, and no one provided a translation or explanation in Spanish.  He also was not given time to review the agreement before signing it.”  (Ibid.)

There is no such oppression here.  Plaintiff saw Defendant’s advertisement on a YouTube channel and reached out to Defendant to learn more about a precious metal IRA.  (Nava Decl. ¶ 5.)  He was told he “should make this purchase ‘quickly’ because the U.S. dollar was going to collapse” and Defendant “did not explain what [Plaintiff] was buying.”  (Nava Decl. ¶ 5.)  Plaintiff electronically signed the agreement but “was rushed through the process and they made [him] feel as though [he] had to sign right then and there.”  (Nava Decl. ¶ 6.)  As Defendant observes, this pressure is subjective and conclusory; Defendant did not impose any deadline, refuse him time to review the terms, or tell him the offer would be withdrawn if he did not sign immediately.  (See Reply at pp. 4-7.)

Plaintiff has not shown any procedural unconscionability.

C.        After Severing, There Is No Substantive Unconscionability.

Even if the Arbitration Agreement was a contract of adhesion and there was slight procedural unconscionability, Plaintiff has not shown any substantive unconscionability.

“‘Substantive unconscionability pertains to the fairness of an agreement’s actual terms and to assessments of whether they are overly harsh or one-sided.  [Citations.]  A contract term is not substantively unconscionable when it merely gives one side a greater benefit; rather, the term must be “so one-sided as to ‘shock the conscience.’”’  [Citation.]’”  (Carmona v. Lincoln Millennium Car Wash, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 74, 85.)

Plaintiff argues that the Arbitration Agreement is substantively unconscionable due to the limitation of liability clause, which “explicitly bars Nava from being awarded ‘incidental’ damages but permits AHG being awarded the same.”  (Opposition at p. 7.)  Under the Shipping and Transaction Agreement, Defendant has no liability “for any indirect, incidental, economic, non- economic, or consequential damages sustained, relating to, or arising from any transaction hereunder.”  (Baecker Decl., Ex. A at p. 4, § 10 [formatting omitted].)  On the other hand, if Plaintiff were to breach the agreement by failing to pay or not accepting delivery, Defendant “shall be entitled to recover from Customer the difference between the contract price and resale price, plus all incidental damages resulting from Customer’s breach.”  (Baecker Decl., Ex. A at p. 1, § 2(B).)  This is one-sided and substantively unconscionable, and the Court severs this provision under the Severability and Integration clause.  (See Baecker Decl., Ex. A at p. 5, § 14.)

Plaintiff argues that the one-year statute of limitations, instead of the statutory three-year or four-year limitations, is substantively unconscionable.  (Opposition at pp. 8-9.)  But in Plaintiff’s cited cases, the shortened limitations period applied only to the consumer.  Here, “any claim or legal proceeding hereunder shall be filed within one year of its accrual,” and it is not limited to only Plaintiff’s claims.  (Baecker Decl., Ex. A at p. 5, § 12 [formatting omitted]; see Reply at p. 8.)

Plaintiff argues that the pre-dispute jury trial waiver “is contrary to California law.”  (Opposition at p. 9.)  But “arbitration involves a waiver of the right to a jury trial.”  (Prima Donna Development Corp. v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2019) 42 Cal.App.5th 22, 35.)  “Requiring that a claim be resolved by a jury (and, therefore, rejecting the possibility that it could be decided by an arbitrator) is plainly incompatible with arbitration,” and such a waiver does not make an arbitration agreement unconscionable.  (Id. at p. 44.)  Plaintiff’s cited cases involved a waiver of jury trial for claims where arbitration was not allowed.  (See Opposition at p. 9.)

Plaintiff argues that his “claims for public injunctive relief under the UCL and CLRA cannot, under any circumstance, be compelled to arbitration.”  (Opposition at p. 11.)  The Court will stay these claims while the parties arbitrate the other claims.

After severing the provision about prohibiting Plaintiff’s recovery of incidental damages, there is no substantive unconscionability, and the Arbitration Agreement should not be invalidated.

CONCLUSION

The motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED.  Plaintiff’s second, third, fourth, and seventh causes of action are to be arbitrated.

The entire action is STAYED pending the conclusion of the arbitration.

A Status Conference re: Arbitration is scheduled for September 23, 2027 at 8:30 a.m. in Department 506 at Stanley Mosk Courthouse.  Five court days before, the parties are to file a joint report stating the name of their retained arbitrator and the status of arbitration.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING AMENDED PETITION FOR APPROVAL OF TRANSFER OF STRUCTURED SETTLEMENT PAYMENT RIGHTS

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

September 22, 2026

 

On August 10, 2025, Petitioner J.G. Wentworth Originations, LLC filed a Petition, and on August 25, 2026, it filed an Amended Petition for approval of transfer of structured settlement payment rights (“Petition”).  The Petition seeks transfer of certain structured settlement payment rights held by T.M. (“Payee”) under a settlement in connection with a wrongful death claim.  If approved, Payee will transfer to Petitioner 108 monthly life-contingent payments of $2,430.00 (beginning on June 1, 2052 and ending on May 1, 2061).  In exchange, Petitioner will pay Payee $11,200.00.

DISCUSSION

A direct or indirect transfer of structured settlement payment rights is not effective unless the transfer has been approved in a final court order based on certain express written findings by the Court.  (Ins. Code, § 10139.5, subd. (a).)

The transfer must be in the best interest of the payee, taking into account the welfare and support of the payee’s dependents.  (Ins. Code, § 10139.5, subd. (a)(1).)  When determining whether the transfer is fair, reasonable, and in the payee’s best interests, the Court must consider the totality of the circumstances, including but not limited to: (1) the reasonable preference and desire of the payee to complete the proposed transaction, taking into account the payee’s age, mental capacity, legal knowledge, and apparent maturity level; (2) the stated purpose of the transfer; (3) the payee’s financial and economic situation; (4) the terms of the transaction, including whether the payee is transferring monthly or lump sum payments or all or a portion of his or her future payments; (5) whether, when the settlement was completed, the future periodic payments that are the subject of the proposed transfer were intended to pay for the future medical care and treatment of the payee relating to injuries sustained by the payee in the incident that was the subject of the settlement and whether the payee still needs those future payments to pay for that future care and treatment; (6) whether, when the settlement was completed, the future periodic payments that are the subject of the proposed transfer were intended to provide for the necessary living expenses of the payee and whether the payee still needs the future structured settlement payments to pay for future necessary living expenses; (7) whether the payee is, at the time of the proposed transfer, likely to require future medical care and treatment for the injuries that the payee sustained in connection with the incident that was the subject of the settlement and whether the payee lacks other resources, including insurance, sufficient to cover those future medical expenses; (8) whether the payee has other means of income or support, aside from the structured settlement payments that are the subject of the proposed transfer, sufficient to meet the payee’s future financial obligations for maintenance and support of the payee’s dependents, specifically including, but not limited to, the payee’s child support obligations, if any; (9) whether the financial terms of the transaction, including the discount rate applied to determine the amount to be paid to the payee, the expenses and costs of the transaction for both the payee and the transferee, the size of the transaction, the available financial alternatives to the payee to achieve the payee’s stated objectives, are fair and reasonable; (10) whether the payee completed previous transactions involving the payee’s structured settlement payments and the timing and size of the previous transactions and whether the payee was satisfied with any previous transaction; (11) whether the transferee attempted previous transactions involving the payee’s structured settlement payments that were denied, or that were dismissed or withdrawn prior to a decision on the merits, within the past five years; (12) whether, to the best of the transferee’s knowledge after making inquiry with the payee, the payee has attempted structured settlement payment transfer transactions with another person or entity, other than the transferee, that were denied, or which were dismissed or withdrawn prior to a decision on the merits, within the past five years; (13) whether the payee, or his or her family or dependents, are in or are facing a hardship situation; (14) whether the payee received independent legal or financial advice regarding the transaction; and (15) any other factors or facts that the payee, the transferee, or any other interested party calls to the attention of the reviewing court or that the court determines should be considered in reviewing the transfer.  (Ins. Code., § 10139.5, subd. (b).)

Payee is a single 32-year-old with a seven-month-old child.  (Meniefield Decl. ¶ 8.)  Payee is employed part time, earning around $350 each day while driving for Door Dash.  (Meniefield Decl. ¶ 8.)  She receives no financial assistance from the government or from her family.  (Meniefield Decl. ¶ 8.)  She also does not receive any medical assistance for her child, but she has been trying to work with Medicare to provide insurance for her child because she was born with a disability.  (Meniefield Decl. ¶ 8.)  Payee intends to use the funds to find a new apartment because she is being evicted.  (Meniefield Decl. ¶ 12.)  She also intends to use the funds for her child’s medical bills and her sister’s funeral.  (Meniefield Decl. ¶ 12.)

Payee has completed eight prior transfers, dismissed one Petition, and had one transfer denied.

On February 7, 2025, in Case No. 24STCP04081, the Court approved a transfer of thirty-six monthly payments of $2,430.00 each (beginning on December 1, 2026 and ending on November 1, 2029) in exchange for $56,000.00.  Those funds were intended to be used to secure more permanent housing and purchase a reliable vehicle so Payee could expand her search for employment.

On March 3, 2025, in Case No. 24CMCP00190, a Petition was voluntarily dismissed.

On April 3, 2025, in Case No. 25STCP00644, the Court approved a transfer of twenty-four monthly payments of $2,430.00 each (beginning on December 1, 2029 and ending on November 1, 2031) in exchange for $24,500.00.  Those funds were intended to be used to help furnish Payee’s new apartment and stay current on bills, including a car loan, while unemployed.

On June 11, 2025, in Case No. 25STCP01672, the Court approved a transfer of twenty-six monthly payments of $2,430.00 each (beginning on December 1, 2031 and ending on January 1, 2034) in exchange for $16,000.00.  Those funds were intended to be used to stay ahead of rent payments and get caught up on bills while unemployed.

On September 16, 2025, in Case No. 25NNCP00580, the Court approved a transfer of thirty-six monthly payments of $2,430.00 each (beginning February 1, 2034 and ending on January 1, 2037) in exchange for $30,013.04.  Those funds were intended to be used for IVF fertility treatments while single and unemployed.

On December 12, 2025, in Case No. 25STCP04287, the Court approved a transfer of thirty monthly payments of $2,430.00 each (beginning on February 1, 2037 and ending on July 1, 2039) in exchange for $20,000.00.  Those funds were intended to be used for IVF fertility treatments while unemployed.

On January 8, 2026, in Case No. 25STCP04687, the Court approved a transfer of twenty-five monthly payments of $2,430.00 each (beginning August 1, 2039 and ending on August 1, 2041) in exchange for $10,000.00.  Those funds were intended to be used for fertility treatments while unemployed.

On March 16, 2026, in Case No. 26STCP00406, the Court approved a transfer of eight monthly payments of $2,430.00 each (beginning on April 1, 2026 and ending on November 1, 2026) and nine monthly payments of $2,430.00 each (beginning on September 1, 2041 and ending on May 1, 2042) in exchange for $18,138.17.  Those funds were intended to be used for fertility treatments while unemployed.

On May 12, 2026, in Case No. 26STCP01341, the Court approved a transfer of 120 monthly life-contingent payments of $2,430.00 each (beginning on June 1, 2042 and ending on May 1, 2052) in exchange for $30,000.00.  Those funds were intended to be used for the purchase of a used vehicle.

On July 31, 2026, in Case No. 26STCP01982, the Court denied a transfer of 144 monthly payments of $2,430.00 each (beginning on June 1, 2052 and ending on May 1, 2064) in exchange for $10,000.00.  Those funds were intended to be used for expenses accrued with Payee’s new child while she was unemployed.  Specifically, Payee “need[ed] funds to help with [her] pediatrician bills since [her] new born [sic] has disabilities.”  The Court found that “[t]he exchange of $10,000 for a total sum of $349,920 is extremely disproportional, particularly in light of the fact that TM is unemployed without other sources of income and has an infant to provide for.”  The Court also observed that “[f]rom just February, 2025, [T.M.] has sold off her right to collect $2,430 a month from 4/1/2026 to 5/1/2052.  In eight separate petitions, [T.M.] has received $204,500, and surrendered her right to receive a total of $775,170.”  The Court concluded that, “based upon [T.M.]’s prior cases and financial management, the Court cannot find such a transfer is remotely in her best interests.”

With this similar Petition, the Court shares the concerns of the Court in Case No. 26STCP01982.  The Court is also concerned about the inconsistencies in Payee’s proffered reasons for the transfers.  Payee’s February 2, 2026 declaration (Case No. 26STCP00406) stated that she had no minor children, she needed the funds to further her attempts at pregnancy, and she had “unfortunately been unsuccessful in [her] first round of treatments and then conception, but the doctors still have hope.”  Less than three months later, Payee’s April 30, 2026 declaration (Case No. 26STCP01341) stated that she had a four-month-old child.  Payee’s July 29, 2026 declaration (Case No. 26STCP01982) also stated that she had a four-month-old child and remained unemployed.  Now, Payee’s August 6, 2026 declaration—only eight days later—states that she has a seven-month-old child, works part-time, and is being evicted.

In less than twenty months, Payee has received and spent $204,651.21 while remaining unemployed, aside from her recent part-time employment.  She has sold nearly all of her entitlement to future payments.  Considering all the factors, under these circumstances, the Court cannot find that the transfer would be in Payee’s best interest.

CONCLUSION

The Petition is DENIED.
