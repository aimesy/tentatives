DEPARTMENT SEL LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 22NWCV00028    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: SEL

REVISED TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Plaintiff September Properties, LLC Motions to Compel on Defendants Cynthia Jocson and Randolph Jocson to Appear for Deposition are GRANTED.

 

Plaintiff is awarded $810.00 in total for both Motions, payable within 60 days.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is an action for breach of a lease agreement regarding real property located at 10754 Artesia Blvd., Cerritos, California. On January 13, 2022, Plaintiff September Properties, LLC (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants Pageantry Props and Decor, Inc., Striking Elegance Corporation, Total Production Group, Inc., Cynthia Jocson, Stephen Thomas Mcallister, Randolph Jocson, and Does 1 to 50.

 

On June 1, 2022, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (FAC). The FAC alleges the following causes of action: 1) Breach of Contract, 2) Breach of Guaranty, and 3) Common Count: Goods and Services Rendered.

 

On October 23, 2025, Plaintiff served deposition notices on Defendants Cynthia Jocson and Randolph Jocson (collectively, “Jocson Defendants”), setting their depositions for November 13 and 14, 2025, respectively. (LeMaster-Dwyer Decls., ¶ 2, Exh. 2.) To date, Jocson Defendants have not attended their depositions and have not provided alternative deposition dates. (LeMaster-Dwyer Decls., ¶ 11.)

 

Plaintiff now moves to compel the depositions of Jocson Defendants.

 

The Court notes that Plaintiff has filed two separate Motions for each Defendant. However, as the Motions are substantially similar, the Court will address the Motions together.

 

No oppositions have been filed as of June 29, 2026.


Legal Standard

 

“If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action. . .without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice, the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.450, subd. (a).)¿ 

 

Meet and Confer

 

“The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).)

 

The parties’ meet and confer efforts are detailed in the section below.

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiff now moves to compel the depositions of Jocson Defendants.

 

Plaintiff provides the following context regarding Plaintiff’s attempts to notice Jocson Defendants’ depositions: After Plaintiff initially noticed Defendants’ depositions, Defendants’ prior counsel requested that Plaintiff take the depositions off-calendar to allow Defendants to find a new attorney. (LeMaster-Dwyer Decls., ¶ 3.) Plaintiff served a second deposition notice, setting the depositions for December 2 and 4, 2025. (LeMaster-Dwyer Decls., ¶ 4, Exh. 2.)

 

On November 21, 2025, Jocson Defendants’ new counsel emailed Plaintiff’s counsel, requesting that the depositions be delayed and stating that he would provide dates shortly after the New Year for the depositions to take place. (LeMaster-Dwyer Decls., ¶ 5, Exh. 3.) On December 8, 2025, Plaintiff’s counsel requested an update on the alternative dates. (LeMaster-Dwyer Decls., ¶ 6. Exh. 4.) On December 12, 2025, Plaintiff’s counsel sent another follow-up email, and Defense counsel responded stating that he would provide deposition dates by December 17, 2025. (LeMaster-Dwyer Decls., ¶ 7, Exh. 5.)

 

On December 23, 2025, after Defense counsel failed to provide dates, Plaintiff’s counsel served deposition notices on Jocson Defendants, setting the depositions for January 21 and 21, 2026. (LeMaster-Dwyer Decls., ¶ 8, Exh. 6.) On January 16, 2026, Defense counsel informed Plaintiff’s counsel that Jocson Defendants would not be available on those dates, but did not provide further explanation. (LeMaster-Dwyer Decls., ¶ 9, Exh. 7.)

 

After giving Defense counsel one more chance to provide alternative dates for the depositions, Defense counsel thereafter failed to provide dates, and Plaintiff filed this Motion on January 21, 2026. (LeMaster-Dwyer Decls., ¶¶ 10-11, Exh. 8.)

 

No oppositions have been filed.

 

The Court initially notes that, on October 31, 2025, Jocson Defendants filed Substitutions of Attorney, removing their former counsel. Based on the case file, Jocson Defendants remain self-represented. No Substitution of Attorney has been filed.

 

The Court finds that, to date, Jocson Defendants’ depositions have not been taken, and they have not provided alternative dates for the depositions.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s Motions to Compel Jocson Defendants to Appear for Deposition are granted. The parties shall meet and confer to determine the date, time, and location of Jocson Defendants’ depositions. The depositions shall take place no later than 30 days from the date of the Court’s issuance of this Order. The date may be extended by agreement of the parties.

 

Sanctions

 

“If a motion under subdivision (a) is granted, the court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) in favor of the party who noticed the deposition and against the deponent or the party with whom the deponent is affiliated, unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.450, subd. (g)(1).)

 

The Motion regarding Defendant Randolph Jocson requests $2,607.40 in sanctions for preparing the Motion. The Motion regarding Defendant Cynthia Jocson requests $4,171.40 in sanctions, reflecting $2,476.70 for preparing the Motion plus $1,694.70 for preparing an ex parte to advance the Motion to a date before trial.

 

The Court clarifies that, at the time the Motions were filed, trial was set for April 27, 2026. The anticipated ex parte to advance the Motion regarding Defendant Cynthia Jocson was never filed. Assumingly, the ex parte to advance was not needed because the parties filed a Stipulation to Continue Trial, and trial was continued to October 5, 2026.

 

The Court therefore reduces sanctions, considering that no opposition and reply were filed and that the Motions are substantially similar. Plaintiff is awarded $810.00 in total for both Motions, payable within 60 days.

 


Case Number: 22NWCV01123    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: SEL

REVISED TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendants Access Services, California Transit, Inc., and Mynor Ruben Hernandez’s Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the first and third causes of action and is GRANTED as to the fourth and fifth causes of action.

 

Defendants to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is a motor vehicle negligence action. On October 27, 2022, Plaintiff Raul Heredia (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants Access Services, California Transit, Inc., Los Angeles County, Mynor Ruben Hernandez, Melissa Elaine Doolittle, and Does 1 to 20.

 

On April 24, 2023, Defendants/Cross-Complainants Access Services and California Transit filed the Cross-Complaint against Cross-Defendant Melissa Elaine Doolittle and Roes 1 to 100, alleging cases of action for 1) Total Equitable Indemnity, 2) Partial Equitable Indemnity, 3) Declaratory Relief, and 4) Property Damage.

 

On June 26, 2023, Defendant Los Angeles County was dismissed without prejudice.

 

On July 31, 2024, Defendants FCA USA LLC and FCA US LLC were named as Doe 1 and 2, respectively.

 

On October 18, 2024, Defendant FCA USA LLC was dismissed without prejudice.

 

On December 27, 2024, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (FAC) against Defendants Access Services, California Transit, Inc., Mynor Ruben Hernandez, Melissa Elaine Doolittle, FCA US LLC, and Does 3 to 20. The FAC alleges that Plaintiff is a legally blind and disabled person. On December 15, 2021, Plaintiff requested transportation services from Defendant Access Services, which coordinated his request to Defendant California Transit, Inc., a paratransit operator. Defendant California Transit picked up Plaintiff in a van driven by Defendant Hernandez. The Complaint alleges that Defendant FCA US LLC and Defendant Access Service manufactured and/or modified the van in such a manner that affected the seating and safety systems of the van. The Complaint alleges that Defendant Hernandez failed to properly secure Plaintiff and/or failed to warn. The van came to a complete stop for the traffic conditions in front of it. At the same time, Defendant Doolittle was driving a Honda CRV at an excessive speed and was unable to stop in time, colliding into Defendant Hernandez’s van. As a result, Plaintiff was impacted by the collision and suffered injuries.

 

The Complaint alleges the following causes of action:

 

1.    Negligence (against all Defendants);

2.    Negligence Per Se (against Defendant Doolittle);

3.    Negligent Entrustment (against Defendants Access Services and California Transit, Inc.);

4.    Strict Product Liability: Design Defect (against Defendants Access Services and FCA US LLC); and

5.    Strict Product Liability: Manufacturing Defect (against Defendants Access Services and FCA US LLC).

 

Defendants Access Services, California Transit, Inc., and Mynor Ruben Hernandez (hereinafter, “Defendants”) now move for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication, as to the first, third, fourth, and fifth causes of action.

 

Requests for Judicial Notice

 

Defendants’ Request for Judicial Notice of the FAC is granted.

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

Plaintiff’s Evidentiary Objections are overruled as to Nos. 1-4. The Court notes that Nos. 1-2 are improper objections to asserted facts made in the separate statement, not specific evidence.

 

Defendants’ Evidentiary Objections are overruled as to Nos. 1, 2, and 7, and sustained as to Nos. 3-6.

 

Defendants’ Evidentiary Objection No. 7 argues that the Declaration of Declaration of Christopher T. Aumais in support of the Opposition refers to “Exhibit H” offered in support of Defendants’ Motion, but no such exhibit exists. The Court notes that the Supplemental Declaration of Christopher T. Aumais in support of the Opposition explains that such reference to Exhibit H was a mistake, and Plaintiff submitted the evidence as Exhibit 5. (Aumais Supp. Decl., ¶¶ 2-9.) Defendant Access Services provided and verified such exhibit in discovery. (Ibid.) The Court considers such evidence.


Legal Standard

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119, internal citations omitted.) “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67, citing FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 381-382.)

 

In moving for summary judgment, a defendant has met its “burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)

 

Once a moving party has satisfied the initial burden of proof, the burden “shifts to the opposing party to show, by responsive separate statement and admissible evidence, that triable issues of fact exist.” (Ostayan v. Serrano Reconveyance Co. (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1411, 1418, disapproved on other grounds by Black Sky Cap., LLC v. Cobb (2019) 7 Cal.5th 156, 165.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

 

Discussion

 

          First Cause of Action – Negligence

 

In order to state a claim for negligence, Plaintiff must allege the elements of (1) “the existence of a legal duty of care,” (2) “breach of that duty,” and (3) “proximate cause resulting in an injury.” (McIntyre v. Colonies-Pacific, LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 664, 671.) 

 

Defendants argue that Plaintiff cannot prove the element of breach of duty. Firstly, it is undisputed that Defendants’ van was not operated in a reckless or unsafe manner. The FAC admits that Defendant Doolittle, not Defendant Hernandez, negligently operated her vehicle, causing the collision. (See FAC, ¶ 9.) Next, there is no evidence that Defendant Hernandez improperly secured Plaintiff in the back seat. Defendants offer evidence that Hernandez assisted Plaintiff into the Access vehicle, guided him into a seat, and helped Plaintiff put on his seat belt. (Hernandez Decl., ¶ 3; McEwan Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. A, Heredia Depo., 41:25-42:9.) Plaintiff was restrained in the back seat at the time of the accident. (Hernandez Decl., ¶ 3, Chrisman Decl., ¶¶ 2-6, Exh. A; McEwan Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. A, Heredia Depo., 42:15-22; 43:2-4; 48:13-15; 49:8-14.) A few seconds after the incident, Plaintiff removed his seat belt restraint himself, as seen in the video footage. (Chrisman Decl., ¶¶ 2-6, Exh. A; McEwan Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. C.) Further, Plaintiff had never had any issues with the seat belt restraint prior to the accident, where he had ridden with Access Services hundreds of times. (McEwan Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. A, Heredia Depo., 29:18-21, 33:5-10.) Plaintiff did not believe that there was anything wrong with the seat belt in the van. (McEwan Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. A, Heredia Depo., 44:8-10.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that a higher standard of care applies for common carriers pursuant to Civil Code section 2100 [“A carrier of persons for reward must use the utmost care and diligence for their safe carriage, must provide everything necessary for that purpose, and must exercise to that end a reasonable degree of skill.”]. Plaintiff asserts that such duty includes an obligation to protect passengers from reasonably foreseeable harm, such as by securing a disabled passenger. Although Defendants raised a defense to common carrier liability in their Answer, Defendants do not address this heightened duty of care on the Motion.

 

Plaintiff does not allege that Defendant Hernandez negligently drove the van, causing the collision. Rather, Plaintiff alleges that Hernandez improperly secured Plaintiff and/or failed to provide a safe environment. Even if the ordinary care standard applied, triable issues of fact exist as to duty and causation. Plaintiff offers the following evidence in support:

 

-      Plaintiff does not recall whether Defendant Hernandez or Plaintiff fastened his seatbelt, but testified that both the lap and shoulder belts were on at the time of the impact. (Aumais Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 1, Heredia Depo., 42.)

-      When asked whether the seat belt restrained him during the impact, Plaintiff testified “No,” and that the belt did not restrain him “the way it should have.” (Aumais Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 1, Heredia Depo., 48-49.)

-      In response to Defendants’ Form Interrogatory No. 17. 1 (offered in support of the Motion), regarding Request for Admission No. 23, Plaintiff states that the dashcam video footage “shows that Plaintiff was not in fact restrained properly at the time of the incident” and that the restraint systems “show that Plaintiff was thrown from his seat, and ended up in a reclined position.” (McEwan Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. C.)

-      The video footage of the incident further shows that Plaintiff was wearing only the shoulder portion of his restraint and had no lap belt fastened across his pelvis. (McEwan Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. A; Aumais Supp. Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. 5.)

 

Even if Plaintiff’s own seatbelt was fastened, Plaintiff argues that a second triable issue of fact exists. Specifically, Plaintiff theorizes that an unsecured seatbelt from another seat caused Plaintiff’s laceration above this eye right. Plaintiff offers the following evidence in support:

 

-      Plaintiff testified that, though belted, he was struck in the head and sustained a laceration above his right eye. (Aumais Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 1, Heredia Depo., 49-51.)

-      Plaintiff testified that, on impact, his body went “forward and … back,” something “struck [him] in the head,” his sunglasses “flew off,” he lost consciousness, and he regained awareness “on the floor” of the van. (Aumais Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 1, Heredia Depo., 51-53.)

-      While the van was stopped, Defendant Doolittle’s vehicle struck the rear and right rear quarter of the van. (Aumais Supp. Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. 6.)

-      The dashcam video footage is reasonably susceptible to interpretation that, on impact, an unsecured seat belt at the C-pillar (forward of Plaintiff, serving a seat position other than his own) swung freely through the cabin. (McEwan Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. A.)

 

Plaintiff further argues that it is immaterial that he had no prior issues with the seat belts in Access Transit’s vehicles. Plaintiff additionally asserts that UMF No. 11 [“Hernandez was not at fault for the subject accident.”] and UMF No. 25 [“Plaintiff was not injured as a result of any design or manufacturing defect […].”] are improper legal conclusions and not facts.

 

Alternatively, Plaintiff requests that the Court continue the Motion pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (h), to allow Plaintiff to take the depositions of Defendant Hernandez and Defendants’ Person Most Qualified. Plaintiff’s counsel served notices of deposition prior to filing the opposition, but Defendants have not yet provided dates for the deposition. (Aumais Decl., ¶ 10.) Plaintiff argues that the depositions will reveal pertinent information including the adequacy of Plaintiff’s securement, the fitness of the subject van, and other issues raised in the Motion. (Aumais Decl., ¶ 11.)

 

In reply, Defendants note that the pleadings frame the issue on summary judgment and that Plaintiff did not raise a “common carrier” theory of liability in the FAC. Further, Plaintiff offers no evidence that Defendants are common carriers under Civil Code section 2168 such that a heighted standard of care applies.

 

Further, Defendants argue that a party cannot introduce their own discovery responses as evidence. To the extent Plaintiff relies on his own testimony that he was thrown from his seat, lost consciousness, and ended up on the floor, such assertions are contradicted by the video footage. In the video, Plaintiff remained in his seat after the impact and removed the seatbelt himself. Additionally, Plaintiff speculates that an unsecured seatbelt hit him in the head, but the video footage shows that Plaintiff’s head struck the top interior of the van as a result of the collision.

 

Defendants further argue that the Court should not grant Plaintiff’s request for a continuance, because Plaintiff has not shown diligence in noticing the subject depositions. The Motion was filed on February 13, 2026. Plaintiff served the notices of deposition only 12 days before the opposition was due—such notices do not provide for the minimum notice period required under Code of Civil Procedure sections 2025.270, subdivision (a) and 1010.6. (McEwan Decl., ¶¶ 3-4, Exhs. A-B.) Defendants timely objected to the deposition notices.

 

Firstly, the Court finds that, as to “common carrier” duty of care argument, such issue was not raised in the pleadings, and is thus, is not the proper subject of this Motion. (See Huntsman-West Foundation v. Smith (2024) 104 Cal.App.5th 1117, 1133 [“[A] defendant moving for summary judgment need address only the issues raised by the complaint; the plaintiff cannot bring up new, unpleaded issues in his or her opposing papers.”]; see also Lemke v. Sutter Roseville Medical Center (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 1292, 1299, fn. 2 [“A party cannot successfully resist summary judgment on a theory not pleaded.”].) The Court agrees that Plaintiff offers no authority supporting the contention that Defendants affirmatively placed the common carrier question at issue when they raised it in their Answer. Regardless, Plaintiff has not presented evidence that Defendants are “common carriers” pursuant to Civil Code 2168.

 

The Court further notes that Plaintiff’s own responses to Defendants’ Form Interrogatories cannot serve as evidence to create a triable issue of fact. (See Great American Ins. Cos. v. Gordon Trucking, Inc. (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 445, 450 [“[A] responding party may not use its own interrogatory responses in its own favor.”], citing Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.410.) The Court primarily relies on the video footage, Plaintiff’s deposition testimony to the extent it is consistent with the video footage, and Defendant Hernandez’s declaration in making its decision.

 

The Court finds that it is generally undisputed that Defendant Hernandez buckled Plaintiff’s seat belt. Hernandez declared that he assisted Plaintiff into the van and helped him to put on his seatbelt. (Hernandez Decl., ¶ 3.) Plaintiff testified that both the lap and should belts were on at the time of impact, but that he does not remember whether he or Defendant Hernandez put on the buckle. (Aumais Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 1, Heredia Depo., 42.) Although the video footage is unclear as to the initial positioning of the seat belt, it clearly shows Plaintiff unbuckling his seat belt immediately following the accident. (McEwan Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. A.)

 

However, the Court finds that Defendants have failed to meet their burden of proof, and a triable issue of fact exists, as to whether Defendant Hernandez put on the seat belt properly such that his duty of care to Plaintiff was breached. A triable issue of facts also exists as to whether the van’s other seat belts and fixtures were properly secured. The Court observes from the video footage that, as a result of the accident, Plaintiff jolted forward and then backward into a reclined position, where the seat had reclined. (McEwan Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. A.) Plaintiff remains in this reclined position after the accident. (Ibid.) Further, it is undisputed that Plaintiff suffered a laceration above his left eye as a result of the jolting. (Aumais Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 1, Heredia Depo., 49-51.) Despite both parties’ arguments, the Court finds that it is unclear from the video footage 1) whether Plaintiff’s seat belt is on correctly, and 2) what caused Plaintiff’s laceration. Interpreting the evidence in light most favorable to Plaintiff, the Court finds that a reasonable jury could determine from the evidence that Defendant Hernandez did not properly secure Plaintiff or ensure that the vehicle was in appropriate condition.

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the first cause of action.

 

          Third Cause of Action – Negligent Entrustment

 

To hold a defendant liable for negligent entrustment, the plaintiff must prove that (1) the driver was negligent in operating the vehicle; (2) that the defendant was an owner of the vehicle operated by the driver; (3) that the defendant knew, or should have known, that the driver was incompetent or unfit to drive the vehicle; (4) that the defendant permitted the driver to use the vehicle; and (5) that the driver’s incompetence or unfitness to drive was a substantial factor in causing harm to the plaintiff. (See Jeld-Wen, Inc. v. Superior Court (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 853, 863-64.)

 

Defendants argue that the undisputed facts demonstrate that Defendant Hernandez was not negligent, incompetent, or unfit to operate the vehicle on the date of the incident. Further, as previously argued by Defendants, there is no evidence that any negligent entrustment was a substantial factor in causing harm to Plaintiff. Defendants cite to the same evidence in support of the negligence cause of action.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the negligent entrustment claim is based on two theories: 1) Defendants entrusted operation to a driver they knew or should have known was not competent to properly load, secure, and report on transport of disabled passengers (FAC, ¶ 55), and 2) Defendants entrusted a van they knew or should have known “contained defective passenger seating and safety systems that posed a significant risk to its occupants” (FAC, ¶ 56). Defendants’ evidence does not negate either theory. Further, as to the vehicle unfitness argument, the video footage shows that “Plaintiff’s seat folded rearward on impact and failed to provide occupant support.” (Opp., 12:18.) Additionally, pre-incident maintenance history for the van shows that it experienced a passenger sliding-door malfunction requiring a tow on November 6, 2021, and a sliding-door re-adjustment on November 8, 2021. (Aumais Supp. Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. 6.) Such evidence suggests that a triable issue of fact exists as to whether Defendants knew or should have known the van was not fit to safely transport a disabled passenger.

 

In reply, Defendants reiterate their arguments. Defendants assert that Plaintiff provides no evidence of negligent entrustment. Further, Plaintiff fails to demonstrate how an issue with the sliding door would be a substantial factor in causing Plaintiff’s injuries.

 

The Court finds that Defendants’ Motion primarily rests on the argument that Defendant Hernandez did not negligently operate the vehicle (as discussed in the first cause of action), and thus, the first element of negligent entrustment cannot be met. Defendants do not discuss the other elements of negligent entrustment either by offering evidence or pointing to Plaintiff’s lack of evidence. As discussed, the Court finds that a triable issue of fact exists as to whether Defendant Hernandez was negligent, and thus, the negligent entrustment action does not necessarily fail.

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the second cause of action.

 

          Fourth and Fifth Causes of Action – Strict Product Liability Causes of

          Action

 

A strict products liability cause of action can be based on manufacturing defects, design defects, or inadequate warnings or failures to warn. (See Anderson v. Owens-Corning Fiberglas Corp. (1991) 53 Cal.3d 987, 995.) In order to be liable for strict products liability, a plaintiff must allege that the defendant “produced, manufactured, sold, or was in some way responsible for the product.” (Garcia v. Joseph Vince Co. (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 868, 874.) 

 

Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s strict product liability causes of action fail because Defendant Access Services is not in the business of manufacturing, selling, or distributing motor vehicles or seat belt restraint systems, and did not engage in such activities as to the subject van. (Rodriguez Decl., ¶¶ 8-12; McEwan Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. B.) Access Services purchased the subject van in 2016, and thus, did not place the van on the market. (Rodriguez Decl., ¶ 7.)

 

Plaintiff does not oppose the summary adjudication of the strict product liability causes of action.

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ Motion for Summary Adjudication of the fourth and fifth causes of action are GRANTED.


Case Number: 23NWCV01861    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: SEL

REVISED TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Plaintiff Magda J. Lizardo’s Motion to Enforce Settlement and Request to Enter Judgment is GRANTED.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is a breach of contract action. On June 15, 2023, Plaintiff Magda J. Lizardo (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants GEJ Construction, Inc., Juan Gerardo Perez, Jose Antonion Gonzalez (collectively “Defendants”), American Contractors Indemnity Company (“ACIC”), and Does 1 through 100. The Complaint alleges Plaintiff owns a property located at 1642-1644 South Spaulding Ave., Los Angeles (the “Property”). (Compl., ¶ 1.) Plaintiff and Defendants allegedly entered into a written agreement (the “Contract”) on June 22, 2022, regarding remodel work at the Property. (Compl., ¶ 19.) The Complaint alleges that Defendant’s workers compensation expired during the course of the project; however, Defendant GEJ Construction, Inc. continued to utilize employees, in violation of the Contract. (Compl., ¶¶ 23, 39.) Plaintiff further alleges Defendants are responsible for defective construction. (Compl., ¶ 38.)

 

The Complaint alleges five causes of action: 1) Breach of Contract, 2) Negligence, 3) Fraud and Deceit, 4) Recovery on Contractor’s License Bond, and 5) Recovery of Money Paid to Unlicensed Contractors.

 

On June 20, 2024, ACIC obtained an order depositing $15,000 in bond funds with the Court and was subsequently dismissed from the case (“Stipulation”). (See 6/20/2024 Stipulation and Order of Deposit; Campbell Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.)

 

On September 29, 2025, the parties reached a settlement (the “Agreement”), providing that Defendants would pay Plaintiff the total sum of $35,000.00. (Campbell Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. B.)

 

Plaintiff now moves to enforce the Agreement, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6.

 

On May 28, 2026, the instant Motion came on for hearing. The Court ordered the parties to meet and confer regarding payments made and the Stipulation for return of bond. (5/28/26 Minute Order.)

 

No opposition has been filed as of June 30, 2026.

 

Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 provides the following:

 

“(a) If parties to pending litigation stipulate, in a writing signed by the parties outside of the presence of the court or orally before the court, for settlement of the case, or part thereof, the court, upon motion, may enter judgment pursuant to the terms of the settlement. If the parties to the settlement agreement or their counsel stipulate in writing or orally before the court, the court may dismiss the case as to the settling parties without prejudice and retain jurisdiction over the parties to enforce the settlement until performance in full of the terms of the settlement.

(b) For purposes of this section, a writing is signed by a party if it is signed by any of the following:

(1) The party.

(2) An attorney who represents the party.

(3) If an insurer is defending and indemnifying a party to the action, an agent who is authorized in writing by the insurer to sign on the party's behalf.”

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 664.6, subd. (a).)

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiff seeks a court order enforcing the settlement agreement between Plaintiff and Defendants, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6.

 

a.    Retention of Jurisdiction

 

On November 7, 2025, the Court dismissed the case without prejudice and retained jurisdiction to make orders to enforce the terms of the settlement, including judgment, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6. (See 11/7/25 Minute Order; 11/7/25 Nunc Pro Tunc Order; Campbell Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. D.)

 

Therefore, the Court has retained jurisdiction to enter judgment.

 

b.    Enforceable Agreement

 

Plaintiff argues that the Agreement is enforceable and that judgment should be entered by the Court. Plaintiff asserts that Defendants have not paid any of the settlement amount. Plaintiff argues that Defendants have refused to sign any stipulation releasing the bond funds and have failed to begin making any monthly payments.

 

The Agreement provides that Defendants will “stipulate with [Plaintiff] that the Court release the bond sum to Plaintiff LIZARDO, in the amount of $15,000.00 upon full execution of the agreement. Defendants agree to execute any further documentation, including a proposed Stipulation to effectuate the release of such funds.” (Campbell Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. B.) The Agreement further states Defendants will begin making payments of $750 per month to Plaintiff for twenty months beginning October 1, 2025, and will continue making payments of $1,2500 per month for four additional months, following the completion of the twenty payments. (Ibid.)

 

The Agreement states “[u]pon notice to counsel of record for Defendants that a payment is late, if not then received within 5 calendar days thereafter, Defendants shall be deemed to be in default and Plaintiff may move for entry of judgment upon motion or ex parte application for the Settlement Amount minus any payments made. Defendants agree to execute a stipulation that the Court may retain jurisdiction to enforce the Settlement Agreement pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6.” (Ibid.)

 

The Court finds an enforceable agreement exists, and the terms are clear. Both parties have signed the Agreement. Plaintiff’s counsel notified Defendant’s counsel on December 3, 2025, of Defendant’s failure to execute a stipulation or make payments. (Campbell Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. C.) Because Defendants have not made payment on the settlement amount after notification, Defendants are deemed in default under the terms of the Agreement.

 

Accordingly, the Court grants Plaintiff’s Motion to Enforce Settlement.

 

c.    Attorneys’ Fees

 

Plaintiff argues that the Agreement provides that Plaintiff may seek attorneys’ fees and costs associated with enforcing the Agreement.

 

Plaintiff requests attorneys’ fees in the amount of $3,000.00, consisting of 4 hours preparing the instant motion, an anticipated 1.5 hours reviewing opposition and drafting a reply, and 0.5 hours attending the hearing of this motion, at an hourly rate of $500.00, plus a $60.00 filing fee. (Campbell Decl., ¶ 6.)

 

The Agreement states that “in the event it shall become necessary for any PARTY to employ an attorney, in connection with a breach or alleged breach of any term of this AGREEMENT, the prevailing PARTY in any such dispute or action shall be entitled to such PARTY’s reasonable costs and fees incurred, including attorney’s fees, in connection with enforcement of the AGREEMENT.” (Campbell Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. B.) The Court thus finds Plaintiff is entitled to attorneys’ fees.

 

The Court finds the requested amount excessive, considering the motion is straightforward, and no opposition or reply has been filed. The Court thus reduces the total amount to $1,310.00, consisting of 2.5 hours at an hourly rate of $500.00, and the $60.00 filing fee.

 

d.    Pre-judgment Interest

 

Plaintiff requests that the Court award 10 percent interest per annum at a daily rate of $9.58, from the date payment was first due, October 1, 2025, to the date of the hearing of this motion, March 26, 2026. Based on the daily rate and the number of elapsed days, Plaintiff asserts that they are entitled to pre-judgment interest of $1,695.66.

 

Plaintiff requests pre-judgment interest in the amount of $1,695.66, pursuant to Civil Code section 3289. (Civ. Code § 3298, subd. (b) [“If a contract entered into after January 1, 1986, does not stipulate a legal rate of interest, the obligation shall bear interest at a rate of 10 percent per annum after a breach.”].)

 

Plaintiff based this amount on the principal amount owed being $35,000, and a 10% per year statutory rate, making a daily interest rate of $9.58. (Campbell Decl., ¶ 7.) Plaintiff asserts that the first payment was due on October 1, 2025, and 177 days will have elapsed between that date and the initial date of this hearing on March 26, 2026. The Court notes the hearing date was continued to May 28, 2026, by the Court’s own motion on February 11, 2026. (2/11/26 Minute Order.) It was then continued to today’s date. (5/28/26 Minute Order.)

 

The Court finds pre-judgment interest in the amount of $2,605.76, calculated by multiplying the daily rate of $9.58 by 272 days. The Court considers the time elapsed between October 1, 2025, and June 30, 2026, the date of this hearing.

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court awards a judgment of $39,099.62 to Plaintiff, reflecting $35,000.00 in principle, plus attorney fees and costs of $1,810.00, and prejudgment interest of $2,605.76.

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Attorney Lusine Ghazaryan, Esq.’s Motion to be Relieved as Counsel is GRANTED.

 

Counsel to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is an action for premises liability. Attorney Lusine Ghazaryan, Esq., counsel for Plaintiffs Martha Portillo and Martha Galvan, now moves to be relieved as counsel.

 

The Motion is unopposed as of June 30, 2026.

 

Legal Standard

 

Good cause exists to grant a motion to be relieved as counsel based on any of the grounds under California Rules of Professional Conduct Rule 1.16(b). Rule 1.16(b) provides that an attorney may withdraw based on any of the following: (1) the client insists upon presenting a claim or defense that is not warranted under existing law and cannot be supported by good faith argument for an extension, modification, or reversal of existing law, (2) the client seeks to pursue a criminal or fraudulent course of conduct, (3) the client insists that the lawyer pursue a course of conduct that is criminal or fraudulent, (4) the client by other conduct renders it unreasonably difficult for the member to carry out the representation effectively, (5) the client breaches a material term of an agreement with, or obligation, to the lawyer relating to the representation, and the lawyer has given the client a reasonable warning after the breach that the lawyer will withdraw unless the client fulfills the agreement or performs the obligation, (6) the client knowingly and freely assents to termination of the representation, (7) the inability to work with co-counsel indicates that the best interests of the client likely will be served by withdrawal, (8) the lawyer’s mental or physical condition renders it difficult for the lawyer to carry out the representation effectively, (9) a continuation of the representation is likely to result in a violation of these rules or the State Bar Act, or (10) the lawyer believes in good faith, in a proceeding pending before a tribunal, that the tribunal will find the existence of other good cause for withdrawal.

 

California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1362 requires that the following be submitted in support of an attorney’s motion to be relieved as counsel pursuant Code of Civil Procedure section 284, subdivision (2): (1) a notice of motion and motion directed to the client (made on Notice of Motion and Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel—Civil (Judicial Council Form, MC-051)); (2) a declaration stating in general terms and without compromising the confidentiality of the attorney-client relationship why a motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 284, subdivision (2) is brought instead of filing a consent under Code of Civil Procedure section 284, subdivision (1) (made on Declaration in Support of Attorney’s Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel—Civil (Judicial Council Form, MC-052)); (3) a proof of service evidencing service of the notice of motion and motion, declaration, and proposed order on the client and on all other parties who have appeared in the case; and (4) a proposed order relieving counsel (prepared on Order Granting Attorney’s Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel—Civil (Judicial Council Form, MC-053)). (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subds. (a), (c), (d), (e).)

 

The court has discretion to allow an attorney to withdraw, provided that there is no prejudice to the client. (Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 904, 915.)

 

Discussion

 

Attorney Lusine Ghazaryan, Esq. (“Counsel”) filed a Notice of Motion and Motion to be Relieved as Counsel. (See Form MC-051.) Counsel also filed a proposed order. (See Form MC-053.) Counsel’s declaration demonstrates good cause for withdrawal based on an “irremediable breakdown in the attorney-client relationship that stands in the way of effective representation.” (Ghazaryan Decl., ¶ 2.)

 

Counsel states that the clients were served copies of the Motion papers by email and mail at the clients’ last known address (same address), which was confirmed via telephone, in the past thirty (30) days. (Ghazaryan Decl., ¶ 3.)

 

Counsel has filed a Proof of Service (attached to the Proposed Order) indicating that the clients and all other appearing parties have been served the Motion papers via mail and/or email.

 

Accordingly, the Motion to be Relieved as Counsel is granted.

Case Number: 25NWCV03962    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: SEL

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Plaintiff Cindy Dempsey’s Motion for Trial Preference is DENIED without prejudice.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is an action for premises liability regarding premises located at 13806 La Mirada Blvd., La Mirada, California (“Subject Premises”). On November 7, 2025, Plaintiff Cindy Dempsey (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants City of La Mirada, USA Swimming, Inc., La Mirada Armada, Southern California Swimming, Inc., (collectively, “Defendants”) and Does 1 to 50. The Complaint alleges that Plaintiff tripped over unsecured electrical cords at the Subject Premises, where she was attending her granddaughter’s swimming meet. The Complaint alleges two causes of action for 1) Negligence and 2) Premises Liability.

 

Plaintiff now moves for a trial preference pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 36, subdivision (a).


Legal Standard

 

“On petition of any party over 70, [Code of Civil Procedure] section 36, subdivision (a), provides that the granting of calendar preference is mandatory in some circumstances.” (Fox v. Superior Court (“Fox”) (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 529, 533.) A court must grant a trial preference under Code Civil Procedure section 36, subdivision (a) where the following two factors are present: (1) the party has a substantial interest in the action as a whole; and (2) the health of the party is such that a preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing the party’s interest in the litigation. (Ibid.)

 

Where a motion for trial preference is granted, the clerk shall set the case for trial not more than 120 days from that date. (Koch-Ash v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 689, 694.)

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiff requests a trial preference under Code of Civil Procedure section 36, subdivision (a). Plaintiff is currently 73 years old. (Erakat Decl., ¶ 5.) Plaintiff has a substantial interest in this action as the sole plaintiff. Plaintiff asserts that a trial preference is necessary to prevent prejudice to her interest in the litigation due to her pre-existing health conditions and injuries she suffered from as a result of the subject incident. Specifically, Plaintiff has a history of atrial fibrillation, angina pectoris, heart disease, hypertension, Stage 3b chronic kidney disease, and heart murmur. (Erakat Decl., ¶ 6.) As a result of the subject incident, Plaintiff suffered an acute left hip intertrochanteric fracture and an acute left proximal humerus fracture. (Erakat Decl., ¶ 7.) After being released from rehabilitation, Plaintiff continues to suffer from pain. (Ibid.) Plaintiff has lost range of motion in her left arm and needs to use a cane on occasion. (Ibid.) Plaintiff’s health, mobility, activity level, and independence have been limited, and she is in constant fear of another fall. (Ibid.)

 

In opposition, Defendants argue that Plaintiff fails to submit sufficient evidence to entitle her to a trial preference. Specifically, Defendants note that “Plaintiff provides no physician declaration, no medical opinion, no evidence of diminished life expectancy, no evidence of a terminal condition, and no evidence that Plaintiff's condition is expected to deteriorate in a manner that threatens her ability to participate in the litigation process.” (Opp., 5:21-24.)

 

Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s own testimony demonstrates her independence and improvement. Plaintiff has testified that she "can do most things now." (Bathh Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Dempsey Depo.,130:1-25.) For example, Plaintiff has testified that she cooks, does laundry, and engages in activities intended to improve her physical condition. (Bathh Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Dempsey Depo.,130:1-131:23.) Plaintiff also cares for her granddaughter and drives. (Bathh Decl., ¶¶ 5-6, Exh. A, Dempsey Depo., 134:1-135:8.) Such testimony demonstrated that Plaintiff remains active and is capable of participating in the litigation process.

 

In reply, Plaintiff argues that counsel’s declaration regarding Plaintiff’s health condition is sufficient evidence to support a trial preference. Plaintiff’s testimony that her condition has been improving is not enough to defeat the request for trial preference.

 

As pointed out by both parties, that a motion for trial preference under subdivision (a) does not require a doctor’s declaration and “may be supported by nothing more than an attorney’s declaration based upon the information and belief as to the medical diagnosis and prognosis of any party.” (Fox, supra, 21 Cal.App.5th at p. 534, internal citation omitted [explaining that an attorney declaration under CCP § 36.5 can consist entirely of hearsay and conclusions].)

 

The Court acknowledges Plaintiff’s age and health conditions. However, the Court is not persuaded at this time that Plaintiff’s health is such that she would be prejudiced if a trial preference is not granted. It appears from both Plaintiff’s counsel’s declaration and from Plaintiff’s testimony that Plaintiff’s condition has been improving since the subject incident. Considering all evidence, the Court is not convinced that Plaintiff’s current and future condition would prevent her from participating in the case.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s Motion for Trial Preference is denied without prejudice.
