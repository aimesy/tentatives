      I.          Background

 

This is an action arising from Defendant FCA US, LLC’s (Defendant’s) alleged violations of its statutory obligations under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (Song-Beverly Act). Defendant presently moves for judgment on the pleadings.

 

On May 31, 2024, Plaintiff Edward Julian III (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendant, asserting four causes of action for (1) violation of subdivision (d) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (2) violation of subdivision (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (3) violation of subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code section 1793.2, and (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability. Plaintiff asserts these violations arose after he purchased a 2018 Dodge Challenger and entered into a warranty contract with Defendant on January 22, 2020. (Compl., ¶ 7.)

 

On July 1, 2024, Defendant filed an answer to the complaint.

 

On August 13, 2025, Plaintiff filed a motion for leave to file a first amended complaint.

 

On September 16, 2025, the Court denied Plaintiff’s leave to amend. Plaintiff submitted to the Court’s tentative ruling denying the motion and informed the Court he intended to refile the motion for leave to amend in accordance with the Court’s tentative ruling.

 

On April 7, 2026, Defendant filed the present motion for judgment on the pleadings.

 

On July 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed his opposition.

 

On August 4, 2026, Defendant filed its reply.

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Meet and Confer Requirement – Before filing a motion for judgment on the pleadings, the moving party is required to meet and confer at least five days before the date the motion is filed in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading subject to the motion for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 439 , subd. (a).) The moving party shall file and serve with the motion a declaration stating the means by which the parties met and conferred and that the parties did not reach an agreement to resolve the issues raised, or that the party who filed the pleading failed to respond or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith. (Ibid.)

 

Here, Defense counsel Amanda E. Dobson asserts that she sent electronic correspondence on February 19, 2026 to Plaintiff’s counsel attempting to meet and confer regarding the present motion and proposing dates for telephonic conference in early March, but that Plaintiff’s counsel did not respond to these efforts. (Dobson Decl., ¶¶ 3, 5, Ex. B.) Plaintiff argues this requirement is not satisfied, as Defense counsel failed to meet and confer by telephone, videoconference, or in person as required by Section 439. (Opp., p. 1:21-26.)

 

The Court finds this requirement is satisfied. Contrary to Plaintiff’s argument, where a party moving for judgment on the pleadings has provided a declaration attesting that the party who filed the pleading failed to respond to a meet and confer effort, the requirement is satisfied. Defense counsel has done so here, and Plaintiff may not argue that the motion was improperly brought by failing to respond to a meet and confer effort.

 

-----

 

 III.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings – A defendant may move for judgment on the pleadings where “[t]he complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (c)(1)(B)(ii).) “A motion for judgment on the pleadings may be made at any time either prior to the trial or at the trial itself. [Citation.]” (Ion Equipment Corp. v. Nelson (1980) 110 Cal.App.3d 868, 877 (disapproved on other grounds).) 

 

“A motion for judgment on the pleadings performs the same function as a general demurrer, and hence attacks only defects disclosed on the face of the pleadings or by matters that can be judicially noticed. Presentation of extrinsic evidence is therefore not proper on a motion for judgment on the pleadings.” (Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 999 (citations omitted).) “The standard for ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings is essentially the same as that applicable to a general demurrer, that is, under the state of the pleadings, together with matters that may be judicially noticed, it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Bezirdjian v. O'Reilly (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 316, 321–322 (citing Schabarum v. California Legislature (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1216).) “All properly pleaded, material facts are deemed true, but not contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law.” (Adams v. Bank of America, N.A. (2020) 51 Cal.App.5th 666, 670.)

“If a pleading is defective but amendable, judgment on the pleadings should be granted but with leave to amend.” (Olsen v. Lockheed Aircraft Corp. (1965) 237 Cal.App.2d 737, 742.)

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendant moves for judgment on the pleadings pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 438 on the grounds that the Song-Beverly Act does not apply to the subject vehicle, a used 2018 Dodge Challenger, and Plaintiff’s complaint thus fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action under the Song-Beverly Act. (Notice of Mtn., p. 1:21-26.) This motion is also made pursuant to the California Supreme Court’s 2024 ruling in Rodriguez v. FCA US, LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189. (Notice of Mtn., p. 1:27.)

 

a.     Viability of Complaint in Light of Rodriguez

 

"The Song-Beverly Act is a remedial statute designed to protect consumers who have purchased products covered by an express warranty." (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 798.) The Song-Beverly Act requires that "[e]very manufacturer of consumer goods sold in this state and for which the manufacturer has made an express warranty shall…[m]aintain in this state sufficient service and repair facilities reasonably close to all areas where its consumer goods are sold to carry out the terms of those warranties or designate and authorize in this state as service and repair facilities independent repair or service facilities reasonably close to all areas where its consumer goods are sold to carry out the terms of the warranties." (Civ. Code, § 1793.2, subd. (a).) “Consumer goods” means any new product that is used, bought, or leased for use primarily for personal, family, or household purposes. (Civ. Code, § 1791, subd. (a).)

 

At issue here is whether Plaintiff’s causes of action fail as a matter of law in light of the Rodriguez decision. In Rodriguez, the plaintiffs brought suit against FCA US, LLC under the Song-Beverly Act to enforce the refund-or-replace provision in Section 1793.2, subdivision (d)(2) following their purchase of a two-year-old used car which had over 55,000 miles on it and was sold with an unexpired manufacturer’s new car warranty. (Rodriguez, supra, 17 Cal.5th at 195-96.) The Supreme Court upheld the trial and appellate court’s granting of summary judgment in favor of the defendant, and held that “a motor vehicle purchased with an unexpired manufacturer's new car warranty does not qualify as a ‘motor vehicle sold with a manufacturer's new car warranty’ under section 1793.22, subdivision (e)(2)'s definition of ‘new motor vehicle’ unless the new car warranty was issued with the sale.” (Id at 196.)

 

Turning to the parties’ arguments, Defendant argues that because the vehicle purchased by Plaintiff was not a new motor vehicle at the time of purchase, under Rodriguez, Plaintiff may not maintain his causes of action under the Song-Beverly Act. (Motion, p. 2:15-18.) Defendant notes that Plaintiff failed to allege he purchased a new motor vehicle and that the Complaint, brought in part under Civil Code section 1795.5 governing used motor vehicles, states Plaintiff purchased the car in 2020 with a balance on the 2018 New Vehicle Limited Warranty applicable to the vehicle. (Motion, p. 4:4-5, 8-14.) Defendant argues that in light of the Rodriguez decision, and because the complaint fails to allege that any new car warranty was issued in connection with the purchase of the used vehicle, that his Song-Beverly claims fail as a matter of law. (Motion, p. 6:16-20.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues the holding in Rodriguez is limited to the narrow issue of whether Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (d)(2) is applicable to used vehicles, but has no bearing on whether a purchaser of a used vehicle may seek remedies under other provisions of the Song-Beverly Act as none of the other statutes under which Plaintiff seek relief contain any limitation of applicability to only “new” vehicles. (Opp., p. 3:26-4:5.) Therefore, Plaintiff argues that the narrow holding of Rodriguez does not bar his causes of action. (Opp., p. 5:6-8.)

 

On reply, Defendant argues that Plaintiff mischaracterizes the holding in Rodriguez, and that Rodriguez reaffirms a broader principle that manufacturers are not liable under new vehicle provisions of the Song-Beverly Act to used vehicle purchasers unless a new warranty was issued with the sale or the manufacturer substantially participated in the sale. (Reply, p. 1:25-2:5.) Defendant further argues that Plaintiff concedes that purchasers of used vehicles are not entitled to Section 1793.2, subdivision (d)(2)’s enhanced protection for refund or repurchase, but that Plaintiff’s complaint relies on the fact that Defendant failed to refund or repurchase the vehicle, and thus, the complaint generally lacks statutory standing. (Reply, p. 3:15-28.)

 

Here, Plaintiff is correct in noting that Rodriguez dealt with the meaning of ‘new motor vehicle’ under Section 1793.22, subdivision (e) specifically. However, the holding of Rodriguez extends further than just its implications on whether Plaintiff may maintain his cause of action under Section 1793, subdivision (d)(2). The Song-Beverly Act as a whole only applies to “consumer goods,” which are expressly defined as “any new product or part thereof.” (Civ. Code, § 1791, subd. (a) (emphasis added).) Any argument that the definition of a “new motor vehicle” as established in Rodriguez has no bearing on the definition of a “new product” for the purposes of determining whether a vehicle is defined as a consumer good such that it falls within the Song-Beverly Act is unpersuasive and cannot be reconciled with Rodriguez. Here, Plaintiff’s vehicle was purchased used, and thus is not a “new product” or “consumer good” for the purposes of the Song-Beverly Act. Furthermore, the Supreme Court addressed the general statutory framework of the Song-Beverly Act in Rodriguez, in which it noted that its interpretation of the meaning of “new vehicle” under Section 1793.22(e) is “bolstered by the framework of the Song-Beverly Act, which distinguishes between new and used products and calibrates manufacturers’ and sellers’ obligations accordingly.” (Rodriguez, supra, 17 Cal.5th at 200.)

 

The Supreme Court analyzed the statutory and Legislative history behind the Song-Beverly Act and set forth the general framework of the Song-Beverly Act and the clear distinction between used and new motor vehicles as follows:

 

First, in enacting and amending the Song-Beverly Act, the Legislature has maintained a distinction between “new” and “used” products and has specified the warranty protections applicable to each category. The Legislature has shown it knows how to legislate on “used” products (§ 1795.5), and it has employed clear language when it wants to make a “used” product subject to the warranty protections that apply to the product when new [citation]. In defining “new motor vehicle,” section 1793.22, subdivision (e)(2) does not mention “used” vehicles; the word “used” nowhere appears. This bolsters the inference that the phrase “other motor vehicle sold with a manufacturer's new car warranty” was not intended to cover any used car with an unexpired new car warranty.

 

Second, section 1795.5, which governs used goods, makes clear that the same warranty protections that consumers of new products have against manufacturers apply to consumers of used products against distributors or retail sellers “in a sale in which an express warranty is given.” In other words, just as with new products, the Act's remedies for failure to repair a used product in conformity with an express warranty have applicability where the express warranty accompanies the sale of the product. For new products, liability extends to the manufacturer; for used products, liability extends to the distributor or retail seller and not to the manufacturer, at least where the manufacturer has not issued a new warranty or played a substantial role in the sale of a used good. [Citation.] In both cases, the Act's protections are premised on an express warranty arising from the product's sale. (See Rodriguez [v. FCA US, LLC (2022) 77 Cal.App.5th 209,] 218 [“[A] hallmark of the Act is that its consumer protections apply against the party who sold the product to the buyer and issued the express warranty.”].) Against this backdrop, it would be anomalous to construe “other motor vehicle sold with a manufacturer's new car warranty” (§ 1793.22, subd. (e)(2)) to include any used car sold by a distributor or retailer with a preexisting, unexpired manufacturer's warranty. If the Legislature had meant to depart from the basic scheme and extend manufacturer liability in this way, again we think it would have spoken more clearly.

 

(Rodriguez, supra, 17 Cal.5th at 202 (emphasis added).)

 

Simply put, “Rodriguez held that because the Act protects only the purchasers of ‘new’ motor vehicles, it does not apply to a person who buys a used vehicle, even if the manufacturer’s warranty is still in effect for that vehicle.” (Towns v. Hyundai Motor America, 117 Cal. App. 5th 958, 973 (citing Rodriguez, supra, 17 Cal.5th at 195-96).)

 

Plaintiff’s brief reliance on Leber v. DKD of Davis, Inc. (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 402, 409 in support of the notion that had the Legislature intended for the Song-Beverly Act to apply only to new cars is misplaced. First, Leber was decided by a lower court before the Supreme Court’s ruling in Rodriguez, and any contrary interpretation of the Song-Beverly Act and the definition of “new motor vehicle” within is necessarily superseded by Rodriguez. (See Jensen v. BMW of North America, Inc. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112; Rodriguez, supra, 17 Cal.5th at 205 [Jensen disapproved to the extent that it is inconsistent with the Rodriguez opinion].) Second, Leber stated that “[i]f the Legislature had intended the definition of ‘new’ vehicle in section 1793.22, subdivision (e) to apply throughout the Act, it would not have explicitly limited its applicability by providing: ‘For the purposes of subdivision (d) of Section 1793.2 and this section, the following terms have the following meanings …,’ explicitly limiting the ambit of the various definitions that followed.” (Leber, supra, 237 Cal.App.4th at 409.) However, it so held because the Leber plaintiff was not suing a manufacturer, and the new motor vehicle language does not necessarily apply to a distributor or retailer. (Ibid [“that a used vehicle sold with a manufacturer's warranty qualified as a new vehicle under that statute is inapplicable to this case, which does not involve manufacturer's liability” (referring to Jensen)].)

 

Third, and most notably, the Leber Court also accurately stated that “after defining consumer goods to refer to new goods, and having provided one exception for used assistive devices, the Legislature presumably intended the Act to apply to and only to new products, unless otherwise explicitly so stated. [Citation.] Leber does not persuade us that a used, as-is vehicle qualifies as a new vehicle for purposes of triggering the implied warranties in section 1791.1, part of the Act.” (Id at 407.) Therefore, Leber supports that the Song-Beverly Act applies only to new motor vehicles, the definition of which was narrowed by Rodriguez, unless otherwise expressly stated (e.g., Civil Code section 1795.5).

 

Here, there is no dispute that the vehicle purchased by Plaintiff is a used vehicle, and such facts are made clear from the complaint and attached 2018 manufacturer’s warranty. Therefore, the subject vehicle is not a “consumer good” as defined by the Song-Beverly Act, and Plaintiff is not entitled to bring his causes of action for Defendant’s alleged violations under the Song-Beverly Act.

 

Furthermore, the plaintiff in a Song-Beverly action has the burden of pleading that the Song-Beverly Act applies. (Park City Services, Inc. v. Ford Motor Co., Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 295, 309.) The operative complaint here fails to allege that Defendant was acting as a retailer, had any substantial participation in the sale of the vehicle, or issued a new express warranty along with the purchase of the sale. While Plaintiff argues in opposition that Defendant reaffirmed the warranty by attempting to repair the vehicle, the Court does not find this meets the standard of ‘substantial participation’ or issuance of an express warranty. (See Kiluk v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 334, 340 [“the assumption baked into section 1795.5 is that the manufacturer and the distributor/retailer are distinct entities. Where the manufacturer sells directly to the public, however, it takes on the role of a retailer”].) There is no dispute that Plaintiff may have a remaining balance on the warranty originally issued to the first buyer of the vehicle, and that such a warranty may have been in effect at the time Plaintiff attempted to repair the vehicle. However, Defendant’s compliance with the warranty and failure to breach it does not bring this case within the Song-Beverly Act. To so hold would result in either encouragement for vehicle manufacturers to breach express warranties on any and all used cars to avoid liability under the Song-Beverly Act or to render ineffectual the holding of Rodriguez and the express language of the Act itself omitting manufacturers from liability for used vehicles under the Song-Beverly Act where the manufacturer was not acting as a retailer and did not issue a new express warranty at the time of the sale.

 

In light of these circumstances, pursuant to the Song-Beverly Act and the Supreme Court’s holding in Rodriguez, the vehicle purchased by Plaintiff is not a new motor vehicle or a consumer good under the Song-Beverly Act, and Plaintiff’s causes of action against Defendant as manufacturer thereunder fail as a matter of law.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion for judgment on the pleadings is GRANTED.

 

b.     Leave to Amend

 

Plaintiff seeks leave to amend the complaint if the Court is inclined to grant the MJOP. As Plaintiff correctly points out, leave to amend is typically granted:

 

“Where a demurrer is sustained or a motion for judgment on the pleadings is granted as to the original complaint, denial of leave to amend constitutes an abuse of discretion if the pleading does not show on its face that it is incapable of amendment.” (Virginia G. v. ABC Unified School Dist. (1993) 15 Cal.App.4th 1848, 1852; see also Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal. 4th 962, 971; Beckstead v. Superior Court (1971) 21 Cal. App. 3d 780, 782.)

 

(Opposition, at 5.)

 

The problem here is that Plaintiff does not seek leave to amend the existing causes of action. Rather he acknowledges that if the Court rules that the current claims are barred under Rodriguez, those claims cannot be cured by amendment. In other words, the existing causes of action do show on their face that they are incapable of amendment. Plaintiff seeks instead to plead new – not amended -- claims under the Magnuson-Moss Act. (Opposition, at 5-6.)  

 

Defendant argues leave to amend should be denied, as Plaintiff previously sought leave to amend by removing the existing Song-Beverly causes of action and replacing them with claims under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, the California Uniform Commercial Code, and the California Legal Remedies Act, which was denied. (Motion, p. 7:20-25.) Defendant further argues on reply that the renewed request for leave to amend fails to comply with California Rule of Court rule 3.1324, which outlines the specific requirements for seeking leave to amend, and that amendment would be futile, as any CLRA claim would  fail because Plaintiff cannot allege that Defendant made a misrepresentation or failed to honor a claim, and any MMWA claim would fail because Plaintiff cannot assert a viable state-law warranty claim against Defendant as manufacturer under Rodriguez. (Reply, p. 6:13-23.) Finally, Defendants note that trial is presently scheduled for September 18, 2026, less than five weeks from the date of hearing, and granting leave to amend would result in prejudice to Defendant because it would require additional discovery, expert disclosures, and trial delay. (Reply, p. 7:1-5.)

 

Plaintiff’s motion for leave to file a first amended complaint, filed on August 13, 2025, was filed in response to the Rodriguez decision. (Notice of Motion for Leave, p. 2:9-14.) The motion sought leave to allege causes of action under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, the California Uniform Commercial Code, and the California Legal Remedies Act, all of which were, unlike the Song-Beverly Act, unaffected by Rodriguez. (Notice of Motion for Leave, p. 2:15-27.) Plaintiff asserted that the proposed amendments to the complaint “would remove Song-Beverly claims that are now untenable.” (Motion for Leave, p. 1:20.) Thus, Plaintiff knew of the deficiency of the Complaint ages ago.

 

On September 16, 2025, the Court denied that motion without prejudice. The minute order states: “Plaintiff's counsel informs the Court that they plan to re-file the Motion for Leave to Amend in accordance with the Court's tentative.” The stated grounds for denial in the Court’s tentative ruling were that the motion for leave failed to comply with California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324. Plaintiff had submitted a proposed FAC, but it was not redlined. The August 13, 2025 motion for leave did not state what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted or added by page, paragraph, and line number. Furthermore, the declaration in support did not adhere to  California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324(b) nor does the motion attempt to address what this subdivision requires. 

 

Regardless of the merits of the amendments Plaintiff sought, Plaintiff understood what fixes to make. But no fixes followed. In the months after that hearing Plaintiff did nothing. And on January 28, 2026, the Court granted the stipulation of the parties to continue trial in this matter. The Court interlineated on the parties’ proposed order “No further continuances shall be granted.” And still Plaintiff did not, and has not, filed the promised motion for leave.

 

The Court denies leave to amend for two independent reasons. First, this is not a situation of seeking leave to amend to rehabilitate existing claims, but to assert wholly new claims. This is in effect a motion for leave to amend under CCP Section 473. The Court may consider both Plaintiff’s lack of diligence in seeking the amendment and the prejudice that would be occasioned by granting leave. (Magpali v. Farmers Grp., Inc. (1996) 48 Cal. App.4th 471, 486,  as modified on denial of reh'g (Aug. 20, 1996).) Here, Plaintiff has unaccountably delayed eleven months in re-raising the motion for leave issue, and has not even yet filed a motion for leave. We are now just over a month from trial. As Defendant correctly points out, granting leave now would necessitate a continuance of the trial and thus prejudice Defendant. These circumstance alone warrant denying leave:

 

Nevertheless, our independent review of the record reveals no abuse of discretion in the denial of the request for leave to amend. Magpali's amendment was proposed on the eve of trial, nearly two years after the complaint was originally filed. He did not give an explanation for leaving the Act claim out of the original complaint or bringing the request to amend so late.

(Magpali, supra, 48 Cal.App.4th at 486.)

 

The second and independent reason for denying leave is futility. Defendant correctly summarizes the futility of the proposed amendment:

 

Any CLRA claim would fail for the same reasons as Plaintiff's Song-Beverly claims because Plaintiff cannot allege that FCA made a misrepresentation or failed to honor a warranty issued at the time of sale [of the used vehicle]. Likewise, any Magnuson-Moss claim requires a viable state-law warranty claim, which Plaintiff cannot assert under Rodriguez.

 

(Reply, at 6.)

 

Accordingly, the request for leave to amend is denied.

 

-----

 

    V.          Conclusion

 

Defendant FCA US, LLC’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is GRANTED without leave to amend. Plaintiff’s causes of action arising under the Song-Beverly Act are dismissed with prejudice.

      I.          Background

 

This is a personal injury action arising from a motor vehicle collision. Third party Amya Hart (Hart) presently moves the Court for an order substituting her as successor in interest in place of Plaintiff Trevin Andrea Hart (Plaintiff).

 

On January 27, 2025, Plaintiff filed his complaint against Defendants Jay Alan Walsma (Walsma) and Walsma Oil Company, Inc. (WOC) (together Defendants), asserting a cause of action for personal injury and property damage arising from a motor vehicle collision. Specifically, on November 28, 2023, Plaintiff alleged that his vehicle was struck by Walsma when Walsma made a left turn into Plaintiff’s path of travel and the two vehicles collided, resulting in severe bodily injuries to Plaintiff. (Huelse Decl., ¶ 2.)

 

On April 2, 2025, Defendants jointly filed an answer to the complaint.

 

On February 28, 2026, Plaintiff passed away due to causes unrelated to the subject accident. (Motion, p. 4:10-11.)

 

On April 30, 2026, Hart filed the present motion to be substituted in Plaintiff’s place as his successor in interest.

 

The motion is unopposed.

 

-----

 

   II.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Substitution of Successor in Interest – A cause of action for a person is not lost by reason of the person’s death, but survives subject to the applicable limitations period except as otherwise provided by statute. (Code Civ. Proc., § 377.20, subd. (a).)

“A pending action or proceeding does not abate by the death of a party if the cause of action survives,” and a surviving cause of action passes to the decedent’s successor in interest.¿(Code Civ. Proc., §§ 377.21, 377.30.) “On motion after the death of a person who commenced an action or proceeding, the court shall allow a pending action or proceeding that does not abate to be continued by the decedent’s personal representative or, if none, by the decedent’s successor in interest.”¿(Code Civ. Proc.,¿§ 377.31.)¿

A certified copy of the decedent’s death certificate must also be attached to the¿affidavit¿or declaration.¿¿(Id., § 377.32(c).)¿

Code of Civil Procedure¿section 377.32 provides that a person who seeks to commence such an action as the decedent’s successor in interest must file¿an affidavit¿or declaration providing the following:¿

1.     The decedent’s name;

2.     The date and place of the decedent’s death;

3.     “No proceeding is now pending in California for administration of the decedent’s estate”;

4.     If the decedent’s estate was administered, a copy of the final order showing the distribution of the decedent’s cause of action to the successor in interest;

5.     Either of the following, as¿appropriate, with facts in support thereof:¿

a.      “The affiant or declarant is the decedent’s successor in interest (as defined in Section 377.11 of the California Code of Civil Procedure) and succeeds to the decedent’s interest in the action or proceeding”, or

b.     “The affiant or declarant is authorized to act on behalf of the decedent’s successor in interest (as defined in Section 377.11 of the California Code of Civil Procedure) with respect to the decedent’s interest in the action or proceeding”;

6.     “No other person has a superior right to commence the action or proceeding or to be substituted for the decedent in the pending action or proceeding”;

7.     “The affiant or declarant affirms or declares under penalty of perjury under the laws of the State of California that the foregoing is true and correct.”¿

(Code Civ. Proc., § 377.32(a).)¿ 

“Decedent’s successor in interest’ means the beneficiary of the decedent’s estate or other successor in interest who succeeds to a cause of action or to a particular item of the property that is the subject of a cause of action.”¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 377.11.)¿

 

-----

 

 III.          Discussion

 

Application – Hart moves for an order substituting her as successor in interest in place of Plaintiff pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 377.31 on the grounds that Plaintiff passed away on February 28, 2026. (Motion, p. 1:26-2:4.)

 

The Court has examined the filing and finds that Hart has submitted a declaration that sets forth most of the required affirmations under Code of Civil Procedure section 377.32, subdivision (a). However, Hart has provided no facts in support of her status as successor in interest, and no copy of Plaintiff’s death certificate has been attached to the declaration as required. Thus, Hart’s motion does not comply with Section 377.32. However, Plaintiff’s counsel previously provided the Court with a copy of Plaintiff’s death certificate and stated that Hart is Plaintiff’s daughter in the stipulation to continue the trial, filed on April 28 and granted as Order on April 29, 2026.

 

Therefore, in the absence of any opposition to the present motion and in the interests of moving the case forward, the Court GRANTS Hart’s motion as it is in substantial compliance with Section 377.32 and all requisite information has been provided to the Court.

 

-----

 

 IV.          Conclusion

 

Amya Hart’s Motion to Substitute Successor in Interest is GRANTED.

      I.          Background

 

This is an action arising under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (Song-Beverly Act). Defendant General Motors, LLC (Defendant) presently moves the Court for an Order compelling Plaintiff’s compliance and appearance at deposition.

 

On June 9, 2025, Plaintiff Henry Corbett (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendant, asserting five causes of action for (1) violation of subdivision (d) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (2) violation of subdivision (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (3) violation of subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability, and (5) fraudulent inducement – concealment. Plaintiff alleges that on November 30, 2022, Plaintiff entered into a warranty contract with Defendant regarding a 2022 Chevrolet Silverado, which was manufactured by Defendant, and that at the time of and after purchase, nonconformities to warranty existed within the express warranty period, which Defendant failed to remedy as required pursuant to the Song-Beverly Act. (Compl., ¶¶ 6, 11, 15.)

 

On August 18, 2025, Defendant filed a demurrer to the complaint.

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Compelling Production – A party responding to requests for inspection must either provide a statement of compliance, represent that it lacks the ability to comply, or object. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.210.)

If a party responds with a statement of compliance, the statement must indicate whether production will be allowed in whole or in part and that all documents in the responding party’s possession, custody or control to which no objection is being made will be included in the production. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.220.)

If a party responds that a particular demand cannot be complied with it must include a representation that a diligent search and reasonable inquiry have been made and that the inability to comply is because the item has never existed, has been lost, stolen, or misplaced, or has never been in or is no longer in the responding party’s possession, custody, or control; such response must also identify the name and address of any person or entity known or believed to have possession, custody, or control of the item or category of item.¿ (Code Civ. Proc.  § 2031.230.) If a response includes objections, a privilege log identifying documents being withheld must be provided. (Code Civ. Proc.  § 2031.240.)

When a party propounding demands for inspection deems responses to the responses to be incomplete or evasive, or deems objections to be without merit, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses. Such motion must set forth facts showing good cause for the discovery, be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration, and include a separate statement. Such motion must also be made within 45 days of verified responses or supplemental responses, or on or before any specific later date the parties have agreed to in writing. (Code Civ. Proc.  § 2031.310, subds. (a)-(c).)

A motion to compel further response to requests for production of documents “shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) “To establish good cause, a discovery proponent must identify a disputed fact that is of consequence in the action and explain how the discovery sought will tend in reason to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact.” (Digital Music News LLC v. Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 216, 224, disapproved on other grounds in Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531.)

                                                                               i.     Attorney-Client and Work Product Privilege

 

As to attorney-client privilege and work product, PAA submitted a privilege log which asserts a privilege as to only “Romero’s personnel file documents.” (Shoga Decl., Ex. 2.) PAA argues that this request seeks all writings relating to Romero’s alleged sexual misconduct towards Plaintiff, including documents generated during counsel’s investigation of anticipated litigation. (Opp. Sep. Statement, p. 9:3-16.) PAA argues that under Code of Civil Procedure section 2018.030, investigative materials prepared by counsel are subject to the work-product doctrine and are privileged.

 

The attorney-client privilege protects confidential attorney-client communications, defined as "information transmitted between a client and his or her lawyer in the course of that relationship and in confidence." (Evid. Code § 952.) The work-product doctrine provides absolute or qualified protection against the disclosure of attorney work-product, depending on whether the work-product contains the "impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal research or theories." (Code Civ. Proc. § 2018.030, subd. (a).) Writings containing an attorney's impressions, conclusions, and opinions are absolutely protected from discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.030, subd. (a).) "When a witness's statement and the attorney's impressions are inextricably intertwined, the work product doctrine provides that absolute protection is afforded to all of the attorney's notes". (Rico v. Mitsubishi Motors Corp. (2007) 42 Cal.4th 807, 814.)

 

All other work product is entitled to only qualified protection. The court may order disclosure if it determines that "denial of discovery will unfairly prejudice the party seeking discovery in preparing that party's claim or defense or will result in an injustice." (Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.030, subd. (b).) Witness statements taken (in writing or recorded) by attorneys or their agents are entitled, at a minimum, to qualified work product protection. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.030, subd. (b).). Such statements are entitled to absolute work product protection if the party resisting discovery makes a showing that the discovery would reveal the attorney's tactics, impressions or evaluation of the case, or would result in opposing counsel taking undue advantage of the attorney's industry or efforts. (Coito v. Superior Court (2012) 54 Cal.4th 480, 495-500 [disapproving prior contrary authority]; Curtis v. Superior Court (2021) 62 Cal.App.5th 453, 469-470.)

 

The court may order disclosure upon determining that "denial of discovery will unfairly prejudice the party seeking discovery . . . or . . . result in an injustice." (Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.030, subd. (b); Meza v. H. Muehlstein & Co. (2009) 176 Cal.App.4th 969, 977.) A party seeking disclosure of an attorney-directed witness statement over a claim of work product protection has the burden to establish that withholding disclosure would unfairly prejudice the party in preparing its claim or defense. (Coito v. Superior Court, supra, 54 Cal.4th at 499-500.)

 

PAA argues the documents at issue contain materials generated during counsel’s investigation in anticipation of litigation. However, Romero’s personnel file, the only item identified as privileged as to this request on PAA’s privilege log, facially does not fall within attorney work product. Furthermore, not a single document identified on the privilege log identifies any attorney as author of a document, nor contains any information or detail surrounding the asserted work product privilege. Additionally, PAA’s assertion that all writings related to Romero’s sexual assault of Plaintiff were made in anticipation of litigation by an attorney is unbelievable, and there is no indication that any or all writings were made by an attorney in anticipation of litigation, nor that any of the documents requested contain attorney opinion, impression, conclusion, or legal research or theories, particularly where the request seeks documents such as student and teacher reports of misconduct and disclosures from Plaintiff regarding the assaults and harassment. Many of these writings would be expected to be produced, memorialized, and retained in the normal course of the operation of a school, and PAA’s privilege log fails to meet the burden of establishing that this privilege applies to any or all of the requested documents.

 

The same is true of the attorney-client privilege asserted by PAA. The privilege log is silent to this end, and documents maintained in the course of employment, investigation of student and staff complaints, and internal investigations of a teacher for sexual molestation of a student are not subject to blanket protection on the basis that PAA anticipated that a lawsuit may ensue. PAA does not assert that any record sought contains communications with an attorney, and even if such documents were ultimately provided to counsel, this does not invoke attorney-client privilege, as it does not indicate the documents were prepared for counsel or prepared in confidence with counsel. Here, the clear dominant purpose of the creation of most of the materials sought was for PAA as an institution to investigate complaints of teachers and students of an employee’s sexual misconduct and maintain an appropriate and safe learning environment, not to prepare for trial.

 

Therefore, PAA’s objection on this ground is unmeritorious.

 

                                                                             ii.     Privacy

 

PAA also argues this request invades Romero’s privacy rights in his personnel file and employment records and students’ privacy rights to the extent they are named within the personnel file. The privacy rights of students are equivalent in California under Education Code section 49076 are equivalent to the rights under FERPA. As such, potential infringements on student privacy will be addressed in the following section.

 

When evaluating invasions of the right to privacy in discovery, the party asserting a privacy right must establish “(1) a legally protected privacy interest; (2) a reasonable expectation of privacy in the circumstances; and (3) conduct by defendant constituting a serious invasion of privacy.” (Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1, 39-40.) A responding party may prevail by negating any of these three elements “or by pleading and proving, as an affirmative defense, that the invasion of privacy is justified because it substantively furthers one or more countervailing interests.” (Id at 40.) “[T]he party seeking protection may identify feasible alternatives that serve the same interests or protective measures that would diminish the loss of privacy.” (Williams v. Superior Ct. (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 533.) A court then balances these competing considerations. (Ibid.) “[O]bvious invasions of interest fundamental to personal autonomy must be supported by a compelling interest.” (Id. at 557.) When lesser interests are at stake, “the strength of the countervailing interest sufficient to warrant disclosure of private information var[ies] according to the strength of the privacy interest itself, the seriousness of the invasion, and the availability of alternatives and protective measures.” (Id. at 556.)  

 

California’s Constitutional right to privacy protects against the unwarranted, compelled disclosure of various private or sensitive information regarding one’s personal life.¿(See Britt v. Superior Court (1978) 20 Cal.3d 844, 855-856.) This privacy right extends to employment records.¿(See Board of Trustees v. Superior Court (1981) 119 Cal.App.3d 516, 526, disapproved on other grounds by Williams, supra, 3 Cal.5th at fn. 8.)

 

Thus, Romero has a legally protected privacy interests in his employee record and personnel file. The Court also finds that Romero has a reasonable expectation of privacy in these records. (Riske v. Superior Court (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 647, 656 [recognizing this privacy right in the context of production of a peace officer's records]; Associated Chino Teachers v. Chino Valley Unified School Dist. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 530, 539 [in production of employment records of a public school teacher]; Board of Trustees v. Superior Court (1981) 119 Cal.App.3d 516, 528 ["It is manifest that the subject documents and communications of Dr. Dong's personnel, tenure, and promotion files, whether relating only to his initial employment, or also to his 'promotion, additional compensation, or termination,' were communicated to the University in confidence, and were thus covered by the communicators' constitutional right of privacy"] (disapproved of on a narrower point by Williams, supra, 3 Cal.5th 531).)

 

However, as to the seriousness of the invasion, Plaintiff’s requests are narrowly tailored and limit the discovery request to documentation relating to Plaintiff and Romero’s sexual misconduct, reports of sexual misconduct, and discipline as a teacher. Much of the discovery would, at most, reveal records related to Romero, his alleged misconduct, or records related to teacher and staff reports of Romero’s alleged sexual assault, harassment, and molestation of Plaintiff, which is the central issue of this case, and presents a very limited privacy concern in the reporting of such conduct. Plaintiff has also agreed to redaction or withholding as appropriate of Romero’s personal contact information, address, financial and benefit documentation, and tax documents. Furthermore, not only would the names of any individuals involved with reporting or investigating Plaintiff’s assault identify witnesses to Romero’s alleged sexual misconduct, but a protective order limiting disclosure of confidential materials will quash any serious privacy concerns. 

 

To the extent that Plaintiff seeks production of documentation related to Romero’s assault of other students, these documents, including any reports, investigations, and findings bear directly on PAA’s knowledge of Romero’s misconduct prior to the incidents involving Plaintiff, another central issue of this case. For the same reasons above, the invasion cannot be construed as serious in light of the circumstances, particularly because Romero is a party to the case, his assault of other minor students is a central issue, there has already been criminal action taken against Romero for these alleged acts, and he acted in the course and scope of his employment with a public school district.

 

Thus, the invasion is not serious enough to invoke constitutional privacy concerns.

 

Even if the Court were to find that the invasion into Romero’s right to privacy was serious, Plaintiff has established a countervailing interest which supersedes his right to privacy in these circumstances.

 

Invasions of interests fundamental to personal autonomy must be supported by a compelling interest in providing the discovery. (Williams, supra, 3 Cal.5th at 556, 557.) Under this test, the discovery should be "directly relevant" to a plaintiff's claim and its disclosure be essential to fair resolution of the lawsuit. (Britt v. Superior Court (1978) 20 Cal.3d 844, 859.) If the information sought or something equivalent could be obtained through other means, such as by conducting a deposition without reaching for documents, or a less intrusive method of discovery exists,  the court will not compel production of the private information. (Allen v. Superior Court (1984) 151 Cal.App.3d 447, 449.)

 

Plaintiff states she needs the information within Romero’s personnel file to establish his well-known, documented history of abusing minor students in his care, to establish that PAA hired Romero after an investigation confirmed he abused children at a different school within PSD, and to establish that PAA knew about Romero’s prior misconduct, all of which are critically relevant to Plaintiff’s claims. (Motion, p. 4:1-8.) Plaintiff also claims any complaints, disciplinary reports, investigation materials, and other documentation reflecting complaints made against Romero by students at PAA show PAA’s negligent supervision, and that there are no available alternative means to obtain this information outside of the contents of his personnel file. (Motion, p. 4:8-14.)

 

Here, Romero is a party to this case for his alleged sexual battery of Plaintiff in the course and scope of Romero’s employment. Romero was employed as a public school teacher allegedly after PAA and PSD discovered his sexual assault and molestation of other minor children during his employment as a teacher, and Plaintiff specifically alleges that PAA and PSD, employers of Romero and mandated reporters, negligently hired, retained, and supervised Romero, an allegation based on their purported knowledge of his prior and ongoing sexual misconduct against minor students. Romero’s personnel file may likely contain information directly relevant to the claims against him, PAA, and PSD, relevant to the claims that PSD and PAA failed to make a mandatory report upon reports of misconduct, and supportive of the claims that PSD and PAA were made aware of the conduct and failed to act after reports from multiple students and staff.

 

This discovery is directly related to Plaintiffs’ claims, and would also lead to the discovery of the identities of other witnesses. PAA fails to identify any alternatives available to discover documents related to Romero’s alleged misconduct, the identities of other witnesses, the extent of PAA’s knowledge of the assault, or the actions taken in response to the reports and knowledge of sexual misconduct, all of which are essential to a fair resolution of this case concerning PSD and PAA’s knowledge and negligent hiring and supervision. Plaintiff thus has a significant interest in discovering the requested information, which outweighs any concern of infringement on Romero’s right to privacy. Because the invasion of the privacy interests at issue is minimal compared to the strength of Plaintiffs’ interest in discovering the information, the circumstances weigh in favor of allowing the discovery. 

 

                                                                           iii.     FERPA

 

Educational records and personally identifiable information of students are protected by FERPA. (20 USC § 1232g.) FERPA¿bars federal funding to educational agencies and institutions with a "policy or practice" of releasing either "educational records" or "personally identifiable information" without parental consent.¿(20 USC § 1232g.)¿The term "educational records" is broadly defined to include "records, filed, documents and other material which ... contains information directly related to a student" and "are maintained by an educational agency or institution or by a person acting for such agency or institution." (20 USC § 1232g, subd. (a)(4)(B).) "Personally identifiable information" does not extend to "directory information," which includes the student's name,¿address¿and telephone number.¿(20 USC § 1232g, subd. (a)(5)(A).) FERPA¿only punishes educational institutions that release such records as a matter of policy or practice. (See Ragusa v.¿Malverne¿Union Free School Dist.¿(E.D.N.Y. 2008) 549 F.Supp.2d 288, 291-292;¿see also Rim of the World Unified School Dist. v. Superior Court¿(104 Cal.App.4th 1393, 1396-1398 [FERPA¿preempted former California statutory provisions requiring all student expulsions to be made public].) It does not create a "privilege" preventing disclosure of students' records or bar disclosure¿pursuant to¿court order. (Ragusa, supra, 549 F.Supp.2d at p. 291, 293.) Additionally, Cal. Educ. Code § 49076 allows access to pupil records with either parental consent or under court order. (See¿Cal. Educ. Code §¿49076, subd. (a) [“A school district shall not permit access to pupil records to a person without written parental consent or under judicial order except as set forth in this section and as permitted by Part 99 (commencing with Section 99.1) of Title 34 of the Code of Federal Regulations.”].)  

 

Here, the records at issue are not protected by FERPA. None of the records within Romero’s personnel file constitute educational records of any third party student at PAA, nor does the request seek any personal educational record of any third party student. All of Plaintiff’s requests pertain to Romero directly, and any collateral mention of another student does not render the documents in which they are mentioned coverable by FERPA. Furthermore, even where a student’s name is contained within Romero’s personnel file, for example in connection with a report of misconduct or statement relating thereto, student names are not considered personally identifiable information within the meaning of FERPA, nor are statements made in connection with disciplinary action of a teacher considered educational records. In BRV, Inc. v. Superior Court (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 742, 754-55, the Court held that even where a report identified students by name and detailed actions taken by and against those students, the report did not directly relate to the private educational interests of the students, and the purpose of the report was to investigate complaints of misconduct by an employee. The Court reasoned that the report did not fall within FERPA’s ambit, as it was not regularly done in the normal course of business and was not the type of report regularly maintained in a central location along with education records such as in separate files for each student. (Id at 755.) While PAA attempts to distinguish this case on the grounds that BRV is a CPRA case, the reasoning stands.

 

In any event, even if these records risk disclosing information protected by FERPA, disclosure of such information in civil discovery does not constitute a policy or practice of releasing educational records or personally identifiable information. Even if it did, the Court may nonetheless order PAA to produce the information, and the Court notes that a FERPA Order has been drafted and agreed upon by all parties to this action, minimizing privacy concerns. Thus, FERPA does not prohibit PAA from producing the requested documentation. Furthermore, “[b]y its plain terms, FERPA does not create an evidentiary privilege, and the Court agrees with prior decisions holding that documents covered by FERPA are indeed discoverable in the context of a civil action.” (Garza v. Scott & White Mem. Hosp. (2005) 234 F.R.D. 617, 624.)

 

Therefore, PAA is not entitled to withhold the production of Romero’s personnel file on the basis of FERPA protection.

 

                                                                           iv.     Vague, Overbroad, and Unduly Burdensome

 

“The court shall limit the scope of discovery if it determines that the burden, expense, or intrusiveness of that discovery clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” (Code Civ.¿Proc. § 2017.020, subd. (a).)¿Objections¿on the ground of “burden must be sustained by evidence showing the quantum of work required” and “to support an objection of oppression there must be some showing either of an intent to create an unreasonable burden or that the ultimate effect of the burden is incommensurate with the result sought.”¿(West Pico Furniture Co. of Los Angeles v. Superior Court¿In¿and For Los Angeles County¿(1961) 56 Cal.2d 407, 417.)¿It is the objecting party’s burden to supply evidence that the responsive burden would be “undue or excessive, relative to the likelihood of admissible evidence being discovered.” (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 550.) Even if discovery requests are found to be burdensome and oppressive, trial courts do not deny discovery completely but set “partial limitations on discovery” by weighing “the relative importance of the information against the hardship entailed by its production.” (Borse v. Superior Court¿(Southern Pac. Co.) (1970)¿7 Cal.App.3d 286, 289.)¿“When discovery requests are grossly overbroad on their face, and hence do not appear reasonably related to a legitimate discovery need, a reasonable inference can be drawn of an intent to harass and improperly burden.” (Obregon v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 424, 431.)

 

The Court does not find any basis in this objection for Romero’s personnel file. PAA has not established how production of this file would result in any undue burden, cost, expense, or time, how the production efforts would be incommensurate with the result sought or the relevance of the documents, or that there would be any hardship in producing the records requested. Furthermore, the Court does not find that the request for Romero’s personnel file, created and maintained during his employment with PPA beginning in 2024, to be vague or ambiguous or overbroad. Finally, this request is unquestionably related to Plaintiff’s claims related to PAA and PSD’s knowledge of, and failure to take action regarding, Romero’s alleged misconduct. This objection is without merit.

 

Therefore, Plaintiff’s motion is GRANTED as to request number 6 with limitations set forth below.

 

d.     Request No. 7

 

Request No. 7 seeks “All WRITINGS RELATED TO YOUR INVESTIGATION of ROMERO’s SEXUAL MISCONDUCT towards Plaintiff K.Z.”

 

PAA objected on the grounds of attorney-client and attorney work product privileges, that the request forces PAA to disclose its trial strategy and witnesses, that it invades the privacy rights of third parties, that the request is vague, overbroad, unduly burdensome, and that it seeks confidential student information protected by FERPA. PAA declared it could not disclose information without a court order authorizing disclosure, and that it would produce responsive non-privileged documents following entry of a court order.

 

While PAA is correct that generally, witness statements obtained by an attorney are subject to qualified work product protection, here, PAA has failed to make any showing on a privilege log as required to establish that any part of the investigation was conducted by an attorney or that any statements made by students or staff were obtained by counsel in anticipation of litigation. (Coito v. Superior Court (2012) 54 Cal.4th 480, 496.) PAA identifies only “Investigations and Reports” from “Multiple dates in 2024” as privileged information. (Shoga Decl., Ex. 2.) As above, PAA has failed to establish that any of these reports are privileged, authored by counsel, contain attorney impressions, opinions, conclusions, or legal analysis, or state with any specificity the title of the document, the date it was authored, or who authored it. Absent any showing by PAA that these investigations and reports were made with the primary purpose of preparing for trial (as opposed to investigations and reports being made to fulfill PAA’s necessary obligation as an educational institution to investigate complaints and reports of sexual abuse of a minor child in the school’s care in the normal operation of the school), the Court cannot give weight to this objection.

 

The Court is also not convinced that disclosure of such materials would force PAA to disclose its trial strategy. The privilege log identifies reports and investigations taking place throughout 2024, Plaintiff did not submit a written claim for damages pursuant to the Government Claims Act until April 22, 2025, and this case was not initiated until September 16, 2025. (Compl., ¶ 43.) While the Court recognizes the possibility that PAA began trial preparations and legal strategy decision-making for litigation of the present action before any such action was actually raised or filed, PAA has not established that counsel was retained for the purpose of defending or prosecuting this action or any other action, nor that any of the reports or investigative materials contain any trial strategy, research, or other preparation material. PAA may not withhold discoverable, relevant documentation or evidence solely on the basis that it plans to use such evidence itself.

 

The Court also finds PAA’s objection on the grounds of disclosure of witnesses unfounded. Plaintiff, as a party to this action, has a right to discover any matter relevant to the subject matter involved in this action, or to the determination of any motion made in this action, if the matter is admissible or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010.) “This right includes an entitlement to learn the identity and location of persons having knowledge of any discoverable matter.” (Williams, supra, 3 Cal.5th at 541.) Discovery of witnesses to Romero’s alleged harassment and abuse of Plaintiff unquestionably falls within the ambit of basic discovery, and PAA cannot withhold information of percipient witnesses to the acts alleged in Plaintiff’s complaint because it may itself use certain individuals as witnesses at trial.

 

Finally, for the reasons stated above as to Request No. 6, the Court finds that none of Defendants objections to the production of writings related to the investigation of Romero’s misconduct towards Plaintiff are meritorious.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion as to request number 7 is GRANTED with limitations set forth below..

Penal Code section 11167.5, subdivision (b) “presents an exhaustive list of the agencies and individuals to whom a report of known or suspected child abuse may be disclosed.” (Cuff v. Grossmont Union High School Dist., 221 Cal. App. 4th 582, 590-591.) Section 11167.5, subdivision (b)(1) states that reports of suspected child abuse and information contained therein may be disclosed to persons to whom disclosure of the identity of the reporting party is permitted under Section 11167.

 

Penal Code section 11167 sets forth that the identity of the mandated reporter who made the report shall remain confidential except to limited agencies, persons, and entities, and subdivision (d)(1) states that the identity of the reporter may be disclosed by court order. Therefore, this report is, on court order, discoverable in a civil case. PAA does not argue that this request is irrelevant or should not be disclosed, only that it was prohibited from so disclosing. The Court agrees with PAA that absent a Court order, it has no authority or ability to disclose this report, and to do so would be a misdemeanor. Plaintiff also argues that the identification of the mandated reporter should be disclosed so Plaintiff may depose the individual about what they saw and observed which gave rise to the mandated report. (Reply, p. 9:26-28.)

 

Here, the Court recognizes the strong policy in favor of confidentiality of mandated reporters. The Court also recognizes the importance of the mandated report of Plaintiff’s alleged abuse perpetrated by Romero, the relevance of the information contained on the report to the claims asserted by Plaintiff, and the request to depose the mandated reporter as to what was witnessed leading to the reporting. The Court finds that production of the report and disclosure of the mandated reporter is warranted in this case.

 

For the reasons stated above, the Court rejects PAA’s remaining objections to production.

   II.          Legal Standard

Standard for Reconsideration – When an application for an order has been made to a judge and granted, any party affected by the order may move the court for reconsideration of the matter and modify, amend, or revoke the prior order. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a).)

A party may make such a motion if the motion is: (1) brought before the same judge or court that make the order sought to be reconsidered; (2) made within 10 days after service upon the party of the notice of entry of the order; (3) based on new or different facts, circumstances or law than those before the court at the time of the original ruling; and (4) supported by a declaration stating what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and the new or different facts, circumstances or law claimed to exist. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1008, subd. (a).)  

 

The moving party must present new facts, circumstances, or law in order to grant a motion for reconsideration. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a); see also Mink v. Superior Court (1992) 2 Cal.App.4th 1338, 1342.) Further, “the party seeking reconsideration must provide not just new evidence or different facts, but a satisfactory explanation for the failure to produce it at an earlier time.” (Glade v. Glade (1995) 38 Cal.App.4th 1441, 1457.)  The legislative intent was to restrict motions for reconsideration to circumstances where a party offers the court some fact or circumstance not previously considered and some valid reason for not offering it earlier. (Gilberd v. AC Transit (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 1494, 1500.)

 III.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendant moves for reconsideration of the Court’s April 14, 2026 Order granting Plaintiffs’ motion to compel discovery responses and awarding $2,872.50 in monetary sanctions pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a) on the grounds that new and different facts and circumstances justify reconsideration. (Motion, p. 2:5-10.) Defendant argues that on April 15, 2026, Plaintiffs reserved a hearing on, and thereafter filed, a motion to compel further responses to the same discovery, which presupposes that initial responses had been served, and Plaintiffs’ own filing confirms what Defendant previously advised the Court, which is that substantive, written responses to all three sets of discovery were served on February 23, 2026, with verifications on March 3, 2026, rendering the motion to compel responses underlying the April 14th Order moot. (Motion, p. 2:10-19.)

 

The timeline of discovery leading up to this motion is as follows:

 

On January 6, 2026, Plaintiffs served their first sets of Form Interrogatories, Special Interrogatories, and Requests for Production. (Motion, p. 4:18-19.) On February 22, 2026, Plaintiffs served their motion to compel, which was subsequently filed on February 23rd. (Motion, p. 4:19-21.) On February 23, 2026, Defendant served written responses to all three sets of discovery on Plaintiff’s counsel by email. (Motion, p. 4:22-23.) As these responses were unverified, they were legally invalid and tantamount to no response at all. (See Laguna Auto Body v. Farmers Ins. Exchange (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 481, 487.) Defendant served verifications to each set on March 3, 2026. (Motion, p. 4:24-25.) Defense counsel filed a declaration in opposition to the motion to compel on April 13, 2026, one day before the hearing on the motion, advising the Court that the motion was moot because responses had been served, with copies of the responses attached as exhibits. (Motion, p. 4:26-5:1.) Nonetheless, the Court granted the motion to compel on April 14th and awarded monetary sanctions for Defendant’s failure to timely respond to discovery in the amount of $2,872.50. (Motion, p. 5:1-3.) On April 15, 2026, Plaintiffs reserved the present hearing date for a motion to compel further responses to the same three sets of discovery, which states that Defendant provided responses on the dates specified above. (Motion, p. 5:4-9.)

 

Defendant argues that the motion to compel was brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.290 and 2031.300, which authorize a motion to compel only when a party has failed to serve a response, while a motion to compel further responses under sections 2030.300 and 2031.310 presupposes that responses have been served, and the two motions are mutually exclusive. (Motion, p. 5:22-27.) Thus, Defendant argues Plaintiff contradictorily claimed both that the responses had not been served at all, and that responses were served but were not sufficient. (Motion, p. 6:5-9.) Defendant relies on Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390 in support of his contention that the motion to compel initial responses was moot on April 14th when the motion was granted.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that Defendant offers no new facts or evidence in support of the motion, failed to oppose the motion to compel, already raised the mootness issue prior to the April 14, 2026 hearing, and misrepresents Sinaiko, which holds exactly the opposite of what Defendant claims. (Opp., p. 2:4-17.)

 

On reply, Defendant argues that the Court’s July 21, 2026 Order granting Plaintiffs’ motion to compel further responses constitutes a new circumstance which could not have been presented on April 14, 2026, supersedes the April 14, 2026 Order, and adjudicated that Defendant’s responses to Special Interrogatories Nos. 4 and 6 and Form Interrogatory No. 50.4 were code-compliant, a determination which cannot coexist with an order compelling responses to the same requests on the premise that none had been served. (Reply, p. 2:1-20.)

 

As a preliminary matter, the Court disregards Defendant’s argument that the Court’s July 21, 2026 Order presents a new circumstance or facts on which to grant this motion. First, Defendant may not raise a new argument on reply or submit new evidence when the motion itself was based on separate circumstances. (See Reichardt v. Hoffman (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 754, 764.) Second, even if the Court were to consider this argument, for the same reasons as discussed below, the argument is unavailing.

 

Here, there are no new facts offered by Defendant. The Court was aware, as Defendant himself admits in his motion, that Defendant had served untimely and unverified responses on February 23, 2026, and served verifications on March 3, 2026. Not only did Defendant raise this argument at the hearing, but Plaintiffs also expressly admitted these facts in their reply brief, filed on April 13, 2026. (See Reply, p. 2:10-11.) Thus, Plaintiffs’ admission of these facts in the subsequent motion to compel further responses is not a new circumstance or fact which did not exist at the time of the hearing. Defendant raised the mootness argument prior to and at the hearing on the motion to compel. The Court considered this argument and rejected it. Defendant has offered no new facts or evidence on which this ruling should be abandoned.

 

Furthermore, contrary to Defendant’s argument, Sinaiko did not not hold anywhere in the opinion that a motion to compel is moot upon untimely service of discovery responses after such a motion is filed. On the contrary, Sinaiko expressly authorizes the Court to hear a motion to compel initial responses even where a party filed untimely responses after the motion to compel was filed but before the hearing. The Court held that to accept that a Court has no discretion to hear and rule on a motion to compel initial responses after untimely responses are provided “would remove an important incentive for parties to respond to discovery in a timely fashion” and “a party to whom interrogatories were directed could wait until the hearing on a section 2030.290 motion was imminent, then serve a set of evasive and incomplete responses, and thereby unilaterally deprive the trial court of authority to hear the motion.” (Sinaiko, supra, 148 Cal.App.4th at 408.) Therefore, “once a party has failed to serve timely [discovery] responses, the trial court has the authority to hear a propounding party's motion to compel responses… regardless of whether a party serves an untimely response.” (Ibid.)

 

The County of San Benito v. Superior Court (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 243, 256 echoed Sinaiko’s reasoning in holding the same as to motions to compel further responses, stating that to adopt such an “interpretation of the discovery act—that a party opposing a motion to compel further responses may unilaterally divest the court of authority to rule on the motion by serving a further response in the interim—would permit a party faced with a motion to compel to delay its adjudication indefinitely by successive supplemental but inadequate responses, each prompting a further motion to compel, each of which in turn would be defeated by piecemeal supplementation.”

 

Defendant goes on to argue that Sinaiko holds that while a Court is not divested of authority to hear a motion to compel when a party serves untimely responses, whether the Court should proceed with such a motion rests in the Court’s discretion, and contends that the April 14, 2026 Order should not remain in place now that the July 21, 2026 Order has fully addressed the concerns raised in Sinaiko. (Reply, p. 5:5-12.) Defendant states that the “protection Sinaiko preserves has been vindicated through the July 21 Order. Nothing remains for the April 14 Order to accomplish.” (Reply, p. 5:11-12.)

 

The Court rejects this argument outright. Defendant failed to provide timely discovery responses. Plaintiffs’ motion to compel was granted and monetary sanctions issued, as warranted and permitted by law, for Defendant’s failure to abide by the lawful discovery process in serving initial responses. Plaintiffs were then obligated to file a secondary motion to compel further discovery responses due to Defendant’s evasive, incomplete, and non-compliant responses. That motion was also granted in large part, and monetary sanctions awarded, as warranted and permitted by law. The Court fails to see any inconsistency between these Orders, and fails to see how the July 21, 2026 Order addresses precisely the same objective as the April 14th Order, as both motions were brought under separate statutes and with different objectives, as Defendant notes in his motion. Rather, these Orders demonstrate the exact concern addressed by Sinaiko, which is that a party serving evasive responses on the eve of a hearing on a motion to compel may be engaging in gamesmanship and attempting to avoid the effectiveness and application of the discovery statutes by serving untimely, insufficient discovery responses and shifting the burden to the propounding party and avoiding the consequences of failure to abide by discovery deadlines. The Court finds that both the April 14, 2026 Order and the July 21st Order are valid and warranted, and sees no reason to abandon either. Additionally, as stated above, Defendant has failed to offer any new evidence or argument not already raised prior to and at the hearing, as Defendant’s arguments relating to the April 15th reservation, the filing of the motion to compel further responses, and the Court’s July 21st Order all appear to be grounded on admission of Defendant’s service of discovery responses prior to April 14th.

 

Furthermore, even where a party provides untimely responses after a motion to compel has been filed, the Court may still award monetary sanctions. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1348(a) [“The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though…the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.”].) Even were the Court to entertain Defendant’s arguments and overturn its Order granting the motion to compel, there are no new grounds on which this Court finds it warranted to abandon its Order mandating monetary sanctions be paid for failure to comply with timely initial discovery responses.

 

Therefore, Defendant’s motion for reconsideration is DENIED. The Court’s April 14, 2026 Order will stand, as will its July 21, 2026 Order compelling further responses.

 

Plaintiffs’ request for an increase in monetary sanctions under the July 21, 2026 Order is DENIED.

Defendant Hrant Danakian’s Motion for Reconsideration is DENIED.

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Meet and Confer Requirement – Before filing a demurrer or motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due, in person, by telephone, or by video conference, with the party who filed the pleading for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer or motion to strike. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a), 435.5, subd. (a).) The demurring or moving party shall file and serve with the demurrer or motion to strike a declaration stating the means by which the parties met and conferred and that the parties did not reach an agreement to resolve the issues raised, or that the party who filed the pleading demurred or objected to failed to respond or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith.  III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Demurrer – A party against whom a complaint has been filed may object by demurrer to the pleading on the ground that the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations reasonably, liberally, and in context. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) Courts must “treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at 318.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 126.) The threshold for meeting this burden is low, and “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.)

 

Standard for Motion to Strike – A motion to strike lies where either (1) there is “irrelevant, false or improper matter inserted in any pleading,” or (2) where a pleading or part thereof is “not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule or order of court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The Court has the authority to strike out any part of a pleading not filed in conformity with an order of the court. (See Ibid; Ferraro v. Camarlinghi (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 509, 528.) 

When the pleadings are defective, “the defect may be raised by demurrer or motion to strike, or by motion for judgment on the pleadings.” (Coyne v. Krempels (1950) 36 Cal.2d 257, 262.) The grounds for a motion to strike shall¿appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take¿judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (a).)¿A matter to be judicially noticed shall be specified in the notice of motion.¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (b).)¿The court then may¿strike out any irrelevant, false, or¿improper¿matter inserted in any pleading¿and strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.)¿When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is¿capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.¿(Perlman v. Municipal Court¿(1979) 99 Cal.App.3d 568, 575.)

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendants demur to the third cause of action for violation of Business and Professions Code section 10176, and the complaint as a whole Paulauskas separately demurs to the fourth cause of action for broker liability pursuant to Civil Code section 2338, and to the complaint as a whole. (Demurrer, p. 2:9-26.)

 

a.     Defendants’ Demurrer to Third Cause of Action – Violation of Business and Professions Code § 10176(a)

 

 

This cause of action alleges a violation of B&P Section 10176(a).  The cause of action reads, in relevant part, “Defendant Baker’s conduct constitutes grounds for discipline by the California Department of Real Estate, including possible suspension or revocation of her license.” (Compl., ¶ 18.)

 

Defendants argue that the Court lacks jurisdiction over this cause of action because the Section provides only for administrative and disciplinary relief to be determined by the Califonia Depart of Real Estate (“DRE”). It vests the DRE with authority to “investigate the actions of any person engaged in the business or acting in the capacity of a real estate licensee within this state, and he or she may temporarily suspend or permanently revoke a real estate license at any time where the licensee, while a real estate licensee, in performing or attempting to perform any of the acts within the scope of this chapter has been guilty of any of the following: (a) making any substantial misrepresentation.” (Id.)

 

 and revocation, both temporary and permanent, of all [real estate] licenses …” [Bus. & Prof. C. § 10071].”

 

 

Defendants argue that because this cause of action seeks discipline by the California Department of Real Estate (DRE), including suspension or revocation of Baker’s license, and because the Court has no jurisdiction over Baker’s license, the Court has no jurisdiction over Plaintiffs’ cause of action for Baker’s alleged violation of the Business and Professions Code.

 

Plaintiffs’ opposition is silent on this point.

 

 

The Court would not phrase this so much as a jurisdictional issue, but that  Business and Professions Code section 10176, subdivision (a) by its plain terms does not give rise in and of itself to a private cause of action for enforcement of the administrative and disciplinary relief Plaintiff seeks. As the Cause of Action is incapable of amendment – under no circumstances may the Court grant the relief sought by Plaintiff in the Cause of Action – the Court SUSTAINS the Demurrer to the Third Cause of Action WITHOUT LEAVE.

 

But there is an important qualification. The reason the Court would not characterize this as a jurisdiction issue is that the Court does have jurisdiction over the types of conduct proscribed by B&P Section 10176. Although § 10176(a) does not itself create a cause of action, its prohibitions on substantial misrepresentation establish a standard of conduct that California courts have held can support a negligence per se theory in a private civil action. In  Jorgensen v. Beach 'N' Bay Realty, Inc. (1981) 125 Cal. App. 3d 155, the Court of Appeal held that § 10176, subdivisions (a), (b), and (i) — which prohibit substantial misrepresentation, false promises, and fraud or dishonest dealing — set a statutory standard of care for real estate agents, and that violation of those provisions constitutes negligence per se.  Thus Plaintiff could rely on alleged violations of B&P Section 10176 to support surviving causes of action, or to support a motion for leave to allege additional cause of action based on alleged misrepresentations. The Court expressly is not making any statement in support of or in discouragement of such a motion.  

 

.

 

b.     Paulauskas’s Demurrer to the Entire Complaint and Fourth Cause of Action – Broker Liability – Civil Code § 2338

 

Paulauskas demurs to the Complaint as a whole and the fourth cause of action for broker liability on the same grounds. Thus, the Court addresses both concurrently.

 

Paulauskas argues that under the statutory scheme regulating real estate brokers and salespersons, Business and Professions Code section 10000 et seq., a corporation may be a real estate broker if it is licensed through an officer of the corporation who qualifies as a broker. (Demurrer, p. 6:18-20.) Paulauskas argues that pursuant to Walters v. Marler (1978) 83 Cal.App.3d 1, 34-35, he owed no duty to Plaintiffs in his supervisory role with Rafeh Corporation, but rather, may only be liable to Rafeh Corporation, that any action taken by him must be regarded as an action by Rafeh Corporation and not Paulauskas as an individual, and that he therefore cannot be held liable for Baker’s misrepresentations. (Demurrer, p. 6:21-8:4.)

 

Defendants are correct in that Walters controls. In Walters,

 

 

 

In Walters, the corporation (Lampliter) was the corporate real estate broker. It held its license  through a qualifying broker (Proulx). The real estate agent (Leseman) was an agent of Lampliter, and supervised by Proulx. The Court of Appeal affirmed the trial court’s grant of a motion for non-suit on finding that he owed no duty to plaintiff, who was represented in the sale by the agent Leseman. :

 

Despite this evidence of negligence on Proulx' part, Proulx may not be held individually liable to Walters for injuries sustained as a result of Leseman's misrepresentations. The noncontractual rights and liabilities of a real estate broker are governed and determined by the general laws of agency. (1 Miller & Starr, Cal. Real Estate (rev. ed. 1975) pt. 2, § 4:4, pp. 9-10.) In the instant case Leseman was undeniably an agent for both Walters and Lampliter; however, he was not an agent for Proulx. Any action by the qualifying broker, Proulx, must be regarded as an action by the corporation and not by the broker as an individual. Thus, as an agent of the corporation, Proulx owed a duty to Lampliter to supervise the work of Lampliter employees. Proulx may therefore be liable to Lampliter in an action for indemnification. However, Proulx owed no duty to Walters to supervise Leseman's work; he therefore may not be held personally liable to Walters for Leseman's negligent misrepresentations.

(Walters v. Marler (1978) 83 Cal. App. 3d 1, 35, disapproved of on other grounds by Gray v. Don Miller & Assocs., Inc. (1984) 35 Cal. 3d 498, 674 P.2d 253 (1984).)

 

 

Here, Plaintiffs’ complaint characterizes Paulauskas as “employing broker of Keller Williams Realty / Rafeh Corporation” and asserts that he “is vicariously liable for all acts of Defendant Baker committed within the course and scope of her employment.” (Compl., ¶ 21.) Plaintiffs’ complaint also characterizes Baker as “a licensed real estate agent under Broker Thomas Paulauskas (Keller Williams Realty / Rafeh Corporation).” (Compl., ¶ 19.) The complaint also named Paulauskas specifically in his role as employing broker of Keller Williams and Rafeh Corporation, naming both corporations as Paulauskas’s “akas.”

 

The complaint makes clear that Keller Williams and/or Rafeh Corporation was the corporate broker in this transaction and not Paulauskas, who only served as the qualifying broker for the corporation(s). This is further evidenced by the documents attached to the complaint, in which Baker signs off as an agent for Keller Williams Realty. The complaint fails to allege any independent wrongdoing on Paulauskas’s behalf, and per Walters, because Paulauskas is merely the qualifying broker of Keller Williams and Rafeh Corporation, no agency relationship legally exists between Paulauskas and Baker. Thus, Baker is not, as a matter of law, an agent of Paulauskas, but rather is an agent of Keller Williams and/or Rafeh Corporation in connection with this transaction, and vicarious liability does not extend to Paulauskas in his individual capacity or his capacity as qualifying broker.

 

Therefore, Paulauskas’s demurrer to the complaint as a whole and to the fourth cause of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend. However, as discussed, Baker is, by law, an agent of the broker, and Plaintiffs’ complaint may reasonably be amended to sufficiently state a cause of action against the corporate broker involved in the transaction based on vicarious liability principles, or against Paulauskas as an individual if independent wrongdoing or an agency relationship is sufficiently pled. Thus, Plaintiffs are granted leave to amend to plead factual allegations as to Paulauskas’s independent wrongdoing giving rise to liability outside of any agency relationship with Baker, to establish a direct agency relationship between Baker and Paulauskas, or to name the appropriate broker defendants to support their claims of vicarious liability.

 

c.      Motion to Strike

 

Defendants move to strike Plaintiffs’ requests for (1) emotional distress damages and (2) attorney’s fees from Rafeh Corporation. (Motion, p. 2:8-19.)

 

As to emotional distress damages, Defendants argue that courts have consistently held that non-economic damages, such as emotional distress damages, are not recoverable in real property cases where the underlying cause of distress is economic activity, such as the purchase of real property. (Motion, p. 4:9-11.) Defendants rely on three California cases addressing this issue, outlined below.

 

In Kruse v. Bank of America (1988) 202 Cal.App.3d 38, 67, the Court held that emotional distress damages are not recoverable for fraud.

 

In Erlich v. Menezes (1999) 21 Cal.4th 543, 554-55, 558, the Supreme Court held that where a property builder negligently built the plaintiffs’ home, the plaintiffs were not entitled to recover in contract or in negligence where the property resulted in no physical injury, following the general rule in California that recovery for mental suffering or emotional distress is generally unavailable where the defendant’s tortious conduct resulted only in economic injury to the plaintiff.

 

In Gravillis v. Coldwell Banker Residential Brokerage Co. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 761, 782, the Court held that even severe emotional distress led to bodily injury (diabetes), emotional distress  did not qualify as “bodily injury” sufficient to support a claim for emotional distress injuries.  . The Court reasoned that the “bodily injury” exception is limited to bodily injury caused by the condition of the property itself, not from the emotional distress caused by the underlying misrepresentation or concealment. (Id. at pp. 781-782.) As the Court stated, “A similar analysis about the unavailability of damages for emotional distress applies here. Under California law, it is doubtful the Gravillises can recover for emotional distress given that (1) their claims are based on a broker's alleged failure to disclose property (termite) damage, and (2) the emotional distress was caused solely by what they saw and heard after the property had been damaged.” (Id., at 776.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that they do not merely allege simple economic injury, but allege false advertising, negligent misrepresentation, reliance on material representations concerning safety-related conditions, undisclosed hazardous electrical conditions, water intrusion, structural deterioration, financial hardship to elderly plaintiffs with limited resources, and emotional suffering. (Opp., p. 3:19-4:3.) Plaintiffs argue that fraud-based misconduct may support emotional distress damages where the conduct is intentional, reckless, or particularly egregious, and that such damages are warranted here. (Opp., p. 4:4-5.)

 

The Court agrees with Defendants that as currently pled, the factual allegations are insufficient to support a request for emotional distress damages. The Court also notes that damages for intentional fraud and negligent misrepresentation in the purchase, sale, or exchange of property are limited to damages specified by Civil Code section 3343. (Devin v. United Services Auto. Assn. (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 1149, 1162.) Civil Code section 3343 does not provide for emotional distress damages. Furthermore, “a plaintiff may not recover damages for emotional distress in an action for negligent misrepresentation where the ‘plaintiff’s direct loss resulting from the negligent conduct of the defendant was [only] economic.’” (Butler-Rupp v. Lourdeaux (2005) 134 Cal.App.4th 1220, 1227, citing Branch v. Homefed Bank (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 793, 801.)

 

The underlying complaint is based wholly on the purchase of real property and the misrepresentations regarding the Property’s condition giving rise to the sale, which, as the cases above demonstrate, does not entitle a plaintiff to recover for emotional distress absent physical injury caused by the property. Accordingly, the Motion to Strike as to emotional distress damages is GRANTED.

 

 However, because Plaintiffs have alleged dangerous conditions on the property, the Court will grant leave to amend, and Plaintiffs may plead factual allegations of any personal injury they may have sustained from the Property’s condition as a basis for emotional distress damages, if any exist.

 

 

 

 

 

As to attorney’s fees, Defendants argue that Plaintiffs’ request for fees should be stricken because Plaintiffs have failed to attach a contract with a fees clause between Plaintiffs and Defendants and do not identify any law that entitles them to attorneys’ fees should they prevail. (Motion, p. 7:17-20.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that attorney’s fees should not be stricken because discovery may reveal contractual provisions, agency relationships, indemnity obligations, or statutory or equitable bases supporting the recovery of attorney’s fees, and Plaintiffs are not required to prove entitlement to attorney’s fees with finality at the pleadings stage. (Opp., p. 4:21-24.)

 

Plaintiff’s argument that discovery might provide grounds for awarding attorneys’ fees is tantamount to an admission that the Complaint does not now support a plea for attorneys’ fees. Accordingly, the Motion to Strike is GRANTED as to attorneys’ fees.

 

As Plaintiffs have not presented the Court with any new allegations that they could plead on amendment, the Court ordinarily would be inclined to grant the Motion to Strike without leave to amend. However, as the Court is granting leave to amend as to the claims against the Court exercises its discretion to grant the Motion to Strike with leave. Plaintiffs must come forward with allegations that support, as a matter of law, their prayer for attorneys’ fees and emotional distress damages..

   II.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Motion to Quash Service  – A defendant who contends service of summons is defective may, on or before the last day of his or her time to plead or within any further time that the court may allow for good cause, may move to quash service of summons on the ground of lack of personal jurisdiction. (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a)(1).)

 

“[C]ompliance¿with the statutory procedures for service of process is essential to establish personal¿jurisdiction. [Citation.]” (Dill v. Berquist Construction Co.¿(1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1444.) “When a defendant challenges the court’s personal¿jurisdiction¿on the ground of improper service of process, ‘the burden is on the plaintiff to prove the existence of¿jurisdiction¿by proving the facts requisite to an effective service.’” (Summers v. McClanahan¿(2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 403, 413 (internal citations omitted).) “The filing of a proof of service creates a rebuttable presumption that the service was proper. However, the presumption arises only if the proof of service¿complies with¿the applicable statutory requirements.” (Floveyor¿Internat., Ltd. v. Superior Court¿(1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 789, 795.)¿¿

 

“‘On a motion to quash service of summons, the plaintiff bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that all jurisdictional criteria are met. [Citations.] The burden¿must be met by competent evidence in¿affidavits¿and authenticated documents; an unverified complaint may not be considered as supplying the necessary facts.’ [Citation.]” (Brown v. Garcia¿(2017) 17 Cal.App.5th 1198, 1203; see also¿Sheard v. Superior Court¿(1974) 40 Cal.App.3d 207, 211 [“[W]here a defendant properly moves to quash service of summons the burden is on the plaintiff to prove facts requisite to the effective service.”].)¿

 

Some California courts have held that “[a] motion to quash service is the only method by which the defendant can test whether the complaint states a cause of action for unlawful detainer.” (Delta Imps. v. Municipal Court (1983) 146 Cal.App.3d 1033, 1036.) However, others have disagreed, holding that a “tenant may not challenge the allegedly defective service of the three-day notice via a motion to quash service of summons because the three-day notice is an element of an unlawful detainer action.” (Borsuk v. Appellate Division of Superior Court (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 607, 610.)

 III.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendants move for an order quashing the service of summons pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 418.10 and 1167.4 on the grounds that service was not properly effectuated. (Motion, p. 1:18-20, 2:3-6.)

 

Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s complaint fails to state a cause of action for unlawful detainer, as Civil Code section 1942.6(b)(2)(A)(1) lists family or owner move-in as a just cause reason, but only applies for leases entered into on or after July 1, 2020, or July 1, 2022 if the lease is for a tenancy in a mobile home, if the tenant agrees in writing to the termination, or if a provision of the lease allows the owner to terminate the lease if the owner, their spouse, domestic partner, children, grandchildren, parents, or grandparents, unilaterally decides to occupy the residential real property. (Motion, p. 3:7-12.) Defendant Marenco declares there is no written agreement between Defendants and Plaintiff allowing for owner or family move-in. (Marenco Decl., p. 1:6.) Defendants also argue that the five day summons is only permitted for unlawful detainer actions pursuant to Delta, that Borsuk is bad law as it conflicts with Delta, and Plaintiff’s service of summons must be quashed and her action dismissed. (Motion, p. 2:12-26, 3:14-15.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues she met all statutory requirements to execute service on Defendants, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 414.10, and that a non-party adult, Rose Pliego, personally handed the summons and complaint to the Defendants. (Opp., p. 2:5-9.) Plaintiff also argues the Court already obtained personal jurisdiction over the Defendants when it entered default against Defendants. (Opp., p. 2:12-18.) Plaintiff further asserts that Civil Code section 1946.2, subdivision (b)(2)(A) does not apply, as the Property is not a mobile home, and that Defendants lack standing to file motions due to their default in this case. (Opp., p. 3:9-22.)

 

On reply, Defendants argue that Plaintiff improperly invoked the expedited five-day unlawful detainer procedures when the complaint fails to state a legal sufficient cause of action for unlawful detainer. (Reply, p. 1:25-27.) Defendants also argue that entry of default does not create personal jurisdiction, and Plaintiff misinterprets Civil Code section 1946.2. (Reply, p. 2:20-26, 3:4-13.)

 

California Code of Civil Procedure section 1166, subdivision (a) sets forth the pleading requirements of an unlawful detainer complaint. Section 1166(a) states, in relevant part: “The complaint shall: (1) be verified and include the typed or printed name of the person verifying the complaint. (2) Set forth the facts on which the plaintiff seeks to recover. (3) Describe the premises with reasonable certainty… (5) State specifically the method used to serve the defendant with the notice or notices of termination upon which the complaint is based. This requirement may be satisfied by using and completing all items relating to service of the notice or noticed in an appropriate Judicial Council form complaint, or by attaching a proof of service of the notice…of termination served on the defendant.”

 

Here, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s complaint states facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for unlawful detainer. Plaintiff’s complaint (1) is verified and includes the printed name of Plaintiff who is verifying the complaint, (2) sets forth the facts on which Plaintiff seeks to recover, being that Defendants are in possession of her Property, received notice to vacate the premises for non-fault just cause, and that Plaintiff intended to move in and occupy the Property for a period of at least 12 months, (3) describes the Property with reasonable certainty, and (4) states with certainty the method used to serve Defendants with notice of termination upon which the complaint is based and attached a copy of said notice and proof of service. Plaintiff also complied with the notice requirements of Civil Code section 1946.2. No more is needed at the pleading stage.

 

While Defendants are correct that Civil Code section 1946.2, subdivision (b)(2)(i) only applies to leases entered on or after July 1, 2020, if Defendants agreed with Plaintiff that the lease may be terminated if Plaintiff intends to occupy the Property or if the lease agreement provides termination on these grounds, Plaintiff is under no obligation to provide evidence to that effect in the complaint. Defendants may appropriately contest the validity of the just cause at a hearing on the matter before judgment is entered in this case. Finally, the Court notes that Plaintiff is mistaken that entry of default establishes personal jurisdiction or terminates Defendants’ rights to file any substantive motion with the Court. However, on review of the proofs of personal service of summons and the moving, opposition, and reply papers filed on this motion, the Court finds personal service was proper and that the Court has established personal jurisdiction over the Defendants through the valid service of process.

 

Therefore, Defendants’ motion to quash service of summons is DENIED. This denial is without prejudice to Defendants’ arguments as to the validity of just cause raised in the moving and reply papers. Should Defendants wish to appear in this action and file a responsive document, they may move the Court to set aside the entry of default.

 IV.          Conclusion

 

Defendants Jocelyn Cebreros and Cristian Marenco’s Motion to Quash Service of Summons is DENIED.
