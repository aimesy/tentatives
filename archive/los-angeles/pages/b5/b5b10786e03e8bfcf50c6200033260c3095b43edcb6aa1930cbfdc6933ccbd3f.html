BACKGROUND

Plaintiff, Guisheng Xu, filed the instant action on 8/19/2024 against Defendants – (1) Jiaqi Han, an individual, (2) Fuzhou Liu, an individual, (3) Yinming Liu, an individual, (4) First Stop Auto LLC, a California limited liability company, and (5) Does 1 to 10, inclusive.

 

Entry of default was entered against all of the non-Doe Defendants on 10/11/2024.

 

Does 1 to 10 were dismissed from the Complaint on 10/29/2024.

 

On 02/04/2025, default judgment was entered against (1) Jiaqi Han, an individual, (2) Fuzhou Liu, an individual, (3) Yinming Liu, an individual, and (4) First Stop Auto LLC, a California limited liability company.

 

For purposes of this hearing, the Court considers Defendant, Yinming Liu’s, amended notice/motion filed on 2/24/2025 and not the earlier filed notice/motion that was filed on 2/10/2025.

 

ANALYSIS
Preliminary Matter

In the instant action, Plaintiff filed four proofs of substituted service on 8/29/2024 for all of the named Defendants in this action.

 

Moving Defendant, Yinming Liu, moves for an order to vacate and set aside the default and default judgments entered against him on the basis that he was not properly served.

 

Movant appears to move pursuant to CCP § 473(d), and in the alternative, § 473.5.

 

The Court notes that neither party’s papers is a model of clarity. For example, neither party makes clear why § 473(d) or § 473.5 is or is not the appropriate statute to move under to vacate the default/default judgment. Generally speaking, Defendant argues that service upon him was improper, and Plaintiff argues that service upon Defendant was proper. To the Court, this motion appears to be akin to a motion to quash service of summons. Either way, since Defendant’s motion does not make clear why § 473(d) or § 473.5 is the appropriate statute, and since Plaintiff did not oppose Defendant’s motion on the grounds that those two statutes are not the proper statutes, the Court will assume Defendant moved under the proper statute.

 

Substantive

On 8/29/2024, Plaintiff filed a proof of service of summons that alleged substituted service on Defendant, Yinming Liu, on 8/27/2024.

Defendant, Yinming Liu, argues that service upon him was not proper.

 

Compliance with the statutory procedures for service of process is essential to establish personal jurisdiction. (American Express Centurion Bank v. Zara (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 383, 387.)

 

As to how Defendant believes that service upon him was improper is not entirely clear. Many times Defendant asserts arguments without citing legal authority. Or at times, Defendant will make reference to legal authority, but in no clear manner does Defendant explain how his argument is tied to the legal authority that he cited.

 

For example, Defendant first appears to argue that service upon him was not valid because Plaintiff did not personally serve him.

 

The argument that service was improper because Defendant was not personally served appears to be unavailing for several reasons.

 

First, Plaintiff’s proof of service lists service upon Defendant via substituted service; it does not list service upon Defendant via personal service.

 

Second, it does not appear that Defendant has to be personally served, because substituted service is an alternative to personal service.

 

As explained in American Express Centurion Bank v. Zara:

 

The Code of Civil Procedure specifies the various methods by which service may be made upon defendants who are sued as individuals.

 

The method described as “personal service” means service that is accomplished “by personal delivery of a copy of the summons and of the complaint to the person to be served.” (§ 415.10.) If the complaint and summons were personally delivered to, i.e., handed to, defendant then he could be said to have been “personally served.”

 

A defendant may also be “personally” served by delivering a copy of the summons and complaint to an agent authorized to accept service on behalf of that defendant. (§ 416.90; see Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2011) ¶¶ 4:128 to 4:132, pp. 4–19 to 4–21; (rev. # 1, 2010) ¶ 4:184, p. 4–27 (rev. # 1, 2004) (hereafter Weil & Brown, Civil Procedure Before Trial).) An authorized agent might include, for example, an attorney who has been expressly authorized to accept service, or a sheriff or jailer having custody of a prisoner. (Weil & Brown, Civil Procedure Before Trial, supra, ¶ 4:128, p. 4–19, ¶¶ 4:130.2, 4:132, pp. 4–20 to 4–21.)

 

Another alternative available for serving individual defendants is what is commonly known as “substitute service.” Substitute service on an individual is accomplished by “leaving a copy of the summons and complaint **103 at the person's dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge of his or her office, place of business, or usual mailing address ..., at least 18 years of age, who shall be informed of the contents thereof, and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and of the complaint by first-class mail, postage prepaid to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left.” (§ 415.20, subd. (b).)

 

However, an individual may be served by substitute service only after a good faith effort at personal service has first been made: the burden is on the plaintiff to show that the summons and complaint “cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered” to the individual defendant. (§ 415.20, subd. (b); Evartt v. Superior Court (1979) 89 Cal.App.3d 795, 801, 152 Cal.Rptr. 836.) Two or three attempts to personally serve a defendant at a proper place ordinarily qualifies as “ ‘reasonable diligence.’ ” (Weil & Brown, Civil Procedure Before Trial, supra, ¶ 4:196, p. 4–30.)

 

(American Express Centurion Bank v. Zara (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 383, 389.)

 

Thus, Defendant’s argument that service was improper because he was not personally served is unavailing.

 

Substituted Service – Usual Place of Business

The proof of service pertaining to Defendant, Yinming Liu, states that Defendant was substitute served at “314 E Garvey Ave Monterey Park, CA 91755.”

 

Further, Plaintiff’s proof of service checks a box for substituted service via business to “a person at least 18 years of age apparently in charge at the office or usual place of business of the person to be served.” The proof of service also indicates that the documents were left with “Jane Doe – Angela, refused last name (Gender: F Age: 35 Height : 6’0” Weight: 120 Race: Asian American Hair: Black Other: Hazel Eyes) Manager.”

 

Under CCP § 415.20(b), substitute service is effectuated when:

 

If a copy of the summons and complaint cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to the person to be served, as specified in Section 416.60, 416.70, 416.80, or 416.90, a summons may be served by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the person’s dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge of his or her office, place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, at least 18 years of age, who shall be informed of the contents thereof, and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and of the complaint by first-class mail, postage prepaid to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left. Service of a summons in this manner is deemed complete on the 10th day after the mailing.

 

(CCP § 415.20(b).)

 

Defendant appears to be arguing that substitute service was not proper under § 415.20(b) because he was not served at his “usual place of business,” and because service was not left with “a person apparently in charge of his or her office, place of business, or usual mailing address.”

Attached to the motion, the declaration of Defendant Yinming Liu is attached.

 

In relevant part, Liu’s declaration states:

 

2. Plaintiff Guisheng Xu, states that service of a Summons and Complaint was affected [sic] by substitute service on me on August 27, 2024. Plaintiff claims that substitute service was made by serving on one Jane Doe – “Angela” -- who refused to provide her last name, who held a position as a “Manager.” I do not know this individual nor do I have any personal or business relationship with her.

 

3. The address of 314 Garvey Avenue Monterey Park, CA 91755 is a small building with several businesses at that location, and it is not my place of business. The building does not have any front desk person or onsite management office. The corporate defendant First Stop Auto LLC is a dormant entity with no revenue, employees, or operations. It does not have any manager, employees, or representatives at that address.

 

4. I first became aware of this lawsuit on January 2, 2025, upon receipt of a WeChat message from defendant Fuzhou Liu. A review of the lawsuit revealed that it concerns a dispute concerning an unpaid loan of $50,000 between two individuals, Plaintiff and Mr. Fuzhou Liu. It has nothing to do with me as an individual or the corporate defendant, First Stop Auto LLC.

 

5. On February 4, 2025, I appeared at the Court and sought to express my position to the Court. The Court advised me that it cannot give me any legal advice and the only advice to me was to retain a lawyer to file a motion for relief. Accordingly, I have retained the law firm of WHGC, PLC to file this Motion.

 

(Liu Decl. ¶¶ 2-5.)

 

To the extent that Defendant is arguing that substitute service was not proper under § 415.20(b) because he was not served at his “usual place of business,” Defendant’s declaration appears to address this in ¶ 3 of the Liu declaration wherein Liu states, “The address of 314 Garvey Avenue Monterey Park, CA 91755 is a small building with several businesses at that location, and it is not my place of business.” (Liu Decl. ¶ 3.)

 

In Opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendant Yinming Liu was in fact properly substitute served at his “usual place of business.” Plaintiff argues that Liu is making misrepresentations to the Court and that the 314 Garvey address is in fact Liu’s “usual place of business.”

 

To support this argument, Plaintiff attaches the declaration of Plaintiff’s attorney (Tom F.Y. King), along with Exhibits A-E cited in King’s declaration.

 

King states as follows:

 

2. 5. As a result of the two successively filed statement of information First Stop Auto LLC filed with California Secretary of State in January 2025, all previously filed statement of information were purged from the public record. Attached hereto are search results from Google based on the search terms "California Secretary of State purging prior statement of information." The search result shows a general result followed by links to specific webpage results. Attached hereto as Exhibit E is a page from the general search result and a specific search results from California Secretary of State website.

 

(King Decl. ¶¶ 2-5.)

 

However, even assuming the truth of King’s declaration and the attached exhibits, Plaintiff’s argument is not on point and appears to miss the mark.

 

For example, to the extent that the California Secretary of State lists Yinming Liu as manager, member, CEO, and agent for service of process at the 314 E Garvey address, that information is applicable to First Stop Auto LLC. That information says nothing about the Defendant as an individual that is filing the instant motion. The instant proof of service that Defendant is arguing is improper is the proof of service as to the individual, Yinming Liu. Yinming Liu is arguing that the 314 address is not his usual place of business. At best, the information attached from the California Secretary of State would maybe seem to indicate that Yinming Liu could accept service at the 314 E Garvey address for First Stop Auto LLC since he was listed as agent of service of process for First Stop Auto LLC. However, to reiterate, that information says nothing about where Yinming Liu, the individual’s usual place of business is. At best, it may show where First Stop Auto LLC’s usual place of business is. Ultimately, First Stop Auto LLC is not moving to vacate default/quash service of summons. Here, moving Defendant is Yinming Liu the individual.

 

At best, maybe the successive filings with the Secretary of State tests Liu’s credibility for changing the address of First Stop Auto LLC and changing the name of the agent for service of process. However, again, even assuming there were bad faith, the Court fails to see how Plaintiff’s arguments addresses the usual place of business of the moving Defendant, Yinming Liu, the individual.

 

Despite all this, in Defendant’s Reply Defendant confusingly argues that even if 314 E Garvey were Liu’s place of business, service was not proper because the crucial question is whether service was proper where Plaintiff served an unknown individual at a location that housed several other businesses where there is no indication that the individual ever informed Liu of service.

 

Therefore, the Court will hear argument from the parties about the “usual place of business” of Defendant.

 

Substituted Service – Person Apparently in Charge of his or her office, place of business

The proof of service pertaining to Defendant, Yinming Liu, states that Defendant was substitute served at “314 E Garvey Ave Monterey Park, CA 91755.”

 

Further, Plaintiff’s proof of service checks a box for substituted service via business to “a person at least 18 years of age apparently in charge at the office or usual place of business of the person to be served.” The proof of service also indicates that the documents were left with “Jane Doe – Angela, refused last name (Gender: F Age: 35 Height : 6’0” Weight: 120 Race: Asian American Hair: Black Other: Hazel Eyes) Manager.”

 

Under CCP § 415.20(b), substitute service is effectuated when:

 

If a copy of the summons and complaint cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to the person to be served, as specified in Section 416.60, 416.70, 416.80, or 416.90, a summons may be served by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the person’s dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge of his or her office, place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, at least 18 years of age, who shall be informed of the contents thereof, and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and of the complaint by first-class mail, postage prepaid to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left. Service of a summons in this manner is deemed complete on the 10th day after the mailing.

 

(CCP § 415.20(b).)

 

Defendant argues that the individual who was served on Defendant’s behalf is not an individual with whom Liu had any personal or business relationship.

 

Further, in relevant part, Liu’s declaration states:

Defendant’s argument – that the individual who was served on Defendant’s behalf is not an individual with whom Liu had any personal or business relationship – is confusing because it isn’t entirely clear what portion of § 415.20(b) Defendant is arguing that Plaintiff did not comply with.

 

Presumably, Defendant is trying to argue that “Jane Doe – Angela” was not “a person apparently in charge of his or her office, place of business,” and thus service was improper. Defendant’s declaration does not state that Jane Does/Angela was not “a person apparently in charge of his or her office or place of business” in any explicit terms. Instead, the Liu declaration states in relevant part, “I do not know this individual nor do I have any personal or business relationship with her.” (Liu Decl. ¶ 2.)

 

In Opposition, Plaintiff does not address Defendant’s argument regarding the individual that was allegedly served in any clear manner. Plaintiff’s Opposition seems to mainly contest that 314 E Garvey was in fact the usual place of business of Defendant. It seems like Plaintiff attempts to argue that Defendant is not credible based on the filing with the secretary of state because Defendant would know the people at the 314 E Garvey address because it is his address.

 

In Reply, Defendant argues that the key issue here is that Plaintiff served an unknown individual that housed several other businesses where there is no indication that the individual ever informed Liu of the service.

 

Here, the Court will hear argument. Defendant appears to be arguing, although not explicitly stated in Defendant’s motion or Defendant’s declaration, that “Jane Doe – Angela” was not a person apparently in charge of his or her office, or place of business. Opposition does not do much to contest this argument. Plaintiff’s Opposition seems to just be implying that Defendant would in fact know who Angela is because the 314 Garvey address is Defendant’s business. The Reply reiterates that Defendant has no idea who Angela is and that there is no evidence to suggest that she worked with or for Liu.

 

473.5

Under CCP § 473.5(a):

 

When service of a summons has not resulted in actual notice to a party in time to defend the action and a default or default judgment has been entered against him or her in the action, he or she may serve and file a notice of motion to set aside the default or default judgment and for leave to defend the action. The notice of motion shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of: (i) two years after entry of a default judgment against him or her; or (ii) 180 days after service on him or her of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.

 

(CCP § 473.5(a).)

 

Defendant argues that the Summons and Complaint did not result in actual notice to Liu for Liu to defend the action.

 

However, the Court notes that neither party’s arguments regarding § 473.5 adds any further substance to their arguments. Plaintiff’s arguments that Defendant did in fact have notice are based on the same arguments previously discussed, i.e., Plaintiff argues that Defendant is lying and in fact had notice because Defendant is/was CEO of First Stop Auto LLC which is located at 314 E Garvey.

 

Overall

 

Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. states:

 

It has been held that the filing of a proof of service creates a rebuttable presumption that the service was proper. (M. Lowenstein & Sons, Inc. v. Superior Court (1978) 80 Cal.App.3d 762, 770 [145 Cal.Rptr. 814], quoting from Judicial Council Rep., supra, com. to § 417.10, p. 56; but see Johnson & Johnson v. Superior Court (1985) 38 Cal.3d 243, 255, fn. 7 [211 Cal.Rptr. 517, 695 P.2d 1058], overruling Lowenstein on a related issue.) However, that presumption arises only if the proof of service complies with the statutory requirements regarding such proofs.

 

(Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1441-1442.)

 

Here, Defendant appears to have shown how Plaintiff’s proof of service as to moving Defendant did not comply with the statutory requirements for proof of substituted service. Defendant appears to have shown this by showing that the location served was not his usual place of business. Plaintiff’s argument in Opposition appeared to be unavailing because at best Plaintiff may have shown that the address served was First Stop Auto LLC’s usual place of business; however, First Stop Auto LLC is not seeking to vacate the default/default judgment. Here, moving Defendant is Yinming Liu. Plaintiff did not submit proof or evidence about Yinming Liu, the individual’s, usual place of business. Even setting aside the issue of “usual place of business” and focusing on whether or not a “person apparently in charge of his or her office or  place of business” was served, Defendant seems to argue that he had no idea who the person is that Plaintiff served. In Opposition, Plaintiff simply seems to argue that Defendant is lying and Defendant would know who was served because the location served was where Defendant’s business was located.

Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. states, “In the absence of a voluntary submission to the authority of the court, compliance with the statutes governing service of process is essential to establish that court’s personal jurisdiction over a defendant. When a defendant challenges that jurisdiction by bringing a motion to quash, the burden is on the plaintiff to prove the existence of jurisdiction by proving, inter alia, the facts requisite to an effective service.” (Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1439-1440.)

Tentatively, the Court plans to GRANT, Defendant Yinming Liu’s motion to vacate the default and default judgment entered against Yinming Liu. The Court notes that this order does not apply to the other Defendants, as no other Defendants moved to vacate default/judgment.

Generally speaking, Plaintiff alleges that he was a lawful invitee at the subject premises [a banquet hall owned and/or managed by Defendants] and that he fell and injured himself on a dangerous condition due to the premises being maintained in a dangerous condition/Defendants failing to inspect the premises. (Compl., ¶¶ 9-12.)

Defendants – (1) Palladio, Inc. and (2) Palladio Venue, LLC – move for summary judgment in their favor pursuant to CCP § 437c.

 

In the alternative, Defendants move for summary adjudication on the basis that there is no triable issue of material fact, and the undisputed evidence demonstrates that Plaintiff’s causes of action for negligence and premises liability fail because: (1) Defendants neither created nor were responsible for the alleged dangerous condition, (2) the alleged dangerous condition that caused Plaintiff’s fall was open and obvious, and (3) Defendants did not have notice of any dangerous condition.

LEGAL STANDARD – MOTION SUMMARY JUDGMENT


The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)  “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67, citing FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal. App. 3d 367, 381-382.)

 

As to each claim as framed by the complaint, the defendant moving for summary judgment must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate an essential element, or to establish a defense. (CCP, § 437c(p)(2); Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.) The plaintiff or cross-complainant shall not rely upon the allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists, but instead, shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto. (CCP, § 437c(p)(2).)

 

Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.”  (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)

 

“Once the defendant or cross-defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff or cross-complainant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto. The plaintiff or cross-complainant shall not rely upon the allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists but, instead, shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (CCP, § 437c(p)(2).)

 

To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence sufficient to establish a triable issue of material fact on the merits of the defendant’s showing. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 163.)

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

Failing to Meet Initial Burden – Problem 1

 

The parties’ papers submitted for this MSJ/MSA are not the model of clarity.

 

Defendants begin by first explaining that discovery has established that Plaintiff (Mazian; a wedding guest) was attending a wedding reception at Defendants’ banquet hall and that Plaintiff slipped and fell on a dollar bill that was on the ground from other wedding guests participating in the “money dance” – a tradition wherein during the bride and groom’s first dance, wedding guests throw money into the air over the bride and groom, which causes the money to fall onto the dance floor.

 

By and large, Defendants’ moving and reply papers are borderline incomprehensible.

 

For example, Defendants’ reply argues that Defendants met their initial burden by presenting evidence that “negates multiple essential elements of Plaintiff’s claims[.]” (Def. Reply, p. 3, lns. 11-12.)

 

Here, the Court has no idea how Defendants met their initial burden in negating multiple essential elements of Plaintiff’s claims because the Court has no idea which elements Defendants’ arguments are negating in Plaintiff’s claims.

 

The elements of a cause of action for negligence are: (1) the defendant had a duty to use due care, (2) that he [or she] breached that duty, and (3) that the breach was the proximate or legal cause of the resulting injury. (Vasquez v. Residential Investments, Inc. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 269, 278 citing Nally v. Grace Community Church (1988) 47 Cal.3d 278, 292-293.)

 

The elements of a cause of action for premises liability are the same as those for negligence. (Jones v. Awad (2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 1200, 1207 citing Castellon v. U.S. Bancorp (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 994, 998.) Accordingly, the plaintiff must prove a legal duty to use due care, a breach of such legal duty, and the breach as the proximate or legal cause of the resulting injury. (Jones v. Awad, supra, 39 Cal.App.5th at 1207 citing Beacon Residential Community Assn. v. Skidmore, Owings & Merrill LLP (2014) 59 Cal.4th 568, 573.)

 

The defendant bears the initial burden of showing that the plaintiff cannot establish one or more elements of the cause of action, or that there is an affirmative defense to it. (Jacobs v. Coldwell Banker Residential Brokerage Co. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 438, 443 citing CCP § 437c(o) & Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.)

 

Defendants here move for summary judgment/adjudication based on three arguments.

 

Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s claims for negligence/premises liability fail under the three arguments that:

 

(1) Defendants neither created nor were responsible for the alleged dangerous condition [Argument One];

 

(2) The alleged dangerous condition that caused Plaintiff’s fall was open and obvious [Argument Two]; and

 

(3) Defendants did not have notice of any dangerous condition [Argument Three].

 

With respect to Defendants’ Argument One – Plaintiff’s claims fail because Defendants neither created nor were responsible for the alleged dangerous condition – it is not entirely clear what element, or elements [duty, breach, causation, damages] of a negligence/premises liability claim the Defendants are allegedly negating.

 

In Defendants’ moving papers, with respect to Argument One, Defendants argue that Plaintiff cannot establish duty or breach as a matter of law. (Def. Memo., p. 6, lns. 1-3.)

 

Confusingly, in Defendants’ reply, with respect to Argument One, Defendants only argue that Plaintiff cannot establish breach as a matter of law. (Def. Reply, p. 7, lns. 2-4.)

 

It is unclear if Defendants’ Argument One is allegedly negating the elements of duty and breach [as the moving papers indicate] or just the element of breach [as the reply papers indicate].

 

With respect to Defendants’ Argument Two – the alleged dangerous condition that caused Plaintiff’s fall was open and obvious – it appears that Defendants are attempting to negate the element of duty in a negligence/premises liability cause of action. (Def. Memo., p. 7, lns. 2-4.)

 

With respect to Defendants’ Argument Three – Defendants did not have notice of any dangerous condition – neither Defendants’ moving papers nor Defendants’ reply attempts to explain what element [duty, breach, causation, damages] Defendants are attempting to negate. Defendants’ moving papers argue that “Plaintiff cannot meet his burden of establishing notice, an essential element of his premises liability claim,” (Def. Memo., p. 9, lns. 20-21) but Defendants do not cite to any legal authority supporting the position that notice is an essential element of a claim for premises liability. To the Court’s understanding, duty, breach, causation, and damages are the elements to a claim for premises liability.

 

At the hearing, the Court expects to hear from Defendants as to which element, or elements, their three arguments [Argument One, Argument Two, and Argument Three] allegedly negate.

 

Argument One Revisited

 

With respect to Defendants’ Argument One – Plaintiff’s claims fail because Defendants neither created nor were responsible for the alleged dangerous condition – it is not entirely clear what element, or elements [duty, breach, causation, damages] of a negligence/premises liability claim the Defendants are allegedly negating.

 

To the extent that Defendants are arguing that Argument One – Plaintiff’s claims fail because Defendants neither created nor were responsible for the alleged dangerous condition – demonstrates that Plaintiff cannot establish duty as a matter of law, the Court does not find Defendants’ argument availing.

 

The threshold element of a cause of action for negligence is the existence of a duty to use due care toward an interest of another that enjoys legal protection against unintentional invasion. (Laabs v. Southern California Edison Co. (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1260, 1271 citing Bily v. Arthur Young & Co. (1992) 3 Cal.4th 370, 397.)

 

As a general rule, each person has a duty to use ordinary care and is liable for injuries caused by his failure to exercise reasonable care in the circumstances. (Laabs v. Southern California Edison Co. (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1260, 1271 citing Rowland v. Christian (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108, 112.)

 

Whether a given case falls within an exception to the general rule, or whether a duty of care exists in a given circumstance is a question of law to be determined by the court on a case-by-case basis. (Laabs v. Southern California Edison Co. (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1260, 1272.)

 

Whether a duty should be imposed on a defendant depends on a variety of policy considerations known as the Rowland factors. (Jacobs v. Coldwell Banker Residential Brokerage Co. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 438, 446 citing Rowland v. Christian (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108, 112-113.)

 

This determination involves the balancing of these [Rowland] factors, including:

(1) The foreseeability of harm to the plaintiff;

(2) the degree of certainty that the plaintiff suffered injury;

(3) the closeness of the connection between the defendant’s conduct and the injury suffered;

(4) the moral blame attached to the defendant’s conduct;

(5) the policy of preventing future harm;

(6) the extent of the burden to the defendant and consequences to the community of imposing a duty to exercise care with resulting liability for breach; and

(7) the availability, cost, and prevalence of insurance for the risk involved.

 

(Laabs v. Southern California Edison Co. (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1260, 1272 citing Ann M. v. Pacific Plaza Shopping Center (1993) 6 Cal.4th 666, 675, fn.5.)

 

Defendants state the following:

 

Here, the undisputed evidence establishes that Defendants neither created nor were responsible for the alleged dangerous condition that caused Plaintiff’s injuries. Specifically, the evidence shows that at approximately 8:00 p.m., during the bride and groom’s first dance, wedding guests participated in a traditional “money dance” by throwing money into the air over the bride and groom, causing the money to fall onto the dance floor. [UMF 2.] Neither Defendants nor any employee, agent, or representative of Defendants participated in throwing money onto the dance floor during the money dance. [UMF 3.]

 

Further, the undisputed evidence establishes that the wedding party, not Defendants, controlled the handling of the money thrown during the money dance. Mr. Djiguerian, the groom, did not assign anyone to collect money from the dance floor and does not know who, if anyone, collected the money following the money dance. [UMF 8.] Further, it did not occur to Mr. Djiguerian to raise any concern with Defendants regarding money remaining on the dance floor during the reception. [UMF 10.] Additionally, no employee, agent, or representative of Defendants ever stated that Defendants would be responsible for cleaning the dance floor during the wedding reception. [UMF 11.] Nor did Mr. Djiguerian have any discussions with Defendants, either during or after the money dance, regarding the logistics of removing money from the dance floor. [UMF 12.]

 

(Def. Memo., p. 5.)

 

After Defendants cite to their UMFs 2, 3, 8, 10, 11, and 12, referenced above, Defendants arrive at the conclusion that because they neither created nor were responsible for the condition that allegedly caused Plaintiff’s fall, Plaintiff cannot establish duty or breach as a matter of law.

 

The Court does not find Defendants’ arguments availing.

 

First, as to Defendants’ Argument One – Plaintiff’s claims fail because Defendants neither created nor were responsible for the alleged dangerous condition – Defendants provide zero explanation or legal argumentation as to which Rowland factor – (1) The foreseeability of harm to the plaintiff; (2) the degree of certainty that the plaintiff suffered injury; (3) the closeness of the connection between the defendant’s conduct and the injury suffered; (4) the moral blame attached to the defendant’s conduct; (5) the policy of preventing future harm; (6) the extent of the burden to the defendant and consequences to the community of imposing a duty to exercise care with resulting liability for breach; and (7) the availability, cost, and prevalence of insurance for the risk involved – Argument One applies to.

 

Second, generally speaking, Defendants don’t even attempt to explain how the UMFs [2, 3, 8, 10, 11, and 12] that they cite demonstrate that Plaintiff cannot establish duty as a matter of law.

 

In moving for summary judgment, it is Defendants’ burden to establish that the duty to exercise reasonable care did not apply to Plaintiff as a matter of law. (Laabs v. Southern California Edison Co. (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1260, 1279, fn. 10.)

 

Here, Defendants make no attempt to address any of the Rowland factors nor do they make any attempt to explain how the alleged UMFs that they cite demonstrate that Plaintiff cannot establish duty as a matter of law. Defendants simply lumped several alleged UMFs [2, 3, 8, 10, 11, and 12] together and then arrived at a legal conclusion [i.e., there is no duty] without providing legal authority/legal reasoning to explain how their conclusion is supported by the law.

 

To the extent that Defendants are also arguing that Argument One – Plaintiff’s claims fail because Defendants neither created nor were responsible for the alleged dangerous condition – demonstrates that Plaintiff cannot establish breach as a matter of law, Defendants also entirely fail to provide legal authority or explanation as to how their conclusion [i.e., Plaintiff cannot establish breach as a matter of law] is supported by the law.

 

Overall, the Court does not even understand what Defendants’ argument is with respect to the issue of breach.

 

Further, to the extent that Defendants are arguing that Plaintiff cannot establish breach, wouldn’t whether or not there is a breach assume the existence of a duty in order to evaluate if there was a breach of a duty? How does Defendants’ argument on breach not undercut its other argument that there is no duty as a matter of law?

 

Further, the elements of breach of duty and causation are ordinarily questions of fact for the jury’s determination. (Vasquez v. Residential Investments, Inc. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 269, 278 citing Andrews v. Wells (1988) 204 Cal.App.3d 533, 538.)

 

The Court finds Defendants’ Argument One unavailing because Defendants did not meet their initial burden.

 

Argument Two Revisited

 

With respect to Defendants’ Argument Two – the alleged dangerous condition that caused Plaintiff’s fall was open and obvious – it appears that Defendants are attempting to demonstrate that Plaintiff cannot establish duty as a matter of law.

 

Here, the Court is inclined to find that Defendants do not meet their burden in establishing that Plaintiff cannot establish duty as a matter of law; however, the Court will hear argument.

 

Defendants argue as follows:

 

Here, the undisputed evidence establishes that the alleged condition was open and obvious. Plaintiff observed money on the dance floor following the money dance. [UMF 4.] Plaintiff also did not observe anyone removing money from the dance floor following the money dance. [UMF 5.] Further, the dance floor was well-lit at the time of Plaintiff’s alleged fall. [UMF No. 13.] Despite observing money on the dance floor, Plaintiff voluntarily chose to continue dancing in the area.

 

Under these undisputed facts, any potential danger associated with dancing on a floor containing visible dollar bills was open and obvious as a matter of law. Plaintiff’s decision to continue dancing despite observing the condition was entirely voluntary and not compelled by any practical necessity. Because the condition itself served as a warning, Defendants owed no additional duty to warn Plaintiff of, or remedy, the alleged condition. Accordingly, Defendants are entitled to summary judgment under the open and obvious doctrine.

 

(Def. Memo., p. 6-7.)

 

The first problem with Defendants’ Argument Two – the alleged dangerous condition that caused Plaintiff’s fall was open and obvious –  is that Defendants only address the first Rowland factor (foreseeability of harm to plaintiff). Defendants don’t even attempt to address the remaining six Rowland factors.

 

Foreseeability of harm is typically absent when a dangerous condition is open and obvious. (Jacobs v. Coldwell Banker Residential Brokerage Co. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 438, 447 citing Osborn v. Mission Ready Mix (1990) 224 Cal.App.3d 104, 122.)

 

In that situation, owners and possessors of land are entitled to assume others will perceive the obvious and take action to avoid the dangerous condition. (Jacobs v. Coldwell Banker Residential Brokerage Co. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 438, 447 citing Haberlin v. Peninsula Celebration Assn. (1957) 156 Cal.App.2d 404, 408.)

 

Therefore, since Defendants don’t even attempt to address the remaining Rowland factors, Defendants do not meet their initial burden in demonstrating that Plaintiff cannot establish duty as a matter of law.

 

In moving for summary judgment, it is Defendants’ burden to establish that the duty to exercise reasonable care did not apply to Plaintiff as a matter of law. (Laabs v. Southern California Edison Co. (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1260, 1279, fn. 10.)

 

Whether a given case falls within an exception to the general rule, or whether a duty of care exists in a given circumstance is a question of law to be determined by the court on a case-by-case basis. (Laabs v. Southern California Edison Co. (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1260, 1272.)

 

Whether a duty should be imposed on a defendant depends on a variety of policy considerations known as the Rowland factors. (Jacobs v. Coldwell Banker Residential Brokerage Co. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 438, 446 citing Rowland v. Christian (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108, 112-113.)

 

Here, Defendants’ only addressed the first Rowland factor – foreseeability of harm. Defendants did not even attempt to address the remaining six Rowland factors.

 

The second problem with Defendants’ Argument Two – the alleged dangerous condition that caused Plaintiff’s fall was open and obvious – is that the evidence [Defendants’ UMFs 4-5] does not establish that the alleged condition was open and obvious.

 

With respect to Defendants citing to UMFs 4-5 to argue that the alleged dangerous condition that caused Plaintiff’s fall was open and obvious, Defendants misrepresent the evidence that UMFs 4 and 5 are allegedly supported by.

 

With respect to UMF 4, Defendants state, “Plaintiff observed money on the dance floor following the money dance.” (Def. UMF 4; Ex. 2 at p. 18:18-19.)

 

The Court notes Defendants’ Exhibit 2 is Plaintiff’s deposition testimony.

 

With respect to the alleged evidence that supports UMF 4, the deposition testimony states:

 

Q. Okay. So, take me to the time that the incident happened. I think you were mentioning before you're talking, dancing, sounds like maybe sitting at the table again, dancing again. And then, at what point does the incident happen and if you can give me the lead up to it?

 

A. Yes. It occurred around 10:00 p.m. There was music, several people dancing, um, on the dance floor. Um, yeah, there was -- there were dollar bills spread throughout the floor. And while dancing, I slipped on one of those dollar bills and my legs kind of did a criss cross and I landed on my backside.

 

(Def. Ex. 2, p. 18: 10-21.)

 

With respect to the deposition testimony/evidence that Defendants cite to support UMF 4, the evidence does not support Defendants’ argument that the alleged dangerous condition was open and obvious.

 

First, no where in the evidence supporting UMF 4 [Def. Ex. 2, p. 18: 10-21] does Plaintiff explicitly state or opine on whether or not the dollar bills [dangerous condition] were open and obvious.

 

Second, Defendants seem to be imputing/subjectively interpreting/implying their own meaning from Plaintiff’s response, or lack thereof.

 

As a preliminary matter, Defendants’ counsel’s Question and Plaintiff’s Answer are not the model of clarity. It’s difficult to decipher the Question/Answer on their face.

 

Additionally, Plaintiff’s Answer does not say anything explicitly as to the openness or obviousness of the dollar bills that he slipped on.

 

Plaintiff’s Answer is entirely unclear as to if Plaintiff saw the dollar bills that he slipped on before slipping on them, or if he saw them after he slipped on them. Plaintiff’s Answer says nothing about when he saw the dollar bills that he slipped on.

 

Further, is Plaintiff’s Answer generally explaining what happened, i.e., that what he slipped on were dollar bills on the floor, or is Plaintiff stating that he saw the dollar bills that he slipped on immediately before slipping on them?

 

Plaintiff’s Answer is less than clear.

 

With respect to UMF 5, Defendants state, “Plaintiff did not observe anyone picking up money from the dance floor following the money dance.” (Def. UMF 5; Ex. 2 at p. 21: 8-11.)

 

With respect to the alleged evidence that supports UMF 5, the deposition testimony states:

 

Q. At any point before your incident, so the two hours when the money throwing started, did you see anyone picking up any of the money off of the ground?

 

A. I personally did not see it.

 

(Def. UMF 5; Ex. 2 at p. 21: 8-11.)

 

With respect to the deposition testimony/evidence that Defendants cite to support UMF 5, the evidence does not support Defendants’ argument that the alleged dangerous condition was open and obvious.

 

First, nowhere in the evidence supporting UMF 5 [Def. Ex. 2 at p. 21: 8-11] does Plaintiff explicitly state or opine on whether or not the dollar bills [dangerous condition] were open and obvious.

 

Second, Defendants seem to be imputing/subjectively interpreting/implying their own meaning from Plaintiff’s response, or lack thereof.

 

As a preliminary matter, Plaintiff admits that about two hours prior to the slip and fall, guests started throwing money at the bride and groom, and that most recently, about an hour before his fall, money was also thrown. (See Def. Ex. 2, p. 19: 12-19.)

 

Defendants seem to assume that since Plaintiff did not personally see anyone picking up any of the money off the ground, the money was therefore open and obvious for Plaintiff to see.

 

Problematic with Defendants’ conclusion that the money was open and obvious for Plaintiff to see is that Defendants’ conclusion does not necessarily follow from Plaintiff’s Answer.

 

Plaintiff stated that he did not personally see anyone picking up any of the money off of the ground.

 

Plaintiff does not state/opine on whether the money on the floor was open and obvious.

 

It appears that Defendants are making assumptions about Plaintiff’s state of mind/belief of what was on the floor at the time he slipped; however, Plaintiff’s Answer provides no such insight.

 

Defendants seem to be assuming that since Plaintiff knew money was on the ground about an hour before, and since Plaintiff did not personally see anyone picking up money off the ground, Plaintiff thus knew the money must have still been on the ground. Therefore, Defendants seem to assume the money was open and obvious to Plaintiff.

 

Problematic with Defendants’ apparent assumption is that Plaintiff’s Answer only states that he did not personally see anyone pick up the money off the ground.

 

Is it not possible that even though Plaintiff did not personally see anyone pick up the money off the ground, Plaintiff personally thought/assumed someone picked up the money off the ground since about an hour passed from when money was thrown to when he ended up falling?

 

Or to phrase it differently, would it not be reasonable for Plaintiff to assume that even though he saw that money was thrown, and even though he did not personally see anyone pick it up, Plaintiff assumed that a reasonable venue owner would have picked up the money, or that Plaintiff assumed that wedding guests [or the bride or groom] would have picked it up?

 

Ultimately, Plaintiff’s response that he did not personally see anyone pick up the money does not opine on whether or not the money that Plaintiff slipped on was open and obvious. Further, Plaintiff’s response is silent as to what Plaintiff did or did not see immediately before he slipped.

 

Therefore, with respect to UMFs 4-5, it does not appear that Defendants met their burden in establishing that the dangerous condition was open and obvious.

 

Further supporting the Court’s view that UMFs 4-5 do not demonstrate that Defendants met their burden in establishing that the dangerous condition was open and obvious is the case of Beauchamp v. Los Gatos Golf Course (1969) 273 Cal.App.2d 20, 77.

 

As Jacobs describes Beauchamp:

 

The plaintiff in that case slipped and fell while walking in spiked golf shoes across the defendant's veranda, a rough, troweled concrete surface, even though she knew that her footing would not be as stable as it would have been on grass. (Id. at pp. 23-24, 77 Cal.Rptr. 914.) The route taken by plaintiff was not the only entrance to the golf club and there were “no imperfections or defects in the concrete walk surface.” (Id. at p. 24, 77 Cal.Rptr. 914.) Even though the plaintiff knew that golf shoes reduce one's traction, the court found that “in view of the [club's] invitation to use the walkway, she was entitled to consider it reasonably safe” and that “the question of her appreciation of the risk, or her imputed knowledge of it, is not so overwhelming as to properly permit a nonsuit.” (Id. at p. 34, 77 Cal.Rptr. 914.)

 

(Jacobs v. Coldwell Banker Residential Brokerage Co. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 438, 448 explaining Beauchamp v. Los Gatos Golf Course (1969) 273 Cal.App.2d 20, 77.)

 

The Court will hear argument with respect to Argument Two because Defendants also cite to UMF 13 for the proposition that the dangerous condition was open and obvious.

 

Defendants’ UMF 13 states, “The dance floor was well lit at the time of Plaintiff’s fall.”

 

To support UMF 13, Defendants cite to Defendants’ Exhibit 3, p. 86: 13-20, and Exhibit 4.

 

Exhibit 3 is deposition testimony of Aram Djiguerian [groom/cross-defendant/cross-complainant].

 

With respect to the deposition testimony that UMF 13 cites to:

 

Q. And can you describe the lighting of the venue that you see in this video?

 

A. Oh, it’s light - - lighting elements on the stage. It’s purple.

 

Q. But would you say that it’s – the venue is so dark that you’re unable to see the dollar bills on the ground?

 

A. I wouldn’t say that it’s dark.

 

(Def. Ex. 3, p. 86: 13-20.)

 

With respect to UMF 13, Defendants also appear to interpret Aram’s (the groom) response in the manner most beneficial to Defendants; however, Aram’s response does not demonstrate that the dangerous condition was open and obvious.

 

Aram’s response is vague.

 

Most importantly, the Court will hear argument about Argument Two because Exhibit 4 is allegedly the video of the subject incident that was played for Aram.

 

Problematic here is that the papers before the Court say that Exhibit 4 is to be lodged with the Court. At the hearing, the parties and the Court will discuss if the video for Exhibit 4 was lodged with the Court.

 

Defendants should be prepared to attend the hearing with a readily viewable video that can be displayed to the Court so the Court can determine whether or not the dangerous condition was open and obvious.

 

While the Court has its doubts as to the video in Exhibit 4 demonstrating that the dangerous condition was open and obvious, the Court reiterates that even if Defendants demonstrate that the dangerous condition was open and obvious, it could still be all for naught.

 

Defendants’ Argument Two – the alleged dangerous condition that caused Plaintiff’s fall was open and obvious – attempts to demonstrate that Plaintiff cannot establish duty as a matter of law.

 

Problematic with Defendants’ open and obvious argument is that it only addresses the foreseeability factor of the Rowland factors.

 

Defendants are the ones with the burden to demonstrate the absence of a duty of care to Plaintiff as a matter of law. (Laabs v. Southern California Edison Co. (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1260, 1279, fn. 10.)

 

The Court to hear argument on Argument Two.

 

Argument Three Revisited

 

With respect to Defendants’ Argument Three – Defendants did not have notice of any dangerous condition – neither Defendants’ moving papers nor Defendants’ reply attempts to explain what element [duty, breach, causation, damages] Defendants are attempting to negate.

 

Defendants’ moving papers argue that “Plaintiff cannot meet his burden of establishing notice, an essential element of his premises liability claim,” (Def. Memo., p. 9, lns. 20-21) but Defendants do not cite to any legal authority supporting the position that notice is an essential element of a claim for premises liability. To the Court’s understanding, duty, breach, causation, and damages are the elements of a claim for premises liability.

 

The Court is inclined to find that Defendants’ Argument Three is unavailing for the reasons explained below; however, the Court will hear argument.

 

First, the Court expects Defendants to address what element [duty, breach, causation, damages] Defendants are arguing the Plaintiff cannot establish with respect to Argument Three.

 

Second, the Court expects Defendants to provide legal authority that states that “notice is an essential element of a claim for premises liability.” (Def. Memo., p. 9, lns. 20-21.)

 

Third, although Defendants’ moving papers argue that Plaintiff cannot meet his burden in establishing notice (Def. Memo., p. 9, lns. 20-23), the Court notes that the initial burden is not on Plaintiff.

 

Defendants are the moving party; Defendants have the initial burden.

 

The defendant bears the initial burden of showing that the plaintiff cannot establish one or more elements of the cause of action, or that there is an affirmative defense to it. (Jacobs v. Coldwell Banker Residential Brokerage Co. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 438, 443 citing CCP § 437c(o) & Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.)

 

Fourth, Defendants’ argument appears to fail under their own theory.

 

Defendants cite to Morris v. Associated Securities, Inc. (1965) 232 Cal.App.2d 220, 230 for the proposition that there can be no duty to warn unless the possessor of land has knowledge, actual or constructive, of the dangerous condition. (Def. Memo., p. 7, lns. 22-23.)

 

Defendants argue:

 

Here, the undisputed evidence establishes that at approximately 8:00 p.m., wedding guests participated in a traditional “money dance” by throwing money into the air over the bride and groom, causing the money to fall onto the dance floor. [UMF 2.] Neither Defendants nor any employee, agent, or representative of Defendants participated in throwing money onto the dance floor. [UMF 3.] The bride and groom received the money thrown during the money dance, although the groom, Mr. Djiguerian did not assign anyone to collect the money and does not know who, if anyone, collected it following the money dance. [UMF 7, 8.] Mr. Djiguerian also did not observe anyone collecting money from the dance floor after the money dance. [UMF 9.]

 

Further, it did not occur to Mr. Djiguerian to raise any concern with Defendants regarding money remaining on the dance floor during the reception. [UMF 10.] No employee, agent, or representative of Defendants ever stated that Defendants would be responsible for cleaning the dance floor during the reception. [UMF 11.] Nor were there any discussions with Defendants regarding the logistics of cleaning money from the dance floor, either during or after the money dance. [UMF 12.]

 

Plaintiff cannot establish that Defendants had actual or constructive notice of any allegedly dangerous condition. There is no evidence that Defendants created the condition, knew of the condition, or were informed that money remained on the dance floor. Nor is there evidence establishing how long any particular dollar bill was present before Plaintiff’s alleged fall or that Defendants had sufficient time and opportunity to discover and remedy the condition.

 

(Def. Memo., p. 9.)

 

Problematic with Defendants’ argument – that they did not have notice of the dangerous condition – is the case of Ortega v. Kmart Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200.

 

In relevant part of Ortega:

 

The plaintiff need not show actual knowledge where evidence suggests that the dangerous condition was present for a sufficient period of time to charge the owner with constructive knowledge of its existence. Knowledge may be shown by circumstantial evidence “which is nothing more than one or more inferences which may be said to arise reasonably from a series of *1207 proven facts.” (Hatfield, supra, 18 Cal.2d at p. 805.) Whether a dangerous condition has existed long enough for a reasonably prudent person to have discovered it is a question of fact for the jury, and the cases do not impose exact time limitations. Each accident must be viewed in light of its own unique circumstances. (Louie, supra, 81 Cal.App.2d at p. 608.) The owner must inspect the premises or take other proper action to ascertain their condition, and if, by the exercise of reasonable care, the owner would have discovered the condition, he is liable for failing to correct it. (Id. at p. 606.)

 

(Ortega v. Kmart Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1206-1207.)

 

Here, Defendants make it entirely unclear what element [duty, breach, causation, damages] of a negligence/premises liability claim they are attempting to negate with their argument that Defendants did not have notice of the dangerous condition.

 

In any event, at the very least, there is dispute of material fact as to this issue.

 

Defendants’ own evidence demonstrates a dispute of material fact as to whether or not Defendants had constructive notice.

 

Defendants’ Exhibit 2 is the deposition testimony of Plaintiff. Plaintiff explains that money was  on the ground about two hours prior to his fall and probably up to an hour prior to his fall. (See Def. Ex. 2, p. 19: 12-23.)

 

Plaintiff’s deposition testimony, Def. Ex. 2, p. 19: 12-23, demonstrates a dispute of material fact as to whether or not Defendants had constructive knowledge of the dangerous condition.

 

As explained in Ortega:

 

Knowledge may be shown by circumstantial evidence “which is nothing more than one or more inferences which may be said to arise reasonably from a series of *1207 proven facts.” (Hatfield, supra, 18 Cal.2d at p. 805.) Whether a dangerous condition has existed long enough for a reasonably prudent person to have discovered it is a question of fact for the jury, and the cases do not impose exact time limitations. Each accident must be viewed in light of its own unique circumstances. (Louie, supra, 81 Cal.App.2d at p. 608.)

 

(Ortega v. Kmart Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1206-1207.)

 

Therefore, the Court will likely find Defendants’ Argument Three to be unavailing.

 

OVERALL TENTATIVE RULING

 

Defendants’ MSJ/MSA is likely to be DENIED in its entirety.

 

The Court expects to hear from the parties on the issues addressed above.

 

The Court expects Defendants to be prepared with a readily viewable copy of the video in Exhibit 4.

 

With respect to Defendants’ Argument One and Argument Two, the Court is likely to find that Defendants did not meet their initial burden.

 

With respect to Defendants’ Argument Three, although it’s entirely unclear what element Defendants are attempting to negate, Plaintiff demonstrates a dispute of material fact simply by the existence of the evidence that Defendants submitted themselves. (See Def. Ex. 2, p. 19: 12-23.)
