TENTATIVE RULING

August 26, 2026

21STCV37594 (Vaserfirer, et al. v. Uber Technologies, Inc.

           

RELEVANT BACKGROUND

             

            Plaintiffs Anna Vasserfirer and Arkady Vasserfirer were crossing the intersection at Fairfax when Defendant Oscar Moquete hit them with his vehicle.  Defendant Moquete was driving a rental vehicle for Uber at the time of the accident.  Plaintiffs filed this action against Defendants Uber Technologies, Inc. (“Uber”) and its subsidiaries, Raiser LLC and Raiser-CA LLC (collectively “Raiser”).  Plaintiffs also named Defendant Checkr, Inc., a consumer agency that provides background checks for third parties. 

 

            The operative complaint is the Third Amended Complaint filed on February 14, 2025 alleging:  (1) motor vehicle negligence (Uber and Moquete); (2) general negligence (Uber and Moquete); (3) general negligence (Checkr).

 

            On March 16, 2026, the Court sustained Checkr’s demurrer to the Third Amended Complaint without leave to amend.  On March 26, 2026, the Court entered an order of dismissal as to Checkr.

 

            On April 28, 2026, the Court granted Defendants Uber and Raiser’s Motion for Summary Judgment on the Third Amended Complaint.  On May 29, 2026, the Court entered judgment in favor of Defendants Uber and Raiser.  On June 4, 2026, Defendants Uber and Raiser filed and served a Notice of Entry of Judgment.  On June 8, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Notice of Appeal of the summary judgment.

 

INSTANT MOTION

 

            On March 25, 2026, Defendant Uber filed the instant motion for an order sealing documents in support of Defendant Uber’s Motion for Protective Order.  As of August 24, 2026, no opposition or reply has been filed.

 

            On April 29, 2026, Defendant Uber filed the instant motion for an order sealing portions of Plaintiffs’ opposition to Uber’s Motion for Summary Judgment.  On August 13, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an opposition.  On August 18, 2026, Defendant Uber filed a reply.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

            Defendants Uber and Raiser submit requests for judicial notice in support of both motions to seal.  Defendants seek judicial notice of the Court’s prior sealing orders in this case.  Defendants request judicial notice of the Court’s February 13, 2025, May 31, 2024 and February 14, 2025 sealing orders in this action.  Defendants’ request for judicial notice of these orders is denied.  The Court does not need to judicially notice documents issued and filed in this action to rely on them in ruling on these motions to seal. 

 

DISCUSSION

 

I. Uber’s Motion to Seal Documents in Support of Uber’s Motion for Protective Order

 

A.    Applicable Law

 

Unless confidentiality is required by law, court records are presumed to be open to the public.  (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 2.550(c).)  Therefore, pleadings, motions, discovery documents, and other papers may not be filed under seal merely by stipulation of the parties.  The parties' agreement that certain documents be filed under seal is improper and insufficient.  (Savaglio v. Wal–Mart Stores, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 588, 600.)  A prior court order must be obtained.  (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 2.55, subd. (a); H.B. Fuller Co. v. Doe (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 879, 888.)  At a minimum, a party seeking to seal documents must come forward with a specific list of facts sought to be withheld and specific reasons for withholding them.  (Id. at 894.) 

 

“Before substantive courtroom proceedings are closed or transcripts are ordered sealed, a trial court must hold a hearing and expressly find that (i) there exists an overriding interest supporting closure and/or sealing; (ii) there is a substantial probability that the interest will be prejudiced absent closure and/or sealing; (iii) the proposed closure and/or sealing is narrowly tailored to serve the overriding interest; and (iv) there is no less restrictive means of achieving the overriding interest.”  (NBC Subsidiary (KNBC-TV), Inc. v. Supr. Ct. (“NBC Subsidiary”) (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1178, 1217-1218.)   

 

As acknowledged in NBC Subsidiary, courts have found various overriding interests that would justify sealing, including privacy interests of a potential juror during voir dire, protection of trade secrets, protection of information within the attorney-client privilege, and enforcement of binding contractual obligations not to disclose.  (NBC Subsidiary, supra, 20 Cal.4th at 1222, fn. 46.)  Financial data involving confidential matters relating to the business operations of a defendant may be subject to sealing, if public revelation of such matters would interfere with its ability to compete, the proposed sealing order is narrowly tailored, and no less restrictive means exists to protect the defendant’s legitimate proprietary interest.   (Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Supr. Ct. (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 1273, 1286.) 

 

“The burden rests on the party seeking to deny public access to court records to establish compelling reasons why and to what extent the records should be made private. Where the relief extends to sealing permanent court records, the court must be careful to limit its denial of access by narrow and well-defined orders. Due to its temporary nature and its infringement upon the public right to know, a sealing order in a civil case is always subject to continuing review and modification, if not termination, upon changed circumstances.”  (Copley Press, Inc. v. Supr. Ct. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 367, 374.) 

 

California Rules of Court 2.550 and 2.551, which are incorporated into the protective order in this case, make clear that the Court may seal records only upon an evidentiary showing sufficient to warrant the required findings. (CRC 2.550, subd. (d) [“The Court may order that a record be filed under seal only if it expressly finds facts that establish [the required findings].”]; CRC 2.551, subd. (b)(1) [motion to seal “must be accompanied by … a declaration containing facts sufficient to justify the sealing”].)

 

B.     Uber’s motion to seal documents in support of motion for protective order is denied.

 

            Here, Uber moves to seal specific documents and portions of documents it submitted in support of its Motion for Protective Order filed on March 24, 2026, and originally set for hearing on August 27, 2026.  Uber identifies the following documents or portions of documents for sealing: 

 

(1) Exhibit C to W. Christopher Maloney’s Declaration in Support of Uber’s Motion

for Protective Order (“Maloney Declaration”), in its entirety;

 

(2) Separate Statement in Support of Uber’s Motion for Protective Order, at the following: Page 3, line 22 – page 10, line 6; Page 10, line 15 – page 19, line 24;

 

(3) Exhibit A to W. Christopher Maloney’s Declaration in Support of Uber’s Motion for Protective Order, at the following: Page 4 at lines 16-17, 21-23, & 28; Page 5, at lines 1, 5-7, 12-13, 18-19, & 24; Page 6, at lines 1-2, 6, 11-12, 16-19, 23-24, & 28; Page 7, at lines 1, 6-7, 12, 16-17, 22, & 26-27; Page 8, at lines 4-5;

 

(4) Exhibit B to W. Christopher Maloney’s Declaration in Support of Uber’s Motion for Protective Order, at the following: Page 7, at line 15; Page 8, at lines 19-20, 22, & 27-28; Page 9, at lines 1, 7, & 8; Page 10, at lines 11-12, 14, 19-20, & 26; Page 12, at lines 2-3, 5, 10-11, & 17-18; Page 13, at lines 22-23 & 25; Page 14, at lines 4-5, & 11-12; Page 15, at lines 15-16, 18, & 24-25; Page 16, at lines 4-5; Page 17, at lines 8-9, 11, 17, & 23-24; Page 18, at lines 27-28; Page 19, at lines 2, 8-9, & 15-16; Page 20, at lines 19-20, 22, & 27; Page 21, at line 4; Page 22, at lines 8-9, 11, 17-18, & 24-25; Page 23, at line 28; Page 24, at lines 1, 3, 8-11, & 17-18; Page 25,

at lines 21-22, & 24; Page 26, at lines 1-2, & 8; Page 27, at lines 13-14, 16, 21-22, & 28; Page 29, at lines 4-5, 13-14, & 20-21; Page 30, at lines 24-25, & 27; Page 31, at lines 6 & 12; Page 32, at lines 17-18, 20, & 25-26; Page 33, at lines 4; Page 34, at lines 8-9, 11, 17, & 23; Page 35, at lines 27-28; Page 36, at lines 2, 7-8, & 14; Page 37, at lines 18-19, 21, & 26-27; Page 38, at line 6; Page 39, at lines 11-12, & 14;

 

(5) Exhibit E to W. Christopher Maloney’s Declaration in Support of Uber’s Motion

for Protective Order, at the following: Page 2, paragraphs 2 & 6; Pages 3, 4, & 5 in

its entirety; Page 6, at section II; Page 8, at paragraph 5.

 

Uber argues that good cause exists to seal these documents because they contain proprietary information.  Uber argues there is a stipulated protective order in place, and the documents in question were marked “confidential” pursuant to that stipulated protective order.  Uber argues that the information it would like sealed contains information relating to its confidential business operations and disclosure would cause it irreparable harm. 

 

In addition, Uber notes that Exhibit C to the Maloney Declaration is the deposition testimony of Peter Sauerwein.  Uber argues the Court already ordered Sauerwein’s deposition testimony sealed on February 13, 2025.  Uber argues the requested order is narrowly tailored to documents containing information previously sealed by the Court.

 

            No opposition or reply has been filed as of August 24, 2026.

 

            The Court finds Defendant failed to establish an overriding interest to support the proposed sealing.  Defendant failed to submit any evidence to support its assertion that the information provided by Sauerwiner during deposition is confidential or that disclosure of the information would result in irreparable harm.  Defendant asserts in its memorandum of points and authorities that disclosure of Sauerwiner’s testimony regarding Uber’s driver policies would damage its ability to compete.  Defendant’s assertion is unsupported by any evidence from a witness with personal knowledge to make such a claim. 

 

            Defendant’s proposed sealing is also not narrowly tailored.  A review of Exhibit C does not support sealing of the entirety of Sauerwiner’s deposition testimony.  (Maloney Dec., Ex. C.)  The deposition includes multiple pages of foundational questioning and testimony regarding Sauerwiner’s position and general instructions regarding depositions, for example.  (Maloney Dec., Ex. C, Sauerwiner Dep., v. I, pp. 1-9.)  Defense counsel’s objections would also not qualify as proprietary information subject to sealing.  These are obvious examples of portions of the deposition testimony that would not be subject to sealing as confidential proprietary information. 

 

In addition, the following excerpts identified for sealing in Exhibit B seek to redact words used by Sauerwine during his deposition that Defendant Uber quoted in isolation and out of context in its discovery objections:  (1) Exhibit B to W. Christopher Maloney’s Declaration in Support of Uber’s Motion for Protective Order, at the following: Page 7, at line 15; Page 8, at lines 19-20, 22, Page 9, at lines 1, 7, & 8; Page 10, at lines 11-12; Page 16, at lines 4-5; Page 17, at lines 23-24; Page 19, at lines 2, 8-9, & 15-16; Page 29, at lines 4-5, 13-14, & 20-21; Page 36, at lines 2, 7-8, & 14; Page 38, at line 6; and (2) Exhibit E to W. Christopher Maloney’s Declaration in Support of Uber’s Motion for Protective Order, at the following: Page 2, paragraph 2.

 

Defendant relies heavily on the prior order issued by Judge Lee S. Arian sealing Exhibits 29 and 30.  The Court is not bound by the prior order, nor does Defendant cite authority to the contrary.  “Since orders to seal court records implicate the public's right of access under the First Amendment, they inherently are subject to ongoing judicial scrutiny, including at the trial court level.”  (In re Marriage of Nicholas (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 1566, 1575.)  To the extent Defendant presented evidence in connection with its prior motion to seal that would support sealing, Defendant failed to present that evidence here.  The Court is also not required to search through the prior record to locate evidence in support of Defendant’s instant request to seal.

            Moreover, the Court does not believe that the evidence submitted in connection with the previous motions to seal is sufficient to warrant the findings required under the case law and California Rules of Court.

 

            Accordingly, Defendant Uber’s Motion to Seal Documents in Support of Uber’s Motion for Protective is DENIED. 

           

II.  Uber’s Motion to Seal Portions of Plaintiffs’ Opposition to Uber’s Motion for Summary Judgment

 

            A.  Uber’s motion to seal portions of Plaintiffs’ Opposition to its MSJ of the Third Amended Complaint is denied

 

            Defendant Uber moves to seal portions of Plaintiffs’ opposition to its Motion for Summary Judgment of the Third Amended Complaint.  Specifically, Uber moves to seal the following: 

·         Exhibit 9 to Plaintiffs’ Compendium of Evidence (“COE”) in its entirety;

·         Exhibit 10 to Plaintiffs’ COE in its entirety;

·         Exhibit 29 to Plaintiffs’ COE in its entirety;

·         Exhibit 30 to Plaintiffs’ COE in its entirety;

·         Exhibit 41 to Plaintiffs’ COE in its entirety;

·         Plaintiffs’ Separate Statement in Opposition at Nos. 17 (20:27-21:4), 20 (21:19-21) and 46 (26:8-10);

·         Plaintiffs’ Memorandum of Points and Authorities at 10:6-8, 15-17.

Uber argues these documents are confidential and contain proprietary trade secret information.  Uber also argues the Court previously granted a motion to seal Exhibits 9, 10, 29 and 30 in its February 13, 2025 order.  Uber argues Exhibits 9 and 10 were sealed on multiple occasions by this Court. 

In opposition, Plaintiffs do not oppose sealing of Exhibits 9 and 10.  Plaintiffs oppose sealing Exhibits 29, 30 and 41, because Defendant failed to establish that these documents contain trade secrets or an overriding interest to seal them.  Plaintiffs argue there is also no overriding interest that would justify sealing the documents. Plaintiffs argue these documents reflect Defendant Uber’s policies regarding their drivers.  Plaintiffs argue the request to seal Exhibits 29, 30 and 41 is also not narrowly tailored.  Plaintiffs argue Defendant fails to demonstrate that redaction would be an insufficient means to protect any overriding interest.

Plaintiffs also argue the Court’s prior sealing orders are not determinative.  Plaintiffs argue a sealing order is always subject to continuing review and modification.  Plaintiffs argue Defendant asks to seal these documents because they reflect negatively on Defendant.

On reply, Defendant argues the Court already issued an order sealing Exhibits 29 and 30 after making the findings required under NBC Subsidiary.  Defendant argues it has articulated how disclosure of this proprietary information would damage its ability to compete.  Defendant argues the information contained in Exhibits 29 and 30 would provide competitors with insight into Uber’s business operations.  Defendant argues the case law relied on by Plaintiffs to re-examine the Court’s prior sealing orders is factually and/or procedurally inapposite.

Defendant argues Exhibit 41 has never previously been presented to the Court.  Defendant argues Exhibit 41 is an internal corporate document associated with platform access and internal metrics for activating and/or deactivating persons from the platform.  Defendant argues it is proprietary business methodology and satisfies the definition of a trade secret under Civil Code section 3426.1.  Defendant argues the proposed sealing is also narrowly tailored because Exhibit 41 is only 1.5 pages. 

Defendant argues the public’s interest in the documents does not overcome its overriding interest in protecting its proprietary information.  Defendant also argues the documents in question were not material to the Court’s ruling on Uber’s motion for summary judgment. 

Defendant argues the First Amendment interest in public disclosure of civil filing is not absolute and does not apply to discovery.  Defendant argues Exhibits 29 and 30 are transcripts from the deposition of Sauerwein.  Defendant argues Exhibits 29, 30 and 41 were irrelevant and immaterial to the Court’s order granting summary judgment.  Defendant argues the First Amendment interest in such information does not overcome its overriding interest in it. 

The Court finds Defendant fails to establish an overriding interest in the materials it seeks to seal.  Defendant submits no evidence in support of the motion to seal.  Defendant claims the material is confidential but provides no declaration from any witness with personal knowledge to make such a claim. 

Defendant asserts in the notice of motion that the information contains trade secret information.  Defendant, however, provides no evidence establishing the elements of trade secret.  “Trade secret” means information, including a formula, pattern, compilation, program, device, method, technique, or process, that: (1) derives independent economic value, actual or potential, from not being generally known to the public or to other persons who can obtain economic value from its disclosure or use; and (2) is the subject of efforts that are reasonable under the circumstances to maintain its secrecy.  (Civ. Code, §3426.1.)  A trade secret must be established with evidence of the information, how or why that information derives independent knowledge from not being generally known, and the efforts undertaken by Uber to keep it secret.  Defendant provides no such evidence.  Merely asserting that the documents contain a trade secret is insufficient. 

The Court also finds Defendant’s proposed sealing is not narrowly tailored. Defendant asks that the Court seal the entirety of Exhibits 29 and 30 based on trade secret and confidential, proprietary business information.  As discussed above, based on a review of the transcripts, the proposed sealing is overbroad.  There are portions of the deposition transcript that clearly do not qualify as confidential, proprietary information. Exhibit 9 of Plaintiffs’ COE is an agreement with Avis.  Again, Defendant does not establish that the entirety of the agreement is confidential, proprietary information or trade secrets. 

Defendant relies heavily on prior orders sealing Exhibits 9, 10, 29 and 30.  As discussed above, the Court is not bound by these prior orders, and it has a continuing duty to evaluate the propriety of sealing orders when presented with the issue. Moreover, the Court does not believe that the evidence submitted in connection with the previous motions to seal is sufficient to warrant the findings required under the case law and California Rules of Court with respect to the exhibits, other than Exhibit 10.

 

Accordingly, Uber’s Motion to Seal Portions of Plaintiffs’ Opposition to Uber’s Motion for Summary Judgment is GRANTED as to Exhibit 10 only and otherwise DENIED.

Case Number: 25SMCV03208    Hearing Date: August 26, 2026    Dept: O

TENTATIVE RULING

August 25, 2026

25SMCV03208 (Schwarz, et. al. v. Bigos, et. al.)

 

RELEVANT BACKGROUND

 

            On June 23, 2025, plaintiffs Jesse Schwarz (Schwarz) and Kelsey Lewis (Lewis) (collectively, Plaintiffs or Tenants) filed their initial complaint against defendants Christopher J. Bigos (Christopher) and Marley R. Bigos (Marley) (collectively, Defendants or Landlords). On August 12, 2025, Plaintiffs filed their first amended complaint (FAC) against Defendants alleging six causes of action: (1) conversion; (2) fraud; (3) breach of Civ. Code § 1950.5; (4) breach of contract; (5) money had and received; (6) unfair competition.

 

            On September 11, 2025, Landlords filed a cross-complaint against Plaintiffs alleging seven causes of action: (1) breach of contract; (2) anticipatory breach of contract; (3) breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing; (4) promissory estoppel; (5) fraud; (6) unjust enrichment; (7) recission of contract. Landlords allege that Tenants breached the parties’ Lease by, among other things, failing to pay rent and utilities. (Cross-Complaint, ¶¶ 14-40.)

                       

INSTANT MOTION

 

             On November 3, 2025, Tenants filed the instant demurrer to the second through seventh causes of action alleged in Landlords’ Cross-Complaint. Landlords filed an opposition, and Tenants filed a reply.

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

            The Court finds that Tenants have satisfied the meet and confer requirement. (Klein Decl. ¶ 2.)

             

DISCUSSION

 

I.       TENANTS’ DEMURRER TO CROSS-COMPLAINT

 

A.    Second Cause of Action – Anticipatory Breach of Contract

 

            The Court agrees with Tenants that the Cross-Complaint fails to alleged facts sufficient to constitute a claim for anticipatory breach of contract. The Court notes that Landlords do not oppose sustaining the demurrer to the Second Cause of Action but request leave to amend that claim to allege, to the extent supported by the facts, the specific statements, dates, speakers, recipients, and future obligations constituting any alleged repudiation. (Opp. at p. 2:24-25.)

 

            Accordingly, the demurrer to the second cause of action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

B.     Third, Fourth, and Seventh Causes of Action – Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing, Promissory Estoppel, and Recission of Contract             

 

            The Court agrees with Tenants that Landlords’ claims for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, promissory estoppel and rescission of contract are duplicative of their breach of contract claim. (Award Metals, Inc. v. Superior Court (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 1128, 1135 [holding that demurrer should have been sustained as to duplicative causes of action]; Rodrigues v. Campbell Industries (1978) 87 Cal.App.3d 494 [finding demurrer was properly sustained without leave to amend as to cause of action that contained allegations of other causes and “thus add[ed] nothing to the complaint by way of fact or theory of recovery”]; Careau & Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit (1990) 222 Cal. App. 3d 1371, 1401 [good faith and fair dealing].) These cases of action are merely duplicative and add nothing to the Cross-Complaint by way of fact or theory. The alleged breaches of the implied covenant are the very same breaches as those alleged in the breach of contract claim. (Cross Compl. ¶ 69.) For the promissory estoppel cause of action, Landlords allege that Tenants breached the same promises in their breach of contract cause of action. (See Cross-Complaint, ¶¶ 71-74.) Lastly, for the rescission of contract cause of action, Landlords restate the same contract terms and the same conduct. (Id. ¶ 99-113.)

 

            Accordingly, the demurrer to the third, fourth, and seventh causes of action are SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

C.    Fifth Cause of Action – Fraud

 

            “The elements of fraud are (a) a misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) scienter or knowledge of its falsity; (c) intent to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Ctr. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) The facts constituting the alleged fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.) The facts constituting the alleged fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked. (Id. at p. 645.) “[Fraud’s] particularity requirement necessitates pleading facts which ‘show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.’ [Citation.]” (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 73.)

 

            The Court agrees with Tenants that Landlords’ claim for fraud lacks the requisite particularity. The fraud claim alleges conduct that has nothing to do with any representations. (Cross-Complaint, ¶¶ 85. [“Tenants. . . made efforts to prolong the remediation efforts and failed to uphold their end of the Lease and agreement between the Landlords and Tenants”] and 86 [“Tenants removed a battery pack that was required to operate a Smart Lock on the door of the House”].) Also, it is unclear from the allegations what misrepresentations the alleged fraud is based on. (See Id. ¶ 82, 89, 91.) Landlords also fail to allege when the statements were made, which Tenant made them, to whom, whether they were made in writing or verbally, and facts showing detrimental reliance. (Id. ¶ 79-96.) Landlords’ opposition does not adequately address these shortcomings. (See Opp. at p. 4-5.)

 

            Accordingly, the demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

D.    Sixth Cause of Action – Unjust Enrichment

 

            “The elements for a claim of unjust enrichment are receipt of a benefit and unjust retention of the benefit at the expense of another. The theory of unjust enrichment requires one who acquires a benefit which may not justly be retained, to return either the thing or its equivalent to the aggrieved party so as not to be unjustly enriched.” (Lyles v. Sangadeo-Patel (2014) 225 Cal.App.4th 759, 769, quotation marks and citations omitted.) Notably, “[u]njust enrichment is not a cause of action”; it is simply “a restitution claim.” (Hill v. Roll International Corp. (2011) 195 Cal.App.4th 1295, 1307; see also Melchior v. New Line Productions, Inc. (2003) 106 Cal.App.4th 779, 793; McBride v. Boughton (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 379, 384.)

            The Court agrees with Tenants that Landlords’ claim for unjust enrichment fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. Landlords allege only that “Landlords reallege and incorporate by reference the foregoing paragraphs as though fully set forth herein” and “Tenants have been unjustly enriched at the expense of Landlords in an amount subject to proof at trial.” (Cross-Complaint ¶ 97-98.) The alternative theory argument alleged in the opposition (Opp. 7:14-8:5) is not alleged in the Cross-Complaint. The Cross-Complaint does not allege that the Lease or later agreement is void, unenforceable, ineffective, or does not govern the benefits for which restitution is sought.

 

            Accordingly, the demurrer to the sixth cause of action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

            Plaintiffs may have thirty (30) days in which to file and serve an amended complaint.
