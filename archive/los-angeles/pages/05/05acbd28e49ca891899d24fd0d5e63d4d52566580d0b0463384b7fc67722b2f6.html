Case Number: 23STCV20111    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 6 - John Covarrubias, et al. v. Hussan Ahmad Malik, et al.

          Plaintiffs’ Motion to Reinstate Right to Jury Trial

Plaintiffs John Covarrubias and Andrew Gonzales move for relief from their waiver of the right to a jury trial. Plaintiffs concede they did not timely pay jury fees and thereby waived that right.

Code of Civil Procedure section 631, subdivision (g) provides, “The court may, in its discretion upon just terms, allow a trial by jury although there may have been a waiver of a trial by jury.” The law favors liberally granting relief from waiver of jury trial. “Courts have held that, given the public policy favoring trial by jury, the trial court should grant a motion to be relieved of a jury waiver ‘unless, and except, where granting such a motion would work serious hardship to the objecting party.’ [Citation.] Where doubt exists concerning the propriety of granting relief from such waiver, this doubt, by reason of the constitutional guarantee of right to jury trial [citation], should be resolved in favor of the party requesting trial by jury.” (Gann v. Williams Brothers Realty, Inc. (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1698, 1703–1704.) “When a party that has timely given notice that it desires trial by jury then loses the jury right because of technical noncompliance with some element of statutory procedure—such as failure to pay jury fees at the right time or in the right amount—lack of hardship to the other parties or the court is generally controlling, absent other factors that weigh against relief.” (TriCoast Builders, Inc. v. Fonnegra (2024) 15 Cal.5th 766, 782.) 

Granting this motion serves the interests of justice. Plaintiffs present evidence that counsel inadvertently failed to pay jury fees. (Feldman Decl., ¶ 2.) Defendants posted jury fees in July 2025. Plaintiffs posted the required fees on August 5, 2026. Trial is currently scheduled for March 22, 2027. Defendants did not oppose this motion and therefore do not show any resulting prejudice.

After considering all relevant circumstances, the Court concludes denying relief to Plaintiffs would be inconsistent with the “inviolate” right to a trial by jury.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 631(a).)

Disposition

          Plaintiffs John Covarrubias and Andrew Gonzales’s motion to reinstate right to jury trial is granted.


Case Number: 24STCV11617    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: 510

No.  7 - Oscar Perez v. James David Maldonado, et al.

Plaintiff Oscar Perez’s Motion to Quash Deposition Subpoena

Plaintiff Oscar Perez moves to quash a deposition subpoenas Defendant Music Express, Inc. served on the San Fernando Police Department.

Evidentiary Objections

Defendants make 4 evidentiary objections to Plaintiff’s evidence. Objection nos. 1-2 (italicized material only) are sustained based on improper argument. Objection nos. 3-5 are overruled.

Plaintiff makes 4 evidentiary objections to Defendants’ evidence. Objections nos. 1-3 are sustained based on lack of foundation or personal knowledge. Objection no. 4 is overruled.

Quash Subpoena

Upon a party’s motion, a court “may make an order quashing [a] subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders. In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1(a).)

          The Court acknowledges that Plaintiff previously moved to quash four identical subpoenas directed at different law enforcement agencies.

Defendants sought the following documents:

THE RECORDS REQUESTED ARE from 09/05/2014 to 08/27/2022 FOR THE FOLLOWING TYPES OF RECORDS: ALL LAW ENFORCEMENT REPORTS, INCLUDING BUT NOT LIMITED TO, CRIME REPORTS, INCIDENT REPORTS, CONTACT REPORTS, SUSPICIOUS BEHAVIOR REPORTS, FIELD INTERVIEW NOTES OR REPORTS, FIELD INTERVIEW (Fl) CARDS, INCIDENT OR EVENT CARDS OR REPORTS, ANY AND ALL REPORTS WHERE OSCAR PEREZ-SIANUQUI AKA OSCAR PEREZ, OSCAR PEREZSIANUQUI (DOB: 9/4/1996) IS LISTED AS A SUSPECT, A WITNESS, A PERSON OF INTEREST TO A CRIME, OR PRESENT AT THE SCENE OF A CRIME. ALSO, ANY AND ALL REPORTS FROM CASE NUMBER LASPA096960-01, AN INCIDENT OCCURRING ON JANUARY 2, 2021.

(Khatchaturian Decl., Ex. A.)

On August 14, 2026, the Court quashed the subpoenas, finding they were overbroad and sought irrelevant information as drafted.

          Now, Plaintiff seeks to quash an identical subpoena directed to the San Fernando Police Department. In opposition, Defendants request the Court exercise its authority under CCP § 1987.1(a) to modify the subpoena to seek a narrowed set of documents:

Any traffic collision report, crime or criminal report, incident report, arrest report, event or dispatch report, or other document containing information concerning a traffic collision occurring between June 1, 2019 and July 31, 2019 in which OSCAR PEREZ-SIANUQUI aka OSCAR PEREZ aka OSCAR PEREZ-SIANUQUI (DOB 9/4/1996) was involved, and in which Mr. Perez jumped or climbed onto a moving vehicle that thereafter collided with a tree, injuring Mr. Perez and Carla Guzman.

(Opp., at p. 2.)        

          The Court finds that this narrowed subpoena addresses its prior concerns regarding overbreadth and relevance. As revised and narrowed, the subpoena is tailored to seek documents regarding one traffic collision incident. Whether the documents produced in response to the subpoena will ultimately be admissible at trial is irrelevant to whether it is ultimately discoverable at this stage. “[A]dmissibility is not prerequisite to discoverability.”  (Volkswagen of America, Inc. v. Superior Court (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 1481, 1492.)

Plaintiff’s privacy concerns do not outweigh Defendants’ need for disclosure. “The party asserting a privacy right must establish a legally protected privacy interest, an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and a threatened intrusion that is serious.” (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552.) If the objecting party shows all three elements, then the court must balance the need for disclosure against the right to privacy. (Ibid.) The court must also consider “the availability of alternatives and protective measures.” (Id. at p. 556.)

Here, Plaintiff cannot show that he has an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in these police records given the circumstances. Plaintiff sued Defendants to recover damages for a motor vehicle incident in which he was struck by a car as a pedestrian. Plaintiff would reasonably expect that other motor vehicle accidents with which he was involved, especially as a pedestrian, may be discovered in this case. The intrusion is not serious. The narrowed subpoena seeks records related to one incident—not eight years of law enforcement information as was initially sought.

Disposition

Plaintiff Oscar Perez’s motion to quash deposition subpoena directed at San Fernando Police Department is granted in part. The Court hereby modifies the subpoena directed to the San Fernando Police Department to seek the following records:

Any traffic collision report, crime or criminal report, incident report, arrest report, event or dispatch report, or other document containing information concerning a traffic collision occurring between June 1, 2019 and July 31, 2019 in which OSCAR PEREZ-SIANUQUI aka OSCAR PEREZ aka OSCAR PEREZ-SIANUQUI (DOB 9/4/1996) was involved, and in which Mr. Perez jumped or climbed onto a moving vehicle that thereafter collided with a tree, injuring Mr. Perez and Carla Guzman.


Case Number: 25STCV10927    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 8 - Artyom Harutyunyan v. Volvo Cars of North America, LLC

Plaintiff Artyom Harutyunyan’s Motion for Attorney Fees

          Plaintiff Artyom Harutyunyan moves for a total of $62,597 in attorney fees and costs under Civil Code § 1794(d). The prevailing plaintiff may recover “the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” (Civ. Code, § 1794(d).)

Plaintiff seeks $51,390 for 98.3 hours incurred, consisting of 88.5 attorney hours and 9.8 paralegal hours. Plaintiff also seeks an additional $929 in costs. Plaintiff uses a 1.2 multiplier on the attorney fees, for an additional $10,278.

Hourly Rates

Plaintiff requests fees for time spent on the case by two licensed attorneys, Sam Azimtash and Juliana Ter Haroutunian, and one paralegal. Plaintiff presents evidence of both attorneys’ qualifications and experience. (See Kohen Decl., ¶¶ 15-19.) They bill $550 per hour. The paralegal rate is $250 per hour.

Based on its knowledge of the legal market, its review of the credentials and experience of Plaintiff’s attorneys, and the particular tasks performed by counsel, the Court finds Plaintiff claims excessive hourly rates for counsel. This was a straightforward case that did not require superior expertise. Thus, the Court will reduce counsel’s rates to $500 per hour.

Hours

In calculating the lodestar, the court must determine whether the tasks performed by an attorney were necessary and whether the amount of time billed for each task was reasonable. (Baxter v. Bock (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 775, 793.) The moving party has the burden of proof on these issues. (Ibid.) A reduced award is justified when a case is “overlitigated” and the bills are “padded.” (Morris v. Hyundai Motor America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 38.) For a “voluminous fee application,” courts may “make across-the-board percentage cuts either in the number of hours claimed or in the final lodestar figure.” (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 41 (Warren); accord Morris, supra, 41 Cal.App.5th at p. 40 [“The court could properly have made an across-the-board reduction of 30 percent”].) 

Overall, Plaintiff’s counsel billed a reasonable number of hours for tasks performed on the case. However, the Court will not award fees requested for “anticipated” tasks that were not needed. The Court will not award fees incurred for 5.7 anticipated hours spent reviewing an opposition brief and drafting a reply for this fee motion. No opposition was filed; thus, no reply brief was necessary. The Court will therefore award only 82.8 hours (88.5 requested less 5.7 not incurred) of attorney fees.

Multiplier

Plaintiff moves for a 1.2 multiplier. Multipliers may be awarded based on factors including “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.”  (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.) After considering all relevant factors, the Court finds a lodestar enhancement is not warranted. This case did not involve novel or difficult questions. Plaintiff’s counsel did not demonstrate exceptional skill in litigating the case. Counsel’s hourly rates adequately account for representation on contingency. (See Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 395.)

Other Costs

Plaintiff seeks $929 for costs. “[I]n enacting Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) the Legislature intended the phrase ‘costs and expenses’ to cover items not included in ‘the detailed statutory definition of “costs” ’ set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5.” (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 42.) “In awarding costs, a trial court’s first determination ... is whether the statute expressly allows the particular item, and whether it appears proper on its face. If so, the burden is on the objecting party to show them to be unnecessary or unreasonable.” (Benach v. County of Los Angeles (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 836, 855, internal quotes and citations omitted.) As noted, Defendant did not oppose the request for costs.

The Court finds that all costs were reasonably incurred and will award the requested amount of costs in full.

Therefore, the Court will award a total of $44,779 in attorney fees and costs on this motion. This includes (1) 82.8 hours at an adjusted rate of $500 per hour (88.5 total hours subtracted by 5.7 unnecessary anticipated hours) ($41,400) and (2) 9.8 hours at the paralegal rate of $250 per hour ($2,450). This totals $43,850. The Court will then award $929 in costs.

Disposition  

          Plaintiff Artyom Harutyunyan’s motion for attorney fees is granted in part in the total amount of $44,779 in fees and costs.


Case Number: 25STCV12104    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 9 - Christopher Estes v. Virgin Galactic, LLC

Defendant Galactic Co., LLC’s Motion for Summary Judgment or Summary Adjudication

Defendant Galactic Co., LLC moves for summary judgment of this action by Plaintiff Christopher Estes. In the alternative, Defendant moves for summary adjudication of 14 issues: each of Plaintiff’s six causes of action (with two or more issues for the first, second, third, and fifth causes of action) and Plaintiff’s claim for punitive damages.

Evidentiary Objections

          Plaintiff asserts seven objections to the Declaration of Taline Cole filed in support of the motion. Objection nos. 1, 2 and 4 are sustained based on lack of personal knowledge. The remaining objections are overruled.

          Defendant asserts 30 objections to the Declaration of Christopher Estes filed in support of the opposition to the motion. Objection no. 18 (second sentence only) is sustained based on improper opinion. The remaining objections are overruled.

Factual Background

          In October 2023, Plaintiff injured his left shoulder while working for Defendant as a “CDL driver.” Following his injury, Plaintiff was placed on modified, light-duty work and was prohibited from pushing, pulling, or lifting anything over twenty pounds, a restriction that was later reduced to five pounds. In April 2024, Defendant determined that it could no longer accommodate Plaintiff’s modified work duties and placed Plaintiff on a leave of absence. Plaintiff’s leave of absence was continually extended until Defendant terminated him on March 31, 2025. While Plaintiff requested a further extension of his leave, Defendant declined, stating that his restrictions did not allow him to perform essential job functions, but invited him to reapply once his restrictions had been lifted.

Legal Standard for Summary Judgment or Adjudication

Summary judgment should be granted where no triable issues of fact exist and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (CCP § 437c(c); Villa v.  McFerren (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 733, 741.) Courts use a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent’s claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)

1st Cause of Action: Disability Discrimination

Defendant is entitled to summary adjudication of this cause of action. “A prima facie case of disability discrimination under FEHA requires the employee to show he or she (1) suffered from a disability, (2) was otherwise qualified to do his or her job, and (3) was subjected to adverse employment action because of the disability.” (Nealy v. City of Santa Monica (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 359, 378 (Nealy).) For the second element, “a qualified individual is someone who is able to perform the essential functions of his or her job, with or without reasonable accommodation.” (Ibid.) The typical burden-shifting framework used in most discrimination and retaliation cases does “not apply in discrimination cases where … the plaintiff presents direct evidence of the employer’s motivation for the adverse employment action.” (Wallace v. County of Stanislaus (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 109, 123 (Wallace).) 

In Wallace, the Court of Appeal explained, “[D]isability discrimination cases often involve direct evidence of the role of the employee’s actual or perceived disability in the employer’s decision to implement an adverse employment action. Instead of litigating the employer’s reasons for the action, the parties’ disputes in disability cases focus on whether the employee was able to perform essential job functions, whether there were reasonable accommodations that would have allowed the employee to perform those functions, and whether a reasonable accommodation would have imposed an undue hardship on the employer.” (Wallace, supra, 245 Cal.App.4th at p. 123.) “The FEHA itself recognizes that employers have legitimate reasons for discriminating against disabled persons (i.e., treating them differently)–reasons that have no parallel when the discriminatory employment action is based on race, religion, national origin, age or sex.  Those legitimate reasons include whether a disabled employee, even with reasonable accommodation, can perform essential job duties and do so safely.” (Id. at p. 126.)

This case exemplifies the type of disability discrimination claim Wallace described. There is no dispute over why Defendant terminated Plaintiff. Defendant itself asserts it terminated Plaintiff because it could no longer continue offering him a leave of absence. Instead, Plaintiff argues Defendant could have extended his leave of absence or otherwise provided him another reasonable accommodation instead of terminating him.

A. Essential Functions

Defendant meets its burden of showing Plaintiff could not perform his job’s essential functions. “ ‘Essential functions’ means the fundamental job duties of the employment position the individual with a disability holds.” (Gov. Code, § 12926(f).) A job “function may be essential because the reason the position exists is to perform that function.” (Id., subd. (f)(1)(A).) “Evidence of whether a particular function is essential includes” several factors such as “[t]he employer’s judgment as to which functions are essential,” “[w]ritten job descriptions prepared before advertising or interviewing applicants for the job,” “[t]he amount of time spent on the job performing the function,” “[t]he consequences of not requiring the incumbent to perform the function,” “[t]he terms of a collective bargaining agreement,” “[t]he work experience of past incumbents in the job,” and “[t]he current work experience of incumbents in similar jobs.” (Id., subd. (f)(2).)

Defendant presents evidence that Plaintiff worked as a “Senior CDL Driver.” (Cole Decl., Ex. 2.) Lifting heavy items was part of Plaintiff’s official job description. (See Perez-Yañez Decl., Ex. C.) Specifically, the job description lists “[l]oad[ing] materials, parts, & tools onto or into vehicles and/or trailers and properly secur[ing] with strapping, bracing, and padding to prevent shifting or damage during transit” as a job responsibility. (Id., at p. 2.) The job description also lists the following requirements: “Must be able to stoop, bend, crawl, and be able to maneuver in tight spaces . . . Physically able to handle items weighing up to 75 lbs (unassisted).” (Id., at pp. 2-3.) During his deposition, Plaintiff stated he understood his position had these physical requirements. (Def. Comp. of Exhibits, Ex. B, Pl. Depo. 64:13-21, 66:2-6.) When asked what his job duties were, Plaintiff responded:

I would collect [parcels] off that shelf, and I would deliver them to either their desk or their respective areas where they work. And if they had a large tool that needed to be moved from one hangar to the other, then I would use a forklift to put it on a trailer and transport it from one site to the next.

(Id., at 54:2-9.)

Plaintiff fails to raise a triable issue of material fact as to whether loading and unloading materials was an essential function of his job. “An applicant or employee has the burden of proof to establish that the applicant or employee is a qualified individual capable of performing the essential functions of the job with or without reasonable accommodation.” (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11066(a).)  Generally, “the essential functions of a job are a question of fact.” (Hastings v. Department of Corrections (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 963, 968, fn. 6.) But “[a]bsent other evidence, [courts] may properly rely on the employer’s judgment and the written job description to determine the essential job functions.” (Ibid.) 

Plaintiff does not dispute that, in his position, he “was responsible for transporting parcels, commercial freight, large manufacturing tools, supplies, and any other kind of delivery order that was needed within the Mojave campus, and the Tustin, and Pheonix [sic] locations.” (UMF No. 7.) In his deposition, Plaintiff effectively concedes that lifting materials was an essential function: when asked if there was any job he felt he could perform with his restrictions, he responded that he could do all the driving while his codriver “would help [him] load everything at the Mojave end.” (Pl. Comp. of Exhibits, Ex. 1, Pl. Depo. 117:9-20.) Plaintiff agreed that this proposal meant that his co-driver would take on extra duties on top of his already-assigned tasks. (See id., at 122:4-7.) Needing two people to do one person’s job confirms that lifting objects was indeed an essential function of the driver position. Plaintiff does not provide any contrary evidence that would lead a reasonable trier of fact to conclude that loading and unloading materials was not an essential function of his job.

B. Reasonable Accommodations

Although Plaintiff fails to raise a triable issue of fact as to whether he could perform his essential job duties, Plaintiff can still establish the second element of this cause of action—that he is otherwise qualified to do the job—if he can prove that he could have performed his essential job duties with a reasonable accommodation. 

However, Plaintiff does not meet this burden. Plaintiff raises several arguments to show that he could perform the job with a reasonable accommodation.

 First, Plaintiff contends that Defendant’s decision to allow modified light-work after his initial injury confirms that such accommodation was reasonable and allowed him to perform all essential duties. Plaintiff attests that he performed light-duty work after his injury, which required taking light parcels off a receiving shelf, putting them in a cart, and delivering them to a separate building on one of Defendant’s campuses. (Pl. Comp. of Exhibits, Ex. 1, Pl. Depo. 84:21-85:17.) This work, which did not require heavy lifting, allowed him to perform his essential duties with a reasonable accommodation.

The Court disagrees. The fact that Defendant, at one point in time, offered a certain accommodation does not mean that the accommodation was per se reasonable or did not require eliminating the job’s essential functions. “[A]n employer has no affirmative duty to create a new position to accommodate a disabled employee.” (Raine v. City of Burbank (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1224.) Here, Defendant’s accommodation for Plaintiff to only deliver light parcels was effectively a new, different job.

Second, Plaintiff argues that Defendant did not fully explore potential reasonable accommodations with him because there were other jobs within the company he could have performed. To show other jobs were available, Plaintiff cites his own testimony that “Josh Nicotra said he could find something for me to do down at the metal shop” that would only require using one hand. (Pl. Depo., 116:7-13.) This inadmissible hearsay testimony does not raise a triable issue of material fact as to whether Defendant could have assigned Plaintiff to another role in the company given his work restrictions. Importantly, Plaintiff himself testified that he did not know of any vacant roles in the metal shop at that time. (Pl. Depo., 116:14-17; 143:17-19.) In addition, Taline Cole, Senior Director of Culture, Employee Relations, and Community with Defendant, attests that “[t]here is no role in Galactic’s metal shop operating a machine that only requires the use of one hand, and there were no open roles in Galactic’s metal shop in 2024 and 2025.” (Cole Decl., ¶ 25.) Maira Baha, Defendant’s benefits manager, told Plaintiff that they had “already explored possible positions with [his] manager and discovered that there is nothing available within [his] restrictions.” (Def. Comp. of Exhibits, Ex. S.)

Therefore, Plaintiff does not raise a triable issue of material fact that Defendant could have provided him a different role in the company as a reasonable accommodation.

Lastly, Plaintiff claims that Defendant could and should have extended his leave of absence as a form of reasonable accommodation. “[I]n appropriate circumstances, reasonable accommodation can include providing the employee … additional unpaid leave for treatment.” (Hanson v. Lucky Stores, Inc. (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 215, 226.) “[A] finite leave can be a reasonable accommodation under FEHA, provided it is likely that at the end of the leave, the employee would be able to perform his or her duties.” (Ibid.) “ ‘Reasonable accommodation does not require the employer to wait indefinitely for an employee’s medical condition to be corrected.’ ” (Id. at pp. 226-227.)

The record shows that Plaintiff was not likely to be able to perform his duties after a leave of absence. Here, Plaintiff was granted a leave of absence that was continually extended over the course of almost one year, from April 14, 2024 to March 31, 2025. When Plaintiff was placed on leave in April 2024, his work restrictions dictated that he could not push, pull, or lift objects over five pounds. (See Def. Comp. of Exhibits, Ex. F.) In July 2024, Plaintiff’s condition deteriorated, and he was prohibited from lifting or carrying anything. (Id., Ex. I.) In January 2025, Plaintiff was prohibited from using his left upper extremity entirely. (Id., Ex. N.) In March 2025, Plaintiff was further prohibited from prolonged standing/walking, bending, stooping, climbing, kneeling, squatting or crawling. (Id., Ex. R.) After one year of leave, and increased physical restrictions, it was reasonable for the Defendant to decline to extend Plaintiff’s leave further. The accommodation Plaintiff sought was not reasonable because Defendant was not required “to wait indefinitely” for Plaintiff’s “medical condition to be corrected.” (Hanson, supra, 74 Cal.App.4th at pp. 226-227.)

Plaintiff presented no evidence showing when he could have returned to work and performed his essential functions. Instead, Defendant submits a copy of Plaintiff’s email to Defendant’s benefits manager in March 2025, wherein he states he “could possibly be able to go back to work in a couple of months.” (See Def. Comp. of Exhibits, Ex. S.) However, when asked about this email during his deposition, Plaintiff stated that he didn’t know if there were open positions that he believed he could do when he could possibly return—he simply knew that he could drive and help out in the metal shop. (Pl. Depo. 157:5-10.) Therefore, Plaintiff’s prediction that he “could possibly be able to go back to work in a couple of months” was not a prediction he would return to his driver position and perform the job’s essential functions. Plaintiff provides no evidence, such as a doctor’s note, stating that he would be able to return to work after leave and perform his essential functions.

While the Court acknowledges that the “reasonableness of an accommodation generally is a question of fact[,]” (Nealy, supra, 234 Cal.App.4th at p. 374) nothing in the record before the Court would permit a trier of fact to find that Plaintiff could likely return to work as a driver and perform his essential job functions after a finite leave. Defendant had the right to terminate Plaintiff for his indefinite absence from the company.

2nd Cause of Action: Failure to Engage in Interactive Process

          Defendant is entitled to summary adjudication of this cause of action. Like the first cause of action, a claim for failing to engage in the interactive process requires that Plaintiff could perform his essential job duties. “In framing and defining” concepts including the “interactive process”, “ ‘FEHA seeks to assure [that] “those employees with a disability who can perform the essential duties of the employment position with reasonable accommodation” have the opportunity to do so and are not discriminated against based on their disability.’ ”  (Kaur v. Foster Poultry Farms LLC (2022) 83 Cal.App.5th 320, 343.) As stated above, the undisputed facts show Plaintiff was not capable of performing his position’s essential functions. 

          The record also shows that, based on the undisputed facts, no reasonable accommodation was available. “To prevail on a claim for failure to engage in the interactive process, the employee must identify a reasonable accommodation that would have been available at the time the interactive process occurred.” (Nealy, supra, 234 Cal.App.4th at p. 379.) With no available accommodation, Defendant cannot be liable for failing to engage in the interactive process.

3rd Cause of Action: Failure to Provide Reasonable Accommodation

Defendant is entitled to summary adjudication of this cause of action. “ ‘The essential elements of a failure to accommodate claim are: (1) the plaintiff has a disability covered by the FEHA; (2) the plaintiff is a qualified individual (i.e., he or she can perform the essential functions of the position); and (3) the employer failed to reasonably accommodate the plaintiff’s disability.’ ” (Cuiellette v. City of Los Angeles (2011) 194 Cal.App.4th 757, 766.) For the reasons discussed above, the undisputed facts show Plaintiff was not a qualified employee who could perform his position’s essential functions. 

Defendant also meets its burden on summary adjudication by showing no reasonable accommodation was available. “[A]n employer is liable under [Government Code] section 12940(m) for failing to accommodate an employee only if the work environment could have been modified or adjusted in a manner that would have enabled the employee to perform the essential functions of the job.” (Nadaf-Rahrov v. Neiman Marcus Group, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 952, 975.) The record shows the only requested accommodation Defendant did not provide was a leave of absence extending past March 31, 2025. As discussed above, that accommodation was not reasonable. Defendant therefore cannot be liable for failing to provide the accommodation.

4th Cause of Action: Retaliation

          Defendant is also entitled to summary adjudication of this cause of action. “The elements of a cause of action for retaliation in violation of [FEHA] are: ‘(1) the employee’s engagement in a protected activity ...; (2) retaliatory animus on the part of the employer; (3) an adverse action by the employer; (4) a causal link between the retaliatory animus and the adverse action; (5) damages; and (6) causation.’ ” (Brown v. Los Angeles Unified School District (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 1092, 1105.) 

Defendant meets its initial burden by showing Plaintiff cannot establish the second and fourth elements: retaliatory motive and causal link between it and the adverse action.  For retaliation under FEHA, it is unlawful to take an adverse action against an employee: (1) “because the [employee] has opposed any practices forbidden under this part or because the person has filed a complaint, testified, or assisted in any proceeding under this part” (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (h)), or (2) “for requesting accommodation” of disability, “regardless of whether the request was granted” (id., subd. (m)(2)).

As discussed above, there is no dispute over why Defendant terminated Plaintiff.  He requested a further leave of absence, and Defendant denied the request. Plaintiff presents no evidence permitting an inference that Defendant terminated Plaintiff because he opposed FEHA violations or because he requested an accommodation. Plaintiff makes no attempt to do so. He attempts only to dispute whether FEHA required Defendant to grant Plaintiff’s request for a leave of absence as a reasonable accommodation of his disability. Even if so, that would not suffice for Plaintiff’s retaliation claim. There is no triable issue of material fact on the fourth cause of action.

5th Cause of Action: Failure to Prevent Discrimination and Retaliation

Defendant is entitled to summary adjudication of this cause of action.  Government Code section 12940(k) provides that it is unlawful “[f]or an employer … to fail to take all reasonable steps necessary to prevent discrimination and harassment from occurring.” This subdivision “creates a separate actionable tort enforceable upon the establishment of the usual tort elements of duty of care, breach of duty (a negligent act or omission), causation, and damages.” (Dickson v. Burke Williams, Inc. (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 1307, 1313 (Dickson).) 

This claim cannot stand on its own without an underlying claim for violating FEHA. (See Dickson, supra, 234 Cal.App.4th at 1318.) Because Plaintiff cannot maintain a claim for discrimination or retaliation under FEHA, he likewise cannot maintain a claim for failure to prevent discrimination and retaliation.

6th Cause of Action: Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy

          Defendant is entitled to summary adjudication of this cause of action. “[A] common law tort cause of action exists for at-will employees against employers who discharge them in violation of fundamental public policy.” (Prue v. Brady Co./San Diego, Inc. (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 1367, 1377.) Terminating an employee “ ‘ “violates public policy when it contravenes the provision of a statute forbidding termination for a specified reason.” ’ ” (Ibid.) 

Plaintiff alleges his termination violated public policy because it violated FEHA, the California Family Rights Act, and “Article I, Section 1 of the California Constitution.” (Compl., ¶ 82.)  This cause of action thus relies on and fails along with Plaintiff’s first and fourth causes of action.

Punitive Damages

Because Defendant is entitled to summary judgment as to Plaintiff’s complaint, there is no basis for punitive damages against it.

Disposition

Defendant Galactic Co., LLC’s motion for summary judgment is granted.  Defendant shall submit a proposed judgment in its favor forthwith.
