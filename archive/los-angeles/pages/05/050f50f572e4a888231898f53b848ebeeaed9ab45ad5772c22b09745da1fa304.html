Case Number: 20STCV27223    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 407

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Brock T. Hammond, Department 407

 

 

HEARING DATE:     September 17, 2026                                      TRIAL DATE:  Jury verdict

                                                          

CASE:                         Daevieon Towns v. Hyundai Motor America

 

CASE NO.:                     20STCV27223

 

 

PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION FOR ATTORNEYS’ FEES, COSTS AND EXPENSES

 

DEFENDANT HYUNDAI MOTOR AMERICA’S MOTION TO STRIKE, OR IN

THE ALTERNATIVE, TAX COSTS

     

     

I.         BACKGROUND

 

            This is a lemon law action concerning a 2017 Elantra SE (the “Vehicle”).  On July 21, 202, Plaintiffs, Daevieon Towns and Lashona Johsnon, filed the First Amended Complaint against Defendant, Hyundai Motor America, asserting three violations of the Song-Beverly Act.

 

            On September 1, 2022, the jury rendered a verdict awarding Plaintiffs compensatory damages of $9,374.92 and civil penalties in the amount of $18,749.84. 

 

On September 27, 2022, judgment was entered against Plaintiffs in favor.  In post-judgment Judge Yolanda Orozco, then presiding, offset the damages award, awarded prejudgment interest, and granted Defendant’s motion for judgment notwithstanding the verdict in part and entered amended judgment.  The parties filed cross-appeals.

 

            On December 22, 2025, the Court of Appeal issued a published opinion in Towns v. Hyundai Motor America (2025) 117 Cal.App.5th 958 affirming in part and reversing in part the judgment. This court was directed to enter a modified judgment awarding plaintiff Daevieon Towns $24,085.83 in damages, prejudgment interest on that amount, and $18,749.84 as a civil penalty.  

 

            On April 29, 2026, the remittitur was issued.

           

            Before the court are two motions: (1) Plaintiff Daevieon Towns’s Motion for Attorney Fees, and (2) Defendant’s Motion to Tax Costs.  The court addresses these motions in turn.

 

 

II.        MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES

 

A.     Background

 

On June 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed this motion for attorney fees.[1] 

 

On September 3, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition.

 

On September 10, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a reply.

 

B.    Discussion & Legal Standard

 

As the prevailing party, Plaintiffs are entitled to an award of reasonable attorney fees. (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)  California courts apply the “lodestar” approach to determine what fees are reasonable.  (See, e.g., Holguin v. DISH Network LLC (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 1310, 1332.)  This inquiry “begins with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.”  (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.)  From there, the “lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided.”  (Ibid.)  Relevant factors include “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.”  (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.)  The party seeking fees has the burden of documenting the appropriate hours expended and hourly rates.  (City of Colton v. Singletary (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 751, 784.) 

 

Plaintiffs seek a total fee award of $962,152.50, which consists of a lodestar multiplier of 1.5 in the amount of $320,717.50, hours billed by Knight Law Group (“KLG”) in the amount of $275,837.50, and hours billed by Greines, Martin, Stein & Richland LLP (“GMSR”) in the amount of $365,597.50.  

1.  Reasonable Hourly Rate 

KLG charged $250-$600 for trial court proceedings and GMSR charged $650-$925 in connection to the appeal.  (Kirnos Decl., Ex. A; Tobisman Decl. Ex. A.) Defendant argues the rates are not reasonable and requests that the court reduce KLG’s rates to $350 and GMSR’s rates to $700. 

Based upon general prevailing rates in the Los Angeles area for this type of litigation, the Court finds the reasonable rate for attorney billing is as follows: for Lodestar purposes, the court will award a “blended” hourly rate of $500.00 per hour for KLG and $850.00 per hour for GMSR.  (See, e.g., Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240 [trial court has discretion to award a “blended hourly rate” for attorney’s fees motion].)  This determination considers the complexity of the case, the quality of services provided, and the attorneys’ experience. 

2.  Number of Hours Reasonably Expended

Plaintiff provides a copy of counsel’s billing records, which show 634.9 hours billed by KLG (Kirnos Decl., Ex. A) and 440.80 hours billed by GMSR (Tobisman Decl., Ex A).

Defendant argues the amount billed is not reasonable and challenges specific billing entries.  (See Takahashi Decl., Ex. M.)

 

            After reviewing the filings in connection to this matter, the court finds the hours reasonably incurred by KLG and GMSR is 350 hours and 250 hours, respectively.  Applying the blended rates discussed above, the court finds the attorney fees reasonably incurred in this action is $387,500.00.  The court, in its discretion, declines to award a multiplier.

 

C.    Conclusion

 

The motion for attorney fees is GRANTED IN PART.  The court awards Plaintiff $387,500.00 in attorney fees. 

 

III.      MOTION TO TAX COSTS

 

A.    Background

 

On June 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Memorandum of Costs for $61,521.19.

 

On June 25, 2026, Defendant filed a motion to strike or tax the cost memorandum.

 

On September 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. 

 

On September 10, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

 

B.    Legal Standard 

 

A party challenging the amounts claimed may file a motion to tax costs, which must be served and filed 15 days after service of the cost memorandum.¿ If the cost memorandum was served by mail, the period is extended as provided in Code of Civil Procedure section 1013. If the cost memorandum was served electronically, the period is extended as provided in Code of Civil Procedure section 1010.6, subdivision (a)(4).¿ (Cal. Rules of Court., rule 3.1700(b)(1).)¿ “Unless objection is made to the entire cost memorandum, the motion ... must refer to each item objected to by the same number and appear in the same order as the corresponding cost item claimed on the memorandum of costs and must state why the item is objectionable.”¿ (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(b)(2).)¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿ 

The memorandum of cost is a verified statement by the party, attorney, or agent that the costs are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).) “If the items appear to be proper charges, the verified memorandum is prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses and services therein listed were necessarily incurred by the defendant, and the burden of showing that an item is not properly chargeable or is unreasonable is upon the [objecting party].”¿ (Oak Grove School Dist. v. City Title Ins. Co. (1963) 217 Cal.App.2d 678, 698.)¿ “[I]f the correctness of the memorandum is challenged either in whole or in part by the affidavit or other evidence of the contesting party, the burden is then on the party claiming the costs and disbursements to show that the items charged were for matters necessarily relevant and material to the issues involved in the action.”¿ (Id. at p. 699.)¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿ 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5 lists the costs that are recoverable.¿ Section 1033.5 provides: “(1) Costs are allowable if incurred, whether or not paid. (2) Allowable costs shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation. (3) Allowable costs shall be reasonable in amount. (4) Items not mentioned in this section ... may be allowed ... in the court’s discretion.”¿ (Code Civ. Proc., §¿1033.5, subd. (c).)¿¿¿¿ 

 

C.    Application

 

Defendant argues $2,980.71 in claimed costs were not reasonably incurred.  Specifically, Defendant takes aim at costs Plaintiff incurred for filing fee and subpoena costs associated with PMK depositions of non-party South Bay Hyundai (the dealership), meal costs, and undocumented other costs at Item 13 of the costs memorandum.  

 

            The court will tax the filing fee and subpoena costs.  As explained in the moving papers, Plaintiff served notices of taking depositions of numerous employees of South Bay Hyundai in violation of the court’s Addendum to Case Management Conference Order (Song-Beverly Litigation).  Defendant appropriately objected to the depositions, Plaintiff filed a motion to compel, and the court denied the motion.  (See Takahashi Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. B; Minute Order 01/26/23.)  The costs of $1,214.25 in connection to the deposition subpoenas to South Bay Hyundai were not reasonably necessary.  In an apparent concession, Defendant states it withdraws $1,575 from the Attorney Services and Messenger Court Filings and Service section of the worksheet.  (See Opp., p. 9:15-16.)

 

            As to the other costs, Defendant seeks to tax $1,353.30 in relation to the unnecessary participation of an attorney, Kevin Chaffin, in this matter, and $413.16 in undocumented travel costs.  The $1,353.30 figure is taken directly from Judge Orozco’s order granting in part Defendant’s motion to tax costs in Plaintiff’s prior memorandum of costs.  (Minute Order, 01/26/23.)  Defendant fails to sufficiently explain why that same figure should be stricken from the costs memorandum before the court.  The court is similarly puzzled by the reference to $413.16 in travel costs.  The only figure that is not properly described is $301.07 in other costs.  The court will tax the costs bill in the unidentified sum of $301.07 as it is not, on its face reasonably related to the prosecution of this litigation.

 

D.    Conclusion

 

Accordingly, the motion to tax is GRANTED IN PART.  The court taxes Plaintiff’s costs memorandum in the sum of $1,876.07 ($1,575 and $301.07) for a total award of $59,645.12.

 

IV.       DISPOSITIONS

 

1. Plaintiff’s motion for attorney fees is Granted In Part.  Plaintiff is awarded $387,500.00 in attorney fees.

 

2.  Defendant’s motion to strike or tax costs is Granted In part.  Plaintiff is awarded $59,645.12 in costs.

           

Plaintiff to give notice, unless waived.

CASE:                         Sven Altmetz v. Ford Motor Company, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                 24STCV34475

 

 

DEFENDANT FORD MOTOR COMPANY’S MOTION TO COMPEL PLAINTIFF SVEN ALTMETZ’S DEPOSITION AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS AGAINST PLAINTIFF AND STRATEGIC LEGAL PRACTICES, APC, JOINTLY AND SEVERALLY IN THE AMOUNT OF $2,740.00

 

MOVING PARTY:               Defendant Ford Motor Company

 

RESPONDING PARTY:     Plaintiff Sven Altmetz

 

 

I.         BACKGROUND

 

            On December 30, 2024, Plaintiff, Sven Altmetz, commenced this Song-Beverly action against Defendants, Ford Motor Company (“Ford”) and South Bay Ford Lincoln. 

 

            On June 15, 2026, Ford filed this motion to compel Plaintiff to appear for deposition.  Ford requests sanctions against Plaintiff and his counsel of record.

 

On September 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.

 

On September 4, 2026, Ford filed a reply.

 

The motion may be moot.  Plaintiff states he has agreed to appear for deposition on September 16, 2026.  The court will hear from the parties whether Plaintiff appeared for the deposition.  The court proceeds to consider only the request for sanctions.  (See Do v. Superior Court (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1210.)

           

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

¿¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030 is a general statute authorizing the Court to impose discovery sanctions for “misuse of the discovery process,” which includes (without limitation) a variety of conduct such as: making, without substantial justification, an unmeritorious objection to discovery; making an evasive response to discovery; and unsuccessfully and without substantial justification making or opposing a motion to compel or limit discovery.¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010.)¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

If sanctions are sought, Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.040 requires that the notice specify the identity of the person against whom sanctions are sought and the type of sanction requested, that the motion be supported in the points and authorities, and the facts be set forth in a declaration supporting the amount of any monetary sanction.¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

“If a motion under [Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.450] subdivision (a) is granted, the court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) in favor of the party who noticed the deposition and against the deponent or the party with whom the deponent is affiliated, unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (g)(1).)¿¿¿ 

 

III.      DISCUSSION

 

            Here, the court finds monetary sanctions are warranted.  Ford properly noticed Plaintiff’s deposition on four occasions.  Plaintiff asserted only one valid objection to the deposition notices based on attorney illness.  Further, Plaintiff waited to provide alternative deposition dates two months after this motion was filed.  Accordingly, the court will impose sanctions against Plaintiff and his counsel of record in the requested sum of $2,176.75.

           

IV.       CONCLUSION

 

            The court is inclined to impose sanctions against Plaintiff and his counsel of record, jointly and severally, in the sum of $2,176.75 to be paid within 30 days of this order.  However, the court holds in abeyance this sanctions order pending a report on whether Plaintiff appeared for deposition.

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Brock T. Hammond, Department 407

 

 

HEARING DATE:     September 17, 2026                                      TRIAL DATE:  Not set

                                                          

CASE:                         Vivien Vanderlee v. Marcus & Millichap Real Estate Investment Services, Inc., et al.

 

CASE NO.:                 25STCV26125

 

 

DEFENDANT KB EXCHANGE PROPERTIES, LLC’S DEMURRER TO

PLAINTIFF’S SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT

 

I.         BACKGROUND

In 2022, plaintiff Vivien Vanderlee (“Plaintiff”) entered into an agreement with Marcus & Millichap Real Estate Investment Services, Inc. (“M&M), a real estate brokerage firm, to sell her Long Beach home (the “Property”).  Plaintiff alleges that M&M, by and through its former First Vice President, Ryan Gonzalez (“Gonzalez”), fraudulently induced her to sell the Property to purchase an unduly risky and illiquid out-of-state Delaware Statutory Trust (“DST”) property sponsored by KB Exchanged Properties, LLC (“KBE”). 

On September 24, 2025, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants, M&M, Gonzalez, and KBE, asserting causes of action for (1) financial elder abuse, (2) breach of fiduciary duty, (3) professional negligence, (4) constructive fraud, (5) violations of Cal. Corp. Code §§ 25504, 25504.01, and (6) violations of the Securities Act § 12(a)(2).

On February 5, 2026, KBE filed a demurrer to the FAC’s fifth and sixth causes of action.

            On May 29, 2026, the court sustained KBE’s demurrer with leave to amend.

            On June 11, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) against Defendants asserting causes of action for (1) financial elder abuse, (2) breach of fiduciary duty, (3) professional negligence, (4) constructive fraud, (5) violations of Cal. Corp. Code §§ 25401, 25501, 25504, 25504.01.  The fifth cause of action is the only claim alleged against KBE.

 

            On July 14, 2026, KBE filed a demurrer to the SAC.

 

            On September 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.

 

            At the time of this writing, no reply had been filed.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.¿ (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)¿ When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true.¿ (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.)¿ “Because a demurrer challenges defects on the face of the complaint, it can only refer to matters outside the pleading that are subject to judicial notice.”¿ (Arce ex rel. Arce v. Kaiser Found. Health Plan, Inc. (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 471, 556.)¿ 

 

III.      DISCUSSION

Previously, the court sustained KBE’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action on the ground it was time barred and uncertain because it was not clear whether the fifth cause of action against KBE was based liability under Corporations Code section 25501.[1]  Section 25401 makes it unlawful for “any person” to offer or sell a security in this state by means of a written communication containing an untrue statement of material fact or omitting a material fact necessary to make the statements made not misleading, and section 25501 makes “[a]ny person who violates Section 25401” liable to the purchaser for rescission or damages. Primary liability under section 25501 attaches to the actual seller, the person from whom the plaintiff purchased the security. (Apollo Capital Fund, LLC v. Roth Capital Partners, LLC (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 226, 252-254.)  The statute of limitations for violations of Section 25501 is “five years after the act or transaction constituting the violation or the expiration of two years after the discovery by the plaintiff of the facts constituting the violation, whichever shall first expire.” (§ 25506(b).)  Here, the SAC now clarifies that the source of KBE’s liability is Section 25501.  (SAC, ¶¶ 123-131.)  Accordingly, the court rejects KBE’s statute of limitations challenge to the fifth cause of action.[2]  The court must now resolve whether the SAC alleges facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. 

After review, the court finds the SAC states a claim.  The SAC alleges that the Private Placement Memorandum (“PPM”), which was prepared by KBE, concealed by omission the risk of the loan’s cash sweep provisions, failed to warn of loan-reserve risk factors, failed to warn of the potential for an uncured breach of the bank-loan covenants, failed to warn that the excess-cash-flow reserve.  (SAC ¶¶ 62-65, 70-71, 75, 79.)  The allegations sufficiently allege the legal effect of the PPM.  The absence of the PPM as an exhibit to the SAC does not render the fifth cause of action uncertain or substantively defective.  To the extent KBE argues it did not make material misstatements or material omissions is a fact intensive inquiry not suitable for disposition by demurrer.  (Insurance Underwriters Clearing House, Inc. v. Natomas Co. (1986) 184 Cal.App.3d 1520, 1526-1527.)[3]  The court rejects KBE’s remaining arguments for the reasons stated in Plaintiff’s opposition.  (Opp., pp. 14:16-18:7.)  The court incorporates those arguments as though set forth herein.

IV.       CONCLUSION

            The demurrer is Overruled.

 

            Defendant KB Exchange is ordered to serve and file its Answer to the SAC within 10 days.

 

            Defendants to give notice, unless waived.
