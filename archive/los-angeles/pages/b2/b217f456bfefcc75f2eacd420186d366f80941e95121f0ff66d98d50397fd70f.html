MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES

Los Angeles Superior Court Case # 24CHCV04745

 

Motion Filed: 2/23/26

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff Michael Forsythe (“Plaintiff”)

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendant General Motors, LLC (“Defendant”)

NOTICE: NOT OK (fails to specify the amount of attorney fees and costs sought)

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: An order granting Plaintiff his attorney fees in the amount of $31,350.60, and costs and expenses in the amount of $2,450.06.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: The motion is granted in part. Plaintiffs are awarded $24,884.79 in attorney fees and costs.

 

BACKGROUND

 

On 2/23/26, Plaintiff filed the instant motion. On 7/14/26, Defendant filed its opposition. On 7/20/26, Plaintiff filed his reply.

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.    Right to Recovery

 

An award of attorney fees is proper when authorized by contract, statute, or law. (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 1032, subd. (b); 1033.5, subd. (a)(10).) Here, the Song-Beverly Act authorizes a buyer “to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” (Civ. Code § 1794, subd. (d).)

 

Moreover, the judgment entered in the matter expressly states that “Plaintiffs attorney's fees, expenses and costs that have been reasonably incurred pursuant to California Civil Code § 1794(d) to be determined by the Court via noticed motion. For purposes of any such motion, GM agrees that Plaintiff is the prevailing party.” (12/16/25 Judgment ¶ 2.) Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that the instant motion is authorized by law.

 

B.     Attorney Fees Incurred

 

A buyer prevailing under the Song-Beverly Act may recover “reasonable” attorney fees and costs as determined by the Court. (Civ. Code § 1794, subd. (d).) “The burden is on the party seeking attorney fees to prove that the fees it seeks are reasonable.” (Gonzalez v. Santa Clara County Dept. of Social Services (2017) 9 Cal.App.5th 162, 169.) In determining a reasonable fee award, the Court begins with the lodestar method of calculation, i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.¿(Karton v. Ari Design & Construction, Inc. (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 734, 744; PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095–1096.)

 

Here, Plaintiff seeks to recover $26,125.50, encompassing 48.5 hours of his attorneys’ time, billed at the following rates:

1.      Counsel’s Hourly Rate

 

When determining a reasonable hourly rate, courts consider whether the stated rates “are within the range of reasonable rates charged by and judicially awarded [to] comparable attorneys for comparable work.” (Children's Hosp. & Med. Ctr. v. Bonta (2002) 97 Cal.App.4th 740, 783.)

 

Here, Plaintiff asserts that the rates charged by his attorneys are commensurate with the experience and skill of the attorneys involved, and have been approved by courts in the past. (Pl.’s Mot. 6:4–26; Kaufman Decl. ¶¶ 7–16.) The Court notes that Defendant does not challenge the reasonableness of Plaintiff’s counsel’s hourly billing rates.

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that Plaintiff has sufficiently shown that his attorneys’ hourly billing rates are commensurate with their experience, and fall within the range of reasonable rates charged by attorneys in similar cases. Accordingly, the Court declines to reduce Plaintiff’s counsel’s hourly billing rates.

 

2.      Hours Expended

 

Plaintiff seeks to recover attorney fees for the 48.5 hours expended by his attorneys on this matter, totaling $26,125.50. Plaintiff proffers an itemized list of time entries detailing his attorneys’ work on this matter. (Ex. 1 to Kaufman Decl.) Defendant argues that “a close examination of Counsel’s activities reveals several forms of bill padding that GM has seen repeated due to Counsel’s heavy reliance on templates at every phase of litigation.” (Def.’s Opp. 4:6–8.) Specifically, Defendant challenges Plaintiff’s counsel’s time spent on (1) pre-litigation work; (2) reviewing Defendant’s answer and objections; (3) discovery efforts, including drafting requests and responses, reviewing Defendant’s responses and initial disclosures, and deposing Defendant’s Person Most Qualified (“PMQ”); (4) the vehicle surrender; (3) case management; and (5) the instant fee motion.

 

a.                  Pre-Litigation Work

 

Defendant argues that the 3 hours “spent evaluating a potential client prior to engagement are business expenses routinely treated as overhead. Such time is not properly billed (retroactively) to one’s client, and therefore not properly billed to GM.” (Def.’s Opp. 4:15–18.) Defendant “therefore, requests that Counsel’s time be reduced by 2.0 hours ($1,190.00).” (Id. at 4:20–21.)

 

In reply, Plaintiff argues that “these tasks are part of commencing and pursuing the litigation and are recoverable. Indeed, Civ. Code § 1794(d) of the SBA determines the recoverable attorney’s fees, which include those that are ‘reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.’” (Pl.’s Reply 3:21–24.) The Court agrees, and therefore declines to reduce these time entries.

 

b.                  Defendant’s Answer, Deposition Notice Objections

 

Defendant further argues that the 1.8 hours billed by Mr. Kaufman for reviewing Defendant’s answer and objections to Plaintiff’s deposition notices are improperly blockbilled and excessive because “GM, like Counsel, uses the same Answer and objections, so Counsel would not need to spend almost two hours here. Given the templated nature of the work, 15 minutes should be enough.” (Def.’s Opp. 4:22–28.) Defendant “requests a reduction of 1.5 hours ($773.50).” (Id. at 4:28.)

 

In reply, Plaintiff argues that “the entries are not block billed and identify the specific amounts of time for each task.” (Pl.’s Reply 4:8.) Plaintiff further argues that “the remaining 1.0 hour of time (which GM falsely states as reviewing the answer and objections) is for correspondence to the client, preparing a C.C.P. § 2016 demand, and meeting and conferring with GM regarding its objections to the PMQ deposition notice.” (Id. at 4:9–12.)

Based on the foregoing, the Court declines to reduce these time entries.

 

c.                   Discovery

 

Defendant further argues that the “like every other aspect of discovery in this case—neither Plaintiff’s requests nor GM’s responses to those requests were novel nor varied significantly from prior cases. Indeed, Plaintiff’s requests and GM’s responses vary little from case to case.” (Def.’s Opp. 5:3–6.) Defendant challenges the 6.5 hours billed by Plaintiff’s counsel in relation to reviewing Defendant’s discovery responses, and drafting Plaintiff’s discovery responses. (Id. at 5:1–17.) Defendant further argues that “GM’s initial disclosures are nearly the same in every case, so Counsel should not need an hour to review them.” (Id. at 5:20–21.)

 

Defendant also argues that the 3.9 hours billed in relation of the deposition of Defendant’s PMQ “is excessive given the routine and limited scope of GM’s document production and the templated nature of the deposition notice categories. It is unreasonable for Counsel to spend an unknown amount of time reviewing already familiar materials and preparing an outline, given Plaintiff’s deposition notice covered only standard topics repeated from similar cases.” (Id. at 5:24–6:2.)

 

In reply, Plaintiff argues that “while objections to requests overlap between cases, the discovery responses themselves plainly vary by client. In sum, this time was reasonably incurred and is recoverable.” (Pl.’s Reply 5:6–8.) “Further, GM’s assertion that its initial disclosures are similar in other cases is irrelevant, as Plaintiff’s counsel must still review the documents.” (Id. at 5:12–14.) As to the time incurred in relation to the deposition of Defendant’s PMQ, Plaintiff maintains that “the time incurred was reasonable, and GM’s conclusory, arbitrary request should be rejected.” (Id. at 5:25.)

 

Based on the foregoing, and noting the template nature of discovery efforts in Song-Beverly cases, the Court therefore reduces the time actually incurred for these tasks by 5.9 hours (by JK), for a total reduction of $3,510.50.

 

d.                  Vehicle Surrender

 

Defendant further argues that 8 hours billed by Plaintiff’s counsel (5.4 hours by JK, 2.6 hours by ML) for coordinating the post-settlement surrender of the subject vehicle and attending the surrender should be struck because “GM should not be required to reimburse unnecessary fees that were not incurred to litigation Plaintiff’s case, but rather to further pad Counsel’s time.” (Def.’s Opp. 6:5–17.)

 

In reply, Plaintiff argues that “the total time [Mr. Kaufman] billed [for these tasks] is 1.7 hours, and not 5.4 hours.” (Pl.’s Reply 6:5–7.) Plaintiff further argues that “these tasks are part of pursuing and completing a lemon law claim and are therefore recoverable.” (Id. at 6:9–10.) Plaintiff asserts that “because the settlement funds were being exchanged at the time of the surrender, Plaintiff’s associate attended to ensure compliance and to protect the client’s interests due to GM’s violation of the parties’ agreement.” (Id. at 6:26–7:2.)

 

The Court finds that the total amount of time billed in connection with coordinating and attending the vehicle surrender is 6.8 hours (2.3 hours by JK, 1.9 hours by ML, and 2.6 hours by CRH). The Court agrees with Plaintiff that these tasks were reasonably incurred as part of the conclusion of the instant litigation, and therefore declines to strike these time entries.

 

 

e.                   Case Management

 

Defendant further argues that it “should not be billed for various clerical or internal administrative performed by the firm paralegal, Ms. Lopez, for a total of 6.0 hours ($1,170.00).” (Def.’s Opp. 6:18–20.) In reply, Plaintiff argues that “GM makes a meritless and conclusory challenge to entries ‘throughout,’ but GM does not even identify the dates, descriptions, or specific amounts of the entries being challenged. Moreover, one cannot even confirm the veracity of the amount of time challenged under these circumstances. As such, Plaintiff cannot even fully address GM’s vague assertion.” (Pl.’s Reply 7:6–9.) The Court agrees, and therefore declines to strike or tax any of Plaintiff’s counsel’s time entries on this basis.

 

f.                    Fee Motion

 

Defendant further argues that Plaintiff “should not get full credit for the 6.5 hours ($3,997.50) that Counsel claims that it has spent or will spend on its fee motion.” (Def.’s Opp. 6:26–27.) “GM submits that 1.0 hour to prepare the fee motion, 1.0 hour to prepare a reply brief, and 1.0 hour to attend the hearing will adequately compensate Counsel’s efforts, and thus asks for a reduction of 3.5 hours ($2,152.50).” (Id. at 7:1–4.)

 

In reply, Plaintiff clarifies that “the invoice plainly demonstrates that Plaintiff's counsel billed 2.5 hours on 2/17/26 to prepare the fee motion and supporting documents, billed 1.0 hour to review the opposition and prepare a reply on 7/15/26, and 1.0 hour to prepare for and attend the hearing on 7/27/26. This totals 4.5 hours, and not 6.5 hours.” (Pl.’s Reply 7:19–22.) Plaintiff further asserts that “the actual amount of time on these tasks was significantly more than what was billed and has already been reduced.” (Id. at 8:1–2.) “Despite incurring significantly more time than what was billed, Plaintiff maintains the same fee request.” (Id. at 8:5–7, citing Supp. Decl. of Joseph A. Kaufman ¶ 2.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court declines to reduce the time entries relating to the instant fee motion.

 

C.    Multiplier

 

“Once the lodestar has been calculated, the second step is to apply any positive or negative multipliers. The purpose of such adjustment is to fix a fee at the fair market value for the particular action. In effect, the court determines, retrospectively, whether the litigation involved a contingent risk or required extraordinary legal skill justifying augmentation of the unadorned lodestar in order to approximate the fair market rate for such services.” (Santana v. FCA US, LLC (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 334, 351 [internal quotations omitted].) “Perhaps the most common multiplier applied, at least where a plaintiff prevails, is a modifier for the contingent nature of the representation.” (Ibid.) Whether a multiplier is applied to adjust the lodestar amount based on the contingent nature and risk associated with the action and other factors, such as degree of skill required and ultimate success achieved is within the discretion of the trial court.

 

Here, Plaintiff argues that he is entitled to recover a 1.2 multiplier enhancement because they “obtained a highly favorable result” and “Plaintiff's counsel vigorously pursued Plaintiff's case despite the potential for its efforts to go uncompensated.” (Pl.’s Mot. 9:21–10:11.) Defendant argues in opposition that no multiplier enhancement is warranted because “The vast majority of Counsel’s work is dedicated to breach-of-warranty actions under Song-Beverly, for which the issues are virtually identical from case to case.” (Def.’s Opp. 7:25–27.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that a multiplier enhancement is not warranted in this case. While Plaintiff ultimately prevailed, the action appears to have been a relatively straightforward Song-Beverly case. As such, the Court finds the lodestar fee award sufficient to compensate Plaintiff’s counsel.

 

D.    Costs Incurred

 

Plaintiff asserts that he incurred costs and expenses totaling $2,450.06. (Pl.’s Mot. 12:6–11.) The Court notes that Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs lists that the total amount of costs to be recovered is $2,450.10. (1/23/26 Mem. of Costs.) However, the Court finds that the total sum of costs outlined in Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs equals $2,450.05. The Court addresses its analysis of Plaintiff’s cost award in its separate ruling on Defendant’s motion to tax costs, and finds it reasonable to award Plaintiff $2,269.79 in costs.

MOTION TO STRIKE/TAX COSTS

Los Angeles Superior Court Case # 24CHCV04745

 

Motion filed: 2/6/26

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant General Motors, LLC (“Defendant”)

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff Michael Forsythe (“Plaintiff”)

NOTICE: OK¿¿¿ 

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: An order striking or taxing Plaintiffs’ Memorandum of Costs.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: The motion is granted in part. The Court taxes $180.26 from Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs.[1]

 

BACKGROUND

 

On 2/6/26, Defendant filed the instant motion. On 7/14/26, Plaintiff filed his opposition. On 7/20/26, Defendant filed its reply.

 

ANALYSIS

 

A prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs of suit in any action or proceeding. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1032, subd. (b).) Costs recoverable under Section 1032 are restricted to those that are both reasonable in amount and reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1033.5, subd. (c)(2)–(3).)

 

A.    Reasonableness and Necessity

 

A cost award may consist only of allowable costs which are “reasonable in amount” and “reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1033.5, subd. (c)(2)–(3).) In a Song-Beverly action, the prevailing “buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” (Civ. Code § 1794, subd. (d).)

 

If the charges listed on a submitted memorandum of costs appear to be proper charges on their face, the verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of their propriety, and the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show they were not reasonable or necessary. (Ladas v. California State Auto. Ass'n (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 774–776.)

 

Here, Defendant seeks to tax $395.57 from Plaintiff’s memorandum of costs, relating to filing fees, subpoena costs, and other costs. (Def.’s Mot. 4:23–5:10.)

 

1.      Filing Fees

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff improperly seeks $135.81 in filing fees relating to Plaintiff’s motion for attorney fees, his reply to Defendant’s opposition, and the request for dismissal, because “costs are only allowed if actually incurred; Counsel has spent nothing on future filings. These unnecessary costs should be disallowed. (Id. at 4:23–26.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that he “filed his Motion for Attorney’s fees on July 2, 2026, and incurred a $86.57 filing fee. … Further, Plaintiff will incur $24.62 in filing fees for his reply in support of Motion for Attorney’s Fees, and $24.62 for filing a request for dismissal.” (Pl.’s Opp. 6:4–7, citing Decl. of Joseph A. Kaufman ¶¶ 2–3.) “In the unlikely event that these filings do not occur and GM pays for these costs, Plaintiff’s counsel will reimburse GM the $49.24.” (Id. at 6:8–9.)

 

In reply, Defendant maintains that “a cost claimed before it is incurred is not properly recoverable, and Counsel’s belated filing does not cure a Memorandum that demanded the fee prospectively.” (Def.’s Reply 4:18–19.) Defendant further argues that “an anticipated future cost is not a cost ‘incurred.’ Counsel’s offer to ‘reimburse GM’ in the event a filing never occurs only underscores that these costs are contingent and unincurred.” (Id. at 4:22–24.) The Court agrees. Accordingly, the Court taxes $135.81 from Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs for these costs that were not yet incurred on the date of the filing.

 

2.      Subpoena Costs

 

Defendant further argues that Plaintiff improperly seeks $214.22 in costs for “record subpoenas” to third-party dealerships Simi Valley Chevrolet and Rydell Automotive Group. Defendant asserts that “these costs were optional and unnecessary as Plaintiff should have already been in possession of the documents sought (e.g. sales documents and repair orders). GM should not be required to reimburse costs incurred by Plaintiff to obtain documents he failed to preserve.” (Def.’s Mot. 5:2–4.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that “in opposing these costs, GM again relies on the wrong legal standard of Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 1033.5 and erroneously applies a heightened standard instead of the applicable language of the SBA and the Judgment. GM attempts to argue that these costs were unnecessary, but Plaintiff required the complete set of records from the servicing dealerships to pursue his case.” (Pl.’s Opp. 6:12–16.)

 

The Court agrees with Plaintiff that these expenses were reasonably incurred, and therefore declines to tax the costs thereof.

 

3.      Other Costs

 

Defendant further argues that Plaintiff improperly seeks $45.54 in costs for postage and photocopies because “serving documents on opposing counsel and copying documents are inherent costs of doing business. Moreover, such costs are unnecessary when service of discovery via electronic means is available.” (Def.’s Mot. 5:7–9.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the Song-Beverly Act “does not limit expenses for postage or photocopying. Accordingly, under [Civil Code] Section 1794(d), these were legitimate activities in order to pursue the case, were reasonably incurred, and should not be taxed.” (Pl.’s Opp. 6:23–25.)

 

In reply, Defendant maintains that “printing and serving documents on opposing counsel is overhead that every litigant bears.” (Def.’s Reply 5:22–23.) The Court agrees, and further notes that costs for “postage, telephone, and photocopying charges, except for exhibits” are not recoverable unless expressly authorized by law. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1033.5, subd. (b)(3).) Accordingly, the Court taxes $44.45 from Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs for non-recoverable postage and photocopying costs.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The motion is granted in part. The Court taxes $180.26 from Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs.

 


[1] The Court notes that Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs lists that the total amount of costs to be recovered is $2,450.10. (1/23/26 Mem. of Costs.) However, the Court finds that the total sum of costs outlined in Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs equals $2,450.05.

DEMURRER

Los Angeles Superior Court Case # 25CHCV02595

 

Demurrer filed: 5/27/26

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant County of Los Angeles (“Defendant”)

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiffs Robyn Oknyansky; and Mira Oknyansky, a minor by and through her guardian ad litem, Mark Oknyansky (collectively, “Plaintiffs”)

NOTICE: OK

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: Defendant demurs against Plaintiffs’ entire first amended complaint (“FAC”).

 

TENTATIVE RULING: The demurrer is overruled. Defendant is ordered to file its answer to Plaintiffs’ complaint within 20 days.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE: Defendant’s request for judicial notice is denied.

 

BACKGROUND

 

This is a premises liability action in which Plaintiffs allege that on 7/27/23, while visiting the Castaic Regional Sports Complex Aquatic Center (the “Aquatic Center”) located 31350 Castaic Rd., Castaic, CA 91384, Plaintiff “Robyn, while holding Mira, … stepped on the grate [surrounding a pool at the Aquatic Center], it broke, and her foot went through the grate. This caused her to lose balance, and she dropped Mira onto the hard concrete floor.” (FAC ¶¶ 7–14.) Plaintiffs allege that Defendant owned and operated the Aquatic Center. (Id. at ¶ 7.)

 

On 7/22/25, Plaintiffs filed their original complaint, alleging against Defendant a sole cause of action for Dangerous Condition of Public Property. On 10/30/25, Defendant filed its answer. On 4/27/26, after the Court granted Defendant’s motion for judgment on the pleadings, Plaintiffs filed their FAC, alleging the same sole cause of action against Defendant.

 

On 5/27/26, Defendant filed the instant demurrer. On 7/14/26, Plaintiffs filed their opposition. On 7/20/26, Defendant filed its reply.

//

//

ANALYSIS

 

As a general matter, a party may respond to a pleading against it by demurrer on the basis of any single or combination of eight enumerated grounds, including that “the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action” and is uncertain, meaning “ambiguous and unintelligible.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subds. (e) and (f).) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿

 

“A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) 

 

Here, Defendant¿demurs to Plaintiff’s entire complaint on the basis that she fails¿to allege facts sufficient to state any of the causes of action therein, rendering the complaint fatally uncertain.

 

A.    Meet-and-Confer 

 

Before filing its demurrer, “the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41, subd. (a).) “The demurring party shall file and serve with the demurrer a declaration stating either of the following: (A) The means by which the demurring party met and conferred with the party who filed the pleading subject to demurrer, and that the parties did not reach an agreement resolving the objections raised in the demurrer. (B) That the party who filed the pleading subject to demurrer failed to respond to the meet and confer request of the demurring party or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith.” (Id. at subd. (a)(3).)

 

Here, Defendant’s counsel declares that on 5/18/26 and 5/21/26, she attempted to reach Plaintiffs’ counsel to meet and confer regarding the issues raised in the instant demurrer, but received no response (Decl. of Melanie O. Paulick ¶¶ 8–9.) Therefore, the Court finds that counsel has satisfied the preliminary meet and confer requirements of Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a).

 

A.    Statute of Limitations

 

Actions brought against government entities are subject to the Government Claims Act, which typically requires written claims for damages against the public entity to be timely presented to that entity for resolution prior to filing suit. (Gov. Code § 900 et seq.; Gov. Code § 911.2, subd. (a).) “A claim relating to a cause of action for death or for injury to person or to personal property … shall be presented as provided in Article 2 (commencing with Section 915) not later than six months after the accrual of the cause of action.” (Gov. Code § 911.2, subd. (a).)

 

A plaintiff who has presented her government claim must commence her lawsuit against the public entity within six months of the written notice of the state’s action taken, or inaction deemed as rejection, as to the claim. (Gov. Code § 945.6, subd. (a)(1).) If no such written notice was given, the suit must be filed within two years from the accrual of the cause of action. (Id. at subd. (a)(2).)

 

Here, the Court previously found that in their original complaint, “Plaintiffs have failed to allege compliance with Government Code section 911.2, as they allege that they submitted claims to Defendant on or before 11/7/24, nearly 10 months after the deadline to do so.” (3/25/26 Min. Order, pp. 3–4.) In their FAC, Plaintiffs allege that they “submitted claims to the County on or before November 7, 2023,” and that because “Plaintiffs received no written rejection from the County and so pursuant to Government Code section 913, Plaintiff had two years from the date of loss to file this Complaint.” (FAC ¶¶ 22, 24.)

 

Defendant again asserts that it “mailed claim rejection notices to Plaintiffs on February 5, 2024, which triggered the six-month statute of limitations” set forth under Code of Civil Procedure section 945.6, subdivision (a)(1). (Dem. 4:17–18, citing Exs. C–D to RJN.) Defendant therefore argues that “despite Plaintiffs’ assertions that they did not receive notice of their claim rejections, the six-month period begins to run from the date of the deposit of the rejection notice in the mail and therefore their lawsuit is time-barred pursuant to Gov. Code § 945.6(a)(1).” (Id. at 4:25–28.)

 

Defendant argues that “the court is not required to accept as true those allegations which contradict facts that may be judicially noticed by the court.” (Id. at 6:14–16, citing Code Civ. Proc. § 430.30, subd. (a) [“When any ground for objection to a complaint, cross-complaint, or answer appears on the face thereof, or from any matter of which the court is required to or may take judicial notice, the objection on that ground may be taken by a demurrer to the pleading.”].) Defendant maintains that here, “it is immaterial that Plaintiffs or their attorney did not receive the notice of rejection and did not know that Plaintiffs’ claims had been rejected. ‘If the public entity deposits written notice of rejection in the mail, the six-month limitations period within which to file suit applies regardless of whether notice is actually received.’” (Id. at 7:7–14, quoting Dowell v. County of Contra Costa (1985) 173 Cal.App.3d 896, 901.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that “Defendants’ sole objection to the Complaint is a claim to have deposited a rejection letter after the time to accept or reject the claim had expired - a fact outside of the Amended Complaint itself and one that Plaintiffs dispute. The County never sent any such letter, and it fails to provide adequate evidence of such.” (Pls.’ Opp. 2:16–19.) Plaintiffs argue that “the County seeks to insert disputed facts into the complaint based on unauthenticated documents external to the Complaint itself. The only support for these additional facts is a self-serving and inappropriate attorney declaration. There is no declaration of the person who allegedly drafted the letters or even from the County.” (Id. at 3:9–13.) Plaintiffs therefore assert that “the Court cannot take judicial notice of documents which have not been authenticated, for which the foundation has not been laid, and where the authenticity of the documents is in dispute.” (Id. at 5:5–7.)

 

In reply, Defendant argues that “here, this Court may, as it determined previously in Defendant County’s request for judicial notice submitted with this Court in conjunction with its Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings, take judicial notice of the existence of the claim rejection letters, thereby establishing that Plaintiffs’ operative complaint fails to establish a cause of action against it. Plaintiffs’ argument that the rejection letters were not sent by the County but rather by third parties allegedly acting on behalf of the County is unavailing, as the rejection letters were sent by the County’s administrative agency responsible for processing claims brought against it under the Tort Claims Act.” (Reply 6:11–17.)

 

Based on Plaintiffs’ prior authentication objections to the same documents, the Court previously declined “to consider the contents of Defendant’s exhibits at the pleading stage.” (3/25/26 Min. Order, p. 3.) Here, the Court again declines to take judicial notice of the truth of the matters contained in the purported notices of rejection of Plaintiffs’ claims, and agrees with Plaintiffs that these additional facts are disputed and lack sufficient foundation at the pleading stage. The Court acknowledges the inconsistency between the instant ruling and its previous ruling granting Defendant’s request for judicial notice of the existence of Plaintiffs’ government claims for damages and Defendant’s notices of rejection thereof. However, “a court cannot by means of judicial notice convert a demurrer into an incomplete evidentiary hearing in which the demurring party can present documentary evidence and the opposing party is bound by what that evidence appears to show.” (Fremont Indemnity Co. v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 115.)

 

The Court finds that factual issue of whether the six-month statute of limitations set forth by Code of Civil Procedure section 945.6, subdivision (a)(1) was triggered is improper for resolution at the pleading stage. (Dowell, 173 Cal.App.3d at 900.) Accordingly, the demurrer is overruled.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The demurrer is overruled. Defendant to file its answer to Plaintiffs’ complaint within 20 days.
