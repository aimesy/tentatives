BILL HOWELL AND DEBORAH HOWELL, Trustees of the Howell Family Trust dated July 2, 2013,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

OLIVIA CORBETT aka OLIVIA CORVETT aka OLIVIA JADE CORVETT aka OLIVIA JANE CORBETT aka HANIA, et al.,

                        Defendants.

  Case No.:  24SMCV05364

  Hearing Date:  7/29/26

  Trial Date:  7/26/27

 [TENTATIVE] RULING RE:

MOTION FOR ATTORNEY’S FEES

 

Background

 

            Plaintiffs leased residential property to Defendant Olivia Corbett.  Plaintiffs received notification from the Los Angeles County Department of Regional Planning in March 2022 indicating that the unit leased to Defendant was unpermitted and in violation of an

 applicable zoning ordinance.  Plaintiffs were ordered to vacate and remove the unpermitted development.  Plaintiffs issued Notice to Defendant but Defendant refused to leave to premises and ceased paying rent when she discovered the unit was unpermitted.  Plaintiffs were forced to file an unlawful detainer action against Defendant that was ultimately resolved by a stipulated judgment for possession only.  Plaintiffs filed this action thereafter to collect holdover rent for the time Defendant occupied the unpermitted unit after being notified to leave.

            Plaintiffs filed a complaint on November 1, 2024 alleging (1) quantum meruit; (2) common counts; and (3) unjust enrichment. 

 

            On December 5, 2026, Defendant filed an answer to the complaint in pro per against Plaintiffs.  On February 26, 2026, Defendant filed a cross-complaint after obtaining leave of court to do so on January 23, 2026.  Defendant alleged Plaintiffs/Cross-Defendants failed to maintain the property in a habitable condition, including that the property was unpermitted, suffered from plumbing issues, contained nonoperational appliances and suffered from a rat infestation.  Defendant’s cross-complaint alleges (1) breach of the implied warranty of habitability, (2) breach of the covenant of quiet enjoyment, (3) tenant harassment, (4) retaliation, (5) constructive eviction, (6) negligence, (7) intentional infliction of emotional distress, (8) unfair, unlawful and deceptive business act or practice and (9) fraud.

            On March 30, 2026, Plaintiffs/Cross-Defendants (“Cross-Defendants”) filed an answer to Defendant’s cross-complaint.  On April 14, 2026, Cross-Defendants filed a SLAPP Motion to Defendant’s cross-complaint.  On May 12, 2026, the Court granted Cross-Defendants’ SLAPP Motion. 

            On June 1, 2026, Cross-Defendants filed a motion for attorney’s fees as prevailing parties on the SLAPP Motion.  As of July 27, 2026, no opposition or reply has been filed. 

 

Legal Standard

 

The anti-SLAPP statute provides that “in any action subject to subdivision (b), a prevailing defendant on a special motion to strike shall be entitled to recover his or her attorney’s fees and costs.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(c)(1).) “[A]ny SLAPP defendant who brings a successful motion to strike is entitled to mandatory attorney fees.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1131.)

A prevailing party on an anti-SLAPP motion is entitled to an award of attorney fees and costs only for the anti-SLAPP motion itself, not for the entire action.  (Lafayette Morehouse, Inc. v. Chronicle Publishing Co. (1995) 39 Cal.App.4th 1379, 1381, 1383.)  This holding has been slightly expanded, to the extent that it has been interpreted to mean that a prevailing party is entitled to fees “incurred in connection with” the anti-SLAPP motion.  (Wanland v. Law Offices of Mastagni, Holstedt & Chiurazzi (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 15, 21.) 

The fee award is against the losing plaintiff, not the losing plaintiff's attorney. Plaintiff's counsel is not a party to the action and thus not subject to a fee award under CCP § 425.16. (Moore v. Kaufman (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 604, 614.)

An award of fees may also include “the fees incurred in enforcing the right to mandatory fees under Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16.” (Id. at p. 1141.) “It is well established that the amount of an attorney fee award under the anti-SLAPP statute is computed by the trial court in accordance with the familiar lodestar method.” (569 East County Boulevard LLC v. Backcountry Against the Dump, Inc. (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 426, 432 [internal quotations omitted].) Under the lodestar method, the court tabulates the attorney fee lodestar by multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended by the reasonable hourly rate prevailing in the community for similar work.” (Ibid.)

With regard to the number of hours reasonably expended, “the verified time statements of the attorneys, as officers of the court, are entitled to credence in the absence of a clear indication the records are erroneous.” (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 396.) In determining the reasonable hourly rate, the “burden is on the successful party to prove the appropriate market rate to be used in calculating the lodestar.” (MBNA America Bank, N.A. v. Gorman (2006) 147 Cal.App.4th Supp. 1, 13.) The trial court may reduce the award where the fee request appears unreasonably inflated, such as where the attorneys’ efforts are unorganized or duplicative. (Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 635, fn. 21.)

 

 

Analysis

 

            Cross-Defendants request attorney’s fees in the amount of $31,899 and costs in the amount of $2,880.95.  Cross-Defendants are entitled to attorney’s fees and costs pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16 subdivision (c) as prevailing parties.  Cross-Complainant did not file an opposition.

            Cross-Defendants’ fee request is based on the following work:

SLAPP Motion

·       Isaac Aba—34.5 hours @ $500/hr to prepare the SLAPP Motion and the reply to the opposition to the SLAPP Motion (Motion, Aba Dec., ¶17.)

·       Eddy Johnson—8.8 hours @ $900/hr to prepare the SLAPP Motion and the reply to the opposition to the SLAPP Motion (Motion, Johnson Dec., ¶5.) 

SLAPP Fee Motion

·       Isaac Aba—6.2 hours @ $500/hr (Motion, Aba Dec., ¶29.) 

·       Eddy Johnson--.8 hours @ $900/hr (Motion, Johnson Dec., ¶5.) 

·       Estimated additional 3 hours to prepare reply to opposition

·       Estimate 2 hours for oral argument of fee motion

Cross-Defendants’ request for 43.3 hours in fees for preparation of the SLAPP Motion is reasonable given the legal and factual complexity of the matter.  Based on the Court’s familiarity with fee requests on successful SLAPP motions, counsel’s requested market rate is commensurate with the market rate for similar legal services in Los Angeles.  The hourly rates are also reasonable given counsel’s educational and professional experience.  (Motion, Aba Dec., ¶¶3-16; Johnson Dec., ¶¶2-5.) 

However, Cross-Complainant did not file an opposition.  The additional 3 hours estimated for reply preparation is therefore denied.  The additional 2 hours estimated for oral argument is reduced to 1 hour given the lack of any opposition. 

The Court awards Cross-Complainants $20,850 for the work of Isaac Aba and $8,640 for the work of Edward Johnson on the SLAPP Motion and the Fee Motion for a total of $29,490.

Cross-Complainants also request costs in the amount of $2,880.95.  Based on the memorandum of costs filed on May 20, 2026, Cross-Defendants have included general photocopying charges for the SLAPP motion, e.g. 50 cents per page for the motion, the reply and even the opposition papers, under Item 13 for “Models, enlargements, and photocopies of exhibits.”  (Memorandum of Costs, Worksheet, Attachment A, Section 13.)  General photocopying charges, not attributable to exhibits reasonably helpful to the trier of fact, are expressly disallowed.  (Code of Civ. Proc., §1033.5, subd. (a)(13) and (b)(3).)  There is also no basis for Cross-Defendants to recover the costs of Cross-Complainant’s opposition papers.  As such, the request for costs is reduced by $319 and granted in the amount of $2561.95.

 

Conclusion

            Cross-Complainants’ Motion for Attorney’s Fees and Costs pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16 subdivision (c) is GRANTED in the amount of $29,490 in fees and $2,561.95.

WASABI TECHNOLOGIES LLC,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

PACKETFABRIC, INC., et al.

                        Defendants.

  Case No.:  25SMCV06236

  Hearing Date:  07/29//26

  Trial Date:  None

 [TENTATIVE] RULING RE:

Motion for Relief Concerning Default Judgment

 

Background

 

In this breach of contract action, Plaintiff Wasabi Technologies LLC (Plaintiff) alleges that, pursuant to a written agreement with Defendant PacketFabric, Inc. (Defendant), it granted Defendant a license to integrate Plaintiff’s storage service into Defendant’s intellectual property, technology, and services, but Defendant breached the agreement by failing to pay $317,702.75. 

 

On December 2, 2025, Plaintiff filed this action against Defendant and Does 1 to 10, asserting breach of contract.

 

On February 4, 2026, Plaintiff filed Proof of Service of Summons & Complaint, stating that a registered California process server served Defendant on December 22, 2025, at CSC-Lawyers Incorporating Service, 2710 Gateway Oaks Drive, Suite 150N, Sacramento, CA 95833.

 

Default was entered against Defendant on February 4, 2026.

 

On February 13, 2026, Plaintiff filed a request for entry of court judgment and dismissed Does 1 to 10.

 

On May 28, 2026, the Court granted the request for entry of default judgment in the amount of $379,261.33, consisting of damages of $317,702.75, interest of $55,880.87, costs of $610.68, and attorney fees of $5,067.03.

 

On June 5, 2026, Writ of Execution (Money Judgment) was issued against Defendant.

 

On June 22, 2026, Defendant filed the instant “Motion for Relief Concerning Default Judgment,” seeking an order (1) relieving it from default judgment pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473 (“Section 473”), and/or alternatively (2) striking attorney’s fees and interest amounts that Plaintiff was not entitled to.

 

On June 25, 2026, the Court granted Defendant’s ex parte application to stay enforcement of the writ of execution pending the Court’s ruling on this motion.

 

On July 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed its opposition, mainly arguing that (1) the motion is untimely and Defendant has not demonstrated grounds for relief under Section 473; and (2) Plaintiff will be prejudiced because if the motion is granted, funds subject to the writ of execution may be transferred out of Plaintiff’s reach.

 

On July 22, 2026, Defendant filed its reply.  

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice of Defendant’s Statements of Information dated August 4, 2025, and July 7, 2026, from the California Secretary of State website is granted. (Evid. Code, 452, subd. (c) [allowing judicial notice of official acts of the executive department of the state]; see e.g. Jones v. Goodman (2020) 57 Cal.App.5th 521, 529, fn. 6 [“We take judicial notice of the articles of incorporation for Creative Genius, Inc., from the California Secretary of State’s website”]; but see also Searles Valley Minerals Operations, Inc. v. State Bd. of Equalization (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 514, 519 [“although it might be appropriate to take judicial notice of the existence of [government] websites, the same is not true of their factual content” (italics removed)].)

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

Plaintiff’s objections to the declaration of Chad Milam (“Milam”), Defendant’s Chief Executive Officer, Board Member, and Corporate Secretary are overruled.

 

Legal Standard

 

“The trial court has discretion under section 473(b) on a showing of mistake, inadvertence, surprise or excusable neglect to grant relief from a judgment, dismissal or other order based on its evaluation of the nature of the mistake or error alleged and the justification proffered for the conduct that occurred. ‘The general underlying purpose of section 473(b) is to promote the determination of actions on their merits.’ [Citations.]” (Austin v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 918, 928 (Austin) [footnote omitted].)

 

“A party seeking relief under section 473(b) must file the motion within a reasonable time but not longer than six months after the judgment or dismissal has been entered. This six-month time limitation is jurisdictional; the court has no power to grant relief under section 473 once the time has lapsed.” (Austin, supra, 244 Cal.App.4th at p. 928.)

 

When a motion to set aside judgment is brought within the six-month limit of Section 473(b), like in this case, “the moving party must establish [his] motion was made ‘within a reasonable time.’ (§ 473(b).)” (Arega v. Bay Area Rapid Transit Dist. (2022) 83 Cal.App.5th 308, 316.) “What constitutes a ‘reasonable time’ depends on the circumstances of each case ‘but definitively requires a showing of diligence in making the motion after the discovery of the default.’ [Citation.] ‘Whether a party has acted diligently is a factual question for the trial court’ [citation], which exercises a great deal of discretion in making that determination.” (Ibid. [emphasis added].) Here, Defendant filed the motion only nine (9) days after the default was entered, showing diligence. Therefore, the Court declines Plaintiff’s request to deny the motion on the ground of diligence.

 

Analysis

 

A. Whether Defendant Has Shown Mistake, Inadvertence, and Excusable Neglect Within the Meaning of Section 473(b)

 

As an initial matter, the Court finds that Defendant’s motion is timely as it was brought within six months of the entry of default (on February 4, 2026), and default judgment (on February 13, 2026). Plaintiff’s argument that Defendant did not act diligently in bringing the motion is unpersuasive. Milam, Defendant’s CEO, testifies that he did not learn about the existence of the Complaint until receiving a FedEx package containing all mail from the company’s headquarters’ mailbox in or around May 2026. (Milam Decl., ¶ 8.) Defendant filed the motion approximately a month later, on June 22, 2026, showing diligence.

 

“The terms mistake, inadvertence, surprise, and excusable neglect warranting relief under section 473(b) are defined as follows: ‘Mistake is not a ground for relief under section 473, subdivision (b), when “the court finds that the ‘mistake’ is simply the result of professional incompetence, general ignorance of the law, or unjustifiable negligence in discovering the law....” [Citation.] Further, “[t]he term ‘surprise,’ as used in section 473, refers to ‘“some condition or situation in which a party ... is unexpectedly placed to his injury, without any default or negligence of his own, which ordinary prudence could not have guarded against.”’ [Citation.]” Finally, as for inadvertence or neglect, “[t]o warrant relief under section 473 a litigant’s neglect must have been such as might have been the act of a reasonably prudent person under the same circumstances. The inadvertence contemplated by the statute does not mean mere inadvertence in the abstract. If it is wholly inexcusable it does not justify relief.’ [Citation.]” (Henderson v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co. (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 215, 229–230.)

                                                                                       

Milam testifies that Defendant did not know or have reason to know that it had been served with the Complaint and, therefore, could not file its response to the Complaint within the statutory deadline because (1) Defendant’s registered agent for service of process did not email Milam or other officers a copy of the Complaint, and (2) a copy was instead mailed to the company’s headquarters’ mailbox, which is rarely monitored because the company operates remotely and there is no personnel at the mailbox’s address. (Milam Decl., ¶¶ 3-6.)

 

The Court agrees with Plaintiff that Defendant has failed to show mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. A reasonable company in Defendant’s position would have been monitoring its headquarters’ mailbox daily. In addition, the motion is unaccompanied by a declaration from the agent for process of service attesting to their alleged mistake with regard to notifying the company’s executives of the lawsuit.

 

Therefore, Defendant’s request to set aside the default and/or default judgment on the ground of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect under Section 473(b) is denied.

 

B. Whether the Attorney-Fee Portion of the Judgment Was Void

 

As an alternative to its motion for an order setting aside the entire judgment, Defendant moves to set aside the portion of the judgment that awarded Plaintiff attorney’s fees, arguing that there was no statutory or contractual basis for those fees. In its opposition, Plaintiff does not address that argument or dispute that argument.

 

“Under section 473, subdivision (d), a trial court ‘may ... set aside any void judgment or order.’ (§ 473, subd. (d).)” (People v. The North River Ins. Co. (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 226, 232.) “A judgment is ‘void’ only when the court entering that judgment ‘lack[ed] jurisdiction in a fundamental sense’ due to the ‘“entire absence of power to hear or determine the case”’ resulting from the ‘“absence of authority over the subject matter or the parties.”’ [Citations.] …. ‘[J]urisdictional errors can be of two types[:] A court can lack fundamental authority over the subject matter, question presented, or party, making its judgment void, or it can merely act in excess of its jurisdiction or defined power, rendering the judgment voidable.’ [Citation.] Only void judgments and orders may be set aside under section 473, subdivision (d); voidable judgments and orders may not.” (Id. at pp. 233-234.)

 

The Court finds that the attorney fee portion of the judgment is void not voidable. According to case law, “‘[u]nless authorized by either statute or agreement, attorney's fees ordinarily are not recoverable as costs. [Citations.]’ [Citation.]” (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1003.) Here, there is no statute or provision in the agreement that allowed Plaintiff to recover attorney fees.

 

Therefore, the request to set aside the attorney fee portion of the judgment (i.e., $5,067.03) is granted.

 

C. Whether the Pre-Judgment Interest Portion of the Judgment Was Void

 

Defendant also moves to reduce the interest awarded to Plaintiff, arguing as follows.

 

“[T]he $55,880.87 in pre-judgment interest sought by Plaintiff in the Judgment is inaccurate both in terms of the rate of the interest it applies and in its calculation of the interest that would be due under that rate.” (Motion, p. 8:2-4.)

 

“As to the applicable rate of interest that could be charged, the 10% per annum rate applied by Plaintiff contradicts the express terms of the parties’ Contract upon which the alleged damages are based. Section 4.3 of the Contract expressly provides that the rate of interest shall be the lesser of 0.5% per month or the maximum rate under applicable law (i.e., 10% per annum). (Exhibit A.) As there are 12 months in a year, a rate of 0.5% per month would equate to approximately 6% per year, which is obviously less than 10% per year. Despite this, the Judgment sought by Plaintiff and entered by the Court expressly state that pre-judgment interest would be charged at the higher rate of 10% per year. This utilization of the higher interest rate contradicts the Contract’s provisions, which control over the general statutory standard. Cal. Civ. Code § 3289(a).” (Motion, p. 8:5-13; see also Milam Decl., Exh. A – a copy of the contract, Section 4.3, first paragraph, last sentence [“All amounts due and owing to Wasabi hereunder but not paid by OEM on the due date thereof shall bear interest at the rate of the lesser of: (i) one-half per cent (0.5%) per month; and (ii) the maximum lawful interest rate permitted under applicable law. Such interest shall accrue on the balance of unpaid amounts from time to time outstanding from the date on which portions of such amounts become due and owing until payment thereof in full”].)

 

“Furthermore, Plaintiff’s calculation of pre-judgment interest would be inaccurate even if a 10% interest rate is used. The simple formula for calculating prejudgment interest is: [Principal Amount x Annual Rate x Number of Years]. Here, the principal amount is alleged to be $312,702.75, and Plaintiff alleges that the breach occurred on May 12, 2024. Plaintiff submitted its request for judgment on February 13, 2026, which is 642 days after the alleged breach (i.e., around 1.76 years). Under 10% per year interest, that only equates to $55,001.42, which is around $880 less than what is listed in the Judgment. Regardless, a correct calculation of pre-judgment interest would utilize the proper interest rate of 0.5% per month. Using the correct rate, the maximum amount of pre-judgment interest Plaintiff could seek as of the date it submitted its request for judgment would be $33,000.85. Even basing pre-judgment interest off the date that the Judgment was actually entered on May 28, 2026, that equates to a maximum of $38,346.78 in pre-judgment interest. In either case, the amount of interest sought by Plaintiff is approximately $20,000 more than the maximum amount it could legally be entitled to.” (Motion, p. 8:14-20.)

 

In its opposition, Plaintiff has not addressed the interest argument or disputed that the correct interest rate is 6 percent per annum under the parties’ contract.  

 

The Court finds that the portion of the judgment awarding Plaintiff prejudgment interest of $55,001.42 is void. The Court previously relied on the 10 percent per annum interest provided by Plaintiff instead of the 6 percent required under the contract. (See Declaration of Steven A. Booska re Accruing Interest, filed February 13, 2026, ¶ 3 [“The calculations for interest [for the request for court judgment] is determined at the sum of 10% per annum from and after May 12, 2024 to February 13, 2026 on the principal amount of $317,702.75. Since the filing of the Request for Entry of Default, 642 days had elapsed from the day of payment default. Accordingly, plaintiff requests $55,880.87 be added as interest in plaintiff’s judgment”].)

 

Interest at the contractual rate of 6 percent per annum on damages of $317,702.75 for the period between the date of the alleged breach on May 12, 2024, to the date of the request for entry of default on February 13, 2026 (i.e., 642 days) equals $33,528.52. It is unclear how Defendant arrived at $33,000.85.

 

For those reasons, Defendant’s request to reduce the interest fee portion of the judgment is granted. The Court shall enter a new judgment with pre-judgement interest of $33,528.52.

 

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the Motion for Relief Concerning Default Judgment is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART as follows. The request to set aside the portion of the judgment awarding Plaintiff attorney fees of $5,067.03 is GRANTED. The request to reduce the pre-judgment interest is GRANTED. The Court awards Plaintiff pre-judgment interest of $33,528.52. The motion is otherwise DENIED. 

 

The Court sets aside the judgment entered on May 28, 2026, and hereby enters a new judgment of $351,841.95, consisting of damages of $317,702.75, pre-judgment interest of $33,528.52, and costs of $610.68.
