            Respondents City of Pasadena and City Council of the City of Pasadena (collectively “City”) and real party in interest Western Justice Center Foundation (“Western Justice”) jointly demur to all three causes of action in petitioners Michael A. Davis and Mary M. West’s First Amended Petition and Complaint (“FAP”). They also seek to strike all CEQA-related allegations from the FAP as being irrelevant, improper, and failing as a matter of law.

 

I.       BACKGROUND

 

A.           Allegations of the Petition

            On or around September 17, 1989, City, through the Pasadena Surplus Property Authority (“Authority”), acquired title to property located at 55-85 South Grand Avenue Pasadena, California (“Property”), for the purpose of “provid[ing] an institutional center of national repute for study and research in the areas of law reform, improvements to judicial administration and lawyer competency, law-related education and service to the community with respect to improvement in legal processes and the use of alternative processes to litigation.” (FAP ¶¶ 30-34.) The Property consists of the Maxwell House and three other historic buildings. (FAP ¶¶ 41-42.) On April 4, 1989, the Authority leased the Property to Western Justice, and the lease strictly prohibited the use of for-profit activities at the Property. (FAP ¶¶ 35-37.) Notwithstanding this prohibition, “ancillary activities” such as “seminars, conferences, and conventions” in association with Western Justice could be held. (FAP ¶ 38.)

 

            Presently, Western Justice operates from the Maxwell House, uses the other buildings as administrative offices for its nonprofit work, and subleases office space to other nonprofit organizations. (FAP ¶ 45.) On April 7, 2023, Western Justice applied for a Conditional Use Permit (“CUP”) to permit the establishment of a Clubs, Lodges, Private Meeting Hall land use at the Maxwell House pursuant to the City’s Zoning Code. (FAP ¶ 48.) In its application, Western Justice proposed 132 special for-profit activities that would be ancillary to the primary use of the Property. (FAP ¶ 48.) On March 5, 2024, Western Justice supplemented its application. (FAP ¶ 49.) During the application process, the City issued two Temporary Use Permits, which allowed weddings and other events that had been previously scheduled to occur. (FAP ¶ 50.)

 

            Neighbors objected to the issuance of the CUP and argued that the application should be denied on the following grounds: (1) the lease prohibited for-profit activity at the Property; (2) it would cause increased noise, traffic, and greenhouse gas emissions; (3) it would cause adverse noise impacts on the community; (4) neighboring homeowners’ outside enjoyment of their property would be affected; and (5) the Class 1 Categorical Exemption under CEQA did not apply. (FAP ¶ 52.) The City staff report, dated June 5, 2024, recommended the application’s approval and determined that the CUP issuance was exempt from CEQA environmental review. (FAP ¶¶ 53-54.) On June 5, 2024, a hearing was held, and the hearing officer approved the application and determined that it was exempt from environmental review. (FAP ¶ 55.)

 

            On June 13, 2024, two appeals were submitted by petitioners to the Board of Zoning Appeals in response to the hearing officer’s decision. (FAP ¶ 56.) On October 10, 2024, the Board upheld the hearing officer’s decision. (FAP ¶¶ 64-65.)

 

            Thereafter, petitioners appealed the Board’s decision to the City Council on October 21, 2024. (FAP ¶ 66.) By April 7, 2025, the City staff recommended that the City Council uphold the Board’s decision with added conditions. (FAP ¶ 79.) On the same day, the City Council approved the CUP with certain revisions. (FAP ¶¶ 83-84.)

 

Repeatedly throughout these appeals, petitioners requested that the City provide them with notice of any hearings and decisions relating to the CUP application pursuant to CEQA. (FAP ¶¶ 89-94.) Petitioners allege that, despite these requests, they did not receive notice from City regarding the filing of the Notice of Exemption (“NOE”), which they claim was done intentionally. (FAP ¶¶ 95, 98-99.) Petitioners further allege that they reasonably relied on the City’s statutory obligation to provide them with notice pursuant to CEQA. (FAP ¶ 100.) Based on information and belief, petitioners allege that the NOE was filed with the County of Los Angeles and the State of California, which stated that the CUP issuance was exempt under CEQA pursuant to the Class 1, Existing Facilities exemption. (FAP ¶ 97.)

 

B.           Procedural History

 On July 2, 2025, petitioners initiated this action. On December 22, 2025, City and Western Justice jointly demurred to the Petition. In lieu of an opposition, petitioners filed the operative Verified First Amended Petition and Complaint on March 10, 2026.

 

On April 13, 2026, City and Western Justice jointly filed a demurrer with a motion to strike. On June 30, 2026, petitioners filed oppositions to the demurrer and motion to strike. On July 7, 2026, City and Western Justice filed a joint reply.

 

II.      DISCUSSION

 

A.           Judicial Notice

 

          The City and Western Justice request the Court to take judicial notice of the following documents: (1) the NOE filed by City on April 10, 20215 (Exhibit A); (2) a copy of the results of a records search from the Los Angeles County Registrar-Recorder/County Clerk California Environmental Quality Act Notice Search (Exhibit B); (3) a copy of the results of a records search from the California Governor’s Office of Land Use & Climate Innovation State Clearinghouse “CEQAnet Web Portal” (Exhibit C); and (4) Assembly Bill 1860 (Exhibit D). The requests are GRANTED pursuant to Evidence Code § 452(c).

 

Petitioners’ requests for judicial notice of Assembly Bill Nos. 314 and 1860 (Exhibits A and B, respectively) are GRANTED pursuant to Evidence Code § 452(c).

 

B.           Demurrer

1.            The Third Cause of Action for Violation of CEQA is Time-Barred

 

The City and Western Justice assert that the third cause of action for violation of CEQA is time-barred because it was not filed within the applicable 35-day statute of limitations period as required by Public Resources Code § 21167(d). (Demurrer at 14-18.) The Court agrees.

 

When seeking to challenge a public agency’s determination that a project is exempt from CEQA, the petitioner must bring the action within 35-days after a notice of exemption has been filed. (Pub. Res. Code § 21167(d).) If a written request to be provided with notice was made, the public agency must provide notice of such action within five days to the requestor, but the date of mailing of this notice expressly does not affect the time period specified in subdivision (d). (Pub. Res. Code § 21167(f).) “CEQA’s statutory scheme evidences a clear legislative intent to ‘ensure extremely prompt resolution’ of lawsuits claiming violations of the statute.” (Organizacion Comunidad de Alviso v. City of San Jose (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 783, 791 [quoting Stockton Citizens for Sensible Planning v. City of Stockton (2010) 48 Cal.4th 481, 500].)

 

“‘A demurrer based on a statute of limitations will not lie where the action may be, but is not necessarily, barred. [Citation.] In order for the bar ... to be raised by demurrer, the defect must clearly and affirmatively appear on the face of the complaint; it is not enough that the complaint shows that the action may be barred. [Citation.]” [Citation.]’ [Citation.]” (Committee For Green Foothills v. Santa Clara County Bd. of Supervisors (2010) 48 Cal.4th 32, 42.) “‘“[A] complaint otherwise good on its face is subject to demurrer when facts judicially noticed render it defective.” [Citation.]’ [Citations.]” (Evans v. City of Berkeley (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1, 6.)

 

Here, the NOE was filed with the Los Angeles County Clerk and the Office of Planning and Research on April 10, 2025. (Resp. RJN Exs. A-C.) Thus, pursuant to Public Resources Code § 21167(d), the deadline to contest the NOE was May 15, 2025.[1] However, petitioners did not file this action until July 2, 2025.  While it is alleged that City failed to provide notice as required under Public Resources Code § 21167(f) (FAP ¶ 98), “CEQA contains no remedy for that violation.” (Organizacion Comunidad de Alviso, 60 Cal.App.5th at 792.) As explained by the Court of Appeal in Organizacion Comunidad de Alviso, the filing of a legally proper notice as required under Public Resources Code § 21167 “provid[es] constructive notice to all potential litigants” and “triggers the [ ] statute of limitations to challenge the project.” (Id. at 793 [emphasis in original].)

 

Petitioners attempt to distinguish the present case from Organizacion Comunidad de Alviso by arguing that it was the mailing of the defective Notice of Determination that provided constructive notice to the appellant in that case, whereas in this case no mailing of the NOE is alleged to have occurred at all. (Demurrer Opp. at 10:5-23.) The Court finds that this is a distinction without a difference, as petitioners cannot escape the actual words used by the Court, which stated: “As we have discussed, the statute of limitations to challenge the adequacy of an EIR begins to run when a valid NOD is filed.”  (Organizacion Comunidad de Alviso, 60 Cal.App.5th at 794.)

 

Petitioners also attempt to rely on legislative history to argue that the mailing of the notice is a critical distinction and that the agency’s failure to mail the notice warrants a different outcome. (Demurrer Opp. at 8:4-17, 11-12.)  But no remedy for such a violation is prescribed by statute, and “[i]t is not for [the Court] to legislate a remedy for the city’s violation of section 21167, subdivision (f), particularly where the remedy plaintiff seeks would conflict with express provisions of that very statute.” (Organizacion Comunidad de Alviso, 60 Cal.App.5th at 793.) Moreover, “the Legislature has identified the [filing of the notice] as the trigger for the statute of limitations, and the Supreme Court has emphasized that potential litigants must pay close attention to those filings before initiating litigation.” (Id. at 794.)

 

To avoid the time bar of Public Resources Code § 21167(d), the FAP alleges that City is estopped from asserting a statute of limitations defense, because City “intentionally never mailed the NOE” and petitioners “reasonably relied on the City’s statutory obligation to mail notices relating to the Project pursuant to CEQA.”[2] (FAP ¶¶ 100-104.) However, even if the failure to mail the NOE to petitioners was intentional, petitioners’ “reliance on that failure would be unreasonable as a matter of law” because City’s proper filing of the NOE gave constructive notice to all potential litigants and City’s conduct “did not affect the fundamental process followed nor the filing of public notice of that process.” (Organizacion Comunidad de Alviso, 60 Cal.App.5th at 796.) Further undermining petitioners’ estoppel claim, at no point during the appeals process did City ever communicate that it would circulate an environmental impact report, and, indeed, the allegations of the FAP imply that the City always intended to issue a NOE. (FAP ¶¶ 52-84; cf. Citizens for a Responsible Caltrans Decision v. Department of Transportation (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 1103, 1133 [finding that CalTrans was estopped from evoking the statute of limitations as a defense because it had made misrepresentations to the appellant and the public.)

 

Consequently, because petitioners did not timely file this action in accordance with Public Resources Code § 21167(d), the third cause of action is time-barred and the Demurrer is SUSTAINED as to this cause of action.

 

 

 

2.            The Second Cause of Action for Writ of Mandate per CCP § 1094.5 Alleges Sufficient Facts to Support a Cause of Action

 

The City and Western Justice demurrer to the second cause of action for writ of mandate per CCP § 1094.5 on the ground that it is also time-barred because that cause of action, in their view, amounts to a CEQA claim. (Demurrer at 9-10.) The Court disagrees.

 

Here, the FAP alleges that the City abused its discretion in determining that the project was exempt from CEQA, but the FAP also alleges in the second cause of action that City’s findings “that the proposed use is allowed under the City’s Zoning Code as an accessory facility of a principal use, that WJC s for-profit activities of generating revenue through the proposed events are permitted, that the proposed use would not be detrimental to the health, safety, or general welfare of persons residing in the neighborhood, and that the proposed use would not be detrimental or injurious to property in the neighborhood are contrary to the provisions of the Zoning Code and the Noise Restrictions Ordinance.” (FAP ¶ 114.)

 

While the demurring parties emphasize that the second cause of action seeks to challenge the NOE determination (FAP ¶ 115), they fail to cite any authority for the proposition that non-CEQA claims cannot be brought when a CEQA challenge is untimely. Demurrers do not lie as to only parts of causes of action where some valid claim is alleged, because a demurrer “must dispose of an entire cause of action to be sustained.”¿ (Poizner¿v. Fremont General Corp.¿(2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119.)¿Because the demurrer only challenges the CEQA allegations of the second cause of action, the Court finds that the demurrer to that cause of action lacks merit.

 

In reply, City and Western Justice also claim that the allegations in support of their claims under the Zoning Code and Noise Restrictions Ordinance are conclusory. (Reply at 8.) However, the FAP includes allegations in support of petitioners’ other contentions as to how the City abused its discretion in approving the CUP beyond purportedly violating CEQA. (FAP ¶¶ 47-48, 52, 57, 66-68, 74 114.)

 

Therefore, the second cause of action is sufficiently alleged, and, accordingly, the demurrer to the second cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

3.            The First Cause of Action for Writ of Mandate per CCP § 1085 Fails to Allege Sufficient Facts to Support a Cause of Action

 

City and Western Justice demur to the first cause of action on the ground that it fails to allege any ministerial duty. (Demurrer at 20-21.) The Court agrees.

 

“There are two essential requirements to the issuance of a traditional writ of mandate: (1) a clear, present and usually ministerial duty on the part of the respondent, and (2) a clear, present and beneficial right on the part of the petitioner to the performance of that duty.” (California Assn. for Health Services at Home v. State Dept. of Health Services (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 696, 704.) It must also be shown that the petitioner lacks a plain, speedy, and adequate alternative remedy. (CCP § 1086.)

 

Here, the FAP alleges that “[t]he City has abused its discretion because it did not proceed in the manner required by law and made findings not supported by the evidence when it approved the CUP for the Project” and “failed to act as required by law when it employed practices and policies in the processing of WJC’s CUP application.” (FAP ¶ 108.) Given that petitioners are expressly challenging the results of an administrative hearing (FAP ¶ 112), they have available to them an adequate remedy through administrative mandate pursuant to CCP § 1094.5, as alleged in the second cause of action. (See Conlan v. Bonta (2002) 102 Cal.App.4th 745, 752 [“Where a petition challenges an agency’s failure to perform an act required by law rather than the conduct or result of an administrative hearing, the remedy is by ordinary mandate pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1085, not by administrative mandate pursuant to section 1094.5”].) Therefore, the Court finds that the first cause of action does not sufficiently make out a claim for traditional mandate.

 

Petitioners unconvincingly argue that the first cause of action alleges a ministerial duty because City failed to provide notice of the NOE as required under CEQA. (Demurrer Opp. at 14:5-6.) According to its own allegations (FAP ¶¶ 107-112), however, the first cause of action does not concern CEQA or any violation thereof.  Regardless, as discussed above, there is no remedy for such a violation of CEQA even if alleged, and the Court thus would not be able to provide any effectual relief for such violation, thereby rendering the claim moot. (See Wilson & Wilson v. City Counsel of Redwood City (2011) 191 Cal.App.4th 1559, 1574.)

 

Accordingly, because the first cause of action fails to allege a ministerial duty and an alternative remedy exists through CCP § 1094.5, the Demurrer to the first cause of action is SUSTAINED.

 

C.           Motion to Strike

The City and Western Justice seek to strike all references to CEQA in the FAP. (See Notice of Motion at 2-13; FAP ¶¶ 2, 5, 7, 12, 14, 23-29, 66, 69, 79, 83, 85-105, 114-117, 120-126, 128, 129, 134, 135; Prayer for Relief, ¶¶ 2, 4, 6.)

 

The Court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, strike “any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 436(a).) The court may also strike “all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 436(b).)

 

Because petitioners’ third cause of action for violation of CEQA is time-barred and subject to demurrer, any allegations that reference CEQA are no longer essential to petitioners’ pleading. Therefore, it is appropriate to strike them. (See Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].)

 

Accordingly, the motion to strike is GRANTED.

 

III.     CONCLUSION

 

            The demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the first and third causes of action without leave to amend and OVERRULED as to the second cause of action. The motion to strike is GRANTED in its entirety.

II.      DISCUSSION

         

A.           Standard of Review

Pursuant to the CPRA (Gov. Code § 7921.000, et seq.), individual citizens have a right to access government records. In enacting the CPRA, the California Legislature declared that “access to information concerning the conduct of the people's business is a fundamental and necessary right of every person in this state.” (Gov. Code § 7921.000; see also Cal. Const. Art. I, Sec. 3(b); County of Los Angeles v. Superior Court (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 57, 63.)

 

“[E]very person has a right to inspect any public record” of a state or local agency subject to statutory exemptions. (Gov. Code § 7922.530(a); see also Gov. Code § 7920.510(h) [definition of “local agency” includes agent of a city].) The California Constitution mandates that the CPRA be “broadly construed,” while any statute “that limits the right of access” must be “narrowly construed.” (See Cal. Const. Art. I, Sec. 3(b)(2); see also Nat’l Lawyers Guild v. City of Hayward (2020) 9 Cal.5th 488, 507.) The CPRA “does not allow limitations on access to a public record based upon the purpose for which the record is being requested, if the record is otherwise subject to disclosure.” (Gov. Code § 7921.300.)

 

Petitioner bears the burden of proof and persuasion in a mandate proceeding brought under CCP § 1085. (California Correctional Peace Officers Assn. v. State Personnel Bd. (1995) 10 Cal.4th 1133, 1154.) “To establish an agency has a duty to disclose under [the CPRA], the petitioner must show that: (1) the record ‘qualif[ies] as [a] ‘public record[ ]’ …; and (2) the record is ‘in the possession of the agency.’” (Anderson-Barker v Sup.Ct. (2019) 31 Cal.App.5th 528, 538.) “Whether a record falls within the statutory definition of a ‘public record’ involves a ‘distinct inquiry’ from whether the agency is in possession of that record…. The duty to disclose applies only when the petitioner has satisfied both elements.” (Id. at 539.)

 

CPRA exemptions must be narrowly construed, and the agency bears the burden of showing that a specific exemption applies. (Sacramento County Employees’ Retirement System v. Superior Court (2013) 195 Cal.App.4th 440, 453.) A public agency also has the burden to demonstrate that it properly withheld records on the grounds they are non-responsive to a CPRA request or do not constitute public records. (ACLU of Northern Cal. v. Sup. Ct. (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 55, 83-86.) “Because the agency has full knowledge of the contents of the withheld records and the requester has only the agency’s affidavits and descriptions of the documents, its affidavits must be specific enough to give the requester ‘a meaningful opportunity to contest’ the withholding of the documents.” (Id. at 83.)

 

B.           Evidentiary Objections

The County’s evidentiary objections to the Declaration of George Basye are OVERRULED in their entirety. The County’s evidentiary objections to the Declaration of Sigrid R. Waggener are OVERRULED in their entirety.

 

Petitioner’s evidentiary objections to portions of the Declaration of Adrine Arakelian are SUSTAINED in their entirety on the grounds that the objected portions consist of improper argument and legal conclusions concerning whether the County met the applicable statutory deadlines. (See Tuchscher Dev. Enters, Inc. v. San Diego Unified Port Dist. (2003)106 Cal.App.4th 1219, 1240.) Petitioner’s evidentiary objection to the Declaration of Kathy Park is OVERRULED.

 

C.           Ripeness

 

The County contends that the Court is precluded from determining whether the CPRA has been violated at this moment because the June 11, 2025 CPRA Request is still being processed. (Opp. at 20-21.) The Court disagrees.

 

A case is ripe where there is “a real and substantial controversy admitting of specific relief through a decree of a conclusive character.” (Pacific Legal Found. v. Cal. Coastal Com. (1982) 33 Cal.3d 158, 171.) An individual may seek a writ of mandate pursuant to the CPRA “to enforce that person’s right under this division to inspect or receive a copy of any public record or class of public records.” (Gov. Code § 7923.000.)

 

Here, the County concedes that it has refused to produce certain documents in response to petitioner’s CPRA requests on the basis of certain privileges. (County Appx. at 1112 [Park Decl. ¶¶ 19-21].)  Petitioner also argues that the County has violated the CPRA by not producing responsive records promptly.[2] (See County of San Benito v. Superior Court (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 243, 264 [“A trial court remains free to promptly adjudicate whether a public agency has failed to comply with its obligations under the Public Records Act by slow-rolling its production of records or by refusing to propose an enforceable schedule for timely production. . . .”].) Accordingly, the instant matter is ripe for adjudication.

 

D.          Whether County has “Promptly” Produced Documents

 

Petitioner contends that the County has violated the CPRA because it has failed to promptly produce the requested documents subject to the June 11, 2025 CPRA Request. The Court disagrees.

 

Per Government Code § 7922.530(a): “Except with respect to public records exempt from disclosure by express provisions of law, each state or local agency, upon a request for a copy of records that reasonably describes an identifiable record or records, shall make the records promptly available to any person upon payment of fees covering direct costs of duplication, or a statutory fee if applicable.” While records must be produced promptly, the CPRA does not establish a specific timeframe for the actual production.  (Voice of San Diego v. San Diego Unified School District (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 923, 943.)

 

As stated in Voice of San Diego v. San Diego Unified School District, a local agency must “produce records quickly and without delay as the occasion demands,” but “this determination necessarily requires consideration of the scope and burden imposed on the particular agency by the particular request.” (Id. at 946.) As such, the Court of Appeal determined that the following factors are appropriate to assess promptness: (1) “the specificity and breadth of the request”; (2) “the nature and location of the records sought”; (3) “the volume of data to be searched”; (4) “the storage medium that must be accessed”; (5) “the level of review required for any exemptions and redactions”; (6) “the complexity of the request”; and (7) “the size, scope and complexity of the business or activities of the agency responding to the request.” (Id. at 946-47.)

 

Here, it is undisputed that the documents petitioner seeks constitute public records. While petitioner has sought public records from the County since August 17, 2023, the Court notes that, over the course of three years, several requests have been issued and later consolidated twice. (Pet. Appx. at 1-2, 4-6 [Waggener Decl. ¶¶ 5, 14, 19], 156-82 [Ex. 3], 297-303 [Ex. 10], 461-66 [Ex. 16]; see also County Appx. at 1110-11 [Park Decl. ¶ 9].) The requests also encompass multiple County departments. (Pet. Appx. at 462-66 [Ex. 16: June 11, 2025 CPRA Request]; County Appx. at 1103 [Romero Decl. ¶ 5].) Petitioner proposed 259 Boolean search terms for the County’s search efforts on February 3, 2025, and eventually provided 614 amended search terms on December 23, 2025 and January 5, 2026. (County Appx. at 1109-10 [Park Decl. ¶¶ 8(a), 8(e)], 1113-41 [Ex. 48].) In addition to these searches, petitioner provided a set of over 100 custodians and 50 external individuals to be included in the County’s search efforts.

 

In complying with petitioner’s CPRA request, the County has primarily tasked one of its employees to conduct the search efforts, and, since June 2025, he has completed 191 searches, yielding 976 terabytes of potentially responsive records. (County Appx. at 1103-04 [Romero Decl. ¶¶ 6-11].) County represents that this employee is only able to conduct five searches a day while attending to his other job duties. (County Appx. at 1103-05 [Romero Decl. ¶¶ 5-14].) The County has produced fifteen batches of document production since July 2025 on a rolling basis.[3] (County Appx. at 1111 [Park Decl. ¶¶ 13-14], 1176-1209 [Exs. 54-68: Batch Productions Nos. 1-15].) The mere fact that only one employee is tasked with this endeavor does not suggest that the County is engaged in an unlawful practice of delaying disclosure.  Petitioner has not submitted any evidence to suggest otherwise. Therefore, even though petitioner specifically only seek records relating to the ESGVAP and the Project and has been actively involved in refining the search efforts, it is evident that the volume of the records that needs to be searched and reviewed pursuant to petitioner’s CPRA requests is extensive. As a result, this Court does not find that the County has failed to promptly produce records under the circumstances.

 

Because petitioner has not presented sufficient evidence to show that County has not been producing records promptly, the Court finds that the County has not violated Government Code § 7922.530.

 

E.           Whether County has Improperly Withheld Documents

 

Petitioner contends that the County has been withholding documents without a legitimate basis. (Pet. Br. at 12-15.)

 

“To carry its burden, the County must describe the justification for nondisclosure with reasonably specific detail and demonstrate that the information withheld is within the claimed privilege or exemption. This process cannot require an agency to disclose the very information it seeks to protect. Having both the burden of proof and all the evidence, the agency has the difficult task of justifying its withholding the documents without compromising that very act by revealing too much information.” (Golden Door Properties, LLC v. Superior Court (2020) 53 Cal.App.5th 733, 790.)

 

Here, the County claims that it has properly withheld documents under the attorney-client privilege, work product doctrine, and the deliberate process privilege. (Opp. at 17-19.) In support of this claim, the County relies only on conclusory statements made in the Declarations of Adrine Arakelian and Kathy Park. (County Appx. at 1033 [Arakelian Decl. ¶¶ 18-19], 1112 [Park Decl. ¶¶ 19-21].) Neither declaration appropriately satisfies the standard set forth in Golden Door Properties, LLC, as the statements made by the County’s Principal Regional Planner and Deputy County Counsel do not meaningfully show that the withheld documents are actually privileged and subject to withholding. For this reason, it is appropriate for the County to prepare a Vaughn Index/privilege log. (See State Bd. of Equalization v. Sup. Ct. (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1177, 1193 [“Although the Public Records Act does not, like the FOIA, require the maintenance of an index of records available for public inspection and copying, it¿does not prohibit a court from ordering the preparation of a list of the documents which are sought”].)

 

Accordingly, the Court finds on this record that the County has failed to carry its burden for withholding documents, but, before ordering the production of any particular withheld document(s), County shall prepare a Vaughn Index/privilege log for petitioner. Thereafter, if there is any particular identified document for which withholding is contested, such document shall be subject to in camera review prior to an order for production by this Court. (Gov. Code § 6259; Evid. Code § 915; County of Los Angeles v. Superior Court (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 819, 835.)

 

F.           Other Violations

 

Petitioner also argues that the County has violated the CPRA by not producing all responsive records and by not complying with Government Code § 7922.535. The Court is only unpersuaded by the latter argument, which relates to the obligation to state an estimated date and time for production of responsive records.

 

In arguing that County has failed to produce all responsive records, petitioner relies on the discovery responses from the County. (Pet. Br. at 10.) Those responses merely state that the County has not yet completed its review of the records.[4] (Pet. Appx at 559-67, 590-94 [Ex. 21: SROGs Responses Nos. 10-17, 5-57, 59], 603-06, 610-11, 633-38, 649-52 [Ex. 22: RFA Responses Nos. 1, 3, 5, 15, 16, 59-61, 63-64, 90-94].) As discussed above, the Court recognizes that there may be good reason as to why the production of the requested records is still on-going and that a privilege log must still be prepared.  Under the circumstances, the Court thus does not find that the County has failed to produce all responsive records at this juncture.

 

With respect to Government Code § 7922.535, while the CPRA does not establish a specific timeframe to produce records, a local agency must provide the estimated date and time when the records will be available if it is determined that the request seeks disclosable public records. (Gov. Code § 7922.535(a).) Although County has been producing records on a rolling basis and given interim projections for the next batch of production, it has failed to communicate any meaningful timetable or estimate of when the production would be completed. This is a violation of Government Code § 7922.535.

 

III.     CONCLUSION

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the petition is GRANTED IN PART in limited respects and otherwise DENIED in all other respects.  Because County has failed to carry its burden to justify why it has withheld certain documents and has not complied with Government Code § 7922.535(a), a writ shall issue requiring County to produce a Vaughn Index/privilege log to petitioner and to provide a reasonable estimate of when it will complete its production of documents in response to the June 11, 2025 CPRA Request.

I.       BACKGROUND

 

A.           The State Bar Client Security Fund

 

The State Bar is a constitutional agency established in the judicial branch. (Cal. Const, art. VI, §9.) It is a public corporation established for the purpose of performing governmental functions in the administration of justice, which include regulation of the practice of law. (Business & Professions (“B&P”) Code § 6001, et seq.)

 

In 1971, the State Bar’s Board of Governors, now “Trustees” (the “Board”), created a victims’ restitution fund, the Client Security Fund (“CSF”). (B&P Code §6140.5.) The Board created the CSF to mitigate financial injury to clients due to the dishonest conduct of California attorneys, and the Board adopted the Rules of Procedure Client Security Fund Matters (“CSF Rules”) to govern administration of the Fund. As a part of this process, the Board created the “Commission to act as the Board’s delegate to administer the Fund and the CSF Rules. (CSF Rule 3.421.)

 

The CSF is a limited fund financed by a portion of the dues paid by active members of the State Bar. (B&P Code § 6140.55.) The maximum allowable payment per claim is $100,000. (CSF Rule 3.434(A).) “Dishonest conduct” includes the wrongful taking of money or property. (CSF Rule 3.431(A).) It is additionally defined as a failure to refund unearned fees received in advance for services when the attorney performed an insignificant portion of the services rendered, or none at all. (CSF Rule 3.431(B).) The attorney must be disciplined or disbarred before the Commission can grant reimbursement. (CSF Rule 3.432.)

 

Under the CSF Rules, proceedings are initiated when an application for reimbursement is submitted. The application is screened by counsel for the Commission, who may investigate as deemed appropriate. (CSF Rule 3.441(A).) As part of any investigation, the Commission may require supporting documentation or submission of declarations under penalty of perjury or hold a hearing as appropriate. (CSF Rule 3.441(C).) Hearings are not mandated and will be held only if the Commission concludes that such hearing is reasonably necessary. (CSF Rule 3.443(C); Saleeby v. State Bar (1985) 39 Cal.3d 547.)

 

A written Tentative Decision will be issued by CSF Counsel and served upon the applicant and attorney. (CSF Rule 3.443(A).) The Tentative Decision shall include a statement of the findings or reasons for the decision to grant or deny the applicant’s request for reimbursement. (CSF Rule 3.443(A).) The parties have 30 days to lodge any written objection to the Tentative Decision, which must state the legal or factual grounds for the objection and may include supporting documentation, a request for oral hearing, or additional declarations under penalty of perjury in lieu of an oral hearing. (CSF Rule 3.443(B).) Instead of granting an oral hearing, the Commission may require submission of declarations under penalty of perjury. (CSF Rule 3.443(C).) If no objection to a Tentative Decision is received, the Tentative Decision may be deemed final. (CSF Rule 3.443(D).)

 

When a party makes a timely written objection to the Tentative Decision, the Commission will provide the parties an opportunity to submit objections, requests, or declarations in response to the Tentative Decision and will consider such submissions relevant to the application before issuing a Final Decision. (CSF Rule 3.444(A).) If it deems it necessary, the Commission may also require additional investigation or conduct an oral hearing before issuing a Final Decision. (CSF Rule 3.444(A).) The Final Decision must be in writing and may deny reimbursement or direct reimbursement under conditions deemed appropriate. (CSF Rule 3.444(B).)

 

The Commission has sole and final authority to determine whether and to what extent any application for reimbursement should be granted. (CSF Rule 3.421(A).) The Commission must evaluate applications for reimbursement solely by reference to the CSF Rules. (CSF Rule 3.420(B).) Even if an application meets the requirements for reimbursement under CSF Rule 3.430, CSF Rules explicitly provide that the Commission and its Fund Counsel “have the discretion to deny or limit reimbursement.” (CSF Rule 3.430(D); see also CSF Rule 3.420(C).)

 

A Final Decision of the Commission to grant or deny reimbursement to an applicant may be reviewed in the Superior Court pursuant to CCP § 1094.5. (CSF Rule 3.450; Saleeby, 39 Cal. 3d 365.)

 

B.           Application for Reimbursement

 

On April 25, 2018, petitioner submitted an Application for Reimbursement to CSF, seeking reimbursement of $100,000 for Bolanos’ alleged conduct. (AR 2-5 [Application], 6-214 [Application Attachments], 215 [Notice of Receipt].) Petitioner stated in the application that he had retained Bolanos on two separate matters on January 5, 2015, and April 1, 2015. (AR 6, 84, 85.)

 

The first representation concerned a FINRA (Financial Industry Regulatory Authority) matter, and petitioner obtained a six-figure settlement. (AR 8, 63.) Of this settlement amount, petitioner received $319,721.13. (AR 8, 64-70.) A portion of the remaining settlement amount was retained by Bolanos to pay for fees incurred in connection with the first representation, and petitioner contended that what remained was improperly retained for advance payments in the second action, which concerned claims for elder abuse and wrongful death. (AR 6, 84.) Petitioner further contended that, in connection with the first representation, he was entitled to $5,000 Bolanos obtained but kept from the settlement of a fee dispute petitioner had with one of petitioner’s former counsel in his FINA dispute. (AR 8, 71-78.)

 

In connection with the second representation, petitioner also claimed that Bolanos charged illegal fees because the retainer violated MICRA, which, in petitioner’s view, required Bolanos to prove that he is entitled to a reasonable fee pursuant to Business and Professions Code § 6147. (AR 6.) Petitioner additionally claimed that he was entitled to receive $3,010, which was a portion of a retainer refunded to Bolanos. (AR 8.)

 

 On August 12, 2020, petition supplemented his application with a further statement and additional exhibits, which included his bank records, invoices received from Bolanos, payments made to Bolanos, and the work performed by Bolanos. (AR 614-620 [8/12/2020 Statement]; 621-994 [Attached Exhibits].) On June 29, 2022, CSF sought further information from petitioner regarding his application for reimbursement, and, on the same day, petitioner provided his response. (AR 1026, 1027.)

 

In connection with its investigation, on August 24, 2022, CSF acquired information corroborating that petitioner was supposed to receive $5,000 for the first action. (AR 1046-1058.) On August 25, 2022, CSF subpoenaed records relating to Bolanos’ client trust account at JPMorgan Chase Bank for the period January 1, 2015 through December 31, 2017. (AR 1193-1199.) On September 13, 2022, JPMorgan Chase provided its response to the subpoena. (AR 1207-1327.)     

 

C.           CSF’s Determination

On May 9, 2023, the Commission issued its Tentative Decision, proposing to reimburse petitioner in the amount of $5,000 based on the finding that the record demonstrated Bolanos engaged in dishonest conduct as defined under CSF Rule 3.431(A) by improperly retaining a settlement payment from petitioner’s prior attorney. (AR 219-25.) The Commission found that petitioner did not show that he was entitled to reimbursement for any additional amounts claimed. (AR 223-24.) The Tentative Decision notified petitioner and Bolanos that they had 30 days to object to the decision pursuant to CSF Rule 3.433(B). (AR 219.) Only Bolanos submitted a timely objection. (AR 258-61.) After review of Bolanos’ objection, the Commission issued a Final Decision on August 17, 2023, which upheld the Tentative Decision. (AR 240-43.)

 

Because petitioner did not receive actual notice of the Tentative Decision (AR 265-68), CSF provided petitioner and Bolanos with additional time to submit written objections to the Tentative Decision. (AR 278, 247-248.) Between November 23, 2023, and April 15, 2025, petitioner provided a written objection and additional records. (AR 265-68, 284-575, 583-93.)

 

On June 24, 2025, after reviewing the additional information submitted by petitioner and Bolanos, the Commission issued its Amended Final Decision, which continued to find that petitioner was entitled to reimbursement of the $5,000 settlement payment only. (AR 247-54.) In the Amended Final Decision, the Commission denied petitioner’s request for an oral hearing, finding that petitioner had an opportunity to be heard through the submission of written objections and evidence. (AR 249, 253.) The Commission explained that, under CSF Rule 3.430, reimbursement may be obtained for a loss caused by dishonest conduct of an attorney as defined in the CSR rules.  CSF Rule 3.431(A) defines dishonest conduct as theft or embezzlement of money, the wrongful taking or conversion of money or property, or a comparable act. (AR 249.)  CSF Rule 3.431(B) includes a dishonest conduct the failure to refund unearned fees received in advance for services, where the attorney performed an insignificant portion of the services or none at all.  (AR 249.)  CSF Rule 3.431(B) states explicitly that any other instances of an attorney’s failure to return an unearned fee or the disputed portion of a fee are not reimbursable under the CSR Rules. (AR 249.)

 

The Commission found that petitioner demonstrated eligibility for reimbursement of the $5,000 settlement, because Bolanos failed to provide this amount to petitioner. (AR 250.) With respect to petitioner’s other contention that Bolanos failed to disburse $37,980 from the first representation’s settlement, the Commission found that these amounts had been paid towards legal costs and that petitioner failed to show Bolanos had wrongfully retained those funds. (AR 223, 250.)

 

As for petitioner’s contention that Bolanos charged illegal fees in connection with the second representation, the Commission found that Bolanos’s retention of such fees was not illegal under MICRA (Medical Injury Compensation Reform Act) because MICRA’s limitations on fees as a percentage of recovery did not apply, as the second representation did not result in any recovery for petitioner. (AR 251.)  Consequently, the Commission did not find that Bolanos wrongfully retained or stole any funds within the meaning of CSF Rule 3.431(A) for purposes of petitioner obtaining reimbursement. (AR 251.)

 

The Commission, however, did find that the fee agreement for the second representation did violate Business and Professions Code § 6147 in that it did not limit fees in accordance with MICRA. (AR 252.)  As observed by the Commission, in such circumstances, petitioner was entitled to void their fee agreement in favor of petitioner’s counsel receiving a “reasonable fee for the legal services he provided.” (AR 252; see Bus. & Prof. Code § 6147(b).)  As further noted by the Commission, however, it was beyond the scope of the proceedings and the CSF Rules “to assess the quality of an attorney’s legal services and, thereafter, assign a dollar value to those services.” (AR 252.)  Further, observed the Commission, petitioner still would not qualify for reimbursement under the CSF Rules because it found that Bolanos performed more than an insignificant amount of legal services, which meant that petitioner did not establish a reimbursable loss of attorney fees within the meaning of CSF Rule 3.341(B). (AR 252.)

II.      DISCUSSION

 

A.           Standard of Review

 

Under CCP § 1094.5(b), the pertinent issues are whether the respondent has proceeded without jurisdiction, whether there was a fair trial, and whether there was a prejudicial abuse of discretion. An abuse of discretion is established if the agency has not proceeded in the manner required by law, the decision is not supported by the findings, or the findings are not supported by the evidence. (CCP § 1094.5(b).)

 

In determining whether the findings were supported, the Court reviews CSF’s determinations for substantial evidence, as an applicant for reimbursement has no property right or other fundamental vested right in the Client Security Fund, whose funds are paid out on a discretionary basis. (Johnson v. State Bar, (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 1561, 1566-67.) “Substantial evidence” is relevant evidence that a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion (California Youth Authority v. State Personnel Board (2002) 104 Cal.App.4th 575, 585) or evidence of ponderable legal significance, which is reasonable in nature, credible and of solid value. (Mohilef v. Janovici, (1996) 51 Cal.App.4th 267, 305, n.28.)

 

An agency is presumed to have regularly performed its official duties (Evid. Code §664), and the petitioner therefore has the burden of proof. (Steele v. Los Angeles County Civil Service Commission, (1958) 166 Cal.App.2d 129, 137.) “[T]he burden of proof falls upon the party attacking the administrative decision to demonstrate wherein the proceedings were unfair, in excess of jurisdiction or showed prejudicial abuse of discretion. (Afford v. Pierno, (1972) 27 Cal.App.3d 682, 691.)

 

B.           Merits

 

Petitioner raises various arguments as to why the CSF’s decision should be set aside and why remand is necessary.  Such arguments include: (1) the certified administrative record is fatally incomplete (Pet. Br. at 4-5; Reply at 2); (2) the findings by CSF are not supported by the evidence (Pet. Br. at 5-10; Reply at 3-8); and (3) the CSF violated petitioner’s right to procedural due process (Pet. Br. at 10-11; Reply at 8-9). The Court shall address these arguments in turn.

 

1.            The Administrative Record

Petitioner asserts that the Certified Administrative Record prepared by CSF is incomplete. (Pet. Br. at 4-5; Reply at 2-3.) The Court disagrees.

 

As stated in CCP § 1094.6(c), “the complete record of the proceedings . . . shall include the transcript of the proceedings, all pleadings, all notices and orders, any proposed decision by a hearing officer, the final decision, all admitted exhibits, all rejected exhibits in the possession of the local agency or its commission, board, officer, or agent, all written evidence, and any other papers in the case.” Moreover, as provided under CCP § 1094.5(e), the Court may remand a case for further consideration where relevant evidence could not have been produced in the exercise of reasonable diligence or that was improperly excluded at the hearing.

 

Here, CSF certified the contents of the administrative record as being complete. (See 2/2/26 (Notice Regarding Certification and Contents of the Administrative Record, Ex. A.) While petitioner claims in his opening brief that certain bank records have been omitted from the administrative record (Pet. Br. at 4-5) and asserts on reply that CSF’s statement of completeness amounts to circular logic (Reply at 2-3), petitioner fails to show that the bank records were improperly excluded by CSF to warrant relief under CCP § 1094.5(e).

 

In particular, petitioner asserts that the administrative record omits bank statements belonging to Bolanos for the period of January through June 2015. (Pet. Br. at 4-5) The Court recognizes that CSF issued a subpoena on August 25, 2022, seeking bank records from JPMorgan Chase encompassing that date range. (AR 1193-99 [Subpoena].) Upon review of the response provided by JPMorgan Chase and the declaration of the custodian of records on September 13, 2022, it is clear that the subpoenaed bank records included in the administrative record are those records that were provided by JPMorgan Chase in response to the subpoena. (AR 1207-09 [Response Letter].) As stated by the custodian of record in connection with JPMorgan Chase’s response, “the enclosed records are a true and correct copy of the original documents kept by the Bank in the ordinary course of business.” (AR 1208.) The letter further indicated that “[h]ere is the information that fulfills your request on the matter referenced above.” (AR 1207.) A table of contents was also provided with regard to the records produced in connection with the subpoena. (AR 1209.) The following categories were listed: (1) a “Checking - Signature Card,” starting on page 1; (2) “Statement and/or Items,” starting at page 2; and (3) an “Addendum,” starting at page 120. The documents that directly follow the table of contents are numbered from 1 to 119. (AR 1210-1327.) Taking into account the custodian of records’ declaration and the table of contents, the only logical and reasonable conclusion is that those are the only records provided to CSF and that the records provided did not include any bank records corresponding to the period of January through June 2015, as JPMorgan Chase not longer kept in its possession such records.

 

In reply, petitioner asserts that “if Chase did not produce them, CSF has not explained why it treated the production response as sufficient and decided the claim without the requested source records covering receipt and initial disposition of the settlement funds.” (Reply at 2:23-25.) Petitioner fails to cite to any legal authority that would impose such a duty upon CSF. Rather, as provided by CSF Rule 3.441(A), “[t]he Fund may investigate an application as it deems appropriate.” Nowhere in the CSF Rules is there any requirement for CSF to provide an explanation as to why it did not conduct further investigation. Simply put, CSF had no duty or obligation to investigate the bank’s production of records any further.

 

Petitioner also fails to persuasively articulate why the transaction ledger in the record (AR 1200-06) is insufficient for the period January through June 2015. While petitioner asserts that the source records are necessary to identify the “origin of funds, the instrument of deposit, or the disposition of entrusted client funds” (Pet. Br. at 5:7-10), the transaction ledger includes the exact amounts that were either deposited into or transferred from the account, as well as a description indicating whether the transfers were to another checking account or for an online payment. Although the ledger does not identify the source of the deposited funds, there does not appear to be any genuine doubt as to the veracity or accuracy of the listed deposits. In review of the bank statements that were produced, the information contained therein is similar to what appears on the transaction ledger. Thus, there is no indication that the source records would have provided any additional relevant information.

 

For the first time on reply, petitioner asserts that he provided CSF with a “USB drive containing a spreadsheet breaking down each of the Bolanos invoices to identify the duplicate and false charges,” which does not appear in the administrative record. (Reply at 3:13-19; AR 614, 617, 1038.) Generally, new legal argument is not permitted in reply papers. (Jay v. Mahaffey (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 1522, 1537-38.) Even if the Court were to consider such newly raised argument, the Court would find that the claimed substance of the evidence is not relevant to warrant relief under CCP § 1094.5(e).[2] As discussed above, reimbursement through CSF is only available where the loss resulted from dishonest conduct as defined in CSF Rule 3.431. Improper billing practices and inaccurate billing entries do not fall within the categories of “dishonest conduct” for which reimbursement is authorized under the CSF Rules.

 

Petitioner argues that Bolanos’s conduct amounts to wrongful taking per CSF Rule 3.431(A). (Reply at 4:1-10 [citing Johnson v. State Bar (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 1561, 1565-66].) However, petitioner’s reliance on Johnson is misplaced. The dishonest conduct described in Johnson, i.e., retaining payment where no work has been performed, would directly fall under CSF Rule 3.431(B). If CSF Rule 3.431(A) were read to include improper billing entries within its definition of theft and embezzlement as “dishonest conduct,” then the Commission would be reduced to the role of acting as a fee arbitrator and evaluator of the value of legal services rendered, which is not its intended function under the CSR Rules. (See CSF Rules 3.430(B), 3.431; AR  224, 252.)

 

Accordingly, the Court finds that the certified administrative record is not incomplete.

 

2.            CSF’s Findings

Petitioner contends that the Amended Final Decision amounts to an abuse of discretion because the financial records showed that Bolanos failed to lawfully retain client funds and that the Commission applied the wrong legal standard in evaluating his loss (Pet. Br. at 5-10.) Neither argument is persuasive.

 

With respect to the first claim, petitioner asserts that the Commission failed to address Bolanos’ repeated depletion of trust funds and should have analyzed the “financial evidence under the legal standards governing entrusted client funds.” (Pet. Br. at 6-7.) Petitioner clarifies on reply that the Commission was required “to identify the reasonable value of the services” rendered and to perform a transaction-specific analysis. (Reply at 4:11-15.) Petitioner fails to cite any meaningful authority imposing such a duty on the Commission. Further, as discussed above, the purpose of the CSF is to provide reimbursement for losses from “dishonest conduct” as defined under CSF Rule 3.431. In raising his “transaction-specific” analysis, petitioner disputes portions of fees paid to Bolanos based on disputed performance by Bolanos, which is expressly not within the scope of “dishonest conduct” under the CSF Rules (CSF Rule 3.431(B).) While the Court acknowledges there may be inconsistencies with certain invoices (AR 704-09, 958), this does not allow for reimbursement under the CSF Rules.

 

As for the second claim, petitioner asserts that the Commission applied the wrong legal framework by not evaluating “whether entrusted funds were removed from trust before being earned.” (Pet. Br. at 9:19-20; Reply at 8:1-16.) The distinction that petitioner seeks to create through this argument is of little assistance to petitioner in light of the narrow circumstances pursuant to which reimbursement is permitted under the CSF Rules. Even if true that Bolanos may have violated ethical and professional responsibilities in managing his client trust account, such conduct about which petitioner complains amounts to purportedly improper advancing of fees prior to earning them, but, per CSF Rule 3.430(B), fees paid advance may only be reimbursed by CSF if it is shown that the attorney had performed an insignificant amount of the services or none at all, which was not the case here.  In reply, petitioner argues that there are discrepancies in the billing entries and contends the Commission should have reconciled these conflicts. (Reply at 4-6.) However, as discussed above, that role would reduce the Commission to the role of fee arbitrator and evaluator of services rendered, which is not its function.

 

Lastly, as an aside, the Court notes that petitioner argues that the Commission did not address the $3,010 third-party refund, and that, on reply, he asserts that CSF failed to investigate the matter appropriately as it had done with the $5,000 settlement reimbursement. (Pet. Br. at 8:22-23; Reply at 9:8-22.) The Court finds these arguments lack merit. The third-party refund was addressed in the Tentative Decision (AR 223), and the Amended Final Decision incorporated the tentative by reference. (AR 253.) Moreover, there is no discrepancy in how CSF chose to investigate the third-party refund as compared with the $5,000 settlement. The latter was investigated because petitioner had provided the settlement agreement showing that he was entitled to that settlement. (AR 1027 [Pet. 6/29/22 Letter to CSF], 1028-34 [Settlement Agreement].) This warranted CSF to investigate the matter with petitioner’s former attorney, Lendrum. (AR 1046-49 [CSF’s Communications with Lendrum and Copy of Check].) By contrast, to support his claim to the $3,010 refund, petitioner provided only his own statement and emails showing that a retainer was paid to an expert witness. (AR 966-68, 1027.) No document was provided to show that a refund was actual provided.

 

Accordingly, the Court finds that petitioner has fail to establish any abuse of discretion by the CSF.

 

3.            Due Process

 

Petitioner argues that his due process rights were violated based on the contention that he was not provided with a meaningful opportunity to be heard.  (Pet. Br. at 10-11.) Specifically, petitioner asserts that CSF denied his multiple requests for a hearing. (AR 249, 262-65, 268-74, 277.)  This argument ignores the fact that CSF is not required to provide a hearing. CSF Rule 3.443(B) states in pertinent part that, when submitting a timely objection to the CSF’s Tentative Decision, it “may include supporting documentation; a request for an oral hearing; or, in lieu of a request for an oral hearing additional declarations executed under penalty of perjury fact.” Furthermore, the Notice of Tentative Decision informed petitioner explicitly that “the Client Security Fund Commission may not necessarily grant a request for an oral hearing.” (AR 219.)

 

CSF’s established procedures are consistent with due process, as “[a] formal hearing, with full rights of confrontation and cross-examination is not necessarily required.” (Saleeby, 39 Cal.3d at 565 [“[W]e conclude that the bar is not required to hold a formal hearing, but it required to give petitioner and other applicants an opportunity to respond to the bar’s proposed disposition of the request for reimbursement’].) Here, in lieu of a hearing, petitioner was able to provide a response to the Tentative Decision on February 6, 2025. (AR 284-307 [Response], 332-557 [Exhibits].) Petitioner also provided a rebuttal to Bolanos’ response. (AR 583-93.) The Commission acknowledged the submission of petitioner’s objection, which included a substantial amount of information and documents information. (AR 248, 253.)

 

In light of petitioner’s ability to submit a thorough response to the Proposed Decision, he had ample opportunity to be heard. (Saleeby, 39 Cal.3d at 565.)  Accordingly, petitioner’s due process rights were not violated.

 

III.     CONCLUSION

 

            The Petition is DENIED. Pursuant to Local Rule 3.231(n), respondent shall prepare, serve, and ultimately file a proposed judgment.

Case Number: 26STCV10797    Hearing Date: July 14, 2026    Dept: 836

MOTION FOR ORDER APPOINTING RECEIVER

 

Date:               7/14/26 (1:30 PM)

Case:               Pacific RBLF Funding Trust v. Bella Assets LLC, et al. (26STCV10797)

 

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

           

Plaintiff Pacific RBLF Funding Trust’s Unopposed Motion for Order Appointing Receiver is GRANTED.

 

Plaintiff Pacific RBLF Funding Trust’s request for judicial notice of Exhibits A and D are GRANTED pursuant to Evidence Code § 452(c). Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice of Exhibits B-C are GRANTED pursuant to Evidence Code § 452(d).

 

I.                   BACKGROUND

 

On April 2, 2026, plaintiff Pacific RBLF Funding Trust initiated this action against defendant Bella Assets LLC for judicial foreclosure and specific performance concerning real property located at 8524 Beverlywood Street, Los Angeles, California 90034 (“Property”). On April 24, 2026, plaintiff filed the instant motion for order appointing receiver.  The motion is unopposed.  Default was entered against defendant on May 27, 2026.  

 

II.                REQUEST TO APPOINT A RECEIVER

 

Pursuant to CCP § 564 (b)(2), (b)(9), & (b)(11), and Civil Code § 2938, plaintiff seeks the appointment of a receiver with respect to the Property. The receiver would operate the properties and collect rents.

 

            A.        Legal Standard

 

Pursuant to CCP § 564(b), “a receiver may be appointed by the court in which an action or proceeding is pending, or by a judge thereof, in the following cases . . . . (2) In an action by a secured lender for foreclosure where it appears the property is in danger of being lost, removed, or materially injured, or that the conditions of the deed of trust have not been performed. . . . (9) In all other cases where necessary to preserve the property or rights of any party. . . . (11) In an action by a secured lender for specific performance of an assignment of rents provision in a deed of trust, mortgage, or separate assignment document.”

 

Additionally, CCP § 564(b)(12) authorizes the appointment of a receiver “[i]n a case brought by an assignee under an assignment of leases, rents, issues or profits pursuant to subdivision (g) of Section 2938 of the Civil Code.” Civil Code § 2938 provides that an assignment of rents in a real property secured loan transaction constitutes a present and perfected transfer of interest in the rents. These provisions authorize the appointment of a receiver to collect rents pending trial in an action to specifically enforce the terms of a deed of trust (1) assigning rents and profits to the creditor upon default or (2) providing for appointment of a receiver to take possession of the property and collect the rents and profits from it. (Mines v. Superior Court, (1932) 216 Cal. 776, 779; Turner v. Superior Court, (1977) 72 Cal.App.3d 804, 811.  No showing that the property’s value is insufficient to satisfy the debt is required for a “rents and profits receiver.”  Turner, 72 Cal.App.3d at 811.)

 

Appointment of a receiver is a drastic provisional remedy that the court should only grant when facts are presented by admissible evidence that clearly establish a receiver is necessary to protect the property and maintain the status quo. (Barclay Bank of California v. Superior Court (“Barclays”) (1977) 69 Cal. App. 3d 593, 597; City and County of San Francisco v. Daley (1993) 16 Cal.App.4th 734, 744). The appointment of a receiver is an equitable remedy and should be used only when necessary and where other legal remedies are unavailable. (Rogers v. Smith (1946) 76 Cal.App.2d 16, 21).

 

 

            B.        Merits

 

As set forth in the motion, the original lender, Center Street Lending VIII SPE, LLC, loaned $1,870,500 to defendant on June 15, 2022, and defendant had executed a promissory note to evidence its indebtedness. (Alper Decl. ¶ 4, Ex. 1.) The promissory note was then secured by a Deed of Trust, Security Agreement and Fixture Filing (with Assignment of Leases and Rents) (“Deed of Trust”) on the Property, which was recorded on June 23, 2022. (Alper Decl. ¶ 4 & RJN Ex. A.) On March 18, 2026, the Deed of Trust was assigned to plaintiff. (Alper Decl. ¶ 5 & Ex. 2.)

 

Pursuant to the Deed of Trust, defendant consented to the appointment of a receiver to manage the Property and collect rents in the event of default. (Alper Decl. ¶ 4 & RJN, Ex. A at ¶ 12.) Plaintiff has shown that defendant has been in default since January 15, 2026. (Alper Decl. ¶ 6 & RJN Ex. D [Notice of Default].) Despite defendant’s failure to cure the default, plaintiff is informed that the Property is currently occupied by a renter and that defendant is diverting the rent proceeds for its own benefit. (Alper Decl. ¶ 8.)

 

As contemplated by the contractual agreement between the parties, a receiver may be appointed to manage the Property and to collect and receive all rents of the Property. (Alper Decl. ¶ 4 & RJN, Ex. A at ¶ 12; see Barclays, 69 Cal.App.3d at 602.) Without the appointment of a receivership, plaintiff’s right to collect rents is hindered. Under CCP §§ 564(b)(11) and (12), petitioner may be entitled to the appointment of a receiver over the Property as a secured lender to collect rents.

 

The Court notes, however, that appointment of a receiver would not be warranted under CCP § 564(b)(2), as plaintiff makes no meaningfully showing that the Property is at risk of being lost or materially injured. While plaintiff states that defendant’s related company was involved in an involuntary bankruptcy proceeding (RJN Ex. C-D) and that defendant’s principal appears to be absent and has refused to communicate with plaintiff (Alper Decl. ¶¶ 10-12), neither circumstance indicates that the Property is at risk and is in need of protection or oversight by a receiver to mitigate against such risk.

 

Lastly, given that defendant is in default and that plaintiff presumably may soon be able to obtain the relief it seeks in its Complaint by way of entry of a default judgment, the Court questions whether the expensive, drastic, and extreme remedy of appointing a receiver to oversee the property for a brief period of time pre-judgment in warranted.

 

III.      CONCLUSION

 

Subject to plaintiff convincing the Court at the hearing on this matter that appointment of a receiver to oversee the property pre-judgment is desired and warranted now that defendant is in default, the motion of appointment of a receiver would be GRANTED for the purpose of collecting rents from the Property.

 

If the Court GRANTS the request for appointment of a receiver, the Court finds that the appointment of Ryan C. Baker as receiver is appropriate based on his experience and the reasonable rates that he and his staff would charge. (Baker Decl. ¶¶ 1-3 & Exs. 1.).  In addition, the Court would issue the requested preliminary injunction in aid of the receiver’s duties to restrain defendant and its agents from interfering with the receivership.  Lastly, the Court would sign the Proposed Order for Appointment of Receiver, electronically received 4/24/26, with the following exceptions: (1) paragraph 2 shall be modified to reflect that the property placed in receivership is described in Attachment 2a; and (2) the Court declines to allow the receiver to employ “The Ryan Firm” as counsel at a “reasonable mkt rate.”  The Ryan Firm represents the plaintiff in this action, and its simultaneous representation of the Receiver would present a conflict of interest, as the Receiver’s duties, obligations, and loyalties are not to any one party in this action.

ORDER TO SHOW CAUSE

RE: PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION

                       

Date:               7/14/26 (1:30 PM)

Case:               Baron Bodnar, et al.  v. Golden Pocket Financial Inc., et al. (26STCV18786)

 

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Plaintiffs Baron Bodnar and Cannabaron Diversified LLC’s request for a preliminary injunction is GRANTED.

 

Plaintiffs’ requests for judicial notice of Exhibits 1 through 4, consisting of court filings in separate matters, is GRANTED per Evidence Code § 452(d). Plaintiffs’ requests for judicial notice of Exhibits 5 through 8, consisting of various recorded property documents, are GRANTED per Evidence Code § 452(c) and (h). (Fontenot v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 256, 264-65.)

 

I.          BACKGROUND

 

As represented by plaintiffs, plaintiff Baron Bodnar was introduced in 2018 to defendant James C. Gilmer, who is the managing member of defendant GHWL, LLC. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 4.) Defendant Frank J. Perez is also a principal of GHWL, LLC. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 4; Jackson Decl. ¶ 3.) Bodnar is the owner of the real property located at 11166 Burbank Blvd, North Hollywood, California 91601 (“Bodnar Property”). (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 2.) Bodnar previously owned the Bodnar Property in joint tenancy with his late mother, Mary Louisa Bodnar. (Ibid.)

 

On May 6, 2018, defendant James C. Gilmer sent an email to plaintiff Baron Bodnar, soliciting his investment in a cannabis business venture. (Bodnar Decl. ¶¶ 4-5 & Ex. A [5/6/18 Email].) The email indicated that the joint venture related to real properties located at 237, 241, and 245 East Hueneme Road in Port Hueneme, California. (Ibid.) The email further indicated that, in connection with the purchase of the 237 property, “an additional property/guarantor for cross-collateralizing” was needed and that “the investor [would] serve[] as a guarantor and a lien will be placed on the property to [partially] secure the loan.” (Ibid.) The email further claimed that there was potential financing from “Social Equity Lenders” in the amount of $1,160,000, but plaintiffs later discovered that the only lender was defendant Golden Pocket Financial Inc. (“Golden Pocket”), which is a hard money lender. (Bodnar Decl. ¶¶ 5-6.)

 

An “Executive Summary” for the proposed business venture was attached to Gilmer’s email, and it represented that the projected revenue streams between 2018 and 2020 would exceed $9.5 million, resulting in profits of $850,000 per year. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 5 & Ex. B.) It also represented that the business venture had an agreement with an experienced cannabis dispensary operator. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 7.) Defendant Gilmer represented that he had close connections with the City of Port Hueneme to ensure that the required permits would be secured. (Id.) To further induce Bodnar, defendants GHWL, LLC, Gilmer, and Frank J. Perez promised Bodnar that any encumbrance against the Bodnar Property would be limited, and they ultimately agreed for such limit to be $300,000. (Bodnar Decl. ¶¶ 8-9 & Ex. C [5/31/18 Text Message], Ex. D [6/5/18 Draft Lender’s Instructions].) Bodnar was further told that the debt would be non-recourse and that the encumbrance on the Bodnar Property would be removed by July 2020. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 10.) Bodnar contends that he relied on these representations before participating in the business venture. (Bodnar Decl. ¶¶ 10, 17.)

 

On or around June 5, 2018, a promissory note (“Note”) was executed by the parties involved in the business venture, but Bodnar attests that he does not recall signing the Note. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 12 & Ex. F.) By contrast, defendant Golden Pocket possesses a copy of the fully executed Note, which contains Bodnar’s signature. (Huang Decl. ¶ 7 & Ex. A.) Bodnar represents that, on June 18, 2018, the parties executed an Addendum to the Note that capped the total liability against the Bodnar Property at $300,000. (Bodnar Decl. ¶¶ 12-13 & Exs. G-I.) He further represents that Golden Pocket was willing to accept every term in the Addendum, except for a liquidated damages clause, and that Bodnar communicated that his participation was conditioned on the $300,000 limit. (Bodnar Decl. ¶¶ 14-15 & Exs. J-L.) Golden Pocket denies ever agreeing to such a limitation and claims to have rejected the Addendum. (Huang Decl. ¶¶ 9-12 & Ex. C.)

 

By June 19, 2018, plaintiffs entered into a “Letter Agreement re: Cannabis Projects” with defendants GHWL, LLC, James C. Gilmer, and Perez, limiting the liability against the Bodnar Property to $300,000, and, in exchange, plaintiff Cannabaron Diversified LLC (“Cannabaron”) would receive a 23% ownership interest in the business owning the venture’s real estate and a 17% interest in the businesses operating there. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 11 & Ex. E.) Cannabaron was incorporated by Bodnar to participate in the business venture. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 3.)

 

Thereafter, Bodnar executed a Deed of Trust, which was recorded on June 25, 2018 by Golden Pocket. (RJN Ex. 5.) The Deed of Trust indicates the lien against the Bodnar Property is for the full amount of the Note, i.e., $1,150,000. Bodnar represents that it was not his understanding that his property would be encumbered in that manner. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 18.) Plaintiffs also present a title report for the Bodnar Property, which indicates that Golden Pocket only recorded a lien against the property in the amount of $349,375 on June 25, 2018. (Fowler Decl. ¶ 15 & Ex. C [Title Report].) Bodnar maintains that he was denied access to the loan documents. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 16 & Ex. M [6/18/25 Email].) Additionally, Bodnar indicates that the Borrower’s Declaration falsely stated that the purpose of the transaction was for the purchase of the Bodnar Property. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 19 & Ex. N [Borrower’s Declaration].)

 

The GHWL Parties defaulted on the Note soon after closing on the loan, and they did not take measures to cure the default. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 20.) On December 17, 2019, Golden Pocket served a notice of default on Bodnar but later indicated that the notice had been served in error. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 21; Fowler Decl. ¶¶ 4-14 & Exs. A-B.) In communications with Golden Pocket’s former counsel about the notice of default, Bodnar’s counsel stressed that the limit of liability on the Bodnar Property was $300,000, which was not contested in those communications.

 

Years later, on December 4, 2025, Golden Pocket foreclosed on the 237 East Hueneme Road Property. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 22; RJN Ex. 6.) On December 15, 2025, defendant Integrated Lender Services Inc. (“ILS”) recorded a new notice of default against the Bodnar Property; Bodnar claims that he never receive the notice as required under Civil Code § 2924b(b)(1). (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 23 & RJN Ex. 7.) On March 27, 2026, ILS recorded a Notice of Trustee’s Sale, which was scheduled to occur on April 29, 2026, stating that the unpaid balance and charges amounted to $1,622,776.62. (Bodnar Decl. ¶ 24; RJN, Ex. 8.)

 

On June 11, 2026, plaintiffs filed this action against Golden Pocket, ILS, GHWL LLC, Gilmer, Harrison, Aguilar, and Clark, alleging the following causes of action: (1) declaratory relief; (2) reformation; (3) cancellation of instruments; (4) quiet title; and (5) fraud.

 

On June 22, 2026, the Court granted plaintiffs’ ex parte application for a temporary restraining order and order to show cause re: preliminary injunction. The temporary restraining order issued by the Court restrained and enjoined Golden Pocket and Integrated Lender Service Inc. or any of their agents from conducting the Trustee’s Foreclosure Sale of the Bodnar Property.

 

On June 26, 2026, plaintiffs filed their motion for preliminary injunction. On July 6, 2026, Golden Pocket filed its opposition. On July 9, 2026, plaintiffs filed their reply.

 

II.        DISCUSSION

 

Plaintiffs seek a preliminary injunction that enjoins Golden Pocket and ILS from foreclosing on the Bodnar Property while the proceedings in this matter are pending. As discussed below, the Court finds that plaintiffs have established the necessary factors to warrant the requested relief.

 

“[T]he question whether a preliminary injunction should be granted involves two interrelated factors: (1) the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits, and (2) the relative balance of harms that is likely to result from the granting or denial of interim injunctive relief.” (White v. Davis (2003) 30 Cal.4th 528, 554.)

 

            A.        Likelihood of Success on the Merits

 

Plaintiffs assert that they are able to establish a likelihood of success on the merits of their claims for fraud, cancellation of instruments, reformation, and quiet title against Golden Pocket and ILS. The Court so finds with respect to plaintiffs’ claim for quiet title, which is sufficient to support issuance of the requested preliminary injunction; in the interest of judicial economy, the Court declines to address plaintiffs’ likelihood of prevailing on their other claims.

 

A claim for quiet title results where a party seeks to “establish title against adverse claims to real or personal property or any interest therein.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 760.020(a).)

 

Here, Bodnar is the owner of the Bodnar Property, and defendant Golden Pocket claims an interest in that property in the amount of $1,622,776.62, which plaintiffs dispute. (Bodnar Decl. ¶¶ 2, 24; RJN, Ex. 8.)  Plaintiffs present sufficient evidence to support their contention that any encumbrance against the Bodnar Property, if proper, is limited to $300,000 per an agreement between the parties when the Deed of Trust was executed and recorded. (Bodnar Decl. ¶¶ 8-9, 12-15 & Exs. C-D, G-L; Fowler Decl. ¶ 15 & Ex. C).  The Court is unpersuaded by Golden Pocket’s claim that it had rejected the Addendum limiting the security for the loan to $300,000, as Golden Pocket has failed to produce any documents to meaningfully suggest they repudiated such limitation when it was presented. Golden Pocket’s unsigned copy of the Addendum, coupled by a self-serving declaration, is insufficient when weighed against plaintiffs’ evidence. (See Huang Decl. ¶¶ 9-12 & Ex. C.) In addition to plaintiff Bodnar’s declaration and that of his counsel, plaintiff’s evidence notably includes a copy of a title report indicating that Golden Pocket had recorded a lien against the Bodnar Property on June 25, 2028, in the amount of $349,375. (Fowler Decl. ¶ 15 & Ex. C [Title Report].)  All such evidence indicates that Golden Pocket’s ability to record a lien against the Bodnar Property was limited as plaintiffs suggest.

 

Accordingly, the Court finds that plaintiffs have established a likelihood of success on their quiet title claim.

 

            B.        Balance of Harms

 

The Court also finds that the relative balance of harms weighs heavily in favor of plaintiffs, as a foreclosure sale would result in plaintiffs’ permanent loss of the Bodnar Property. By contrast, Golden Pocket fails to identify what hardship it would face if the preliminary injunction were issued. ILS has filed no opposition to the request for a preliminary injunction, thereby failing to advance any possible harm it might face if the injunction were issued.

 

For these reasons, the Court also finds that the required undertaking plaintiffs must post pursuant to CCP 529 for the preliminary injunction shall be set in the nominal amount of $10,000.

 

III.       CONCLUSION

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS plaintiffs’ request for a preliminary injunction as to defendants Golden Pocket Financial, Inc. and Integrated Lender Services, Inc.

 

The Court will sign the Proposed Order Granting Motion for Preliminary Injunction, received electronically 6/26/26, with the addition of an order for a $10,000 undertaking per CCP 529 in the event it is later determined that the preliminary injunction was issued in error.
