Case Number: 23CHCV02211    Hearing Date: July 14, 2026    Dept: F49

Dept. F49

Case Name: Jose Antonio Mesa-Marentes, et al. v. JCC Logistics, Inc., et al.

Case No. 23CHCV02211

 

LOS ANGELES SUPERIOR COURT

NORTH VALLEY DISTRICT

DEPARTMENT F49

 

JULY 14, 2026

 

MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT, OR IN THE ALTERNATIVE, SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 23CHCV02211

 

Motion filed: 4/2/26

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant County of Los Angeles

RESPONDING PARTY: None

NOTICE: OK

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: An order from this Court granting Defendant County of Los Angeles’ motion for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication as to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: The Motion is GRANTED.

 

BACKGROUND

 

This action arises from injuries allegedly sustained by Plaintiff Jose Antonio Mesa-Marentes (“Marentes”) in a motor vehicle accident. On July 26, 2023, Plaintiffs Marentes and Maria Guadalupe Esquivel-Carreon (“Esquivel-Carreon”) (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) initiated this action. On February 2, 2024, Plaintiffs filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants JCC Logistics, Inc. (“JCC”), Steven Bloberg (“Bloberg”), CFJ Properties (“CFJ”), Tujhmm Inc. (“Tujhmm”), City of Lebec (“Lebec”), County of Kern, the State of California (the “State”), County of Los Angeles (“County of LA”), the State of California Department of Transportation (“DOT”), Caltrans, Pilot Travel Centers, LLC, and Does 1 to 100, alleging the following causes of action: (1) motor vehicle negligence, (2) negligent entrustment of a motor vehicle, (3) general negligence, (4) dangerous condition of public property, and (5) loss of consortium. At the request of Plaintiffs, the State, DOT, Caltrans, Lebec, and CFJ have since been dismissed from the action.

 

On April 2, 2026, County of LA filed the instant Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication (the “Motion”).

 

No opposition or reply papers have been filed.

 

ANALYSIS

 

“A party may move for summary judgment in an action or proceeding if it is contended that the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(a)(1).)

 

“The motion for summary judgment shall be granted if all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law. In determining if the papers show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact, the court shall consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers, except the evidence to which objections have been made and sustained by the court, and all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence, except summary judgment shall not be granted by the court based on inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence if contradicted by other inferences or evidence that raise a triable issue as to any material fact.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).)¿ 

 

A party may alternatively seek summary adjudication on whether a cause of action, affirmative defense, or punitive damages claim has merit or whether a defendant owed a duty to a plaintiff. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).) “A motion for summary adjudication … shall proceed in all procedural respects as a motion for summary judgment.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(2).)

 

The moving party “bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.) To meet that burden, the moving party must present evidence sufficient to show they are entitled to summary judgment as a matter of law. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subds. (c) & (f)(2); Monticello Ins. Co. v. Essex Ins. Co. (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 1376, 1385.)

 

“In order to prevail on a motion for summary judgment, a defendant must either disprove an essential element of the plaintiff’s cause of action or prove an affirmative defense that would bar such cause of action.” (Velasquez v. Truck Insurance Exchange (1991) 1 Cal.App.4th 712, 717.)

 

The pleadings serve as the “outer measure of materiality” in a summary judgment motion, delimiting the scope of the issues that may be triable. (FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991), 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 381.)

 

A.    County of LA’s Motion for Summary Judgment

 

Plaintiffs allege two causes of action against County of LA: (1) dangerous condition of public property, and (2) loss of consortium. (FAC, ¶¶ 41-67.)

 

(1)   Dangerous Condition of Public Property

 

Public entities are generally immune from liability from tort claims unless the claims are expressly authorized by law. (Gov. Code., § 815, subd. (a).) Liability for the dangerous condition of public property is governed by Government Code sections 830 et seq. (See Stack v. City of Lemoore (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 102, 108 [“Section 835 … ‘sets out the exclusive conditions under which a public entity is liable for injuries caused by a dangerous condition of public property.’”].)

 

“[A] public entity is liable for injury caused by a dangerous condition of its property if the plaintiff establishes that the property was in a dangerous condition at the time of the injury, that the injury was proximately caused by the dangerous condition, that the dangerous condition created a reasonably foreseeable risk of the kind of injury which was incurred, and that either: (a) A negligent or wrongful act or omission of an employee of the public entity within the scope of his employment created the dangerous condition; or (b) The public entity had actual or constructive notice of the dangerous condition under Section 835.2 a sufficient time prior to the injury to have taken measures to protect against the dangerous condition.” (Gov. Code, § 835.)

 

For a public entity to be liable, the property must be “real or personal property owned or controlled by the public entity.” (Gov. Code, § 830, subd. (c).) Easements, encroachments, and other property that are located on the property of a public entity but not owned or controlled by the entity are not public property. (Ibid.) An essential prerequisite to a cause of action for dangerous condition of public property is thus that the public entity against whom the cause of action is alleged own or control the property claimed to be in a dangerous condition.

 

County of LA contends that Plaintiff cannot establish such ownership or control. County of LA has the initial burden of producing evidence on this issue.

 

Plaintiffs do not oppose the Motion. On July 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed a stipulation executed by counsel for Plaintiffs and counsel for County of LA indicating that the parties agreed that Plaintiff would not file an Opposition to the Motion and County of LA would waive costs and attorney fees incurred in the action. (Stipulation, July 1, 2026, at p. 2.)

 

The undisputed facts thus establish that: (1) the roadway where the accident occurred was acquired from County of LA by County of Kern in the Frazier Mountain Park Road 1977 Joint Powers Agreement (the “Agreement”); (2) the Agreement establishes that County of Kern expressly assumed full maintenance, control, and jurisdiction over the roadway; (3) at the time of the accident, County of Kern was responsible for maintaining and inspecting the roadway; (4) County of Kern did conduct routine inspections of the roadway; and (5) striping and signage for the roadway is maintained by County of Kern’s maintenance program. (Undisputed Material Fact (“UMF”) Nos. 10-13, 15-18.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that County of LA has met its initial burden of producing evidence showing that it did not own or control the roadway where the accident occurred. This is sufficient to shift the burden to Plaintiffs to produce evidence establishing a triable issue of material fact. Plaintiffs have not submitted evidence in opposition. The Court thus finds that County of LA is entitled to judgment as a matter of law on Plaintiffs’ cause of action for dangerous condition of public property.

 

(2)   Loss of Consortium

 

The loss of consortium claim asserted by Esquivel-Carreon against County of LA is predicated upon County of LA’s liability for the injuries to Marentes. (FAC, ¶¶ 64-65.) This derivative claim accordingly stands or falls with the cause of action for dangerous condition of public property. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 746 [“A cause of action for loss of consortium is, by its nature, dependent on the existence of a cause of action for tortious injury to a spouse.”].)

 

Because County of LA has established entitlement to judgment on the cause of action for dangerous condition of public property, it is also entitled to judgment on the cause of action for loss of consortium.

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS the Motion.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The Court GRANTS Defendant County of Los Angeles’ motion for summary judgment.

 

Moving party to give notice.


Case Number: 24CHCV03732    Hearing Date: July 14, 2026    Dept: F49

LOS ANGELES SUPERIOR COURT

DEPARTMENT F49

 

JULY 14, 2026

 

MOTION TO STRIKE

Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 24CHCV03732

 

Motion filed: 2/23/26

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant Los Angeles Unified School District

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff C.S.

NOTICE: OK

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: An order from this Court striking portions of Plaintiffs’ Second Amended Complaint.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: The Motion to Strike is GRANTED IN PART, without leave to amend.

 

I.                   BACKGROUND

 

This action arises from the alleged sexual assault of Plaintiff by a teacher’s assistant while she was enrolled at Hillary T. Broadus Early Education Center. On October 11, 2024, Plaintiff C.S. (“Plaintiff”), a minor by and through her Guardian ad Litem, Ofelia Garay, initiated this action. On January 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) against Defendants Los Angeles Unified School District (“LAUSD”), Eva Lopez Duarte, and Does 1 through 50, alleging the following causes of action: (1) negligent hiring, supervision, training and retention (Government Code sections 815.2, 815.6); (2) sexual assault/sexual battery; (3) and intentional infliction of emotional distress.

 

On February 23, 2026, LAUSD filed the instant Motion to Strike (the “Motion”).

 

On June 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

 

On July 7, 2026, LAUSD filed a Reply.

 

II.                ANALYSIS

 

Upon motion by a party, a court may “strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading … [or] all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subds. (a), (b).) A motion to strike may be directed against a whole pleading or any part of it. (§ 435, subd. (b)(1).) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (§ 437.)

 

“In passing on the correctness of a ruling on a motion to strike, judges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth. [Citations.] In ruling on a motion to strike, courts do not read allegations in isolation. [Citation.]” (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)

 

A.    Meet and Confer

 

Before filing a motion to strike, “the moving party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the motion to strike.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 435.5, subd. (a).) “A determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to grant or deny the motion to strike.” (Id., § 435.5, subd. (a)(4).)

 

LAUSD’s counsel attests to meeting and conferring with Plaintiff’s counsel via telephone regarding the issues to be raised in the Motion. (Uyeshima Decl., ¶ 6.) The Court finds LAUSD’s meet and confer effort to be satisfactory.

 

B.     Motion to Strike

 

LAUSD seeks to strike portions of the SAC referring to the Child Abuse and Neglect Reporting Act (“CANRA”) and Education Code section 44691 as imposing mandatory duties on LAUSD. LAUSD contends that they are improper because neither statute imposes a mandatory duty on a school district. (Mot., at pp. 9-10.)

 

The SAC alleges that LAUSD “failed to perform its mandatory duty under Penal Code Sections 11164-11174.3 and Educational Code and Cal. Educ. Code § 44691.” (SAC, ¶¶ 5, 7, 24, 37-38, 48.) The SAC alleges that two individuals in particular, Jacqueline Lopez Castillo and Diana Moreno, had knowledge of the abuse of Plaintiff and failed to report it. (Id., ¶¶ 36.)

 

As a preliminary matter, Plaintiff contends that whether CANRA or Education Code section 44691 creates a mandatory duty is a merits determination that is not the proper subject of a motion to strike. The Court disagrees. The question of whether Plaintiff has asserted a proper statutory basis for liability bears on the issue of whether the pleading conforms to the law, which is a consideration expressly permitted by Code of Civil Procedure section 436. The Court thus finds that LAUSD’s Motion seeks appropriate relief.

 

Under California’s Government Claims Act, public entities are immunized “from liability for injuries arising from its acts or omissions except as provided by statute.” (Whooley v. Tamalpais Union High School District (N.D. Cal. 2019) 399 F.Supp.3d 986, 1000 [citing Gov. Code, § 815, subd. (a)]; see also Williams v. Horvath (1976) 16 Cal.3d 834, 838 [noting the Legislature’s intent to “confine potential governmental liability to rigidly delineated circumstances”].)

 

Pursuant to section 815.6, a public entity, such as a school district, “is liable for an injury of that kind proximately caused by its failure to discharge [a mandatory statutory duty] unless the public entity establishes that it exercised reasonable diligence to discharge the duty.”

 

“To construe a statute as imposing a mandatory duty on a public entity, ‘the mandatory nature of the duty must be phrased in explicit and forceful language.’ ‘It is not enough that some statute contains mandatory language. In order to recover, plaintiffs have to show that there is some specific statutory mandate that was violated by the [public entity][.]’” (Guzman v. County of Monterey (2009) 46 Cal.4th 887, 910-911, citations omitted [affirmatively imposing a duty and providing implementing guidelines]; see also State Dept. of State Hospitals v. Super. Ct. (2015) 61 Cal.4th 339, 348-349, citations omitted [“A mandatory duty is created only when an enactment requires an act that is clearly defined and not left to the public entity’s discretion or judgment. … When the enactment leaves implementation to an exercise of discretion, ‘lend[ing] itself to a normative or qualitative debate over whether [the duty] was adequately fulfilled,’ an alleged failure in implementation will not give rise to liability.”].)

 

(1)   CANRA Allegations

 

CANRA, codified at Penal Code sections 11164 et seq., requires mandated reporters to report known or suspected instances of child abuse or neglect to law enforcement agencies or county welfare departments. (Pen. Code, §§ 11165.9, 11166, subd. (a).) Under CANRA, only certain individuals are designated as mandated reporters of suspected abuse, including, inter alia: an employee, volunteer, or governing board member of a school or school district; an administrator of a day camp or youth center; a Head Start program teacher; and a public assistance worker. (Id., § 11165.7, subd. (a).) CANRA imposes no explicit reporting duty on school districts. (See ibid.) The Court accordingly agrees that CANRA may not give rise to entity liability under Government Code section 815.6. References to LAUSD’s failure to report pursuant to CANRA are accordingly improper matter and are not drawn in conformity with the law.

 

The Motion is GRANTED as to these portions of the SAC, without leave to amend.

 

(2)   Education Code Section 44691

 

Pursuant to Education Code section 44691, school districts must provide annual training to employees, volunteers, and other persons working on their behalf who are mandated reporters under CANRA. (Educ. Code, § 44691, subd. (c)(1)(A).) The annual training must use the online training module provided by the State Department of Social Services or an equivalent module developed specifically to meet the requirements of section 44691 and approved by the district’s “risk pool joint powers authority or liability insurance provider.” (Id., subd. (d).) The training “shall include information that failure to report an incident of known or reasonably suspected child abuse or neglect, as required by Section 11166 of the Penal Code, is a misdemeanor punishable by up to six months confinement in a county jail, or by a fine of one thousand dollars ($1,000), or by both that imprisonment and fine.” (Id., subd. (c)(1)(A).) School districts must also provide annual training on the prevention of abuse. (Id., subd. (c)(1)(b).) The foregoing training must be provided to school personnel hired during the course of the school year and to volunteers upon commencing volunteer services. (Id., subd. (c)(1)(C).)

 

The foregoing provisions unquestioningly impose duties on school districts. LAUSD contends that these obligations should not be considered mandatory duties within the meaning of Government Code section 815.6 because they do not specifically prescribe “how training must be conducted or what constitutes compliance,” permitting discretion in discharging the duty. (Mot., at p. 10.)

 

The Court disagrees. The statute clearly provides for: (1) the nature of training that must be completed (training regarding the mandatory duty to report under CANRA and the prevention of abuse, including information about the criminal penalties of failure to report); (2) the method by which training must be completed (using either the state training module or an equivalent module designed to comply with statutory requirements, submitted to the State Department of Education, and approved by the governing risk authority or liability insurer); and (3) the timeline for completion (annually and within the first six weeks of a person’s employment or commencing volunteer services). (Educ. Code, § 44691, subds. (c)-(d).) These requirements leave little room for the exercise of judgment as to the content, implementation, or completion of CANRA training, delineating clear guidelines that provide a basis for determining non-compliance.

 

The Court accordingly finds that Education Code section 44691 provides a viable basis for LAUSD’s liability for failure to discharge a mandatory duty. The Motion is therefore DENIED as to references to Education Code section 44691 and LAUSD’s breach of mandatory duty for failure to train.

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS the Motion IN PART, without leave to amend. The following portions of the SAC are stricken:

 

·         Paragraph 5

·         Paragraph 7, at page 3, line 11: “the Child Abuse and Neglect Reporting Act (“CANRA”)”

·         Paragraph 7, at p. 3, lines 11-12: “Penal Code §§¿11164–11174.3 and”

·         Paragraph 24

·         Paragraph 36

·         Paragraph 37, at page 8, line 24: “by CANRA” and “Cal. Penal Code § 1164-11174.3 [sic] and”

·         Paragraph 38

·         Paragraph 48, at page 11, line 2: “under Penal Code §§ 11164-11174.3 and”

 

CONCLUSION

 

The Court GRANTS IN PART Defendant Los Angeles Unified School District’s Motion to Strike, consistent with the foregoing ruling.
