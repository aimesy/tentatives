BACKGROUND

 

            The operative petition in this matter is the Second Amended Verified Petition filed February 24, 2026 (the “Petition”). According to the Petition, Fox News filed this action to obtain public records related to the relationship between certain L.A. County officials and the election systems company Smartmatic, and the process by which Smartmatic was awarded the contract to build voting equipment for and run Los Angeles’s federal, state, and local elections.

 

            The Petition alleges that Smartmatic, its executives, and parts of the L.A. County government have been the subject of allegations of corruption in government contracts. The petition contends that (a) Smartmatic officials provided items of value that were knowingly in excess of the statutory maximums to Dean Logan, who helped Smartmatic obtain the lucrative contract that Logan now oversees; (b) Logan failed to disclose those benefits as required; and (c) based on allegations by federal prosecutors, Smartmatic and its executives have funneled taxpayer dollars from Smartmatic’s L.A. County 2020 election contract to a “slush fund” held by a “shell company” that they used in the same pattern of misconduct that is the subject of federal indictments for bribery in obtaining and maintaining Smartmatic’s contracts in the Philippines.

 

            According to the Petition, the County has not produced all the records that exist related to these issues. Fox News asserts that Smartmatic leaders have instructed employees and third parties to delete incriminating documents and important communications.

 

            To learn more about the relationship between Smartmatic and LA County, Fox News submitted a series of records requests between October 2024 and February 2025. Between January and May 2025, LA County produced approximately 920 pages in response to the existing CPRA requests. (Fox Decl., ¶ 4.)  The CPRA requests between October 2024 and February 2025 are the basis for this writ petition.

 

            Petitioner then submitted additional CPRA requests in October 2025 (which are not related to this current CPRA action.) Since Fox News filed its Trial Brief on March 23, 2026, LA County has made three additional productions in response to Petitioner’s October 2025 CPRA requests. (Fox Decl., ¶ 5.) Petitioner contends that while this new production primarily pertains to documents related to the October 2025 CPRA requests, there are also documents relevant to the October 2024 to February 2025 CPRA requests. (Motion, 5:17-22.) The documents relevant to the October 2024 to February 2025 CPRA requests include documents related to (1) Dean Logan’s Smartmatic-sponsored 2019 Taiwan trip; (2) meals, dinners, and other benefits Logan received from Smartmatic; (3) communications on undisclosed platforms and in Mr. Logan’s personal accounts; and (4) evidence of unproduced text messages.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

            The California Public Records Act, Government Code § 7923.105 allows the Court to decide CPRA cases after considering oral argument and additional evidence as the Court may allow.

 

            Agencies are obliged to disclose records they can locate “with reasonable effort.” (City of San Jose v. Superior Court (2017) 2 Cal.5th 608, 627.) “Reasonable efforts do not require that agencies undertake extraordinarily extensive or intrusive searches, however.” (Ibid.) Agencies are “entitled to a presumption that they have reasonably and in good faith complied with the obligation to disclose responsive information.” (American Civil Liberties Union of Northern California v. Superior Court (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 55, 85.)

 

EVIDENCE SUBMITTED ON REPLY

 

The Court declines to consider the declaration of Jason Hale, which was submitted by Fox News on reply. “The general rule of motion practice…is that new evidence is not permitted with reply papers.” (Jay v. Mahaffey (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 1522, 1537.) The declaration of Hale appears to dispute some of the findings of the forensic examiner, who LA County hired to review records in this action. Fox News could have introduced this evidence with the moving papers.

The Court does consider portions of the supplemental declaration of Fox News’s counsel. Some parts of that declaration respond directly to LA County’s arguments in the opposition and could not have been raised at an earlier time.

MOTION TO AMEND THE TRANSCRIPT

            California courts have recognized that a trial court is not bound by a reporter’s transcription where the language “does not make sense” or “would produce an anomalous result.” (People v. Huggins (2006) 38 Cal.4th 175, 191.)  “Although the parties may call the judge's attention to what they deem inaccuracies in the transcript, it is for the judge to examine the transcript and to determine whether it fully and accurately reflects the proceedings reported. If the determines that it does he will certify the transcript. If he finds the transcript incorrect or incomplete, he will require the reporter to correct or amend it.” (Williams v. Davis (1946) 27 Cal.2d 746, 753.)

 

            Fox News requests to change the transcript of the May 8th hearing. The current transcript indicates that it was Fox News’s fault that the Court had to continue the trial date. Petitioner has been diligently prosecuting its case and pursuing records in this litigation; the Court did not (and does not) place any blame on Petitioner for the delay. LA County does not oppose an amendment.

 

            The Court agrees with Fox News that the transcript must amended, as Fox News was not responsible for the delay in the trial. The line documented on Page 15 of Fox News’s brief must be changed to “it is not your fault.”

 

MOTION TO AUGMENT THE RECORD DISCUSSION

 

            Fox News contends that the new documents would have materially affected Petitioner’s trial brief because they could have further argued that LA County’s response to the original October 2024-December 2024 CPRA requests was deficient. (Motion, 9:24-28.)

 

A.     Gifts, Meals, and Other Benefits

 

Fox News criticizes LA County’s argument that it ran its “standard protocol” search of its own Microsoft Suite and argues that the new productions are full of responsive records that LA County would have found using its standard protocol, if done in a reasonably diligent manner. (Motion, 10:4-9.)

 

Fox News argues that there are new documents in the very same places LA County claims to have searched in responding to the original CPRA requests. (Motion, 10:8-10; Fox Decl., ¶ 10.) Those new documents include (1) a May 6, 2019 email from Murphy to Mr. Logan asking for his “ideal dates for going to Taiwan,”  (2) a January 7–8, 2019 exchange in which Murphy invited Mr. Logan “for a beer tomorrow after work” to discuss Smartmatic costs and change requests; (3) a January 11, 2019 email in which Logan thanks Smartmatic USA’s President for lunch; and (4) a January 31, 2019 calendar entry reflecting a lunch with Logan, Murphy, and the Logan’s chief deputy. (Fox Decl., ¶¶ 11, 18, 19; Exh. E, F, M, N, and O.)

 

Fox News argues that these documents are directly responsive to two of the original CPRA requests, which ask for:

Any records, including communications, that reflect gifts, meals, or other personal benefits received by Dean Logan from the election systems company Smartmatic, any of its corporate affiliates, or any current or former executive, official, employee, contractor, agent, or representative of Smartmatic (hereinafter, “Smartmatic”) between January 1, 2015 and the present.

 

Any records, including communications, related to the July 2019 trip that Dean Logan took to Taiwan with Smartmatic personnel between January 1, 2015 and the present.

 

(Fox News’ Trial Brief, Fox Decl. ¶ 6, Exh. 5.)

 

The Court addresses the meals and gifts issue first. Exhibits N and O do not reflect that Logan received a gift, meal, or personal benefit. Exhibit N notes that Logan had lunch with a Smartmatic official named Kevin Shelly. (Fox Decl., ¶ 19; Exh. N.) Exhibit O has no text but seems to indicate that Logan had lunch with a Smartmatic official. (Fox Decl., ¶ 20; Exh. O.) Exhibit M involves a Smartmatic official asking Logan to have a beer after work. (Fox Decl., ¶ 18; Exh. M.) Exhibit M is a proposal for a drink. It is unclear if this beer date ever occurred. For similar reasons, Exhibits P and Q do not appear to reflect receipt of a meal.

 

            Fox News is not permitted to add any of these exhibits to the record for this case. These exhibits are not responsive to the original CPRA request. Because these exhibits are not responsive, the new records do not bear on the adequacy, reasonableness, or diligence of LA County’s search for records.  

 

Next, the Court addresses the Taiwan issue. LA County argues that the new record is not relevant because the Taiwan CPRA request was narrowed during several meet-and-confers between the parties. (Levin Decl., ¶ 3.) LA County states that it was required to produce only Dean Logan’s notice to the Board of Supervisors regarding the Taiwan trip and the Board of Supervisors’ approval in response. (Levin Decl., ¶ 3.)


            However, at the time of the meet-and-confer, LA County stated only that Fox News had agreed that LA County could limit its search of custodian mailboxes to Dean Logan and members of the Los Angeles County Board of Supervisors in the 2019 calendar year. (Fox Supp. Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, p. 3.) There is no evidence from the time of the meet-and-confer that Fox News was okay with narrowing the Taiwan CPRA request to only the notice and approval. Indeed, LA County promised that it would “contin[ue] to search for records that may be responsive” and “supplement its response.” (Fox Supp. Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, p. 4.) This suggests that LA County anticipated additional records would be produced.

 

The new exhibit asks for Logan’s ideal dates to travel to Taiwan and potential agenda items for Logan’s trip. (Fox Decl., ¶ 11; Exh. F.) This new exhibit is undoubtedly responsive to the original CPRA request and can be included in the record for this case. Other records are similarly responsive (Fox Decl. Exhs. G-K.) The records do not introduce a new theory or new category of evidence and simply supplement the record and fill gaps created by LA County’s responses to the original CPRA request.

 

It is possible that LA County’s search for Logan’s trip-related records might have been reasonable. That is a question that the Court may determine at trial. However, the fact that additional responsive records have since appeared does bear on the adequacy, reasonableness, and/or diligence of LA County’s original search for records.  

 

B.     Meetings between Dean Logan and Smartmatic

 

 

The original CPRA request asked for (1) calendar entries that include meetings or calls with employees or representatives from Smartmatic and Logan; and (2) any communications or records of communications from native mobile messaging applications, WhatsApp, or any other messaging platform, between Dean Logan and Smartmatic between January 1, 2015 and the present. (Fox News’ Trial Brief, Fox Decl. ¶ 6, Exh. 5.)

 

Fox News argues that there are new records related to meetings between Dean Logan and Smartmatic that deserve to be added to the record for this case. (Fox Decl., ¶¶ 17, 23, Exh. L and R.)

 

However, the first (Exh. L) is an email produced in response to Petitioner’s new CPRA Request for all emails with Smartmatic. The second (Exh. R) is an email invite dated August 13, 2025, which falls outside any time period captured by the original CPRA Requests, the last of which was issued in February 2025.

 

Neither Exhibit L nor R is responsive to the original CPRA request.

 

 Additionally, LA County has described how their original response was reasonable: LA County said that it searched for calendar entries reflecting Mr. Logan’s meetings that included someone using the Smartmatic domain (@smartmatic.com) as an invitee, and that it produced the records after de-duping Mr. Logan’s calendar from LA County’s other production. (Levin Decl. iso Respondent’s Trial Brief, ¶ 24; Chu Decl iso Respondent’s Trial Brief, ¶ 2.)

 

Fox News has not met its burden to explain why Exhibits L or R should be included in the record.

 

C.     Alleged Gap in Records

 

Fox News argues that LA County has tried to explain away an unexplained gap in Dean Logan’s texts with a Smartmatic official named Dan Murphy between June 2021 and June 2022. However, Fox News has introduced evidence that a newer CPRA request turned up a Murphy text message to Logan in January 2022. (Fox Decl. ¶ 26, Exh. U.)

 

This record would seem to be responsive to the following CPRA request:

 

any communications or records of communications from native mobile messaging applications, WhatsApp, or any other messaging platform, between Dean Logan and Smartmatic between January 1, 2015 and the present.

 

 (Fox News’ Trial Brief, Fox Decl. ¶ 6, Exh. 5.)

 

LA County argues that there was no gap at all, and there were 92 text messages between Dean Logan and Smartmatic officials, including messages involving Dan Murphy during the June 2021 to June 2022 period. (Levin Decl., ¶ 7.)

 

Fox News explains that these 92 text messages include examples of group texts with Logan and Smartmatic personnel, but there is an actual gap of direct messages between Logan and specific Smartmatic personnel. (Reply, 8:17-9:8.) Fox News argues that the direct messages are more relevant because Logan is more likely to have discussed undisclosed gifts, favors, or illicit schemes in group messages  (Fox Supp. Decl. ¶ 3; Reply 8:17-9:8.) This assertion at this point is speculation. There is no actual evidence that Logan and Smartmatic officials were engaged in illicit schemes in private text messages.

 

The Court finds, however, that it is necessary to include Exhibit U in the record for this case. Exhibit U is directly responsive to the original CPRA request. This record does not introduce a new theory or new category of evidence and simply supplements the record and fill gaps created by LA County’s responses to the original CPRA request.

 

It is possible that LA County’s search for Logan’s text messages during the alleged “gap” was reasonable. That is a question that the Court may determine at trial, not on this motion. However, the fact that additional responsive records have since appeared does bear on the adequacy, reasonableness, and/or diligence of LA County’s original search for records.  

 

Therefore, Exhibit U can be included into the record.

 

D.    Platforms Dean Logan used to interact with Smartmatic

 

Fox News argues that the new records indicates that Logan used Teams and Skype to connect with Smartmatic personnel. (Motion, 13:4-11; Fox Decl., Exhs. R-T.) According to Fox News, Logan previously stated as a response to a special interrogatory that he had only communicated with Smartmatic personnel via email, text messages, and at various times, calls on WhatsApp. (Motion, 13:4-7.)

 

The special interrogatory in requested the following:

 

Identify each method of electronic communication that You used to communicate with any Smartmatic employee, agent, or representative between January 1, 2017 and February 26, 2025, organized as follows: (a) email addresses, including any personal email addresses; (b) phone numbers, including any personal phone numbers; and (c) messaging platforms, whether on personal or County-issued devices.

 

(Levin Decl., ¶ 14, Exh. 6.)

 

The special interrogatory defined messaging platform to include:

 

any application, software, or service used to send and receive electronic text messages, including but not limited to WhatsApp, Slack, Signal, Telegram, Viber, Microsoft Teams, Skype, Discord, WeChat, Line, iMessage, Snapchat, or Facebook Messenger.

 

(Levin Decl., ¶ 14, Exh. 6.)

 

LA County contends that the new records are calendar meeting invites, not text messages on Teams or Skype, so they are not responsive to the special interrogatory. (Fox Decl., Exs. R-T.) LA County is not correct. The special interrogatory did not ask only for text messages. The special interrogatory asked for each method of electronic communication that Logan used to communicate with Smartmatic personnel. Electronic communication includes receiving and accepting (or declining) calendar meeting invites, even if no text-based messages are sent.

 

Courts properly consider evidence that is “supplemental to evidence submitted in the moving papers [and] not brand new” and is offered to “fill gaps in the evidence” already before the court. (RGC Gaslamp, LLC v. Ehmcke` Sheet Metal Co. (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 413, 431 [applying principle in anti-SLAPP context]; Riskin v. Downtown Los Angeles Prop. Owners Association (2022) 76 Cal.App.5th 438, 449 [in CPRA action, trial court properly considered post-judgment supplemental declaration where “[t]he declaration elaborate[d] on the usefulness of documents disclosed through the CPRA”].)

 

The Court finds that these calendar invites are examples of evidence that fills the gap in the current record. Therefore, Fox News can augment the official record with all examples of calendar meeting invites on Teams or Skype.

INTRODUCTION

 

            Petitioners Jeremy A. Gerami (“Petitioner” or “Gerami”), a self-represented litigant, and Gerami Architectural Corporation (“GAC”; collectively “Petitioners”) filed this petition for a writ of mandate directing Respondent Montebello Unified School District (“Respondent” or “MUSD”) to produce all records that are responsive to Petitioner’s requests made under the California Public Records Act (“CPRA”).  Respondent canceled a bid for architectural services, for which GAC was the only bidder, and then issued a new request for proposals after Respondent realized that “miscommunications occurred among its staff that resulted in unfairness in the bidding process.” (Reyes Decl. ¶¶ 8-9.)  Petitioner contends that Respondent “produced only public-facing materials while withholding internal staff emails, draft documents, and internal deliberative communications” related to the decision to cancel the bid and start a new bidding process.  (Opening Brief, (“OB”) p. 1.) 

 

The Court finds that Respondent is not permitted to with\old individual scorer’s worksheets after contract negotiations for the revised project were complete. The Court GRANTS the petition for writ of mandate IN PART, and orders Respondent to release those records to Petitioner in a reasonable amount of time, not to exceed 60 days. The Court DENIES the request for attorneys fees.

BACKGROUND

 

            On November 1, 2024, Respondent published informal request for bids (“IRFB”) number 16 for the 2024-2025 fiscal year.  (Reyes Decl. ¶ 5, Exh. 1.)  IRFB No. 16 sought bids for architectural services for a new cooler in the warehouse for its nutritional services department.  (Ibid.)  On November 16, 2024, GAC submitted a bid proposal in response to IRFB No. 16.  (Id. ¶ 6, Exh. 2.)  Petitioner is the principal architect of GAC.  (Id. Exh. 2.)  GAC’s bid was the only bid received in response to IRFB No. 16, and Petitioner, on behalf of GAC, was the only person who attended the job walk required for IRFB No. 16 bidders.  (Id. ¶ 7.) 

 

            In a declaration filed in opposition to the petition, Noe Reyes, Respondent’s Director of Procurement & Logistics, declares that the following occurred after GAC submitted its bid:

 

Staff in MUSD’s facilities and procurement departments verbally discussed that GAC’s bid appeared too expensive, and MUSD’s staff decided to solicit additional bids for the project. Particularly relevant to this action, it must be noted that MUSD employees regularly engage in verbal discussions and do not always reduce such discussions to writing. Unfortunately, miscommunications between members of MUSD’s facilities and procurement departments resulted in: (1) a member of MUSD’s facilities department verbally contacting new vendors to participate in a second job walk to familiarize themselves with the anticipated project; (2) the second job walk occurring without said job walk being publicly announced; and (3) a new IRFB No. 40 being generated for the same purpose as IRFB No. 16, without cancelling IRFB No. 16 first. Further, MUSD published IRFB No. 40 on January 17, 2025, after GAC’s bid price had already been publicly disclosed through publication of a bid tabulation. Based on verbal discussions not reduced to writing, that bid tabulation was revised to include the bid price proposed by another bidder.

 

Mr. Gerami began contacting MUSD’s staff in January 2025, apparently after realizing that MUSD published IRFB No. 40, asking why a new bidding process began regarding a project for which a bidding process had resulted in the public disclosure of Mr. Gerami’s bid. Those communications caused MUSD to realize that miscommunications occurred among its staff that resulted in unfairness in the bidding process due to Mr. Gerami’s bid price being publicly disclosed while MUSD continued to accept bids, enabling bidders to create their proposal with knowledge of Mr. Gerami’s bid pricing. To avoid awarding any contract through an unfair bid process, MUSD decided to cancel IRBF Nos. 16 and 40, and to begin an entirely new bidding process for a comprehensive “turn-key” project not simply to design a warehouse cooler, but to install the same as well.

 

(Reyes Decl. ¶¶ 8-9.) Apparently, Respondent sought bids for the “turn-key” project to design and install the warehouse cooler through IRFB No. 71.  (See Id. Exh. 5, 8.) 

 

            On or about April 7 and 16, 2025, Petitioner Gerami submitted CPRA requests to Respondent seeking various records related to IRFB Nos. 16 and 40.  (Id. ¶ 10, Exh. 3.)  On April 17, 2025, Respondent notified Petitioner that it was in receipt of the requests and would provide responsive, non-exempt documents within the following 14 days.  (Ibid.)  On May 1, 2025, Respondent notified Petitioner that it needed more time to produce records.  (Id. ¶ 11, Exh. 4.)  Later on May 1, 2025, Petitioner narrowed the scope of his April 16, 2025, request to seek:

 

All communications (emails, memos, meeting notes) between District staff regarding: The cancellation of IRFB No. 16;

The claim that IRFB No. 40 was a “duplicate” of IRFB No. 16;

Bid scoring, internal notes, compliance checks, and reviewer documentation.

The basis for canceling IRFB No. 16. The post-deadline acceptance of any bid under IRFB No. 16;

The issuance of IRFB No. 71, including any discussion of scope similarity.

Any legal advice or administrative communications involving:

Evaluation materials for IRFB No. 16….

 

(Id. ¶ 12, Exh. 5.)  According to Respondent’s letter dated May 13, 2025, Petitioner also made a CPRA request on May 7, 2025, that sought various other records related to IRFB Nos. 16, 40, and 71.  (Id. Exh. 8.) 

 

            On May 7, 13, and 21, and June 6, 2025, Respondent provided documents to Petitioner totaling 191 pages.   (Id. ¶¶ 13-16, Exh. 6-11.)  Respondent asserted that it was withholding certain records based on multiple CPRA exemptions, including the deliberative process privilege and the public interest exemption in Government Code section 7922.000.  (Ibid.)

           

            This petition for writ of mandate was filed on May 27, 2025.

 

SUMMARY OF THE CPRA

 

Pursuant to the CPRA (Gov. Code § 7921.000, et seq.)[1], individual citizens have a right to access government records.  Article 1, Section 3(b) of the Constitution affirms that “[t]he people have the right of access to information concerning the conduct of the people’s business.” The California Constitution mandates that the CPRA be “broadly construed,” while any statute “that limits the right of access” must be “narrowly construed.”  (See Nat’l Lawyers Guild v. City of Hayward (2020) 9 Cal.5th 488, 507.)  “Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be available for inspection by any person requesting the record after deletion of the portions that are exempted by law.”  (Gov. Code § 7922.525, subd. (b).)  

 

“To establish an agency has a duty to disclose under [the CPRA], the petitioner must show that: (1) the record ‘qualif[ies] as [a] ‘public record[ ]’ …; and (2) the record is ‘in the possession of the agency.’”  (Anderson-Barker v Sup.Ct. (2019) 31 Cal.App.5th 528, 538.)  Once that initial burden is met, the agency has the burden to prove “that a particular record is exempt from disclosure.”  (Sacramento County Employees’ Retirement System v. Superior Court (2013) 195 Cal.App.4th 440, 453.)  “Because the agency has full knowledge of the contents of the withheld records and the requester has only the agency’s affidavits and descriptions of the documents, its affidavits must be specific enough to give the requester ‘a meaningful opportunity to contest’ the withholding of the documents.”  (ACLU of Northern Cal. v. Sup.Ct. (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 55, 83; see also Getz v. Sup.Ct. (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 637.)

 

DISCUSSION

 

A.     Dismissal of GAC

 

Respondent contends that “because GAC is not represented by any legal counsel as is required, the petition must be dismissed with respect to GAC.”  (Opposition, p. 16.)  “[A] corporation cannot represent itself in a court of record either in propria persona or through an officer or agent who is not an attorney.”  (Caressa Camille, Inc. v. Alcoholic Beverage Control Appeals Bd. (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 1094, 1101.)  As shown on the cover page of the petition, the petitioners are Jeremy A. Gerami and Gerami Architectural Corporation (“GAC”).  GAC filed a substitution of attorney on December 24, 2025, stating that GAC would be representing itself.  The substitution was signed by Petitioner Gerami, who is not an attorney.  (See Salbert Decl.) 

 

GAC cannot represent itself in this action and cannot be represented by Gerami. On March 6, 2026, the Court (Goorvitch, J.) set an OSC: re: dismissal of GAC with prejudice for failure to obtain corporate counsel. (See CLD Construction, Inc. v. City of San Ramon (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 1141, 1149-52.) No additional filing by GAC or Gerami has remedied this issue.

 

Accordingly, the Court dismisses the petition as it relates to Petitioner GAC with PREJUDICE.

 

B.     Respondent’s Search for Public Records

 

Petitioner contends that Respondent did not conduct a sufficient search for records in response to his CPRA requests.  (Opening Brief (“OB”) 4.)  The CPRA “establishes a basic rule requiring disclosure of public records upon request.”  (City of San Jose v. Superior Court (2017) 2 Cal.5th 608, 627.)  The CPRA defines public records as “any writing containing information relating to the conduct of the public’s business prepared, owned, used, or retained by any state or local agency regardless of physical form or characteristics.”  (Gov. Code § 7920.530, subd. (a).)  In the context of the CPRA, the term “possession” has been defined to mean both actual and constructive possession. (City of San Jose, supra, 2 Cal.5th at p. 623.) However, as relevant to this petition, there are certain limits on a public agency’s duties under the CPRA. 

 

First, the CPRA does not require agencies “to generate new substantive content to respond to a PRA request.”  (National Lawyers Guild, San Francisco Bay Area Chapter v. City of Hayward (2020) 9 Cal.5th 488, 502 (National Lawyers Guild).)  “The rule means that, for example, agencies need not draft summary or explanatory material, perform calculations on data, or create inventories of data in response to a records request.”  (Ibid.; see also Haynie v. Sup. Ct. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1061, 1075.)  Second, the CPRA only requires public agencies to conduct a “reasonable” search for public records:

 

CPRA requests invariably impose some burden on public agencies. Unless a records request is overbroad or unduly burdensome, agencies are obliged to disclose all records they can locate “with reasonable effort.” Reasonable efforts do not require that agencies undertake extraordinarily extensive or intrusive searches, however.  In general, the scope of an agency's search for public records “need only be reasonably calculated to locate responsive documents.”

 

(City of San Jose, supra, 2 Cal.5th at p. 627, citations omitted.) 

 

Respondent submits a declaration of Noe Reyes, its Director of Procurement & Logistics, and written correspondence which summarizes Respondent’s production of records to Petitioner.  (Reyes Decl. ¶¶ 10-16, Exh. 3-12.)  Reyes also explains that Respondent’s employees “regularly engage in verbal discussions” and that not all communications concerning IRFB No. 16 were reduced to writing.  (Id. ¶¶ 8-9.)  Although Respondent does not describe the process by which it searched for records, its evidence suggests that its search was reasonable. 

 

Petitioner does not show that Respondent’s search was deficient.  Petitioner contends that “[i]nternal emails obtained by Petitioner confirm that MUSD staff communicated about … Job-walk scheduling” and that “[n]one of these internal communications were produced under CPRA.”  (OB 4, citing Exh. C, D-1, and D-2.)  Contrary to Petitioner’s assertion, Respondent produced the email communications and “job-walk scheduling” records shown in Petitioner’s Exhibits C, D-1, and D-2.  (See Reyes Decl. Exh. 7 and Exh. 10 at MUSD 1st Prod.000123, 000127, 000132, 000349, 000351.)  Nothing on the face of Exhibits C, D-1, and D-2 suggests that Respondent has additional responsive, non-exempt records that it did not produce.  Petitioner does not submit evidence of any other “internal emails” that show that Respondent failed to conduct a reasonable search for records related to job-walk scheduling.

 

Petitioner next contends that he obtained internal emails regarding “Bid receipt issues[,] Procurement irregularities[,] [and] Bid tabulation revisions” that Respondent did not produce.  (OB 4, citing Exh. E-1, E-2.)  Petitioner cites to his exhibit “E-1,” which is the initial bid tabulation for IRFB No. 16, and exhibit “E-2,” which is the revised bid tabulation.  Respondent produced exhibit E-2 to Petitioner.  (Reyes Decl. Exh. 7, 8 at MUSD 1st Prod.000054, 000346.)  The only change made between the initial bid tabulation (exhibit E-1) and the revised bid tabulation (exhibit E-2) is the addition of one more contractor.  Respondent submits evidence that no written internal communications exist regarding that addition.  (Reyes Decl. ¶ 8.)  Petitioner does not submit evidence of any other “internal emails” that show that Respondent failed to conduct a reasonable search for records related to bid receipt issues, procurement irregularities, and bid tabulation revisions.[2]

 

Finally, Petitioner contends that Respondent has not described “who conducted the search[,] what systems were searched[,] [or] which custodians were contacted.”  (OB 4.)  The CPRA does not require the agency to provide such information in response to a CPRA request.  (See Gov. Code §§ 7922.530, 7922.535, 7922.540; see National Lawyers Guild, supra, 9 Cal.5th at p. 502.)  Petitioner could have conducted discovery to obtain information about Respondent’s record retention policies and the reasonableness of its search.  (County of San Benito v. Superior Court (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 243, 258.)  Petitioner apparently declined to pursue discovery. 

 

Based on the foregoing, Petitioner has not proven that Respondent failed to conduct a reasonable search for records in response to his CPRA requests.  On the current evidentiary record, it appears that Respondent’s search for records was reasonable and legally sufficient. 

 

C.     The Deliberative Process Privilege and Catchall Exemption

 

Respondent asserts that it properly withheld “individual scoring worksheets for the purpose of ensuring candid evaluation and scoring by MUSD’s decisionmakers” pursuant to the deliberative process privilege and the CPRA’s catchall exemption.  (Opposition, pp. 12-15.) 

 

“Under the deliberative process privilege, senior officials of all three branches of government enjoy a qualified, limited privilege not to disclose or to be examined concerning not only the mental processes by which a given decision was reached, but the substance of conversations, discussions, debates, deliberations and like materials reflecting advice, opinions, and recommendations by which government policy is processed and formulated.”¿(Citizens for Open Government v. City of Lodi (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 296, 305.)  “The key question in every case is whether the disclosure of materials would expose an agency’s decisionmaking process in such a way as to discourage candid discussion within the agency and thereby undermine the agency's ability to perform its functions.”  (Rogers v. Superior Court (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 469, 477-478.)  The government agency claiming the deliberative process exemption must show that on the facts of a particular case “the public interest in nondisclosure clearly outweighs the public interest in disclosure.”  (See Citizens for Open Government, supra, 205 Cal.App.4th at 306; see also Times Mirror Co., supra, 53 Cal.3d at 1344.) 

 

Similarly, the catchall exemption in Government Code section 7922.000 “allows a government agency to withhold records if it can demonstrate that, on the facts of a particular case, the public interest served by withholding the records clearly outweighs the public interest served by disclosure.”  (City of San Jose v. Sup. Ct. (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 1008, 1017.)  “The burden of proof is on the proponent of nondisclosure, who must demonstrate a ‘clear overbalance’ on the side of confidentiality.”  (Id. at p. 1018.)  “If the records sought pertain to the conduct of the people’s business there is a public interest in disclosure. The weight of that interest is proportionate to the gravity of the governmental tasks sought to be illuminated and the directness with which the disclosure will serve to illuminate….  [I]n assigning weight to the general public interest in disclosure, courts should look to the nature of the information and how disclosure of that information contributes to the public's understanding of government.”  (Humane Society of U.S. v. Sup.Ct. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 1233, 1268, internal quotation marks omitted.)  Significantly, “for the public interest to carry weight, it must be more than ‘hypothetical’ or ‘minimal.’ Where a requester has an alternative, less intrusive means of obtaining the information sought, the public interest in disclosure is minimal.”  (LAUSD v. Sup.Ct. (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 222, 242, citation omitted.)

 

1.      The Public Interest in Nondisclosure

 

There is a compelling public interest in nondisclosure of individual scoring worksheets before contract negotiations are completed.  As stated by Respondent, “[n]ondisclosure of the preliminary scoring worksheets prepared by individual scorers serves the public interest by dissuading retaliation, interference, lobbying, and solicitation from prospective bidders who are engaged in a competitive process that provides an inherent incentive to influence decisionmakers to award a contract that could financially benefit a prospective bidder.”  (Opposition, p. 12; Reyes Decl. ¶ 13.)  In short, premature disclosure of individual scoring worksheets would impair Respondent’s “negotiation and selection processes.”  (Michaelis, Montanari & Johnson v. Superior Court (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1065, 1074.)

 

However, the contract negotiations for the revised project concluded on January 21, 2026, after the writ petition was filed.  (Reyes Decl. ¶ 9.)  Therefore, the issue is whether the public interest in nondisclosure continues after the bid has been awarded and the contract negotiations have been completed.  In Michaelis, supra, our Supreme Court held that a public agency may withhold the bid proposals it receives until the time an agency completes its contract negotiations related to the project in question.  The Court found a public interest in disclosure “after negotiations are complete.”  (Michaelis, supra, 38 Cal.4th at pp. 1073-74.)

 

Respondent seeks to distinguish Michaelis on the grounds that “future bidders could use individual scoring worksheets from past projects to formulate aggressive lobbying strategies aimed at what the bidder perceives to be a specific scorer’s evaluation methodologies.”  (Opposition, p. 15.)  This is speculation.   Respondent has not provided any evidence that “future bidders” are likely to obtain an unfair advantage from reviewing individual scoring worksheets from past projects.  Further, since this information would be available to all bidders, it is unclear how it would unfairly impact the bidding process for a future project. 

 

Respondent also justifies nondisclosure after contract negotiations have ended based on the following statement in Reyes’ declaration:

 

MUSD did not provide the individual scorer’s worksheets for the purpose of ensuring candid evaluation free from retaliation, interference, lobbying, and solicitation from prospective bidders who are engaged in a competitive process that provides an inherent incentive to influence decisionmakers to award a contract that could financially benefit a prospective bidder. More specifically, MUSD seeks to serve the public interest by ensuring that prospective bidders are unable to improperly exert influence over specific scorers based on any perception gleaned from individual scoring worksheets that some action taken with respect to an individual scorer might be likely to increase the combined final score.

 

(Reyes Decl. ¶ 13 [emphasis added].)  Reyes’ declaration does not justify nondisclosure because the bidding process and contract negotiations are completed.  There is no longer a “competitive process” in which prospective bidders could obtain an unfair advantage by reviewing individual scoring worksheets.  To the extent Reyes contends that individual scoring worksheets will provide an unfair advantage to bidders in future projects, that is speculation as discussed above.   Also, Respondent did not redact the names of the evaluators/scorers, Steve Sandoval and Tim Jones, from the final evaluation score worksheet.  (Reyes Decl., Exh. 7, MUSD 1st Prod.000347; see also Exh. 8, MUSD 1st Prod.000015.)  Therefore, Respondent cannot claim a public interest in nondisclosure of the names of the scorers. 

 

            Respondent asserts that there is not a public interest in disclosure because Respondent “has already disclosed the final score sheet that enables the public to see how it scored bidders on criteria initially disclosed to the public through the informal request for bids.”  (Opposition, p. 13; see Reyes Decl. ¶ 13.)  At the same time, Respondent concedes that the individual scoring worksheets contain information about “evaluation methodologies” that is not disclosed in the final score sheet.  Therefore, the disclosure of the final score sheet does not bolster the argument that there is a public interest in nondisclosure of the worksheets.

 

2.      The Public Interest in Disclosure

 

“[T]he competitive bidding process is intended to assure a healthy degree of competition, to guard against discrimination, favoritism, or extravagance, and to assure the best social, environmental, and economic result for the public.  Accordingly, the public may have a legitimate and substantial interest in scrutinizing the process leading to the selection of the winning proposal.”  (Michaelis, supra, 38 Cal.4th at p. 1074.)

 

In this case, the public interest in disclosure of the individual scoring worksheets is strong because of the circumstances leading up to the revised “turn-key” project.  As explained by Reyes:

 

[M]iscommunications between members of MUSD’s facilities and procurement departments resulted in: (1) a member of MUSD’s facilities department verbally contacting new vendors to participate in a second job walk to familiarize themselves with the anticipated project; (2) the second job walk occurring without said job walk being publicly announced; and (3) a new IRFB No. 40 being generated for the same purpose as IRFB No. 16, without cancelling IRFB No. 16 first. Further, MUSD published IRFB No. 40 on January 17, 2025, after GAC’s bid price had already been publicly disclosed through publication of a bid tabulation.  Based on verbal discussions not reduced to writing, that bid tabulation was revised to include the bid price proposed by another bidder.

 

Those communications caused MUSD to realize that miscommunications occurred among its staff that resulted in unfairness in the bidding process due to Mr. Gerami’s bid price being publicly disclosed while MUSD continued to accept bids, enabling bidders to create their proposal with knowledge of Mr. Gerami’s bid pricing. To avoid awarding any contract through an unfair bid process, MUSD decided to cancel IRBF Nos. 16 and 40, and to begin an entirely new bidding process for a comprehensive “turn-key” project not simply to design a warehouse cooler, but to install the same as well…. Notably, this incident caused MUSD staff to implement new policies and procedures designed to avoid the problems that occurred.

 

(Reyes Decl. ¶¶ 8-9.)  Because there was “unfairness” in the original bidding process, the public has a strong interest in inspecting the individual scoring worksheets for the revised project to ensure that the final bidding process was fair.  Since the contract negotiations for the revised project have ended, there is no longer a strong public interest in nondisclosure.  Therefore, Respondent has not shown a “clear overbalance” on the side of confidentiality for the individual scoring worksheets.  Those records are not exempt from disclosure and must be produced.

 

D.    Privilege Log

 

Petitioner argues that Respondent “did not identify any specific exemption applicable to withheld records and did not provide a privilege log.”  (OB 5.)  Contrary to Petitioner’s assertion, Respondent did identify the exemptions upon which it withheld records.  Specifically, in its May 13, and 21 2025, letters, Respondent asserted that it was withholding records based on the deliberative process privilege and the public interest exemption in Government Code section 7922.000, among other exemptions.  (Reyes Decl. Exh. 8, 9.)  Respondent was not required to produce a privilege log with its response to the CPRA requests.  (National Lawyers Guild v. City of Hayward (2020) 9 Cal.5th 488, 502 [“[A]gencies need not draft summary or explanatory material, perform calculations on data, or create inventories of data in response to a records request.”]; see Haynie v. Superior Court (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1061, 1065.)

 

The issue is whether Respondent should be ordered to prepare a privilege log at this time.   “[B]ecause the party opposing disclosure under any CPRA exemption always bears the burden of proving the exemption applies … on the facts, doubtful cases must always be resolved in favor of disclosure.”  (Essick v. County of Sonoma (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 941, 950.)  While government agencies are generally “entitled to a presumption that they have reasonably and in good faith complied with the obligation to disclose responsive information … that presumption cannot be permitted to obstruct the prodisclosure purposes of the [C]PRA.”  (ACLU of Northern Cal. v. Sup.Ct. (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 55, 83.)   

 

To carry its burden, the [agency] must describe the justification for nondisclosure with reasonably specific detail and demonstrate that the information withheld is within the claimed privilege or exemption. This process cannot require an agency to disclose the very information it seeks to protect. Having both the burden of proof and all the evidence, the agency has the difficult task of justifying its withholding¿the documents without compromising that very act by revealing too much information.  However, declarations supporting the agency’s claims of exemption must be specific enough to give the requester a meaningful opportunity to contest the withholding of the documents and the court to determine whether the exemption applies.  

 

(Golden Door Properties, LLC v. Sup. Ct. (2020) 53 Cal.App.5th 733, 790, citations and internal quotations and alterations omitted.) 

 

Significantly, if the agency’s declarations are not sufficiently detailed, the agency “should be afforded an opportunity to file supplemental declaration(s) in the superior court containing information from which the court may make an informed decision on privilege and exemption claims.” (Golden Door, supra, 53 Cal.App.5th at p. 792.)  “[T]he agency must describe each document or portion thereof withheld, and for each withholding it must discuss the consequences of disclosing the sought-after information.” (Id. at p. 790.)

 

Here, Respondent concedes that it withheld certain records based on the deliberative process privilege and the public interest exemption in Government Code section 7922.000.  (Reyes Decl. Exh. 8, 9.)  Significantly, Respondent’s CPRA correspondence indicated that Respondent may have withheld records based on several other exemptions:

 

The District is not providing records as exempt under one or more of the following:

 

• Attorney-client privilege. Evidence Code § 950 et seq.;

• Attorney work product. Code of Civil Procedure § 2018 et seq.;

• Drafts. Government Code § 7927.500;

• Official Information privilege. Evidence Code § 1040;

• Deliberative process privilege. Government Code § 7922.000;

• Exempted or prohibited pursuant to federal or state law. Gov. Code § 7927.705; and

• Public interest exemption. Government Code § 7922.000.

 

(Id. Exh. 8, 9.) 

 

Although Respondent was not required to produce a privilege log with its response to the CPRA requests, Respondent was required to provide sufficient information so that the court could determine that Respondent properly withheld all the records at issue.  (Golden Door, supra, 53 Cal.App.5th at 790-792.)  After a CPRA petition has been filed, the court has discretion to order the agency to prepare a list of responsive records for which it claims exemptions.  (See Haynie v. Sup. Ct. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1061, 1072-1072; see also State Bd. of Equalization v. Sup. Ct. (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1177, 1193 [“Although the Public Records Act does not, like the FOIA, require the maintenance of an index of records available for public inspection and copying, it¿does not prohibit a court from ordering the preparation of a list of the documents which are sought.”].)  

 

Respondent identifies the “individual scorer’s worksheets” as the only responsive records that were withheld.  (Reyes Decl. ¶ 13; Sandoval Supp. Decl. ¶ 5.) Respondent has complied with the Court’s March 6, 2026 order requiring a supplemental declaration affirming that no other responsive records were withheld. (Minute Order of March 6, 2026, p. 2.)

 

The March 6, 2026 minute order does not require anything else besides an affirmation that no other records were withheld. Petitioner contends that Respondent needed to  “identify the custodians involved in the search, the electronic systems or repositories examined, the search methods employed, or whether the communications relating to vendor outreach and the second job walk were included within the scope of the search.” (Petitioner’s Supplemental Brief, 2:2-5.) Petitioner does not cite any authorities requiring Respondent to provide a declaration that it conducted an “adequate search.” (National Lawyers Guild, supra, 9 Cal.5th at p. 502.) No such search adequacy declaration is required, because a government agency does not need to create new records explaining how it performed a search.

 

In his most recent filing, Petitioner cites Becerra v. Superior Court (2020) 44 Cal.App.5th 897, 930. In that case, the Court of Appeal found that a government’s declaration was lacking in meaningful detail because it did not show that the expense and inconvenience of responding to records outweighed the public interest in disclosure. (Ibid.) In this case, Respondent did not decline to withhold records on the grounds that expense and inconvenience outweighed the public interest in disclosure. Petitioner in this case does not contend that the government improperly balanced competing values (expense vs. public interest in disclosure) which determine whether records will be released. Therefore, Becerra is inapposite.

 

 

E.     In Camera Review

 

In camera review of the unredacted records is permitted under the CPRA, but it is not required.  (See e.g. Gov. Code § 7923.105(a); see American Civil Liberties Union of Northern Cal. V. Sup. Ct. (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 55, 74.)  Neither party has shown that in camera review is necessary at this time. 

 

F.     Attorneys Fees

 

 

Petitioner Gerami requests an award of attorney’s fees pursuant to Government Code section 7923.115.  (OB 5.)  Petitioner has not filed a noticed motion describing the amount of fees that he seeks or the legal and factual grounds for an award of fees.  Thus, Petitioner’s request for attorney’s fees is denied without prejudice.

INTRODUCTION

 

            Plaintiff Jennifer Troia, in her role as Director of the California Department of Social Services (the “Department”) has sued Defendant The Foodbank of Southern California (“Defendant”), along with a number of other defendants, some of whom are briefly mentioned in this ruling. The Department moves for a writ of attachment against Defendant in the amount of $13,074,987.74.

 

            The Court tentatively GRANTS the writ of attachment.  The Court invites argument, however, on the question of whether the attachment amount is properly established.

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“Upon the filing of the complaint or at any time thereafter, the plaintiff may apply pursuant to this article for a right to attach order and a writ of attachment by filing an application for the order and writ with the court in which the action is brought.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 484.010.)  “Except as otherwise provided by statute, an attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney's fees.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 483.010.) 

 

The court shall issue a right to attach order if the court finds all of the following: 

 

(1)   The claim upon which the attachment is based is one upon which an attachment may be issued. 

(2)   The plaintiff has established the probable validity of the claim upon which the attachment is based. 

(3)   The attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based. 

(4)   The amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.  

 

(Code Civ. Pro. § 484.090.) 

 

“A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 481.190.)  “The application shall be supported by an affidavit showing that the plaintiff on the facts presented would be entitled to a judgment on the claim upon which the attachment is based.”¿(Code Civ. Pro. § 484.030.)¿“In contested applications, the court must consider the relative merits of the positions of the respective parties and make a determination of¿the probable outcome of the litigation.”¿  (Hobbs v. Weiss (1999) 73 Cal.App.4th 76, 80.)  “The Attachment Law statutes are subject to strict construction.” (Epstein v. Abrams (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1168.) 

 

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

 

Defendants submitted 27 objections to the evidence presented by the Department. The Court has reviewed the substantive grounds for each objection and overrules all of them.

DISCUSSION

 

A.     Probable Validity of the Department’s Claim

 

The application is based on the Department’s cause of action for breach of contract.

 

The Department previously sought attachment against Defendant. The parties resolved that application through the January 29, 2026, Stipulated Order, which prohibits Foodbank from selling, transferring, or encumbering 1444 San Francisco Avenue and 828 West Cowles Street without further court order or final judgment.

 

The Department oversees government programs that provide critical food to vulnerable communities in need, including the Emergency Food Assistance Program (TEFAP); CalFood Program (CalFood); Emergency Food for Families Voluntary Tax Contribution Fund; California Food Bank Capacity Program; Coronavirus Response and Relief Supplemental Appropriations; Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security Act and Build Back Better Initiative; Coronavirus Relief Funds (CRF); and Trade Mitigation Food Purchase and Distribution Program (collectively, the Programs). (Kaiser Decl., ¶ 35.) Defendant was a TEFAP-eligible food bank from approximately 2006 to October 2024. (Kaiser Decl., ¶¶ 36, 46.)

 

A series of Memoranda of Understanding and other contracts governed the parties’ rights and responsibilities (Agreements) from 2016 to 2024. (See, e.g., Exhs. 1-5, Agreements.) The Agreements required Defendant to receive USDA-provided commodities for packaging, storage, and distribution; maintain accurate records to document their inventory of commodities, maintain written records and procedures demonstrating compliance with all state and federal requirements; and restrict use of funds to purposes approved by the Agreements. (Exhs. 1-3, at pp. 1-2, 4-6; Exh. 4, at pp. 5-7; see 7 C.F.R. § 250.19 [recordkeeping requirements], § 251.10 [reports and recordkeeping]; 2 C.F.R. § 200.337 [access to records].) Defendant was required to limit its expenditure of Program funds to “allowable” costs, i.e., costs that are necessary and reasonable for performance under the Programs. (2 C.F.R. § 200.404 [reasonable costs], § 200.403 [allowability of costs]; see Exh. 17, 2025 Cmpl. Rev., at pp. 4, 8 [allowable costs].)

 

Under the Agreements, Defendant was prohibited from engaging in self-dealing. (Kaiser Decl., ¶¶ 161-163; 2 C.F.R. § 200.318(c)(1) [procurement requirements], § 200.319 [competition], § 200.404(b) [reasonableness of costs]; 7 C.F.R. § 251.8(b) [applicability].) Defendant could not transact with any of its officers or directors, any relative by blood or marriage of such a person, or any entity in which such a person had a beneficial interest, absent full disclosure to and approval by the Board and a determination that the transaction was fair and reasonable to Defendant and for its own benefit. (Exh. 32, 1992 Bylaws, at p. 11; Exh. 33, 2023 Bylaws, at p. 4; Exh. 49, Form 990s [affirming conflict-of-interest policy].)

 

The Programs are “self-certification” programs, so in submitting a signed Request for Reimbursement (RFR) to the Department, Defendant “self-certified” that the expenses met all program eligibility requirements and complied with the Agreements. (Kaiser Decl., ¶ 37; Exh. 17, 2025 Cmpl. Rev., at pp. 2-3; see, e.g., Exh. 20, RFR, at p. 1.) The Department then reimbursed Defendant for expenses that Defendant attested under penalty of perjury that it incurred for the benefit of Defendant and in compliance with the Agreements and governing law. (Kaiser Decl., ¶¶ 38, 164.)

 

The Department retained the right to cancel the Agreement(s) immediately upon receipt of evidence that Defendant was not in compliance with the terms of the Agreement(s) or any TEFAP terms and conditions. (Exhs. 1-3, at p. 7; Exh. 4, at p. 8.) Moreover, federal regulation requires recoupment of “[p]ayments made for costs determined to be unallowable[.]” (2 C.F.R. § 200.410; see also 2 C.F.R. § 200.1.)

 

In June 2024, the Department received an anonymous whistleblower complaint alleging financial misappropriation at Defendant. (Kaiser Decl., ¶ 41; Exh. 6, Whistleblower e-mail.) In response, the Department conducted a compliance review at Defendant’s Long Beach headquarters. (Kaiser Decl., ¶¶ 42-43.) The Department contacted the California Highway Patrol (CHP) regarding its findings, and CHP obtained a search warrant which it executed on September 26, 2024, at Defendant’s headquarters, accompanied by Department staff. (Kaiser Decl. at ¶¶ 44-45; Exh. 7, Search Warrant.)

 

On September 26, 2024, the Department terminated the Grant Agreement and MOU-22 with Defendant. (Kaiser Decl., ¶ 46.) After terminating its contracts with Defendant, the Department repossessed several pieces of equipment that Defendant had purchased with Program funds. (Kaiser Decl., ¶ 47.)

 

Defendant subsequently informed the Department that it had commissioned an investigation earlier that year regarding the suspected theft and fraud of Cooper (apparently. Jeanne Cooper), one of the other defendants. (Matsushima Decl., ¶¶ 17-18.) Defendant provided the Department with the resulting report prepared by Affiliated Monitors, Inc. (AMI Rep.), which articulates findings of financial mismanagement and misappropriation by Cooper, Joe Briguiglio (another defendant), and others. (Matsushima Decl., ¶¶ 18-19.)

 

In March 2025, the Department issued its Compliance Review of Defendant. (Kaiser Decl., ¶¶ 48-49; Exh. 17.) The Department determined that Defendant submitted claims and received Program funds for expenses in violation of federal and state law, the TEFAP Manual, and the terms and conditions of the applicable Agreements. (Exh. 17, at pp. 3, 42.)

 

The evidence indicates that The Department and Defendant entered into several written Agreements between at least 2006 and 2024, and Defendant repeatedly breached these Agreements by requesting and receiving at least $13,074,987.74 in reimbursement based on misrepresentations to the Department that the expenses complied with applicable Agreements. The Department expressly detailed the basis for each dollar of damages it claims. (Kaiser Decl., ¶¶ 50-146; Exhs. 1-5, 15, 20-30, 65-79, 90-92, 102.)  This evidence is sufficient to show probable validity of a breach of contract claim.

 

In opposition, Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not actually established a fixed contract debt. (Opposition, 4:19-5:15.) This argument is unconvincing. Although Plaintiff’s legal claims arise from a variety of misconduct, every dollar identified is based on a contract Defendant had with the Department. Just because some misconduct may also sound in tort does not mean Plaintiff cannot seek attachment on the contract claims. (Samuels v. Superior Court of Los Angeles County (1969) 276 Cal.App.2d 264, 267.)

 

The Court concludes that Departments is likely to prevail on the merits.

 

B.     Basis for Attachment

 

“[A]n attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney's fees.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 483.010, subd. (a).)  “An attachment may not be issued on a claim which is secured by any interest in real property arising from agreement ….”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 483.010, subd. (b).)  “[A]n attachment will lie upon a cause of action for damages for a breach of contract where the damages are readily ascertainable by reference to the contract and the basis of the computation of damages appears to be reasonable and definite.” (CIT Group/Equipment Financing, Inc. v. Super DVD, Inc. (2004) 115 Cal.App. 4th 537, 541.)

 

Here, the Department’s application for writ of attachment is based on the contracts entered into with Defendant. There is no evidence that the claim is secured by an interest in real property.

 

The Department  has explained that typographical errors in the moving papers resulted in the total amount eligible for attachment being different than what is described in the underlying evidence. (Reply, 7:3-7:21.) The Court accepts the Department’s rationale and has not seen any admissible evidence which shows that the total amount subject to attachment is not readily ascertained.

 

The Court, however, does invite arguments as to whether the Department has adequately established the amount of its damages. The requested figure—$13,074,987.74—is very large. Defendant makes a number of arguments that appear to challenge that figure, including those asserted at pages 7-9 of Defendant’s 8/7/2026 Opposition. These arguments appear to be challenging the validity of Plaintiff’s damages figure but the arguments being asserted by Defendant are not clear to the Court. The Court deals with certain of the arguments below in the context of offsets, but is willing to entertain whether there is a flaw in the manner the Department is calculating damages. Both parties should be prepared to discuss this issue at the hearing.

 

 

 

C.     Purpose and Amount of Attachment

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 484.090 states that the Court shall issue a right to attach order if “the attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based . . . [and] the amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.”  The Department satisfied these requirements.  (See Application ¶ 4.)

 

D.    Reduction of Amount to be Secured

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 483.015, subd. (b) provides that the amount to be secured by the attachment shall be reduced by, inter alia: “(2) The amount of any indebtedness of the plaintiff that the defendant has claimed in a cross-complaint filed in the action if the defendant’s claim is one upon which an attachment could be issued.”  Defendant has the initial burden of proof to satisfy the requirements of attachment for any offset claim.  (See Code Civ. Pro. § 483.015 and Lydig Construction, Inc. v. Martinez Steel Corp. (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 937, 945.)  Among other requirements, “the contract sued on must furnish a standard by which the amount due may be clearly ascertained and there must exist a basis upon which the damages can be determined by proof.’” (See CIT Group/Equipment Financing, Inc., supra, 115 Cal.App.4th at 541.)

 

Defendant states:

 

If property or equipment purchased with state funds has been returned, transferred, credited, retained, or otherwise accounted for, those values must be addressed before Plaintiff can claim a fixed amount for attachment. Plaintiff cannot obtain attachment based on a gross figure while reserving credits, offsets, returned property, and valuation issues for another day.

 

(Reply, 9:22-25.)

 

            In calculating the amount to be secured by an attachment, the court shall account for the “amount of any indebtedness of the plaintiff that the defendant has claimed in a cross-complaint filed in the action[.]” (Code Civ Proc., § 483.015, subd. (b)(2).) Defendant, however, has filed no such cross complaint. Defendant has also submitted no evidence which would indicate any indebtedness of the plaintiff. Therefore, the amount to be attached will not be reduced.

E.     Exemptions

 

Defendant requests that the Court exempt their bank accounts. However, because Defendant is a corporation, there is no exemption for personal property, like bank accounts. (Code Civ. Proc., § 487.010 [all corporate property is subject to attachment].)  Defendant also did not introduce evidence to indicate that attaching bank accounts (or any other property) would exceed the claimed damages.

 

 

F.      Undertaking

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 489.210 requires the plaintiff to file an undertaking before issuance of a writ of attachment.  Code of Civil Procedure section 489.220 provides, with exceptions, for an undertaking in the amount of $10,000.  Plaintiff has not yet filed an undertaking at the time of the application, but must do so.  

 

G.    Subject Property

 

Plaintiff requests attachment of any property of Defendant.  (Application ¶ 9a.)  That request is proper.  “[A]ll corporate property for which a method of levy is provided by Article 2 (commencing with Section 488.300) of Chapter 8” may be attached.  (Code Civ. Pro. § 487.010, subd. (a).) 

 

H.    Attorneys Fees

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 482.110, subd. (b) provides, “[i]n the discretion of the court, the amount to be secured by the attachment may include an estimated amount for costs and allowable attorney’s fees.”

No attorneys fees or costs are requested.

 

 

 

 

 

 

CONCLUSION

 

The Court GRANTS the writ of attachment.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

[Tentative] Order on Application for Writ of Attachment 

 

 

INTRODUCTION

 

            Plaintiff Livingston Builders (“Plaintiff”) moves for writ of attachment against Defendant Javier Rodriguez (“Defendant”) in the amount of $34,956.42.

 

            The Court DENIES the writ of attachment.

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“Upon the filing of the complaint or at any time thereafter, the plaintiff may apply pursuant to this article for a right to attach order and a writ of attachment by filing an application for the order and writ with the court in which the action is brought.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 484.010.)  “Except as otherwise provided by statute, an attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney's fees.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 483.010.) 

 

The court shall issue a right to attach order if the court finds all of the following: 

 

(1)   The claim upon which the attachment is based is one upon which an attachment may be issued. 

(2)   The plaintiff has established the probable validity of the claim upon which the attachment is based. 

(3)   The attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based. 

(4)   The amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.  

 

(Code Civ. Pro. § 484.090.) 

 

“A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 481.190.)  “The application shall be supported by an affidavit showing that the plaintiff on the facts presented would be entitled to a judgment on the claim upon which the attachment is based.”¿(Code Civ. Pro. § 484.030.)¿“In contested applications, the court must consider the relative merits of the positions of the respective parties and make a determination of¿the probable outcome of the litigation.”¿  (Hobbs v. Weiss (1999) 73 Cal.App.4th 76, 80.)  “The Attachment Law statutes are subject to strict construction.” (Epstein v. Abrams (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1168.) 

 

DISCUSSION

 

A.    Probable Validity of Plaintiff’s Claim

 

The application is based on Plaintiff’s cause of action for breach of contract.

 

Plaintiff is a construction consulting company based in Los Angeles County, California. (Livingston Decl., ¶ 2.)

 

In or around 2021, Defendant contacted Plaintiff after discovering significant construction defects associated with renovation work that had been performed at Defendant’s property in Ranchos Palos Verdes, California. (Livingston Decl., ¶ 5.) Defendant retained Plaintiff in anticipation of litigation and dispute resolution proceedings arising from these defects. (Ibid.) The contract between Defendant and Plaintiff is found in Exhibit A to the Livingston declaration.

 

Pursuant to the Agreement between Plaintiff and Defendant, Plaintiff agreed to provide consulting and expert witness services, including investigation, inspection, analysis, reporting, expert consultation, arbitration support, and participation in mediation, arbitration, and/or trial proceedings as needed. (Livingston Decl., ¶ 7.)

 

Plaintiff would bill Defendant on a monthly basis for services rendered and invoices would be deemed accepted unless disputed in writing within 10 days. (Livingston Decl., ¶ 8.)  The Agreement further provided that unpaid balances would accrue a service charge of 1.5% per month, including on prior service charges. (Livingston Decl., ¶ 8.) 

 

The Agreement further provided that, in the event litigation arises from or relates to the Agreement or the performance thereof, the prevailing party is entitled to recover its reasonable attorneys’ fees, court costs, and litigation expenses. (Livingston Decl., ¶ 9.)

 

Plaintiff performed substantial professional consulting and expert witness services on Defendant’s behalf over the course of more than a year. (Livingston Decl., ¶ 10.) Plaintiff also provided substantial litigation support and arbitration related services in connection with Defendant’s anticipated arbitration proceedings concerning the defective construction work performed at the Subject Property. (Livingston Decl., ¶ 12.)

 

Defendant obtained an arbitration award and subsequent judgment totaling $794,925. (Livingston Decl., ¶ 15.)

 

Defendant made some payment to cover Plaintiff’s invoices, but eventually stopped paying. (Livingston Decl., ¶ 19.) Defendant did not dispute Plaintiff’s invoices in writing within the time required under the Agreement. (Livingston Decl., ¶ 20.) Defendant presently owes Plaintiff $26,896.42, which is a number ascertained by looking at Plaintiff’s business records. (Livingston Decl., ¶¶ 20-24.)

 

Defendant, however, claims he did not receive any activity logs that the Agreement requires him to receive; instead, Defendant just received monthly invoices far above the initial estimate provided by Plaintiff. (Rodriguez Decl., ¶ 9.)

 

The Court concludes that there appears to be a dispute in whether Defendant really agreed to incur specific expenses. Based on the record, the Court cannot say that Defendant breached a contract for a specific amount of money.

 

Therefore, it is not fully clear whether Plaintiff is likely to prevail on the breach of contract claim.

B.     Basis for Attachment

 

“[A]n attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney's fees.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 483.010, subd. (a).)  “An attachment may not be issued on a claim which is secured by any interest in real property arising from agreement ….”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 483.010, subd. (b).)  “[A]n attachment will lie upon a cause of action for damages for a breach of contract where the damages are readily ascertainable by reference to the contract and the basis of the computation of damages appears to be reasonable and definite.” (CIT Group/Equipment Financing, Inc. v. Super DVD, Inc. (2004) 115 Cal.App. 4th 537, 541.)

 

Here, Plaintiff’s application for writ of attachment is based on a contract entered into with Defendant. There is no evidence that the claim is secured by an interest in real property. The total amount subject to attachment is readily ascertained by a review of Plaintiff’s business records.

 

C.     Purpose and Amount of Attachment

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 484.090 states that the Court shall issue a right to attach order if “the attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based . . . [and] the amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.”  Plaintiff satisfied these requirements.  (See Application ¶ 4.)

 

D.    Reduction of Amount to be Secured

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 483.015, subd. (b) provides that the amount to be secured by the attachment shall be reduced by, inter alia: “(2) The amount of any indebtedness of the plaintiff that the defendant has claimed in a cross-complaint filed in the action if the defendant’s claim is one upon which an attachment could be issued.”  Defendant has the initial burden of proof to satisfy the requirements of attachment for any offset claim.  (See Code Civ. Pro. § 483.015 and Lydig Construction, Inc. v. Martinez Steel Corp. (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 937, 945.)  Among other requirements, “the contract sued on must furnish a standard by which the amount due may be clearly ascertained and there must exist a basis upon which the damages can be determined by proof.’” (See CIT Group/Equipment Financing, Inc., supra, 115 Cal.App.4th at 541.)

E.     Exemptions

 

Defendant did not seek exemptions.

 

F.      Undertaking

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 489.210 requires the plaintiff to file an undertaking before issuance of a writ of attachment.  Code of Civil Procedure section 489.220 provides, with exceptions, for an undertaking in the amount of $10,000.  Plaintiff has not yet filed an undertaking at the time of the application, but must do so.  

 

G.    Subject Property

 

Plaintiff requests attachment of any property Defendant.  (Application ¶ 9c.)  That request is proper.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 483.010, subd. (c) limits attachment against natural persons to claims arising out of a trade, business, or profession of a defendant.  To determine whether a claim arises from a defendant’s trade, business, or profession, courts consider whether the activity is one that “occupies the time, attention and effort” of the defendant for “profit on a continuing basis.” (Nakasone v. Randall (1982) 129 Cal.App.3d 757, 764.)

 

The underlying contractual dispute concerns Defendant’s residence. The undisputed evidence establishes that Defendant is a registered nurse employed by Kaiser Permanente, with no trade, business, or profession involving construction consulting, real estate, or expert-witness services. (Rodriguez Decl., ¶¶ 2–3.) Defendant did not retain Plaintiff in the course of operating a business — he retained Plaintiff solely to evaluate defects at his own personal residence in connection with his own personal arbitration against the home’s seller. (Rodriguez Decl., ¶¶ 6–8.)

 

Plaintiff’s analysis is unconvincing. Plaintiff cites Kadison, Pfaelzer, Woodard, Quinn & Rossi v. Wilson (1987) 197 Cal.App.3d 1, 4 as support for granting the writ of attachment. In that case, a trust hired a law firm to represent the trust in three separate actions involving the trust property. (Id., at p. 3.) The trustees eventually refused to pay the accruing legal fees. (Ibid.) As Plaintiff notes, the Court found that “the retention of an attorney to protect the commercial assets of a trust estate valued at $10 million … cannot be classified as a consumer transaction exempt from prejudgment attachment.” (Ibid.) The trust was engaged in a business of managing property, and so Code of Civil Procedure Section 483.010, subd. (c) could not bar a writ of attachment.

 

Those facts are not the same as the facts of the case currently before the Court. What business is Defendant engaged in? As Defendant noted, he has no trade, business, or profession involving construction consulting, real estate, or expert-witness services. (Rodriguez Decl., ¶¶ 2–3.) This dispute appears to relate to money used by a defendant primarily for personal, family, or household purposes, and Code of Civil Procedure Section 483.010, subd. (c) expressly forbids an attachment for these kinds of disputes.

 

Therefore, the Court cannot grant a writ of attachment.

 

H.    Attorneys Fees

 

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 482.110, subd. (b) provides, “[i]n the discretion of the court, the amount to be secured by the attachment may include an estimated amount for costs and allowable attorney’s fees.”

 

Plaintiff has incurred attorneys’ fees and costs in the amount of $8,060 in connection with enforcing its rights under the Agreement and prosecuting this action. (Livingston Decl., ¶ 9, Ex. A; Gutenplan Decl., ¶¶ 4, 9-11.)

 

 

CONCLUSION

 

The Court DENIES the writ of attachment.
