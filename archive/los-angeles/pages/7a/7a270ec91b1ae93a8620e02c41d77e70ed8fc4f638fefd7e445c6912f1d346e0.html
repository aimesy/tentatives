DEPARTMENT M LAW AND MOTION RULINGS



Case Number: 24SMCV02741    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Tambor, et al., v. Skirball Hospice, et al. 

CASE NO.:                24SMCV02741

MOTION:                  Demurrer and Motion to Strike the Third Amended Complaint  

HEARING DATE:   9/22/2026

 

LEGAL STANDARD 

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (CCP §§ 430.30, 430.70.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732, internal citations omitted.) 

 

A special demurrer for uncertainty is disfavored and will only be sustained where the pleading is so bad that defendant cannot reasonably respond—i.e., cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him/her. (CCP § 430.10(f); Khoury v. Maly’s of Calif., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Moreover, even if the pleading is somewhat vague, “ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Ibid.)  

 

“Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given.” (Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227.) It is an abuse of discretion for the court to deny leave to amend where there is any reasonable possibility that plaintiff can state a good cause of action. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) The burden is on plaintiff to show¿in what manner¿plaintiff can amend the complaint, and¿how¿that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading.¿(Id.) 

  

ANALYSIS 

 

Defendant Faith and Hope Home Health Inc. demurs to Plaintiffs Rina Tambor and Matthew Tambor’s Third Amended Complaint (“TAC”). Defendant moves to strike the requests for attorneys’ fees and punitive damages in connection with the elder abuse cause of action.

 

Medical Negligence

 

            Defendant argues that the first cause of action for medical negligence (survival damages) fails to state sufficient facts. The elements of a professional negligence/medical malpractice cause of action are: “(1) the duty of the professional to use such skill, prudence, and diligence as other members of his profession commonly possess and exercise; (2) a breach of that duty; (3) a proximate causal connection between the negligent conduct and the resulting injury; and (4) actual loss or damage resulting from the professional’s negligence.” (Borrayo v. Avery (2016) 2 Cal.App.5th 304, 310.) “Ordinarily, negligence may be alleged in general terms, without specific facts showing how the injury occurred, but there are ‘limits to the generality with which a plaintiff is permitted to state his cause of action, and . . . the plaintiff must indicate the acts or omissions which are said to have been negligently performed.¿ He may not recover upon the bare statement that the defendant’s negligence has caused him injury.’ [Citation].”¿ (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 527.)¿However, there is no requirement that plaintiff identify and allege the precise moment of the injury or the exact nature of the wrongful act.¿(Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)¿

 

The TAC states a claim for medical malpractice against Defendant. The TAC concerns the death of Yona Tambor. (TAC ¶ 5.) Plaintiffs are Decedent’s heirs and successors-in-interest. (Id., ¶¶ 14, 17.) The events giving rise to the claims occurred from April 15, 2022, through decedent’s death on March 25, 2023. (Id., ¶ 4.) Defendant held itself out as a health care provider licensed and authorized to practice in California. (Id., ¶¶ 7-9.) Defendant sent health care personnel or otherwise provided home health care and hospice care to Decedent. (Id., ¶ 10.) Defendant caused damages Decedent by “negligently, wantonly, recklessly, tortiously and unlawfully:

 

A. Took a history, examined, tested, diagnosed, cared for, treated, advised, supervised, handled and managed [Decedent];

B. Managed, maintained, operated, supervised, selected and controlled the health care providers, practitioners of the healing arts and its staff and equipment;

C. Provided health care, nursing and attendant care to [Decedent];

D. Failed to inform, explain, warn and advise [Decedent] of the dangers, hazards, risks and complications of the various health care procedures, care and treatment to be performed;

E. Failed to guard and protect [Decedent];

F. Withheld from [Decedent] facts and information necessary to enable [Decedent] to form an intelligent, free, real and informed consent to the procedures, care and treatment, of which facts and information [Decedent] was unaware; and

G. Conducted [itself] with reference to [Decedent] so as to proximately cause, permit and allow [Decedent]to suffer medical harm, injury and damages, as a proximate result of said negligence.”

 

(Id., ¶ 11.) The TAC further alleges that during the time period of April 15, 2022, and March 25, 2023, as a proximate result of the negligence of Defendant, Decedent suffered pain and suffering. (Id., ¶ 15.) As a result, Decedent required medical and related health care services. (Id., ¶ 16.)

 

            The TAC states sufficient facts to support a medical negligence cause of action and therefore, the demurrer is OVERRULED.

 

Elder Abuse

 

Defendant argues that the cause of action for Dependent Adult Abuse fails to state a cause of action under Welfare & Institution Code §15610 et. seq. because there are no allegations that it had a substantial caretaking or custodial relationship with Decedent. Further, Defendant contends that the TAC fails to allege facts evidencing reckless conduct on its part.

 

To establish a claim for Elder Abuse and Neglect under the Act, a plaintiff must allege the following: (1) plaintiff is an elder or dependent adult; (2) defendant had responsibility for meeting the basic needs of the elder or dependent adult, such as nutrition, hydration, hygiene or medical care; (3) defendant knew of conditions that made the elder or dependent adult unable to provide for his or her own basic needs; (4) defendant denied or withheld goods or services necessary to meet the elder or dependent adult's basic needs, either (a) with knowledge that injury was substantially certain to befall the elder or dependent adult (if the plaintiff alleges oppression, fraud or malice) or (b) with conscious disregard of the high probability of such injury (if the plaintiff alleges recklessness); and (5) the neglect caused the elder or dependent adult to suffer physical harm, pain or mental suffering. (Carter v. Prime Healthcare Paradise Valley LLC (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 396, 407; Welf. & Inst. Code, § 15610.07.) Elder abuse must be pleaded with particularity in accordance with the pleading rules governing statutory claims. (Ibid.)¿ 

¿ 

First, Defendant contends that the TAC does not allege it had a had a substantial caretaking or custodial relationship. “Neglect” within the meaning of section 15610.57 covers an area of misconduct distinct from professional negligence. (Covenant Care, Inc. v. Superior Court (2004) 32 Cal.4th 771, 783.) “As used in the Act, neglect refers not to the substandard performance of medical services but, rather, to the ‘failure of those responsible for attending to the basic needs and comforts of elderly or dependent adults, regardless of their professional standing, to carry out their custodial obligations.’” (Id.) The Act “does not apply unless the defendant health care provider had a substantial caretaking or custodial relationship, involving ongoing responsibility for one or more basic needs, with the [] patient.” (Winn v. Pioneer Medical Group, Inc. (2016) 63 Cal.4th 148, 152.) “It is the nature of the elder or dependent adult’s relationship with the defendant—not the defendant’s professional standing—that makes the defendant potentially liable for neglect.” (Id.) A caretaking or custodial relationship only occurs where a party “has assumed a significant measure of responsibility for attending to one or more of an elder's basic needs that an able-bodied and fully competent adult would ordinarily be capable of managing without assistance.” (Id. at 158.)

 

            The TAC alleges a robust and/or ongoing caretaking or custodial relationship beyond acts incidental to circumscribed medical care. According to the TAC, Defendant, through its managing agents and employees, had the care, custody or control and/or stood in a position of trust with the Decedent, and had a caretaking and custodial relationship with Decedent. (TAC ¶¶ 21-22.) Decedent was entirely dependent on Defendant’s staff and affiliated providers for her daily needs, including mobility assistance, incontinence care, repositioning, nutrition, hydration, medical attention, hygiene, and general supervision. (Id., ¶ 23.) The cases cited by Defendant were not resolved on the pleadings, and the evidentiary records in those cases demonstrated no custodial relationship. (See Oroville Hospital v. Superior Court (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 382, 388-392, 405-406 [at summary judgment, no caretaking or custodial relationship existed where defendants assumed home health services for wound care, and decedent relied on her granddaughter all other aspects of her care; wound care could neither be considered a “basic need”]; Kruthanooch v. Glendale Adventist Medical Center (2022) 83 Cal.App.5th 1109, 1118 [JNOV was proper where no evidence at trial supported a robust and ongoing caretaking relationship between an emergency room operator and patient; claims arose from burns patient sustained in the MRI machine caused by ECG pads negligently left on patient’s body, the relationship was of a limited duration and any attention to the patient’s basic needs was incidental to the circumscribed medical care it provided].) Defendant also argues that it provided only non-custodial care to decedent for a matter of days, from April 22, 2022, to April 28, 2022. Defendant cites no basis for this contention. To the contrary, the TAC alleges that the events giving rise to the claims occurred from April 15, 2022, through decedent’s death on March 25, 2023. (TAC ¶¶ 4, 15.)

 

Next, Defendant argues that the allegations do not show reckless conduct on its part. As noted, a plaintiff must allege that defendant denied or withheld goods or services necessary to meet the elder or dependent adult’s basic needs, either: (a) with knowledge that injury was substantially certain to befall the elder or dependent adult; or (b) with conscious disregard of the high probability of such injury. (Carter, supra, 198 Cal.App.4th at 407.) This requirement can be understood as constituting an “egregiousness” standard for Elder Abuse liability. (Id. at 405.) The egregious withholding of medical care can, depending on the facts alleged, constitute actionable neglect for the purposes of an elder abuse claim. (Id., at 405-406 [compiling several example cases]; Covenant Care, Inc., supra, 32 Cal.4th at 786; see, e.g., Fenimore v. Regents of the University of California (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 1339, 1349-50 [while lone allegations of understaffing and undertraining leading to injury are insufficient to arise to recklessness, they can demonstrate recklessness when there is a showing of a knowing pattern of violations of staffing regulations].)

 

The TAC alleges Decedent suffered from avoidable pressure ulcers and other injuries while under the care of Defendants, which would not have developed absent a pattern of custodial neglect. (TAC ¶ 24.) The injuries were caused by Defendant’s failure to reposition Decedent, monitor her condition, provide adequate hygiene, or ensure she received appropriate nutrition and hydration. (Id.) Defendant “willfully, intentionally, and/or recklessly caused or permitted Decedent to be injured and/or to be placed in a situation such that her health was in danger.” (Id., ¶ 25.) Defendant “actually or constructively” knew that Decedent was “at high risk for pressure ulcers and other preventable complications due to her medical condition and immobility, yet failed to take the necessary steps to protect her from these foreseeable harms.” (Id., ¶ 26.) Defendants “created circumstances or conditions likely to produce great bodily harm” and “willfully caused or permitted Decedent to suffer, and inflicted upon her, unjustifiable physical pain, injuries, damages, suffering and indignity.” (Id.) Defendant deprived Decedent of needed medical care at home, failed to ensure that said staff was adequately trained, and failed to properly treat and care Decedent, among other things. (Id., ¶ 27.) Defendant failed to exercise that degree of care that a reasonable person would have exercised in the care of Decedent in their willful and deliberate failure to provide to Decedent needed medical care, including their failure to appropriately treat or care for Decedent in light of her bed sores and the likelihood that she would suffer severe injuries from such, to adequately provide Decedent care in accordance with her plan of care, and other deficiencies all which led to Decedent’s serious injuries. (Id., ¶ 28.) As a result of this neglect, Decedent developed bedsores and other injuries. (Id., ¶¶ 29, 31.) Defendant is guilty of recklessness, oppression, and/or malice by due to its willful and intentional failure to provide Decedent with needed medical care. (Id., ¶¶ 30, 32.)

 

In summary, the TAC alleges that Defendant willfully failed to reposition Decedent, provide her hygiene, or ensure she received appropriate nutrition/hydration despite known risks of pressure ulcers. These facts suggest that Defendant acted with conscious disregard of the high probability of such injuries. Caselaw recognizes that failing to provide a patient with sufficient nutrition/hydration and leaving a patient unattended/unmonitored, despite a known risk of pressure ulcers, could be considered neglect under the statute. (Carter, supra, 198 Cal.App.4th at 405-406; Covenant Care, supra, 32 Cal.4th at 778 [failure to provide an elderly man suffering from Parkinson's disease with sufficient food and water and necessary medication, leaving him unattended and unassisted for long periods of time in his own excrement so that ulcers exposing muscle and bone became infected]; Delaney v. Baker (1999) 20 Cal.4th 23, 27, 41 [frequently left patient lying in her own urine and feces for extended periods of time, developed pressure ulcers despite plaintiff's persistent complaints to staff]; Sababin v. Superior Court (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 81, 83–87, 90 [facility ignored a medical care plan requiring the facility to check the dependent adult's skin on a daily basis and failed to notify a physician when pressure ulcers and other skin lesions developed].)

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is OVERRULED.

 

Motion to Strike

 

            In the motion to strike, Defendant asserts that the TAC fails to establish recklessness under the Elder Abuse Act, and therefore, their claim for attorney fees and costs and punitive damages should be stricken. As discussed above, the TAC establishes sufficient facts for imposition of the enhanced remedies under the Act. Accordingly, the motion is DENIED.

 


Case Number: 24SMCV03271    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Alvarado, v. Berk

CASE NO.:                24SMCV03271

MOTION:                  Motion to Dismiss

HEARING DATE:    9/22/2026

 

 

Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 367 provides that every action must be prosecuted in the name of the real party in interest, except as otherwise provided by statute. CCP section 377.31 states: “On motion after the death of a person who commenced an action or proceeding, the court shall allow a pending action or proceeding that does not abate to be continued by the decedent's personal representative or, if none, by the decedent's successor in interest.”

 

ANALYSIS

 

Defendant/Cross-Complainant Laura Berk moves for an order dismissing this action against her with prejudice. Cross-Defendant James Wolf joins. Plaintiff has not filed an opposition.

 

Berk’s request for judicial notice is GRANTED.

 

Defendant argues that dismissal is appropriate because Plaintiff passed away on September 22, 2025, and his counsel has failed to file for a substitution of a personal representative to prosecute this case pursuant to CCP sections 377.31-377.32. Defendant reasons that no party has standing to prosecute the action and it should be dismissed.

 

Defendants do not show any grounds for dismissal under the circumstances. For instance, Defendants do not show grounds for discretionary or mandatory dismissal for failure to prosecute. (CCP §§ 583.310; 583.240.) The text of CCP sections 367, 377.31 and 377.32 do not provide any mechanism for a party to seek dismissal. Defendants fail to show that the instant claims abate or do not survive Plaintiff’s death. (See Adams v. Superior Court (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 71, 79 [causes of action that abate due to the death of a plaintiff include, e.g., a marital dissolution proceeding; otherwise, the personal representative or successor in interest has an absolute right to be substituted for the decedent].) Caselaw suggests that dismissal would be inappropriate even with a three-year delay in appointing an administrator. (See Pham v. Wagner Litho Machinery Co. (1985) 172 Cal.App.3d 966, 969 [reversing dismissal on the five-year statute, finding that the three-year period between death of plaintiff and appointment of administrator tolled the five-year statute].)

 

Accordingly, the motion is DENIED.

 


Case Number: 24SMCV05841    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Menowitz v. Toppani

CASE NO.:                24SMCV05841

MOTION:                  Motion to Compel Deposition

HEARING DATE:   9/22/2026

 

Legal Standard

 

Service of a proper deposition notice obligates a party or “party-affiliated” witness (officer, director, managing agent or employee of party) to attend and testify, as well as produce any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing for inspection and copying. (CCP § 2025.280(a).) If, after service of a deposition notice, a party deponent fails to appear, testify, or produce documents or tangible things for inspection without having served a valid objection under CCP § 2025.410, the deposing party may move for an order compelling attendance, testimony, and production. (CCP § 2025.450(a).) The motion must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration, or, when a party deponent fails to attend the deposition, the motion must also be accompanied by a declaration stating that the moving party has contacted the party deponent to inquire about the nonappearance. (CCP § 2025.450(b)(2).) If the deposition notice included a request for production of documents, the motion to compel attendance must also show good cause to justify the production. (CCP § 2025.450(b)(1).) 

  

A motion to compel production of documents described in a deposition notice must be accompanied by a showing of good cause. (CCP § 2025.450(b)(1).) In other words, the moving party must provide declarations containing specific facts justifying inspection of the documents described in the notice. Courts liberally construe good cause in favor of discovery where facts show the documents are necessary for trial preparation. 

 

The motion to compel must be “made no later than 60 days after the completion of the record of the deposition.” (CCP § 2025.480(b).) This time limit also applies to motions based on a deposition subpoena for production of documents or a business records subpoena. The 60-day time limit runs from the date objections are served because the deposition record is then complete. (Rutledge v. Hewlett-Packard Co. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 1164, 1192.) 

 

Analysis

 

Defendant Dominique Toppani moves to compel Plaintiff Marc Menowitz to comply with his served Notice of Deposition. Defendant also requests monetary sanctions against Plaintiff and/or his attorneys Chase Law & Associates, P.A., jointly and severally, in the sum of $1,994.00.

 

            Defendant explains that they have attempted to depose Plaintiff since October of 2025. Defendant noticed several depositions, including for February 9, 2026, May 5, 2026, June 30, 2026, and July 27, 2026. (Beaton Decl., ¶¶ 5-12.) Each time, Plaintiff’s counsel advised that they or their client was not available and requested a continuance without providing dates for availability. (Ibid.) Most recently, on July 31, 2026, Defendant noticed Plaintiff’s virtual deposition for August 24, 2026. (Id., ¶ 13.) Plaintiff did not serve any objections. (Id., ¶ 14.) On August 24, 2026, defense counsel appeared virtually for Plaintiff’s deposition at the time indicated in the Notice, along with a duly sworn court reporter. (Id., ¶ 15.) Counsel and the court reporter waited for approximately 15 minutes for Plaintiff and his attorneys to appear, but they failed to do so. (Id., Ex. 12 [affidavit of non-appearance].) Counsel inquired with Plaintiff’s counsel regarding the failure to appear, but Plaintiff’s counsel did not substantively respond to the issue. (Id., ¶¶ 17-18, Exs. 13-14.)

 

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED. Plaintiff is ordered to appear virtually and give testimony at deposition pursuant to the terms of the July 31, 2026, deposition notice, within 10 days, at a mutually agreeable time and date. Monetary sanctions are awarded against Plaintiff and his counsel of record, jointly and severally, in the noticed amount of $1,994.00. Sanctions are payable to Defendant’s counsel of record within 30 days.


Case Number: 25SMCV06519    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Stephens v. Prestige Door Services LLC, et al.

CASE NO.:                25SMCV06519

MOTION:                  Motion to be Relieved

HEARING DATE:   9/22/2026

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

The court may order that an attorney be changed or substituted at any time before or after judgment or final determination upon request by either client or attorney and after notice from one to the other. (CCP § 284(2).) The attorney seeking to withdraw must take “reasonable steps to avoid reasonably foreseeable prejudice to the rights of the client, including giving due notice to the client, allowing time for employment of other counsel.” (Cal. Rules of Prof. Conduct, 3-700(A)(2). See, e.g., Vann v. Shilleh (1975) [holding withdrawal prejudicial where attorney withdraw from the representation of defendant on the Friday before trial began the following Monday].) “The determination whether to grant or deny a motion to withdraw as counsel lies within the sound discretion of the trial court.” (Manfredi & Levine v. Superior Court (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1128, 1133.) 

 

An application to be relieved as counsel must be made on Judicial Counsel Form MC-051 (Notice of Motion and Motion), MC-052 (Declaration), and MC-053 (Proposed Order). (CRC 3.1362(a), (c), (e).) The requisite forms must be served “on the client and on all parties that have appeared in the case.” (CRC, 3.1362(d).) When a client is served by mail, the attorney’s declaration must indicate that the client’s address was confirmed within the last 30 days and how it was confirmed. (Id.) If the attorney is unable to confirm the client’s current address, the declaration must state the reasonable efforts made within the last 30 days to obtain the client’s current address. (Id.)

 

Additionally, the declaration “must state in general terms and without compromising the confidentiality of the attorney client relationship why” a motion is brought instead of filing a substitution of attorney. (CRC, 3.1362(c).)

 

ANALYSIS

 

Shantel Yaghoobian, Esq., moves to be relieved as counsel of record for Plaintiff David Stephens.

 

Counsel only submits mandatory forms MC-051 and MC-053. Counsel provides a declaration, but does not provide the declaration on mandatory form MC-052. (CRC Rule 3.1362(c).)

 

In general terms, Counsel states that an irreparable breakdown of the attorney-client relationship has occurred. Counsel does not state that Plaintiff’s address or email were confirmed as current within the preceding 30 days. (See CRC Rule 3.1362(d) [“…‘current’ means that the address was confirmed within 30 days before the filing of the motion to be relieved. Merely demonstrating that the notice was sent to the client's last known address and was not returned or no electronic delivery failure message was received is not, by itself, sufficient to demonstrate that the address is current.”].) These omissions require denial of the motion. Otherwise, the forms note the hearings that are on calendar, including the September 29, 2026, CMC/OSC. Given that trial is not yet set, the client would not be prejudiced by the withdrawal.

 

Accordingly, the motion is DENIED without prejudice.
