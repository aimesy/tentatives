[Tentative] Order Denying Petition for Writ of Mandate

           

INTRODUCTION

 

             Petitioner John Doe (“Petitioner”) seeks a writ of mandate compelling Respondent Naddia Palacios and the University of Southern California (“USC”) (collectively, “Respondents”) to set aside USC’s indefinite suspension of Petitioner. 

 

The Court DENIES the petition for writ of mandate.

BACKGROUND

 

A.     The Attack and USC’s policies

 

On December 10, 2025, USC imposed a  suspension on Petitioner, based on an allegation by a student that on November 29, 2025, during the UCLA-USC football game at the Coliseum, following a verbal argument over seating issues, Petitioner struck the other student in the face with a closed fist which resulted in the student losing two teeth, and then “fled the scene.” (AR 1.)

 

Petitioner’s actions violated a policy in the Student Handbook, which prohibits, among other things, “[c]ausing, intending to cause, or threatening physical harm against a person or group, or behavior that reasonably causes apprehension of harm… This includes fighting or other physical acts that may jeopardize the health, safety, or welfare of an individual or group.” (AR 73.)

 

USC’s rules allow it to take interim measures to protect its community when it receives reports of misconduct. (AR 85.) Interim measures are not disciplinary in nature, and do not indicate the university has decided the veracity of the allegation(s), but are put in place to protect the health, safety, and/or welfare of the university community or any member of it and to prevent the substantial disruption of university operations. (AR 85.)

 

Interim measures may be imposed when there is reasonably credible information that, if assumed true, indicates the student and/or organization poses a substantial threat to any of the following: (1) the safety or well-being of anyone in the university community; (2) the property within the university community; or (3) the orderly operation of the university. (AR 83.)

In deciding to suspend Petitioner, the Interim Action Committee (“IAC”) considered:

 

•         the significant physical harm that Petitioner’s conduct caused another student, i.e., the loss of two teeth;

•         the seemingly random and unprovoked nature of Petitioner’s conduct, and his subsequent decision to flee the scene, which suggests the risk of additional and continued harm to members of the USC community; and

•          the University’s obligation to provide a safe environment.

 

Petitioner’s actions also led to the Los Angeles Police Department pursuing a criminal charge for aggravated assault. (AR 39-40)

 

The suspension was to remain in effect until the final resolution of the university’s disciplinary process and/or conclusion of law enforcement proceedings. (AR 1-3.)

 

USC did not provide any evidence or other information relied upon to impose the immediate suspension with no notice and hearing. (AR 1, 191, 22.)

 

B.     Petitioner’s appeal

 

On December 17, 2025, Petitioner appealed his suspension, arguing that less restrictive measures could adequately reduce or mitigate the threat. (AR 23-27.)

 

On December 22, 2025, Respondents denied Petitioner’s appeal on the grounds that the appeal failed to “present[] a basis from which to conclude that the less restrictive measures you proposed would be sufficient to adequately reduce or mitigate the substantial threat indicated by the reported information.” (AR25.)

 

On December 23, 2025, USC notified Petitioner that he would be able to complete his courses in Spring 2026 remotely or asynchronously. (AR 30.)

 

C.     Student rights at USC

 

The USC Disciplinary Process and the Student Procedural Rights and Protections provides that:

•         the accused student is presumed not responsible, i.e. innocent;

•         the student has a right to decline to participate;

•         the student has a right to inspect the evidence (“relevant documents and relevant information gathered)” but is not entitled to have a copy of the evidence;

•         the student does not have the right to confront or cross-examine (either directly or indirectly) either accusers or witnesses;

•         the student does not have the right to be represented by counsel;

•          the student is entitled to a fair and impartial review of the incident; and

•          a determination of responsibility must overcome the presumption of innocence with a preponderance of the evidence that a violation occurred.

 

(AR 76-103.)

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

The petition is brought pursuant to CCP section 1085.  There are two essential requirements to the issuance of an ordinary writ of mandate under Code of Civil Procedure section 1085: (1) a clear, present, and ministerial duty on the part of the respondent, and (2) a clear, present, and beneficial right on the part of the petitioner to the performance of that duty. (California Ass’n for Health Services at Home v. Department of Health Services (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 696, 704.) “An action in ordinary mandamus is proper where … the claim is that an agency has failed to act as required by law.” (Id. at 705.) 

 

“Generally, mandamus is available to compel a…agency's performance or to correct an agency's abuse of discretion when the action being compelled or corrected is ministerial.”  (AIDS Healthcare Foundation v. Los Angeles County Dept. of Public Health (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 693, 700.)  “‘While, of course, it is the general rule that mandamus will not lie to control the discretion of a court or officer, meaning by that that it will not lie to force the exercise of discretion in a particular manner ... [it] will lie to correct abuses of discretion, and will lie to force a particular action by the inferior tribunal or officer, when the law clearly establishes the petitioner's right to such action.’”  (Flores v. Dept. of Corrections & Rehabilitation (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 199, 208.)   

 

An agency is presumed to have regularly performed its official duties.  (Evid. Code § 664.) Petitioner “bears the burden of proof in a mandate proceeding brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 1085.”  (California Correctional Peace Officers Assn. v. State Personnel Bd. (1995) 10 Cal.4th 1133, 1154.)   

 

“‘On questions of law arising in mandate proceedings, [the court] exercise[s] independent judgment.’ ….¿Interpretation of a statute or regulation is a question of law subject to independent review.”  (Christensen v. Lightbourne (2017) 15 Cal.App.5th 1239, 1251.)   

 

DISCUSSION

 

Petitioner’s opening brief includes a section that describes why he cannot seek relief under Code of Civil Procedure Section 1094.5, subd. (b). (Opening Brief, 8:11-19.)

 

Petitioner’s request for relief under Code of Civil Procedure Section 1085 does not include any discussion of what ministerial duty USC has not complied with. Petitioner also fails to allege how USC’s decision was an abuse of discretion. Therefore, Petitioner cannot seek relief under Code of Civil Procedure Section 1085.

 

Almost all of the California cases cited in Petitioner’s brief relate to notice and a hearing as it relates to a final disciplinary decision, not the emergency, interim suspension at here. In fact, Petitioner does not identify a single case or statute that would require USC to provide notice and a hearing before issuing an emergency, interim suspension to protect its community.

 

The cases from non-California jurisdictions are inapposite. All three cases involve public universities and constitutional due process issues. Petitioner does not have a constitutional right at stake in this case. (Boermeester v. Carry (2023) 15 Cal.5th 72, 87 [“Because this matter involves a private university, no constitutional rights are at stake and a greater degree of flexibility is warranted”].)

 

The Court also finds that USC’s process was fair and was within the university’s discretion, based on the student handbook (AR 83 [“interim measures may be imposed when there is reasonably credible information” that a student poses a substantial threat to anyone in the university community].) At the time the interim suspension was issued, USC provided Petitioner with a detailed notice of the allegations against him, and the facts and analysis that formed the basis of the interim suspension. (AR 1-3.) Petitioner had a clear notice of the factual basis for the interim suspension and had an opportunity to appeal to ask for less restrictive measures. (AR 23-24.) Thus, Petitioner had adequate notice of the charges and reasonable opportunity to respond.

 

Petitioner appears to allege that evidence was withheld, but the record adequately indicates that Petitioner was provided detailed notice of the facts. (AR 1-3.) Petitioner has not identified any facts from the DPS report that he now has that he purportedly did not know and was unable to respond to in his appeal. (AR 23-24.) Petitioner’s argument that the burden has shifted to him to disprove the allegations is also not persuasive. USC has not made any final determination of the allegations against Petitioner or placed the burden on him to prove his innocence.

 

Petitioner also cannot demonstrate that USC’s actions were an abuse of discretion. As discussed above, the handbook gives USC the discretion to issue an interim suspension when there is concern about student safety. That is what USC did here.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The Court DENIES the petition for writ of mandate. Pursuant to Local Rule 3.231, subd. (n), Respondents shall prepare, serve, and ultimately file a proposed judgment.

[Tentative] Order Granting Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

 

 

Respondent California Department of Fish and Wildlife (the “Department”) moves for judgment on the pleadings filed by Petitioner Ballona Wetlands Land Trust (“Petitioner”).

 

             The Court GRANTS the Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings WITH LEAVE TO AMEND. Petitioner must amend within 20 days. The Court CONTINUES the trial setting conference to November 18, 2026.

 

            As explained in more detail below, the Court concludes that the Petition adequately alleges that funds have been allocated by the Legislature for drafting a land management plan. However, the petition does not contain sufficient allegations to overcome the statute of limitations problem raised by the pleading itself. Petitioner will be given an opportunity to address the statute of limitations issue in an amended pleading.

 

BACKGROUND

 

            On January 15, 2025, Petitioner filed a Petition for Writ of Mandate asking the Department to comply with statutory obligations under California Fish and Game Code section 1019 to prepare and publish for public review a draft Land Management Plan (“LMP”) for the Ballona Wetlands Ecological Reserve (“Ballona Wetlands Reserve”).  (Petition, ¶ 1.)

 

            According to the Petition, LMPs are critical guidance documents for the management and operation of Department properties and serve several crucial functions, including outlining the management of habitats and wildlife, balancing public and recreational uses, providing budgetary and management goals, and ensuring compliance with federal, state, and local environmental laws. (Petition, ¶ 2.)

 

            The Petition alleges that the Department was required under Fish and Game Code section 1019, subd. (a) to prepare a draft LMP for public review within 18 months of the date of recordation of the State’s ownership of the Ballona Wetlands Reserve, a deadline that elapsed decades ago. (Petition, ¶ 3.) According to the Petition, the property that now constitutes the Ballona Wetlands Reserve was acquired by the State in a series of transactions occurring between 2000 and 2004, and the parcels at issue were recorded “no later than February 17, 2004.” (Petition, ¶¶ 35-41, 65.) On August 19, 2005, the California Fish and Game Commission designated the property as an “ecological reserve” pursuant to Fish and Game Code section 1580 et seq. (Petition ¶¶ 43, 67) Petitioner alleges that the Department “was statutorily obligated to complete preparation and release of the draft LMP [land management plan] for the Reserve by no later than February 19, 2007 (i.e., within 18 months of August 19, 2005).” (Petition ¶ 67.)

 

Petitioner further alleges that the Department is in the process of preparing an LMP and that the initial expected completion date of 2024 was extended to 2025 and then 2026. (Id. ¶¶ 46, 51.) Petitioner contends that the Department has failed to prepare and release a draft LMP for the Reserve within the 18-month period required under Section 109, subd. (a). (Petition, ¶ 3.)

 

            The Petition also claims that “[a]ny improper delay or conditioning by [the Department] in releasing the [Ballona Wetlands Reserve’s] LMP until implementation of other projects at the Reserve, including, but not limited to, the Ballona Wetlands Restoration Project and its CEQA environmental review, is legally indefensible and would contravene both the intent and purpose of Section 1019(a) and [the Department’s] organizational mission set forth in Fish and Game Code section 712.1(a). (Petition, ¶ 5.)  

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

Either prior to trial, but after the time to answer or demur has passed, or at the trial, the petitioner or respondent may move for judgment on the pleadings and that the appropriate ground for such a motion is the same as that arguable by general demurrer, namely, the failure to state a cause of action or defense. (Dobbins v. Hardister (1966) 242 Cal.App.2d 787, 791; See also Sofias v. Bank of America (1985) 172 Cal.App.3d 583, 586 [The non-statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings can be made at any time, even during trial, since the grounds for a general demurrer are never waived.], see also Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (f).)

A motion for judgment on the pleadings performs the same function as a general demurrer, and hence attacks only defects disclosed on the face of the pleadings or by matters that can be judicially noticed. (See, e.g., Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (TRG 1998) §§ 7:275, 7:322; Lance Camper Manufacturing Corp. v. Republic Indemnity Co. (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 194, 198.) Presentation of extrinsic evidence is therefore not proper on a motion for judgment on the pleadings. (Id.; Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 999.) Both a demurrer and a motion for judgment on the pleadings accept as true all material factual allegations of the challenged pleadings, unless contrary to law or to facts of which a court may take judicial notice. (Mechanical Contractors Assn. v. Greater Bay Area Assn. (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 672, 677; Edwards v. Centex Real Estate Corp, (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 15, 27.)  

 

The motion may be made only after one of the following conditions has occurred: (1) If the moving party is a petitioner, and the respondent has already filed his or her answer to the complaint and the time for the petitioner to demur to the answer has expired; (2) If the moving party is a respondent, and the respondent has already filed his or her answer to the complaint and the time for the respondent to demur to the complaint has expired. (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (f).) The motion provided for in Code of Civil Procedure section 438 may be made even though either of the following conditions exist: (1) The moving party has already demurred to the complaint or answer, as the case may be, on the same grounds as is the basis for the motion provided for in this section and the demurrer has been overruled, provided that there has been a material change in applicable case law or statute since the ruling on the demurrer; (2) The moving party did not demur to the complaint or answer, as the case may be, on the same grounds as is the basis for the motion provided for in this section. (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (g).) No motion may be made pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 438 if a pretrial conference order has been entered pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 575, or within 30 days of the date the action is initially set for trial, whichever is later, unless the court otherwise permits. (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (e).)  

 

JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

The Court grants the request for judicial notice (“RJN”).

DISCUSSION

 

A.     Appropriation of Funds

 

An appropriation is a “legislative act setting aside ‘a certain sum of money for a specified object in such manner that the executive officers are authorized to use that money and no more for such specified purpose.’” (St. John’s Well Child & Family Center v. Schwarzenegger (2010) 50 Cal.4th 960, 977.)  The Department argues that Petitioner has failed to adequately allege the existence of an appropriation of funds by the Legislature for purposes of the Department preparing an LMP. The Court agrees with Petitioner that Petitioner has adequately alleged an appropriation of funds.

 

Fish and Game Code § 1019 (“Section 1019”), subd. (a) provides as follows, with emphasis added:

 

Subject to an appropriation of funds by the Legislature for that purpose, for parcels wholly within its jurisdiction acquired on or after January 1, 2002, the department shall prepare draft management plans for public review within 18 months of the recordation date.

 

            The petition contains specific allegations that the Legislature appropriated funds for the purpose of the Department preparing LMP’s. Specifically, Petitioner alleges:

 

In 2001, the Legislature enacted AB 1602, the California Clean Water, Clean Air, Safe Neighborhood Parks, and Coastal Protection Act of 2002, a State bond measure which, in turn, was adopted and passed into law in 2002 by the voters via Proposition 40 (“Prop 40”). Prop 40 specifically allocated funds to the Wildlife Conservation Board. . . for, among other things, the preparation of LMPs for properties acquired in fee by the State. Thus, via the enactment and passage of Prop 40, the Legislature appropriated funding, as provided in Section 1019(a), for the purpose of [the Department’s] preparation of LMPs, including with specific regard to funding for the preparation of the LMP for the [Ballona Wetlands] Reserve.

 

(Petition, ¶ 28.) The Petition explains that the Wildlife Conservation Board “is a separate and independent board statutorily established within [the Department] with authority to fund the acquisition and development of wildlife conservation areas.” (Petition, ¶ 28 fn 2.) The Petition further alleges: “In 2002, California voters approved Propositions 12 and 50, providing bond funds specifically for the acquisition, protection, and restoration of wetlands. In 2002, California voters also approved and passed Prop 40, via which the Legislature allocated funding to [the Wildlife Conservation Board] for the preparation of LMPs.” (Petition, ¶ 33.)  

 

The Petition alleges  that the Wildlife Conservation Board acquired the land that became the Ballona Wetlands Reserve. (Petition, ¶ 35). Prop 40, which is codified in the Public Resources Code section 5096.600, set seq, expressly provides that $300 million was “continuously appropriated to the Wildlife Conservation Board” for various specified activities, including “to prepare management plans for properties acquired in fee by the Wildlife Conservation Board.” (Public Resources Code, § 5096.650, subd. (a).) 

 

The Department argues that as a matter of law these allegations fail because there must be a direct appropriation between the Legislature to the Department, not to the Wildlife Conservation Board. (Motion, 13:8-9.) This argument has no support, especially at the pleading stage. No language in Section 1019 says that there must be a “direct appropriation” to the Department, only that there be an appropriation for the LMPs. The relevant statutes make explicitly clear that money has been “continuously appropriated” for preparation of LMPs for land the Wildlife Conservation Board acquires, like the Ballona Wetlands Reserve. (Public Resources Code, § 5096.650, subd. (a).) The Legislature deposited such funds with the Wildlife Conservation Board, which sits within the Department and acquired the Ballona Wetlands Reserve for the Department.

 

The Department argues that the WCB must vote to make the monies available to the Department for the LMP. To the extent that the Department is asserting that there has been no appropriation because there is a realistic question as to whether WCB will release the appropriated funds, that is at most a factual argument that can be raised, if at all, at the hearing on the merits. It does not defeat the Petition at the pleading stage.

 

B.     Statute of Limitations

 

            However, there is a statute of limitations problem that appears directly from the allegations of the Petition. Petitioner filed this lawsuit on May 12, 2026, almost two decades after Petitioner states that Respondent had a duty to file an LMP. (Petition, ¶ 67.) Statutes of limitations specified in the Code of Civil Procedure apply to writ proceedings. (Kao v. Department of Corrections & Rehabilitation (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 1326, 1332.) When a petition requests that a government agency comply with a statutory obligation, the three-year limitations period provided in C.C.P. § 338, subd. (a) applies. (Id., at p. 1334.)

 

            Under the theory advanced by Petitioner, Petitioner needed to have filed a lawsuit by February 19, 2010. A lawsuit filed in 2026 is years too late.

 

            Petitioner raises a number of exceptions to the statute of limitations rules. The Court examines each in turn.

 

1.      Exceptions to Statute of Limitations: The Public Trust Doctrine

 

Petitioner argues that the public trust doctrine applies here to relieve it of the effects of the statute of limitations.  In making this argument, Petitioner relies on California Trout, Inc. v. State Water Resources Control Bd. (1989) 207 Cal.App.3d 585. This case concerned the State Water Resources Control Board’s 1974 issuance of water right licenses to the City of Los Angeles, allowing the City to divert water from four tributaries to Mono Lake, which had the effect of harming fish that lived in those tributaries. (Id., pp. 595-599.) Many years later, petitioners claimed that the State Water Resources Control Board failed to issue appropriate conditions on water resources licenses as required by a statute to protect fish in those tributaries. (Id., at p. 592.) The State Board demurred on the basis that the statute of limitations had run on petitioners’ right to review the earlier 1974 water rights license decision. (Id., at p. 598.) The Court of Appeal held that the statute at issue – was a “specific rule concerning the public trust.” (Id., at p. 629.) The Court continued that “[t]he ‘public trust’ interest as to fishery in a non-navigable stream is in the nature of a state ‘property’ interest. One may not oust the state from such an interest by operation of a statute of limitations.” (Id. at p. 630.) 

 

That case does not stand for the proposition that every action or inaction by a state agency concerning a public trust resource is subject to no statute of limitations period. The Court of Appeal was simply explaining that the State Water Resources Control Board retained the authority to revise previously issued water right permits and licenses to include new conditions necessary to protect public trust resources like fish. Here, Respondent retains authority to manage public trust resources with or without a draft management plan.

 

Moreover, it is not even clear that Petitioner has properly evoked a public trust doctrine at all. In its memorandum of points and authorities, Petitioner cites to various paragraphs which it alleges invokes the public trust doctrine. (Petition, 13:20.) The Court does not find that any of the paragraphs invoke the public trust doctrine as a matter of law. The closest Petitioner comes is Paragraph 22, which states that Respondent must manage resources for the “use and enjoyment of the public.” (Petition, ¶ 22.) Managing a resource for the use and enjoyment of the public is not explicitly the same as holding a resource  “in trust for the people of the state…” (Fish & Game Code § 711.7, subd. (a).) By contrast, the public trust doctrine focuses on managing a resource in the present and the future, and focuses on all residents of California.

 

While there may be a public trust doctrine argument to be made with respect to the statute of limitations, Petitioner has not supported such an argument with this pleading. Upon amendment, Petitioner must clarify how the public trust doctrine applies to actual allegations made in this case.

 

2.      Exceptions to Statute of Limitations: Continuous Accrual Doctrine

 

The continuous accrual doctrine provides that “when an obligation or liability arises on a recurring basis, a cause of action accrues each time a wrongful act occurs, triggering a new limitations period.” (Aryeh v. Canon Business Solutions, Inc. (2013) 55 Cal.4th 1185, 1198-1199.) The California Supreme Court has identified examples of obligations that are recurring: the right to pension payments and monthly collection of taxes. (Ibid.)

 

However, the obligation or liability in this case does not arise on a recurring basis. Section 1019, subd. (a) makes clear that the filing of an LMP is a one-time event that is not susceptible to recurring breaches. Either an LMP is filed, and there is no breach of a government duty or no LMP is filed, and there is a singular breach of a governmental duty. (Water Audit California v. Merced Irrigation Dist. (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 1147, 1157 [continuous accrual doctrine can sometimes apply when the duty allegedly breached is a ‘continuing one’ that is ‘susceptible to recurring breaches.’].)

 

The case Petitioner cites in its opposition is not persuasive. In Water Audit, the Merced Irrigation District closed the fish ladder on the C-H Dam in approximately 1972. (Id., at p. 1158.) In August 2022, Water Audit, the petitioner, made a demand to reopen the fishway and gave notice of its intention to commence litigation if remediation was not promised. (Id., at p. 1159.) There were allegations that the Merced Irrigation District opened and then re-closed the fish ladder. (Id., at p. 1162.) The trial court ruled that a demurrer to the petition must be sustained because the petitioner had failed to plead a specific action by a specific agency occurring on a specific date that is not time barred. (Id., at p. 1188.) The Court of Appeal reversed, explaining that the plaintiff was alleging ongoing violations of Fish and Game Code section 5901, which prohibits maintaining “any device or contrivance that prevents, impedes, or tends to prevent or impede, the passing of fish up and down stream.” (Id., at p. 1192.)

 

Fish and Game Code section 5901 is fundamentally different from the statute at issue in the case before the Court. Section 5901 does not prohibit certain activity for 18 months or 24 months. There is an ongoing prohibition against activity that impedes fish from moving up and down stream.

 

By contrast, Section 1019, subd. (a) states that, “subject to an appropriation of funds by the Legislature for that purpose, for parcels wholly within its jurisdiction acquired on or after January 1, 2002, the department shall prepare draft management plans for public review within 18 months of the recordation date.”  (emphasis added.) Section 1019, subd. (a) does not and cannot apply two decades after the property was acquired.

 

Petitioner also argues that Respondent has a duty to manage the Reserve for future generations, and that means Respondent cannot just simply not produce an LMP. But the Legislature did not write Section 1019 in a way that imposed an ongoing duty, only that one is due within 18 months. While it might not be ideal, the Department could theoretically carry out its duties to manage the Ballona Wetlands Reserve for future generations even though it did not draft an LMP.   

 

Finally, the petition alleges that Respondent has been working an LMP in the past few years and has continued to delay putting out. (See Petition, ¶¶ 42-53.) These allegations do not advance Petitioner’s point. State agencies may undertake discretionary actions they are not obligated to take. It does not demonstrate an ongoing duty.

 

Based on the above analysis, the Court concludes that the continuous accrual doctrine does not apply to address the statute of limitations issue.

 

3.      Exceptions to Statute of Limitations: Continuous Violation Doctrine

 

 

The continuing violation doctrine “aggregates a series of wrongs or injuries for purposes of the statute of limitations, treating the limitations period as accruing for all of them upon commission or sufferance of the last of them.” (Willis v. City of Carlsbad (2020) 48 Cal.App.4th 1104, 1124 (Willis).) The continuing violation doctrine appears to be particularly relevant to employment discrimination cases. (Brome v. California Highway Patrol (2020) 44 Cal.App.5th 786, 798-799 (Brome).)

 

The Court concludes that the continuing violation doctrine does not apply here. Petitioner is complaining of Respondent’s alleged failure to take a single, discrete action (not creating an LMP), not a series of wrongs or injuries.

 

Petitioner does not cite any case where one discrete wrong fell under the continuing violation doctrine. Aryeh v. Canon Business Solutions, Inc. (2013) 55 Cal.4th 1185, 1190, 1198, has to do with a defendant who made too many at successive monthly service visits, resulting in excess copy charges each time. Willis, supra, 48 Cal.App.4th at p 1112-1113 had to do with multiple instances of a denial of a promotion. Richards v. CH2M Hill, Inc. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 798, 802-807 dealt with multiple discrete issues of disability access. Brome, supra, 44 Cal.App.5th at p. 798-800 had to do with multiple discrete cases of discrimination based on sexual orientation.

 

This is not a case where Petitioner is complaining of multiple statutory violations. This is also not a case where Petitioner is complaining of Respondent repeatedly violating a statute. Hypothetically, if the statute had required an LMP every four years, there would be grounds to say the continuous violation doctrine could apply. But the statute is not written that way. Respondent must prepare an LMP within 18 months of acquiring a property, and that meant an LMP needed to have been created for the Reserve by February 2007. (Petition ¶ 67.)

 

Petitioner argues that Respondent’s actions had not acquired the “degree of permanence” required to trigger the accrual of the statute of limitations period because Respondent had never formally refused to prepare the LMP. The Court does not find this argument persuasive based on the current petition. As written, the statute required an LMP to be created by February 19, 2007. If an LMP was not created on February 19, 2007, Respondent was in violation of Fish & Game Code Section 1019, subd. (a). Respondent does not need to announce they are not going to comply with a statute for Respondent to be in violation of the statute.

 

Additionally, taken to its extreme, Petitioner’s argument would seem to open the door to the Court hearing petitions based on actions taken long ago. If Respondent had failed to prepare an LMP in 1965 or 1973, but had never officially refused to prepare one, could a moving party seek a writ of mandate directing a governmental agency to comply with a duty they had from 50 or 60 years ago? What about 90 or 100 years ago? Where could courts draw the line?

 

Based on the above analysis, the Court concludes that the continuous violation doctrine does not apply to address the statute of limitations issue.

 

4.      Exceptions to Statute of Limitations: Equitable Estoppel

 

The equitable estoppel doctrine provides that if an entity, including a governmental entity, takes actions which would induce a reasonably prudent person to avoid seeking legal advice or personally commencing litigation, that entity may be estopped from asserting a defense including the statute of limitations. (Citizens for a  Responsible Caltrans Decision v. Department of Transportation (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 1103, 1128.)

 

Respondent has continually and repeatedly stated that an LMP would be forthcoming. (Petition, ¶¶ 42-54, 59.) Petitioner does not allege that this delayed them from filing a lawsuit. Even if Petitioner did allege this, equitable estoppel would not apply.

 

Petitioner would need to provide facts concerning actions Respondent took prior to the running of the statute of limitations that induced Petitioner from timely commencing litigation within the limitations period.  Petitioner has not made any such allegation in the current petition.

 

 

CONCLUSION 

 

             The Court GRANTS the Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings, and GRANTS leave to amend to add any appropriate allegations showing that the claims are not time-barred. Petitioner must amend within 20 days.

Order Denying Application for Preliminary Injunction

 

 

            Petitioner West Coast Arborists (“Petitioner”) requests a preliminary injunction ordering Respondents City of Norwalk and the City Council of the City of Norwalk (collectively, the “City”) from (a) executing any contract or agreement with Real Party in Interest North Star Land Care; and/or (b) issuing any Notice to Proceed or otherwise authorizing the commencement of work under the award made on August 4, 2026, and/or (c) suspending commencement of any contract executed.

 

            The Court DENIES the application for a preliminary injunction.

LEGAL STANDARD

 

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending a decision on the merits.  (Major v. Miraverde Homeowners Ass’n. (1992) 7 Cal. App. 4th 618, 623.)  In deciding whether or not to grant a preliminary injunction, the court looks to two factors, including “(1) the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits, and (2) the relative balance of harms that is likely to result from the granting or denial of interim injunctive relief.”  (White v. Davis (2003) 30 Cal.4th 528, 553-54.) The factors are interrelated, with a greater showing on one permitting a lesser showing on the other.  (Dodge, Warren & Peters Ins. Services, Inc. v. Riley (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 1414, 1420.)  However, the party seeking an injunction must demonstrate at least a reasonable probability of success on the merits.  (IT Corp. v. County of Imperial (1983) 35 Cal.3d 63, 73-74.)  The party seeking the injunction bears the burden of demonstrating both a likelihood of success on the merits and the occurrence of irreparable harm.  (Savage v. Trammell Crow Co. (1990) 223 Cal.App.3d 1562, 1571.) Irreparable harm may exist if the plaintiff can show an inadequate remedy at law.  (CCP § 526(a).)

 

“The granting or denying of a preliminary injunction does not constitute an adjudication of the ultimate rights in controversy.”  (Cohen v. Board of Supervisors (1985) 40 Cal.3d 277, 286; see also Yee v. American National Ins. Co. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 453, 457-458 [“a preliminary injunction is not a determination on the merits….”].) 

 

DISCUSSION

 

A.     Judicial Notice

 

The Court grants the request for judicial notice (“RJN”).

 

B.     Evidentiary Objections

 

The Court overrules the evidentiary objections.

C.     Summary of Facts

 

The application is based on Petitioner’s cause of action for writ of mandate.  

 

Petitioner is a California corporation engaged in providing professional tree-maintenance and urban-forestry services to public agencies throughout California. (Mahoney Decl., ¶ 2.) Petitioner has furnished citywide tree-maintenance services to the City for 32 years. (Ibid.)

 

The City invited bids for citywide tree maintenance services. (Mahoney Decl., ¶ 5.) Petitioner states that they own the equipment necessary to perform the work called for by the City’s Invitation for a Bid for Citywide Tree Maintenance Services. (Mahoney Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.)

 

Petitioner and North Star Land Care both submitted bids. (Mahoney Decl., ¶¶ 6-7.) The request for bids said that the low bidder “shall be determined by rates as provided in Exhibit A.” (Mahoney Decl., Exh. 1, Addendum 2.) The request for bids also stated that “[u]nauthorized conditions, limitations, or provisions attached to a Bid will render the Bid non-conforming and non-responsive and may cause its rejection.” (Exhibit 1, p. 7.)

 

Petitioner argues that it provided the prices for all of its work on Exhibit A, and included no extra changes for equipment rental or usage. (Mahoney Decl, Exh. 3, Exh. A.) However, North Star Land Care submitted a separate addendum on their Exhibit A, an out-of-form “Additional Price Sheet Note”, repricing categories already covered by Exhibit A rates. (Mahoney Decl, Exh. 2, pgs. 40-41.)

 

The City awarded the contract to Noth Star Land Care. (Mahoney Decl., ¶ 10.) The City found North Star Land Care’s bid was $530,406 while West Coast Arborist, Inc.’s bid was $705,762. (Mahoney Decl., Exh. 6.) Petitioner submitted a protest, but the City denied the protest. (Mahoney Decl., ¶¶ 9, 11.) The City stated that the Additional Price Sheet Note was not considered during the evaluation of North Star Land Care’s bid, and would not be incorporated into the resulting contract with the City. (Mahoney Decl., Exh. 7; Mendoza Decl., ¶¶ 6, 9.)

 

The City has also produced the contract it intends to sign with North Star Land Care. The contract states: “The ‘Additional Price Sheet Note’ submitted by Contractor in connection with City’s IFB No. 26-859 shall not be incorporated into or form part of this Agreement.” (RJN, Exh. 3, Agreement, § 1, p. 1.) The Compensation Agreement also states that “Any ‘Additional Pricing Sheet Note’ submitted by Contractor in connection with City’s IFB No. 26-859 shall not be incorporated into or form part of this Agreement.” (RJN, Exh. 3, Agreement, § 5.1, p. 7.) The Compensation Agreement also states that the City will pay North Star Land Care the amount of money ($530,406.00) that shows up in Exhibit A of North Star Land Care’s bid.

 

If the City executes the contract with North Star Land Care, Petitioner argues they will lose a public contract that was not lawfully awarded to the lowest responsive bidder. (Mahoney Decl., ¶ 14.) It will become a lot harder to restore the parties to their present position once North Star Land Care begins performance. (Ibid.)

 

By contrast, the City states delaying execution of the contract with North Star Land Care will cost the City and its taxpayers $175,356 per year, and limit or reduce any savings. (Mahoney Decl., ¶ 12.)

 

Based on testimony from the President of Petitioner, the contract with North Star Land Care would increase costs by 20%. (Mahoney Decl., ¶ 15.)

 

D.    Plaintiff’s Likelihood of Success on the Merits

 

The application is based on Petitioner’s cause of action for writ of mandate.

 

1.      Writ of Mandate

 

There are two essential requirements to the issuance of an ordinary writ of mandate under Code of Civil Procedure section 1085: (1) a clear, present, and ministerial duty on the part of the respondent, and (2) a clear, present, and beneficial right on the part of the petitioner to the performance of that duty. (California Ass’n for Health Services at Home v. Department of Health Services (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 696, 704.) “An action in ordinary mandamus is proper where … the claim is that an agency has failed to act as required by law.” (Id. at 705.) 

 

“A basic rule of competitive bidding is that bids must conform to specifications, and that if a bid does not so conform, it may not be accepted. [Citations.] However, it is further well established that a bid which substantially conforms to a call for bids may, though it is not strictly responsive, be accepted if the variance cannot have affected the amount of the bid or given a bidder an advantage or benefit not allowed other bidders or, in other words, if the variance is inconsequential.” (Ghilotti Construction Co. v. City of Richmond (1996) 45 Cal.App.4th 897, 904-905.) In other words, Petitioner indicates there is a mandatory duty to reject material deviations from the specifications listed in a call for bids.

 

As an initial matter, the Court cannot determine based on this record that there is even a mandatory ministerial duty at issue in this case. Petitioner did not cite a statute laying out a ministerial duty. The Public Contract Code’s competitive-bidding requirement for general law cities applies to “public projects” (Public Contract Code, §§ 20161, 20162), and the Code confirms that “landscape maintenance, including . . . trimming, pruning, planting, [and] replacement of plants” is “maintenance work,” not a public project. (Public Contract Code, § 22002, subds. (c), (d)(4).)

 

Even if there is a ministerial duty at issue, the Court does not find Petitioner has shown it is likely to prevail on the merits.  Petitioner argues that North Star Land Care’s “Additional Price Note” is a material deviation from the specification listed in the bid because it (1) affects the true amount and cost of the bid; (2) confers a competitive advantage because North Star Land Care was able to supply artificial low Exhibit A rates while hiding costs elsewhere; (3) introduces conditions not called for and never authorized by addendum; and (4) is internally inconsistent. (Ex Parte Application for TRO and OSC re: Preliminary Injunction, pg. 10.)

 

Petitioner also argues that the City cannot cure its violation of a mandatory duty by saying that the Additional Price Sheet Note was not considered during the evaluation of North Star Land Care’s bid, and would not be incorporated into the resulting contract with the City. (Mahoney Decl., Exh. 7.) Without any citation to legal authority, Petitioner states: “A city cannot retroactively transform a non-responsive bid into a responsive one by promising to ignore the offending terms after the award.” (Ex Parte Application for TRO and OSC re: Preliminary Injunction, 11:11-12.)

 

The Court does not agree with Petitioner. The City has confirmed that the additional price note played no part in the City’s selection of North Star’s contract. (Mendoza Decl., ¶¶ 6, 9.) The City’s own analysis (Mahoney Decl., Exh. E.) does not indicate that any of the rates in the Additional Price Sheet factored into the City’s decision. There is nothing left for North Star’s deviation from the requirements to have affected. Since nothing was affected, North Star’s deviation was not material, and the City can select North Star’s bid. 

 

Petitioner cites a variety of cases on Page 9 of the ex parte application but they involve cases where the awarding agency used, credited, or relied upon a deviation in determining the winning bid or the resulting contract terms. Thus, those cases are inapposite here.

 

The argument that North Star Land Care’s bid was “internally inconsistent” does not mean that Petitioner would prevail on a petition for writ of mandate claim. Petitioner cites no authority holding that an agency's decision to excise and disregard a non-conforming attachment — rather than accept it — renders the underlying, conforming bid ambiguous or non-responsive.

 

On reply, Petitioner argues that the City only announced that it would not “honor” the Additional Price Sheet after Petitioner contested North Star Land Care’s bid. (Reply, 3:1-2.) This argument is unpersuasive. There is nothing suspicious or problematic about the City stating it would not honor the price sheet. The City was not required to announce anything about the Additional Price Sheet at any point.

 

Petitioner also claims that an inspector for the City of Azusa will testify that North Star Land Care does intend to use its supplemental pricing in the performance of its public contracts – including by billing public agencies separately for equipment. (Mahaffey Decl., ¶¶ 5–6.) This argument is also unpersuasive.

 

The Mahffaey declaration provides no explanation for why an inspector for the City of Azusa knows more about plans for the City of Norwalk than the City of Norwalk itself. The City has confirmed that the additional price note played no part in the City’s selection of North Star’s contract. (Mendoza Decl., ¶¶ 6, 9.) The actual Agreement between between the City and North Star Land Care states the following:   “The ‘Additional Price Sheet Note’ submitted by Contractor in connection with City’s IFB No. 26-859 shall not be incorporated into or form part of this Agreement.” (RJN, Exh. 3, Agreement, § 1, p. 1.) The Compensation Agreement also states that “Any ‘Additional Pricing Sheet Note’ submitted by Contractor in connection with City’s IFB No. 26-859 shall not be incorporated into or form part of this Agreement.” (RJN, Exh. 3, Agreement, § 5.1, p. 7.)

 

Finally, the mere fact that the City chose to be very explicit that the Additional Price Note would not be part of the contract is not evidence that the “City understood the document to carry real competitive and financial significance.” (Reply, 4:16-17.) The City could have decided to be explicit about the Additional Price Note to reduce the risk of future litigation. This does not provide any indication of what the City was doing when deciding on the bid. 

 

Based on the record, the Court finds Petitioner is unlikely to prevail on a petition for writ of mandate.  

 

E.     Balance of Harms

 

For the second factor, the court must consider “the interim harm that the plaintiff would be likely to sustain if the injunction were denied as compared to the harm the defendant would be likely to suffer if the preliminary injunction were issued.”  (Smith v. Adventist Health System/West (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 729, 749.)  “Irreparable harm” generally means that the defendant’s act constitutes an actual or threatened injury to the personal or property rights of the plaintiff that cannot be compensated by a damages award.  (See Brownfield v. Daniel Freeman Marina Hospital (1989) 208 Cal.App.3d 405, 410.)  The showing of harm is even more demanding when a Plaintiff seeks injunctive relief against a public entity because “[t]here is a general rule against enjoining public officers or agencies from performing their duties.” (Tahoe Keys Property Owners’ Assn. v. State Water Resources Control Bd. (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1459, 1471.)

 

Petitioner argues that once the City moves forward with a contract with North Star Land Care, it will be a lot harder to unwind the award and restore the parties to their present positions. (Mahoney Decl., ¶ 14.)

 

As a matter of California law, a disappointed bidder may not recover the lost contract or its lost profits; its sole damages remedy is recovery of bid-preparation costs. (Kajima/Ray Wilson v. Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority (2000) 23 Cal.4th 305.) Thus, Petitioner argues there is no way to make it whole if the contract moves forward.

 

Petitioner further states that interim harm to the City from a preliminary injunction is minimal because Petitioner is currently performing the work North Star Land Care would on a month-to-month basis. Finally, Petitioner argues that enforcing competitive bidding laws serves the public interest.

 

The Court disagrees. The record indicates that the City addressed the public interest – it evaluated all bidders on the same required form and awarded the contract to the bidder with the lowest price. The Court also notes that the City will suffer a loss if it is required to continue relying on Petitioner to maintain trees.

 

As the Court noted above, Petitioner must meet a high threshold to obtain injunctive relief against a public entity. Petitioner did not meet that standard here. By all accounts, the City evaluated all the bids and gave the contract to the bidder with the lowest price. Petitioner’s injuries are speculative, especially the idea that it might be harder to unwind a contract with North Star Land Care once the contract has started. There is no actual evidence to support why it would be harder to unwind such a contract.

 

The balance of harms favors the Court denying a preliminary injunction.

 

 

CONCLUSION 

 

            The Court DENIES the application for a preliminary injunction.

[Tentative] Order on Application for Writ of Attachment 

 

INTRODUCTION

 

            Plaintiff Metropolitan Adjustment Bureau (“Plaintiff”) moves for a writ of attachment against Defendant Janna Holdings L.P. c/o San Pasqual Fiduciary Trust Company (“Defendant”) in the amount of $900,290.32.  The amount includes $80,000.00 in costs and $8,500.00 in attorneys fees.

 

 

            The Court DENIES the application for writ of attachment.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“Upon the filing of the complaint or at any time thereafter, the plaintiff may apply pursuant to this article for a right to attach order and a writ of attachment by filing an application for the order and writ with the court in which the action is brought.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 484.010.)  “Except as otherwise provided by statute, an attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney's fees.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 483.010.) 

 

The court shall issue a right to attach order if the court finds all of the following: 

 

(1)   The claim upon which the attachment is based is one upon which an attachment may be issued. 

(2)   The plaintiff has established the probable validity of the claim upon which the attachment is based. 

(3)   The attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based. 

(4)   The amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.  

 

(Code Civ. Pro. § 484.090.) 

 

“A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 481.190.)    “The application shall be supported by an affidavit showing that the plaintiff on the facts presented would be entitled to a judgment on the claim upon which the attachment is based.”¿(Code Civ. Pro. § 484.030.)¿“In contested applications, the court must consider the relative merits of the positions of the respective parties and make a determination of¿the probable outcome of the litigation.”¿  (Hobbs v. Weiss (1999) 73 Cal.App.4th 76, 80.)  “The Attachment Law statutes are subject to strict construction.” (Epstein v. Abrams (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1168.) 

 

DISCUSSION

 

A.    Judicial Notice

 

The Court grants Defendants’ request for judicial notice.

 

B.     Probable Validity of Plaintiff’s Claim

 

The application is based on Plaintiff’s cause of action for breach of contract and quantum meruit. 

 

To establish a claim for breach of contract, a plaintiff must prove: (1) the existence of a contract; (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendant’s breach of the contract; and (4) damages incurred by plaintiff as a result of the breach.  (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1367.)

 

To establish a claim for quantum meruit, a plaintiff must prove: (1) the plaintiff acted pursuant to an explicit or implicit request for the services by the defendant, and (2) the services conferred a benefit on the defendant. (Port Medical Wellness, Inc. v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Company (2018) 24 Cal.App.5th 153, 180.)

 

Defendant owns three apartment buildings are 16150, 16162, and 16174 W. Sunset Blvd. in Pacific Palisades, California. (Davis Decl., ¶ 5.) The Buildings were insured against fire by a primary policy with a $5,000,000 limit and an excess policy with a $5,147,379 limit, for a total combined policy limit of $10,147,379. (Davis Decl., ¶ 7.) On January 8, 2025, the Palisades Fire destroyed the three apartment buildings. (Davis Decl., ¶ 5.)

 

Total Commercial Real Estate, Inc. (“TCRE”) was the property manager of the Buildings for Defendant. (See Davis Decl., ¶ 6.) TCRE, on behalf of Defendant, signed a contract with Plaintiff agreeing that Plaintiff would be Defendant’s public insurance adjuster with respect to the destroyed buildings. (Davis Decl., ¶ 8.)

 

The negotiations relating to the contract included the following. No later than January 9, 2025, TCRE’s COO, Santiago Sciupac, discussed with Plaintiff’s President the retention of Plaintiff to provide public insurance adjuster services to present and collect on Defendant’s claim on its insurance policies on the Buildings. (Sciupac Decl., ¶ 3.)  On January 10, 2025, Plaintiff’s President emailed TCRE the initial version of its proposed contract with Defendant, which had already been signed and dated by Plaintiff’s President. (Sciupac Decl., ¶ 4.)

 

On January 14, 2025, Plaintiff’s President emailed TCRE a revised contract to correct the insurance information. (Sciupac Decl., ¶ 6.) Plaintiff’s revision failed to modify any dates in the initial version of Metropolitan’s contract, including on the statutorily mandated Notice of Cancellation form, the date of the contract (“1/10/2005”) and the midnight deadline for cancellation (“1/13/2025”), both of which already were in the past.

 

On January 15, 2025, TRE emailed Plaintiff’s President a signed version of Plaintiff’s revised contract. (Sciupac Decl., ¶ 7.) Plaintiff never provided TCRE or Defendant with a duplicate signed original of the contract. (Sciupac Decl., ¶ 9.)

 

The Contract states that “Insured [Janna] agrees to pay and assigns to Metropolitan Adjustment Bureau for services rendered on behalf of the Insured 8% of the amount received from the insurer by the insured for services rendered to the insured by the public insurance adjuster, plus necessary expenses as approved by the insured.” (Application, Exh. 1.)

 

At no point prior to TCRE executing the Contract on January 15, 2025 did Plaintiff’s President or anyone else advise TCRE or anyone else at Defendant that (1) the estimated value of the loss substantially exceeded the aggregate policy limits of the primary and excess policies; and (2)  it was likely that Defendant’s insurance carriers would declare Defendant’s buildings to be a covered total loss, and Defendant would recover a full policy limits payout from its insurance carriers for the destruction of its buildings. (Davis Decl., ¶ 9.)

 

Had Defendant been aware of these things, Defendant asserts that it would not have authorized TCRE to sign the contract that included an 8% contingency fee to Plaintiff, or over $800,000, given the apparent certainty of coverage and loss in excess of policy limits. (Davis Decl., ¶ 10.)

 

Less than three weeks after the destruction of the Buildings, Defendants’ carriers declared them to be a total loss. (Drummy Decl., ¶ 6.) Defendant received payment in full on its policy limits by three checks. (Davis Decl., ¶ 13.)

 

Plaintiff invoiced Defendant for 8% of all insurance proceeds ($10,147,379) that Defendant received—for a total fee of $811,790.32. (Application, Exh. 2.)

 

On October 7, 2025, Defendant sent Plaintiff a Notice of Cancellation of the Contract pursuant to Insurance Code Section 15027, and noted that the Notice of Cancellation form in the Contract did not comply with the first sentence of Insurance Code section 15027, subd. (f)(1). (Davis Decl., ¶ 15, Exh. E.)

 

While not mentioned in the notice of cancellation, the contract also arguably violates numerous other provisions of Insurance Code Section 15027.

 

Insurance Code Section 15027, subd. (a), (s) and (t) require Plaintiff to provide the insured with a duplicate signed original of the contract. Plaintiff did not do so. (Davis Decl., ¶ 12; Sciupac Decl., ¶ 9.)

 

Insurance Code Section 15027, subd. (b)(2) requires that a public insurance adjuster’s contract shall include both its “name” and its “business name”. Although Plaintiff’s contract includes its business name, “Metropolitan Adjustment Bureau”, the Contract fails to include Plaintiff’s “name”, which appears to be either “Metropolitan Adjustment Bureau Corporation” (according to the California Secretary of State) or “M. Adjustment Bureau Inc.” (according to the Department of Insurance’s Check A License website). (Drummy Decl., Exh. C, E-G.)

 

Insurance Code Section 15027, subd. (b)(3) requires that a contract include the name and address of the insured. Nothing in Plaintiff’s contract identifies Defendant’s address. (Application, Exh. 2.)

 

A failure to comply with Section 15027 allows the client to cancel the contract. (Insurance Code § 15027, subd. (r).) Since there were deficiencies in the contract, Defendant had every right to cancel the contract, and did so on October 7, 2025.

 

Insurance Code § 15027, subd. (n) explains that “[t]he licensee is not entitled to compensation for services performed prior to cancellation, other than for reimbursement of moneys paid by the licensee for out-of-pocket emergency expenses for the client or on behalf of the client.” Plaintiff paid no “out-of-pocket emergency expenses” of Defendant. (Davis Decl., ¶ 14.) Plaintiff did not perform services after Defendant cancelled the contract. (Davis Decl., ¶ 16.) Thus, Plaintiff is barred from recovery in this case.

 

Plaintiff contends that the violations of the statute do not matter because Defendant did not attempt to cancel the contract for months, and at this point, Plaintiff has fully performed under the contract and Defendant must not be unjustly enriched.

 

The Court disagrees. The operative trigger for a contract to be cancelled under Section 15027, subd. (r) is not the passage of time, but rather the licensee’s compliance status. This means that if a public adjuster presents a contract that is deficient in any respect required by Section 15027, the insured's cancellation right under subdivision (r) is not extinguished after three days, or after a month, or after any other fixed period. The right continues until the licensee achieves full compliance.

 

By contrast, other cancellation and rescission provisions do have specific time frames. For example, Insurance Code § 15027 subd. (f)(1) states that the client has the right to cancel the contract within three business days of signing and receiving a copy of the signed contract. Insurance Code § 15027, subd. (y) extends the cancellation window to five calendar days when the property loss is included in an area subject to a catastrophic disaster. Had the Legislature wanted to limit subdivision (r) to a specific period of time, they were more than capable of doing so, like they did for other subdivisions in Section 15027.

 

Plaintiff has not shown the probable validity of its breach of contract claim for one other reason.

 

Insurance Code § 15027, subd. (x) allows an insured to rescind a contract when a licensee misrepresents or conceals a material fact from the insured prior to the execution of the contract. Insurance Code § 330 and § 331 define concealment as “[n]eglect to communicate that which a party knows, and ought to communicate” and whether intentional, or unintentional, can warrant an insured rescinding insurance. Under Insurance Code § 334, a concealed fact is material based on “the probable and reasonable influence of the facts upon the party to whom the communication due.” (Nieto v. Blue Shield of California Life & Health Ins. Co. (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 60, 77.)

 

Defendant has introduced evidence from a public insurance adjuster that a reasonably competent California public insurance adjuster in Plaintiff’s position would have recognized the following before the contract with Defendant was executed:

 

·         The Buildings were insured against fire by a primary policy with a limit of $5,000,000 and an excess policy with a limit of $5,147,379, for a total policy limit of $10,147,379.

·         The cost to replace the Buildings would be approximately $16.3 to $27.3 million.

·          It therefore was highly likely that Janna’s insurance carriers would declare the Buildings (which has been destroyed by a covered loss—fire) to be a total loss

 

(Major Decl., ¶ 3.)

 

A reasonably competent public insurance adjuster would have advised Defendant of its loss. (Major Decl., ¶ 4.) Had Plaintiff advised Defendant of its loss, Defendant would not have authorized TCRE to sign the contract that included an 8% contingency fee to Plaintiff, or over $800,000, given the apparent certainty of coverage and loss in excess of policy limits. (Davis Decl., ¶ 10.)

 

At minimum, the asserted concealment of these facts raises factual questions about whether Plaintiff will prevail on the underlying claims at trial.

 

Finally, Insurance Code § 15028, subd. (d) prevents a public insurance adjuster from offering to pay a fee, commission, or other consideration greater than $100, to a person for referring a loss.

 

There was an offer by Plaintiff to pay a referral fee to Defendant’s agent, TCRE. (Drummy Decl., ¶ 10, Exh. H [1% to Referral]; Exh. I [Per GN – 1% back to Michael & Santiago]; Exh. J [“Guys please send payment when possible and then we can get your portion sent out”].)

 

However, nothing in Insurance Code § 15028, subd. (d) renders a contract void, voidable, or unenforceable as a result of noncompliance. The Court therefore rejects Defendant’s argument that the contract is void because of Plaintiff’s failure to comply with Insurance Code § 15028, subd. (d).

 

In conclusion, Plaintiff has not shown that it is likely to prevail on a claim for breach of contract.

 

Plaintiff also argues that it is entitled to recovery in quantum meruit. However, Plaintiff does not support what the value of its efforts are, assuming that the contract is invalid. Moreover, the gist of Defendant’s argument is that it would have received the insurance proceeds in any event so Plaintiff’s services were not necessary or helpful. Accordingly, Plaintiff has failed to establish the probable validity of its quantum meruit claims.

 

C.     Basis for Attachment

 

“[A]n attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney's fees.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 483.010(a).)  “An attachment may not be issued on a claim which is secured by any interest in real property arising from agreement ….”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 483.010(b).)  “[A]n attachment will lie upon a cause of action for damages for a breach of contract where the damages are readily ascertainable by reference to the contract and the basis of the computation of damages appears to be reasonable and definite.” (CIT Group/Equipment Financing, Inc. v. Super DVD, Inc. (2004) 115 Cal.App. 4th 537, 541.)

 

Here, Plaintiff’s application for writ of attachment is based on contract claims for which the total amount allegedly due is in excess of $500. The record before the Court does not indicate that the claim is secured by an interest in real property. The damages are readily ascertainable by reviewing the invoiced amounts.

 

The parties’ papers brief the effect of the Court’s earlier ruling of July 29, 2026 on the outcome of this present application. Plaintiff appears to be correct that there was a miscommunication between counsel and court staff about taking that hearing off calendar. Accordingly, the Court does not give the July 29 order binding effect. Counsel are advised that—as set forth explicitly in the Court’s updated courtroom information—that withdrawal of a pending motion or application requires a notice in writing by the moving party.

 

D.    Purpose and Amount of Attachment

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 484.090 states that the Court shall issue a right to attach order if “the attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based . . . [and] the amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.”  Plaintiff satisfied these requirements.  (See Application ¶ 4.)

 

E.     Reduction of Amount to be Secured

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 483.015, subd. (b) provides that the amount to be secured by the attachment shall be reduced by, inter alia: “(2) The amount of any indebtedness of the plaintiff that the defendant has claimed in a cross-complaint filed in the action if the defendant’s claim is one upon which an attachment could be issued.”  Defendant has the initial burden of proof to satisfy the requirements of attachment for any offset claim.  (See Code Civ. Pro. § 483.015 and Lydig Construction, Inc. v. Martinez Steel Corp. (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 937, 945.)  Among other requirements, “the contract sued on must furnish a standard by which the amount due may be clearly ascertained and there must exist a basis upon which the damages can be determined by proof.’” (See CIT Group/Equipment Financing, Inc., supra, 115 Cal.App.4th at 541.)

 

Defendant has not requested any offsets.

 

F.      Exemptions

 

Defendant has not claimed any exemptions.

 

G.    Undertaking

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 489.210 requires the plaintiff to file an undertaking before issuance of a writ of attachment.  Code of Civil Procedure section 489.220 provides, with exceptions, for an undertaking in the amount of $10,000.  Because the Court does not grant the writ of attachment, no undertaking is necessary.  

 

H.    Subject Property

 

Plaintiff requests attachment of any property of Defendant, a corporation.  (Application ¶ 9a.)  That request is proper.  “[A]ll corporate property for which a method of levy is provided by Article 2 (commencing with Section 488.300) of Chapter 8” may be attached.  (Code Civ. Pro. § 487.010, subd. (a).) 

 

I.        Attorneys Fees

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 482.110, subd. (b) provides, “[i]n the discretion of the court, the amount to be secured by the attachment may include an estimated amount for costs and allowable attorney’s fees.”   The Court, however, does not grant the attachment.

CONCLUSION

 

The Court DENIES the application for writ of attachment.
