DEPARTMENT N LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 23SMCV03929    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: N

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff L.W.’s motion for an order for leave to proceed under the initials L.W. is GRANTED.

Plaintiff L.W. to give notice.

REASONING

Plaintiff L.W. (“Plaintiff”) moves the court for an order for leave to proceed under the initials L.W.  Plaintiff asserts Defendant Geffen Academy at UCLA (“Defendant”) advised Plaintiff via email on July 20, 2026, that it does not oppose this motion.  Plaintiff argues he should be permitted to proceed under his initials because the subject matter is highly sensitive and personal in nature and the events giving rise to this action occurred while Plaintiff was a minor. 

Unless confidentiality is required by law, court records are presumed to be open. (Cal. Rules of Court rule 2.550(c).) The public’s First Amendment right of access to civil proceedings includes the right to know the identity of the parties, and allowing a party to litigate anonymously implicates that right much as sealing a record does. (Dept. of Fair Employment & Housing v. Superior Court (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 105, 111.)   Relief is warranted only if an overriding interest will likely be prejudiced without use of a pseudonym, and it is not feasible to protect that interest with less impact on the constitutional right of access.  (Ibid.)  Outside of circumstances in which anonymity is authorized by statute, litigating by pseudonym should occur “only in the rarest of circumstances.” (Id., at p. 112.) However, anonymity may be granted when necessary to preserve an important privacy interest. (Id., at p. 110.)  The Court may seal a record if the following are met: an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access; that the interest supports the relief; a substantial probability that the interest will be prejudiced absent relief; that the relief is narrowly tailored; and that no less restrictive means exist. (Rule 2.550(d).)

Applying that standard, the Court finds the showing satisfied here.

First, an overriding interest exists. The subject of this action is Plaintiff’s medical and psychological history, his disability diagnoses, the mental-health treatment he required as a child, and his confidential educational records.  Privacy of records of this kind are a substantial interest.  Here, the interest is heightened because the events underlying the action occurred while Plaintiff was a minor. 

Second, the overriding interest supports the relief and would be prejudiced without it. If Plaintiff’s true name is placed in the public record, it will be searchable and permanently linked to his medical, psychological, and educational history.  The prejudice is the disclosure itself.

Third, the relief is narrowly tailored and no less restrictive means exist. Plaintiff seeks only to withhold his name; he does not seek to seal the pleadings, close the courtroom, or restrict public access to any substantive filing. Because it is the linkage between Plaintiff’s name and his records that causes the harm, withholding the name is the least restrictive means of preventing it.

Finally, granting the relief does not impair the competing interests the overriding-interest test protects. The public’s legitimate interest lies in the legal questions this action presents concerning a federally funded school’s obligations to a disabled student, not in the individual student’s name.  The Court, the parties, and the public may follow every aspect of the litigation without Plaintiff’s name appearing in the caption. Further, Defendant is not prejudiced.  Defendant has known Plaintiff’s identity throughout the litigation, has litigated on that basis, and does not oppose the Motion. 

Based on the foregoing reasons, Plaintiff L.W.’s Motion for Leave to Proceed Under Initials “L.W.” is GRANTED. Plaintiff may proceed under his initials in all publicly filed documents, with his true name available for use in discovery subject to any appropriate protective order.

Case Number: 23SMCV05198    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: N

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant Elie Samaha’s Motion to Set Aside Default Judgment is DENIED.

Plaintiff The Wilshire Condominium Association to give notice. 

REASONING

Defendant moves to vacate the default judgment entered against him, arguing he did not receive actual notice in time to defend.  Defendant contends the substituted service was left with a front desk security guard at a location where he does not reside, and that he never received the documents.  Defendant contends he acted immediately upon learning of the judgment on April 15, 2026.  

In opposition, Plaintiff argues first the instant motion was never served on Plaintiff and therefore should be denied.  Plaintiff further argues the motion should be denied as untimely. Finally, Plaintiff argues service on Defendant was proper. 

First, the record reflects the instant motion was never served on Plaintiff.  All moving and supporting papers must be served and filed at least 16 court days before the hearing. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1005.)  Proof of service of the moving papers must be filed no later than five court days before the hearing. (Cal. Rules of Court rule 3.1300.)  Defendant’s motion contains no proof of service, and no proof of service has been filed with the Court. 

Second, Defendant’s motion is untimely. A motion “shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of: (i) two years after entry of a default judgment against the party; or (ii) 180 days after service on the party of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5, subd. (a).)  The Court may set aside a default judgment “[u]pon a finding by the court that the motion was made within the period permitted by subdivision (a).” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5, subd. (c).) 

Here, judgment was entered in favor of Plaintiff on May 15, 2025, and written notice of entry was served on Defendant on May 16, 2025.  Therefore, the 180-day period expired on November 12, 2025.  Defendant did not file the instant motion until July 1, 2026.  Because the instant motion was not made within the period permitted by subdivision (a), the Court cannot make the finding that section 473.5(c) requires.  

Regardless, service of the summons and complaint on Defendant was proper.  Substituted service on an individual is authorized where the summons and complaint "cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to the person to be served," by leaving a copy at the person's dwelling house or usual place of abode "in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge," and by thereafter mailing a copy to that address, with service deemed complete on the tenth day after mailing. (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.20, subd. (b).)  

Here, the process server made three attempts to serve Defendant personally at 1331 North Cahuenga Boulevard, Apt. 3409 — on March 5, 8, and 9, 2024 — before effecting substituted service on the front-desk security guard on March 11, 2024, and mailing a copy to Defendant at the same address on March 12, 2024. (Medioni Decl., ¶ 5.) 

Further, Defendant's contention that he does not reside at the service address is contradicted by his own sworn testimony at his July 6, 2026, judgment debtor examination, where he testified that he lived at that address in 2024 and 2025, and therefore lived there at the time service was made.  (Medioni Decl., ¶ 9.)

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s motion to vacate default judgment is denied.  




Case Number: 25SMCV05241    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: N

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendants Sean Combs, Daddy’s House Recordings, Inc., CE OPCO, LLC d/b/a Combs Global f/k/a Combs Enterprises LLC, Bad Boy Entertainment Holdings, Inc., Bad Boy Books Holdings, Inc., Bad Boy Entertainment LLC, and Bad Boy Productions, LLC’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Complaint is OVERRULED in part, and SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND IN PART. 
 
The demurrer to the first cause of action is sustained with leave to amend as to the Corporate Defendants.  The demurrer to the second cause of action is overruled as to Combs and sustained without leave to amend as to the Corporate Defendants.  The demurrer to the third cause of action is sustained with leave to amend as to all Defendants.  

Defendants Sean Combs, Daddy’s House Recordings, Inc., CE OPCO, LLC d/b/a Combs Global f/k/a Combs Enterprises LLC, Bad Boy Entertainment Holdings, Inc., Bad Boy Books Holdings, Inc., Bad Boy Entertainment LLC, and Bad Boy Productions, LLC’s Motion to Strike portions of Plaintiff’s Complaint is DENIED. 

Plaintiff Lakeisha Ward may file and serve an amended complaint within ten (10) days of entry of this order.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1320(g).)

Moving parties to give notice. 

REASONING

Legal Standard
“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967), but the Court does not “assume the truth of contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law” (Moore v. Regents of University of California (1990) 51 Cal.3d 120, 125).

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

Uncertainty
Defendants demur to all three causes of action on the ground they are uncertain as to the Corporate Defendants. Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (f) provides for a demurrer where a pleading is uncertain. Demurrers for uncertainty are disfavored and are only sustained where a pleading is so incomprehensible a defendant cannot reasonably respond. (A.J. Fistes Corp. v. GDL Best Contractors, Inc. (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 677, 695; Khoury v. Maly’s of California (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)   

The allegations in the complaint are sufficiently clear that Plaintiff is attempting to hold Defendants liable for sexual assault, gender violence, and negligent supervision and retention.  Defendants’ demurrer is overruled on the grounds of uncertainty. 

Statute of Limitations
Defendants demur to all three causes of action on the grounds they are barred by the applicable statutes of limitation. 

Code of Civil Procedure section 340.16 governs civil actions for damages suffered as a result of a sexual assault occurring on or after the plaintiff’s 18th birthday. (Code Civ. Proc., § 340.16, subd. (a).)  An action for sexual assault shall be commenced on the later of the following: “within 10 years from the date of the last act, attempted act, or assault with the intent to commit an act, of sexual assault against the plaintiff,” or “within three years from the date the plaintiff discovers or reasonably should have discovered that an injury or illness resulted from an act, attempted act, or assault with the intent to commit an act, of sexual assault against the plaintiff.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 340.16, subds. (a)(1)-(2).)

Here, Plaintiff alleges the assault occurred in 2018, and filed suit in 2025, falling within the ten-year period.  Therefore, Plaintiff’s first cause of action is not barred by the statute of limitations. 

Further, Code of Civil Procedure section 340.16 applies to an entire civil action seeking recovery of damages suffered as a result of sexual assault.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 340.16; see also Jane Doe #21 (S.H.) v. CFR Enters., Inc. (2023) 93 Cal.App.5th 1199,1211.)  Claims based on conduct occurring on or after January 1, 2009, commenced on or after January 1, 2019, that would otherwise be time-barred, are permitted to be commenced until December 31, 2026. (Code Civ. Proc., § 340.16, subd. (b)(3).)  

As discussed above, Plaintiff’s alleged assault sits squarely within the revival windows, and the date she filed suit falls before the December 31, 2026, deadline.  Accordingly, Plaintiff’s second and third causes of action are not barred by the statute of limitations. 

Defendants’ demurrer based on the grounds of statute of limitations is overruled. 

First Cause of Action: Sexual Assault 
Civil Code section 1708.5, subdivision (a), states that “[a] person commits a sexual battery who does any of the following: [¶] (1) Acts with the intent to cause a harmful or offensive contact with an intimate part of another, and a sexually offensive contact with that person directly or indirectly results. [¶] (2) Acts with the intent to cause a harmful or offensive contact with another by use of his or her intimate part, and a sexually offensive contact with that person directly or indirectly results. [¶] (3) Acts to cause an imminent apprehension of the conduct described in paragraph (1) or (2), and a sexually offensive contact with that person directly or indirectly results.” Subdivision (b) of Civil Code section 1708.5 makes “[a] person who commits a sexual battery upon another [] liable to that person for damages, including, but not limited to, general damages, special damages, and punitive damages.”  

Defendants demur to the first cause of action on the grounds it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against Corporate Defendants and is uncertain as to Corporate Defendants. 

The Complaint fails to sufficiently allege facts against the Corporate Defendants to support a cause of action for sexual assault. The Complaint alleges the following.  The sexual assault by Combs was ratified and/or perpetrated by the Corporate Defendants. (Compl., ¶ 53.) The Corporate Defendants enabled and conspired with Combs to commit the offending sexual battery because Combs sexually assaulted Plaintiff at a party related to the Corporate Defendants. (Compl., ¶ 56.)  The Corporate Defendants routinely committed sexual assault and sexual battery to further the business purpose of Combs’ enterprise. (Compl., ¶ 56.)  Corporate Defendants failed to protect Plaintiff from a known danger or have sufficient policies in place. (Compl., ¶ 57.)  The Complaint does not allege any Corporate Defendants were present at, participated in, or committed any act toward Plaintiff in connection with the alleged assault.  Conclusory statements are not admitted on demurrer.  

Further, ratification and alter ego are not sufficiently plead.  Ratification “occurs when the principal accepts the benefit of the agent’s act either with actual knowledge of the material facts or with knowledge of facts that would have led a reasonable person to investigate further.” (Brown v. DirecTV, LLC (2021) 562 F. Supp. 3d 590, 610.)  Plaintiff alleges the sexual assault act was ratified by the Corporate Defendants, however Plaintiff does not allege facts that the Corporate Defendants learned of, investigated, or approved of Plaintiff’s alleged sexual assault. (Compl., ¶ 53.) 

“In California, two conditions must be met before the alter ego doctrine will be invoked. First, there must be such a unity of interest and ownership between the corporation and its equitable owner that the separate personalities of the corporation and the shareholder do not in reality exist. Second, there must be an inequitable result if the acts in question are treated as those of the corporation alone.” (Sonora Diamond Corp. v. Superior Court (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 523, 538.) “Among the factors to be considered in applying the doctrine are commingling of funds and other assets of the two entities, the holding out by one entity that it is liable for the debts of the other, identical equitable ownership in the two entities, use of the same offices and employees, and use of one as a mere shell or conduit for the affairs of the other,” or “inadequate capitalization, disregard of corporate formalities, lack of segregation of corporate records, and identical directors and officers.”  (Ibid.)

Here, the Complaint merely alleges the Corporate Defendants are alter egos of Combs and that Plaintiff alleges Combs owned and/or controlled the Corporate Defendants. (Compl., ¶¶ 24, 28, 29, 32.)  However, Plaintiff does not allege any facts that there was commingling of funds, that one entity is liable for the debts of another, disregard of corporate formalities, or lack of segregation of corporate records. 
Accordingly, the demurrer to the first cause of action is sustained with leave to amend as to the Corporate Defendants. 


Second Cause of Action: Gender Violence 
Civil Code section 52.4, subdivision (a), allows “[a]ny person who has been subjected to gender violence” to “bring a civil action for damages against any responsible party,” and “[t]he plaintiff may seek actual damages, compensatory damages, punitive damages, injunctive relief,” and attorney’s fees and costs. “‘[G]ender violence’ is a form of sex discrimination” and means either an act “that would constitute a criminal offense under state law that has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person or property of another, committed at least in part based on the gender of the victim, whether or not those acts have resulted in criminal complaints, charges, prosecution, or conviction,” or “[a] physical intrusion or physical invasion of a sexual nature under coercive conditions, whether or not those acts have resulted in criminal complaints, charges, prosecution, or conviction.” (Civ. Code, § 52.4, subd. (c).)  

Defendants demur to the second cause of action on the grounds it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for gender violence as to all Defendants. 

As to Combs, the Court finds the Complaint sufficiently alleges a cause of action for gender violence.  Defendants argue the Complaint fails to plead gender-based motivation, relying on Doe v. Kachru (2025) 115 Cal.App.5th 175, which held that “some discriminatory motivation is a requisite element” of a section 52.4 claim. (Doe v. Kachru (2025) 115 Cal.App.5th 175, 198.)  This argument does not defeat the claim at the demurrer stage, because Plaintiff pleads the alternative definition in subdivision (c)(2), a physical intrusion of a sexual nature under coercive conditions, and the allegations of the complaint satisfy that definition. 

The Complaint alleges the following. Plaintiff is a female residing in Clark County, Nevada. Plaintiff was invited to the party where she was subsequently sexually assaulted. Defendants’ acts committed against Plaintiff constitute gender violence and a form of sex discrimination that would constitute a criminal offense under state law and was committed at least in part based on the gender of the victim.  (Compl. ¶¶ 23, 35-44, 65.) For purposes of demurrer, Plaintiff’s allegations are taken as true.  This is sufficient to support a cause of action of gender violence against Combs.  Defendants’ demurrer is overruled as to the second cause of action as to Combs. 

As to the Corporate Defendants, the Complaint fails to sufficiently allege a cause of action for gender violence. “Notwithstanding any other laws that may establish the liability of an employer for the acts of an employee, this section does not establish any civil liability of a person because of his or her status as an employer, unless the employer personally committed an act of gender violence.” (Civ. Code, § 52.4, subd. (e).)  The Complaint does not allege any Corporate Defendants personally committed an act of gender violence, only alleging the Corporate Defendants enabled, failed to supervise, or ratified Combs’s actions.  Further, because a corporation cannot personally commit an alleged sexual assault, there is no room to amend.  Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer is sustained without leave to amend as to the second cause of action as to the Corporate Defendants.

Third Cause of Action: Negligent Supervision and Retention
The elements of a cause of action for negligent hiring, retention, or supervision are: (1) the employer’s hiring, retaining, or supervising an employee; (2) the employee was incompetent or unfit; (3) the employer had reason to believe undue risk of harm would exist because of the employment; and (4) harm occurs. (Evan F. v. Hughson United Methodist Church (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 828, 836-837.)   The theory applies only where a person or entity in a supervisorial position over the actor had prior knowledge of the actor’s propensity to do the wrongful act. (Z.V. v. County of Riverside (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 889, 902.)

Defendants demur to the third cause of action on the grounds it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for negligent supervision and retention against all Defendants. 

The Complaint fails to sufficiently allege a cause of action for negligent supervision and retention.  The Complaint alleges Combs owned and/or controlled the Corporate Defendants. (Compl., ¶ 24.) The Complaint alleges the Corporate Defendants enabled and conspired with Combs to commit the offending sexual battery by failing to properly supervise or to protect Plaintiff from a known danger. (Compl., ¶¶ 74-77.)  The Complaint alleges the Corporate Defendants actively placed, maintained, or employed Combs in a position of power, despite the knowledge that Combs had a widespread and well-known practice of committing sexual assault. (Compl., ¶ 77.) 

However, none of these allegations support a cause of action for negligent supervision and retention.  No person or entity is plead to have occupied a supervisorial position over Combs. Indeed, the alter-ego theory Plaintiff advances to reach the entities on the other causes of action is in tension with the negligent supervision cause of action.  An entity that is a mere instrumentality “completely dominated” by Combs cannot simultaneously be an independent supervisor who could have controlled him.  Further, as against Combs, the claim fails as a matter of law.  The perpetrator of a sexual assault cannot be the supervisor charged with negligently failing to prevent his own act. 

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer is sustained with leave to amend as to the third cause of action as to all Defendants.

Motion to Strike 
Further, the court may, upon motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false, or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.)  

Defendants move to strike portions of Plaintiff’s Complaint, namely references Combs’ history of other allegations involving other individuals, incidents, or lawsuits.  

A motion to strike is disfavored and reaches only “irrelevant, false, or improper matter,” and should be used “cautiously and sparingly.” (Code Civ. Proc., 436, subd. (a); PH II, Inc. V. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1680, 1682-1683.)  The challenged portions of Plaintiff’s Complaint of Combs’ history are relevant to the matters that remain.  The allegations of prior misconduct may bear on notice and motive, as well as punitive damages against Combs and the gender violence cause of action.  Further, Defendants’ concern that the allegations invite an improper propensity inference is better addressed through objections to evidence, not by excising the allegations at the pleading stage. 

Accordingly, Defendants’ motion to strike is denied. 

Case Number: 25SMCV06441    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: N

TENTATIVE RULING

Accordingly, the Court finds that Defendant Maria has sufficiently demonstrated a settlement made in good faith. Accordingly, Defendant Maria’s Application for Good Faith Settlement Determination is GRANTED. 

Any other joint tortfeasors or co-obligors are barred from asserting any further claims against Defendant Maria based on comparative negligence or comparative fault.

Defendant Maria Garcia Diaz to give notice.

REASONING

Code of Civil Procedure section 877.6 enables a settling defendant to free itself not only from any further claims of the plaintiff but also from any subsequent liability to any non-settling defendant for equitable comparative contribution, or partial or comparative indemnity. A settling defendant obtains this immunity by settling in “good faith.” Code of Civil Procedure section 877.6, subdivision (b), provides that the court may determine the good faith of a settlement on the basis of affidavits and counter affidavits or may, in its discretion, receive other evidence at the hearing. Section 877.6 grants the trial court broad discretion in determining whether a settlement is in good faith for purposes of that statute, and “its decision may be reversed only upon a showing of abuse of discretion.” (TSI Seismic Tenant Space, Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 159, 165.)

To determine whether a settlement was in “good faith” the court should inquire as to whether the amount of the settlement is “within the reasonable range” of the settling defendant’s proportional share of comparative liability for the plaintiff’s injuries. (Abbott Ford, Inc. v. Superior Court (1987) 43 Cal.3d 858, 872.) Ultimately, the “settlement figure must not be grossly disproportionate to what a reasonable person, at the time of the settlement, would estimate the settling defendant’s liability to be.” (Tech-Bilt v. Woodward-Clyde & Assocs. (1985) 38 Cal.3d 488, 499 (Tech-Bilt).) In determining the good faith of a settlement, the court should consider a number of factors, including:

(1) A rough approximation of the total recovery and the settlor’s proportionate liability;
(2) The actual settlement amount;
(3) The allocation of settlement proceeds among plaintiffs;
(4) The recognition that a settlor should pay less in settlement than he or she would if liability is established after trial;
(5) The financial conditions and insurance policy limits of the settlor; and
(6) The existence of facts showing collusion, fraud, or tortious conduct aimed to injure the interests of nonsettling defendants.

(Ibid.) Furthermore, the evaluation must “be made on the basis of information available at the time of settlement.” (Ibid.)

The party contesting the settlement bears the burden of proving that the settlement is in bad faith. (Code Civ. Proc., § 877.6, subd. (d).) Notably, when the good faith nature of a settlement is uncontested, the Court need not consider and weigh the Tech-Bilt factors. (City of Grand Terrace v. Superior Court (1987) 192 Cal.App.3d 1251, 1261.) “[W]hen no one objects, the barebones motion which sets forth the ground of good faith, accompanied by a declaration which sets forth a brief background of the case is sufficient.” (Ibid.)

Defendant Maria Garcia Diaz (“Defendant Maria”) moves the court for a determination of good faith settlement with Plaintiff George Doctorian (“Plaintiff”).  Defendant Maria and Plaintiff reached a settlement agreement on October 22, 2025, for Defendant Maria’s $15,000.00 policy limits in exchange for the release of Defendant Maria for all claims relating to the automobile accident that occurred on July 17, 2024. (Mot., pp. 4-5.) 

Defendant Hector Antonio Garcia Diaz (“Defendant Hector”) opposes the motion, arguing Defendant Maria has failed to carry her burden of presenting sufficient information to evaluate the settlement against the Tech-Bilt factors. 

Applying the Tech-Bilt factors, the Court finds the settlement between Plaintiff and Defendant Maria was made in good faith.  The Court notes there is only one plaintiff in this matter, therefore the third factor is inapplicable.  

a. Rough Approximation of Plaintiff’s Total Recovery and Settlor’s Proportionate Liability 

Defendant Maria argues the $15,000 settlement equals Plaintiff’s total recovery and leaves little for Plaintiff to claim against Defendant Hector. (Mot., p. 9.)  Defendant Maria argues the settlement is within the reasonable range of the Defendant Maria’s proportional share of comparative liability for Plaintiff’s injuries. (Mot., p. 9.) The Court agrees that the settlement amount is a fair estimation of Defendant Maria’s apparent liability to Plaintiff for rear ending Plaintiff’s vehicle. 

b. Amount Paid in Settlement  

The settlement amount of $15,000, to be paid to Plaintiff in exchange for a full release of claims against Defendant Maria arising from the incident, supports a finding of good faith.  

c. Settlor’s Financial Condition and Insurance Policy Limits 

The party contesting the settlement bears the burden of proving that the settlement is in bad faith. (Code Civ. Proc., § 877.6, subd. (d).)

Defendant Hector argues Defendant Maria fails to provide information sufficient to permit the required Tech-Bilt analysis, and that the settlement appears disproportionate to Defendant Maria’s share of liability. (Opp., p. 5.)  Defendant Hector argues he will be prejudiced if the motion is granted because he does not have the benefit of a settlement extinguishing his own exposure, and remains fully exposed to any verdict in excess of his insurance coverage. (Opp., p. 8.) 

Defendant Maria has sufficiently supported the settlement with competent evidence.  As to insurance limits, Defendant Maria has submitted the declarations page of her automobile insurance policy, which sets bodily injury limits of $15,000 per person and $30,000 per occurrence.  (Asare Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. A.)  Additionally, Defendant Maria submitted a Declaration of Assets, stating she had no umbrella, excess, or other liability coverage beyond the National General policy, and that her insurance would not cover all of Plaintiff’s claimed damages. (Asare Decl., ¶ 7, Ex. C.)  

In contrast, Defendant Hector has offered no evidence in support of his argument that the settlement was made in bad faith.  

d. Collusion, Fraud, or Tortious Conduct  

The settlement agreement was reached by Plaintiff and Defendant Maria following an arm’s length negotiation through insurance claims representatives. (Asare Decl., ¶ 13.)  Upon this record, the Court finds no collusion, fraud, or tortious conduct present.  

Accordingly, the Court finds that Defendant Maria has sufficiently demonstrated a settlement made in good faith. Accordingly, Defendant Maria’s Application for Good Faith Settlement Determination is GRANTED. Any other joint tortfeasors or co-obligors are barred from asserting any further claims against Defendant Maria based on comparative negligence or comparative fault.
