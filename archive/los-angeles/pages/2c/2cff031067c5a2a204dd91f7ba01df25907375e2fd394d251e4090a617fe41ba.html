DEPARTMENT 6 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24WCUD01154    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: 6

CASE NAME:  John J. Gerard Jr., as Trustee of the Gerard Revocable Marital Trust Agreement v. Janet G. Cuevas, et al. 

Defendants’ Demurrer to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint 

TENTATIVE RULING 

The Court SUSTAINS Defendants’ demurrer to the First and Second Causes of Action of Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint without leave to amend. The Court directs Defendants to submit a proposed judgment of dismissal. 

             Defendants are ordered to give notice of the Court’s ruling within five calendar days of this order. 

BACKGROUND 

This is an unlawful detainer action. On May 31, 2024, plaintiff John J. Gerard Jr., as Trustee of the Gerard Revocable Marital Trust Agreement (Plaintiff) filed this action against defendants Janet G. Cuevas (Janet), Jorge H. Cuevas (Jorge)[1] (collectively, Defendants), and Does I through III, alleging the sole cause of action for unlawful detainer. 

Default was entered against Jorge on July 8, 2024. 

During the jury trial proceedings, the Court granted Plaintiff’s oral motion to strike Defendant Janet’s answer and the matter proceeding with a prove-up trial. On October 23, 2024, the Court entered judgment in favor of Plaintiff and against Defendants. 

On November 21, 2025, the appellate division reversed the judgment and remanded the case to the trial court with instructions to set aside the order striking Janet’s answer. 

On August 11, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (FAC) against Defendants, alleging causes of action for breach of lease and common counts. 

On August 10, 2026,[2] Defendants demurred to the FAC. On September 11, 2026, Plaintiff opposed the demurrer. On September 16, 2026, Defendants replied to Plaintiff’s opposition. 

LEGAL STANDARD 

            A demurrer is a pleading used to test the legal sufficiency of other pleadings. It raises issues of law, not fact, regarding the form or content of the opposing party's pleading (complaint, answer or cross-complaint). (Code Civ. Proc., § 422.10; see Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 (Donabedian).) It is not the function of the demurrer to challenge the truthfulness of the complaint; and for purposes of ruling on the demurrer, all facts pleaded in the complaint are assumed to be true. (Id. at pp. 993-994.) 

A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack; or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318; Donabedian, supra, 116 Cal.App.4th at p. 994.) No other extrinsic evidence can be considered. (Ion Equip. Corp. v. Nelson (1980) 110 Cal.App.3d 868, 881 [error for court to consider facts asserted in memorandum supporting demurrer]; see also Afuso v. United States Fid. & Guar. Co. (1985) 169 Cal.App.3d 859, 862, disapproved on other grounds in Moradi-Shalal v. Fireman’s Fund Ins. Cos. (1988) 46 Cal.3d 287 [error to consider contents of release not part of court record].) 

A demurrer can be utilized where the “face of the complaint” itself is incomplete or discloses some defense that would bar recovery. (Guardian North Bay, Inc. v. Superior Court (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 963, 971-972.) The “face of the complaint” includes material contained in attached exhibits that are incorporated by reference into the complaint, or in a superseded complaint in the same action. (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94; see also Barnett v. Fireman’s Fund Ins. Co. (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 500, 505 [“[W]e rely on and accept as true the contents of the exhibits and treat as surplusage the pleader’s allegations as to the legal effect of the exhibits”].) 

A demurrer can only be sustained when it disposes of an entire pleading, cause of action, or affirmative defense. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1320, subd. (a); Poizner v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redev. Agency (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1046-1047.)  

REQUESTS FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE 

            The Court GRANTS Defendants’ request for judicial notice. (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (d).) The Court also GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice. (Id., § 452, subds. (b)-(c).) However, the Court takes judicial notice only as to “the existence, content and authenticity of public records and other specified documents” and the legal effect of the documents’ language when that effect is clear, it does not take judicial notice of the truth of the factual matters asserted in those documents. (Dominguez v. Bonta (2022) 87 Cal.App.5th 389, 400; Fontenot v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 256, 265.) 

DISCUSSION 

Meet and Confer 

Per Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a), Defendants were required to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference before bringing this demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) Defendants failed to meet and confer by any of these methods. (Post Decl., ¶¶ 3-6.) Nevertheless, the Court may not overrule a demurrer for failure to adequately meet and confer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(4).) The Court admonishes Defendants to comply with the Code of Civil Procedure going forward. 

Amendment After Trial 

(a) Except as provided in subdivisions (b) and (c), if the lessor brings an unlawful detainer proceeding and possession of the property is no longer in issue because possession of the property has been delivered to the lessor before trial or, if there is no trial, before judgment is entered, the case becomes an ordinary civil action in which: 

(1) The lessor may obtain any relief to which he is entitled, including, where applicable, relief authorized by Section 1951.2; but, if the lessor seeks to recover damages described in paragraph (3) of subdivision (a) of Section 1951.2 or any other damages not recoverable in the unlawful detainer proceeding, the lessor shall first amend the complaint pursuant to Section 472 or 473 of the Code of Civil Procedure so that possession of the property is no longer in issue and to state a claim for such damages and shall serve a copy of the amended complaint on the defendant in the same manner as a copy of a summons and original complaint is served. 

(2) The defendant may, by appropriate pleadings or amendments to pleadings, seek any affirmative relief, and assert all defenses, to which he is entitled, whether or not the lessor has amended the complaint; but subdivision (a) of Section 426.30 of the Code of Civil Procedure does not apply unless, after delivering possession of the property to the lessor, the defendant (i) files a cross-complaint or (ii) files an answer or an amended answer in response to an amended complaint filed pursuant to paragraph (1). 

(Civ. Code, § 1952.3, subd. (a).) 

            Defendants demur to both causes of action of the FAC on the grounds that an unlawful detainer plaintiff cannot amend after trial to convert the matter into an ordinary civil action. Defendants contend that Civil Code section 1952.3 is the exclusive mechanism for converting an unlawful detainer into an ordinary civil action, permitting conversion only where possession is delivered to the lessor before trial or, absent a trial, before judgment. They argue that condition is not met because Defendants did not surrender possession, but they were removed by sheriff’s writ after both a prove-up trial and entry of judgment. Defendants further contend that the general amendment statutes, i.e., Code of Civil Procedure sections 473 and 576, cannot override Civil Code section 1952.3’s specific statutory restrictions on conversion. Because possession was recovered after trial and judgment, Defendants argue the action cannot be converted and the FAC’s new causes of action are impermissible. 

            In opposition, Plaintiff contends that section 1952.3 contains no “voluntary surrender” requirement and that Defendants improperly import that term from an unrelated footnote in Green v. Superior Court. Plaintiff argues that Code of Civil Procedure sections 473, subdivision (a)(1), and 576 independently authorized the amendment the Court had already granted, so section 1952.3 is not the sole basis for amending the complaint. Relying on the 1976 Law Revision Commission history, Plaintiff contends section 1952.3 was enacted to promote judicial economy by avoiding a separate action once possession is no longer at issue. Plaintiff maintains that the appellate reversal was purely procedural and restored the parties to their original rights, leaving intact Plaintiff’s right to recover contract damages while only possession cannot be restored. Plaintiff argues Defendants’ reading would deny due process and exceed anything the statute or its legislative history intends, and asks the court to overrule the demurrer. 

            In reply, Defendants contend that Plaintiff never disputes the dispositive fact that possession was delivered only after trial and after judgment — the opposite of what section 1952.3 requires. They argue the “voluntary” debate is beside the point because the statute turns on the undisputed timing, which the post-judgment writ eviction did not satisfy. Defendants contend the restitution-on-reversal doctrine addresses what is restored after reversal, not the historical fact of when possession was taken, and cannot rewrite the section 1952.3 timeline. They argue that section 1952.3 is the specific statute governing conversion and controls over the general amendment provisions of sections 473 and 576. Defendants argue that because the statutory precondition was never met, the matter cannot be converted and the demurrer should be sustained without leave to amend, leaving Plaintiff to a separate action. 

            The Court finds Defendants’ arguments persuasive. First, the Appellate Division reversed the prior judgment solely because striking Janet’s answer was an unauthorized terminating sanction and violated her right to a jury trial, and remanded “with instructions to set aside the order striking defendant’s answer”. (Appeal – Opinion Received (11/21/25).) The Appellate Division did not reach the issues identified in Defendants’ demurrer. (Id.) 

Second, there is no dispute that Defendants did not deliver possession before trial or before judgment and that they were removed from the property after a prove-up trial and entry of judgment. The judgment was entered October 23, 2024, the sheriff’s writ return indicates having placed Plaintiff “in quiet and peaceful possession of the premises” on February 3, 2025, and the FAC alleges Plaintiff regained possession on approximately February 3, 2025. (FAC, ¶ 10; Judgment – Unlawful Detainer (10/23/24); Writ of Possession (10/31/24); Writ – Return (2/3/25); Appeal – Opinion Received (11/21/25); Starr v. Ashbrook (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 999, 1014 [a court can take judicial notice of its own records].) As such, the statutory condition requiring delivery of possession before trial or judgment is not met. (Civ. Code, § 1952.3, subd. (a).) The Court of Appeal in Lynch & Freytag v. Cooper (1990) 218 Cal.App.3d 603 (Lynch) noted a prior case involving a landlord who, after a verdict, attempted to convert an unlawful detainer action into an ordinary civil action for rent through a post-trial motion that was rejected, while the action in the Lynch case “was converted to an ordinary civil action long before trial.” (Id. at p. 610.) Additionally, whether the surrender was voluntary is ultimately beside the point; what matters is whether possession was delivered before trial or judgment. (See Civ. Code, § 1952.3, subd. (a).) 

Third, Plaintiff’s restitution-on-reversal argument does not change the historical sequence here because whatever rights the Appellate Division’s reversal restored, it does not transform possession obtained in February 2025 into possession delivered before trial for purposes of Civil Code section 1952.3, subdivision (a). (See Civ. Code, § 1952.3, subd. (a).) Plaintiff’s opposition acknowledges that “the only ‘right’ not capable of being ‘restored’ is possession.” (Opp., 6:7-13.) Also, if the reversal is absolute, it would have entitled Janet to having possession restored to her, which would put possession back in dispute, so the necessary condition for conversion under section 1952.3, subdivision (a), would not be triggered, but that was not ordered by the Appellate Division. (See Appeal – Opinion Received (11/21/25); Stockton Theatres, Inc. v. Palermo (1953) 121 Cal.App.2d 616, 619; Civ. Code, § 1952.3, subd. (a).) 

Finally, Code of Civil Procedure section 1952.3 is also the specific statute governing conversion and therefore governs here, notwithstanding the general amendment statutes of Code of Civil Procedure sections 473, subdivision (a)(1), and 576. (Civ. Code, § 1859; Med. Bd. of California v. Superior Ct. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 1001, 1004-1005; People v. Superior Ct. of Riverside Cnty. (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 851, 884.) Since possession was not delivered prior to the trial, Plaintiff does not meet the conditions for conversion to an ordinary civil action. 

The Court finds this basis sufficient grounds to sustain the demurrer, and the Court declines to reach the other grounds raised in the demurrer. Additionally, the facts undermining Plaintiff’s claims cannot be remedied by amendment. Therefore, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer to the First and Second Causes of Action of the FAC without leave to amend. 

CONCLUSION 

The Court SUSTAINS Defendants’ demurrer to the First and Second Causes of Action of Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint without leave to amend. The Court directs Defendants to submit a proposed judgment of dismissal. 

             Defendants are ordered to give notice of the Court’s ruling within five calendar days of this order.


[1] The Court refers to Defendants by their first names only to avoid confusion. No disrespect is intended.

[2] It appears Plaintiff served the FAC on Defendants before filing it with the Court given Defendants’ filing of the demurrer before Plaintiff filed the FAC with the Court.

Case Number: 25PSCV03194    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: 6

CASE NAME:  Victor Payan v. San Gabriel Valley Water Company, et al. 

Plaintiff Victor Payan’s Motion to Bifurcate Defendant San Gabriel Valley Water Company’s Cross-Complaint Claims 

TENTATIVE RULING 

The Court DENIES Plaintiff Victor Payan’s Motion to Bifurcate Defendant San Gabriel Valley Water Company’s Cross-Complaint Claims without prejudice to the trial being conducted in phases. A new trial date and related deadlines will be set at the October 6, 2026 trial-setting conference. 

            Defendant/Cross-Complainant San Gabriel Valley Water Company is ordered to give notice of the Court’s ruling within five calendar days of this order. 

BACKGROUND 

This is an auto accident case. On September 3, 2025, plaintiff Victor Payan (Plaintiff) filed this action against defendants San Gabriel Valley Water Company (Water Company) and Dany Alejandro Alvarado (Alvarado) (collectively, Defendants), alleging two causes of action for motor vehicle and general negligence. 

On August 10, 2026, Water Company filed a cross-complaint against cross-defendant Glendora Employment Agency, Inc. (Employment Agency) and Roes 1-20, alleging causes of action for comparative equitable indemnity, contribution, and declaratory relief. 

On August 25, 2026, Plaintiff moved to bifurcate Water Company’s cross-complaint claims. On September 11, 2026, Defendants opposed the motion. On September 15, 2026, Plaintiff replied to Defendants’ opposition. 

LEGAL STANDARD 

“The court may, when the convenience of witnesses, the ends of justice, or the economy and efficiency of handling the litigation would be promoted thereby, on motion of a party, after notice and hearing, make an order, no later than the close of pretrial conference in cases in which such pretrial conference is to be held, or, in other cases, no later than 30 days before the trial date, that the trial of any issue or any part thereof shall precede the trial of any other issue or any part thereof in the case, except for special defenses which may be tried first pursuant to Sections 597 and 597.5.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 598.) 

“The court, in furtherance of convenience or to avoid prejudice, or when separate trials will be conducive to expedition and economy, may order a separate trial of any cause of action, including a cause of action asserted in a cross-complaint, or of any separate issue or of any number of causes of action or issues, preserving the right of trial by jury required by the Constitution or a statute of this state or of the United States.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1048, subd. (b).) 

DISCUSSION 

Summary of Arguments 

Plaintiff moves under Code of Civil Procedure sections 1048, subdivision (b), and 598 to try his negligence claims separately from, and before, Water Company’s cross-complaint against Employment Agency for comparative equitable indemnity, contribution, and declaratory relief. Plaintiff argues the cross-claims are contingent and downstream — arising only if he recovers — and present a discrete employer-to-employer allocation dispute that need not be resolved to adjudicate his claims, especially because Water Company has admitted Alvarado was its employee acting in the course and scope of his employment. Plaintiff contends Water Company’s late cross-complaint should not displace his trial date, that separate trial avoids prejudice and confines collateral staffing evidence to a later phase that a first verdict may narrow or eliminate, and that any overlap is limited. Plaintiff asks the Court to keep his claims on the earliest available date, stay discovery directed solely to Water Company – Employment Agency allocation, or alternatively order a two-phase trial. 

In opposition, Defendants argue the October 28, 2026 trial date was already vacated by the August 5, 2026 order granting leave to file the cross-complaint, and that Rule 3.1332 does not revive a vacated date. They contend bifurcation would create two duplicative trials because the claims arise from the same collision and share overlapping issues — Alvarado’s negligence and employment, the parties’ respective control, comparative fault, causation, and damages — requiring the same witnesses to testify twice and risking inconsistent findings. Defendants argue Employment Agency, which has been served and will appear by October 1, 2026, has a right to litigate the underlying issues, so Plaintiff’s proposal either impairs that right or forces relitigation. Defendants further assert the case is not trial-ready given Plaintiff’s newly disclosed throat condition requiring a November ENT examination, uncompleted expert discovery, and a pending discovery conference, and that the proposed allocation-only discovery stay is unworkable because joint-employment evidence overlaps with the underlying claims. Defendants ask the Court to deny the motion and set a new trial date at the October 6, 2026 trial-setting conference, or, if bifurcation is granted, to set a reasonable non-October date. 

In reply, Plaintiff contends that Defendants identify no prejudice from bifurcation itself — only from the trial date — and that severing the cross-complaint removes the reason the date was vacated. Plaintiff argues section 1048, subdivision (b), expressly authorizes separate trial of a cross-complaint and that American Motorcycle endorses this as the cure for indemnity-cross-complaint prejudice, with its caveat that this is not an attractive alternative limited to cases where a cross-defendant must litigate the plaintiff's proportionate fault — inapplicable because Employment Agency’s exposure is derivative of Alvarado’s fault, which the first trial will resolve. Plaintiff contends that “two duplicative trials” is a fiction because the second phase would resolve only the narrow Water Company– Employment Agency allocation using contracts and employment records, not retry the collision. Plaintiff asserts his throat and swallowing complaints were disclosed at his June 30, 2026 deposition rather than first on August 25, 2026, that remaining discovery and the ENT examination bear only on which trial date to set, and that Loney’s reasoning condemns letting a late cross-complaint reopen pleadings and delay trial. Plaintiff narrows the requested stay — stating no objection to parallel cross-complaint discovery so long as it is not used to continue his trial — and requests the earliest available trial date. 

Analysis           

            The Court finds Plaintiff’s motion unpersuasive. Code of Civil Procedure sections 1048, subdivision (b), and 598 vest the Court with discretion to order separate trials only where doing so furthers convenience, avoids prejudice, or is conducive to expedition and economy, and the moving party bears the burden of demonstrating that those purposes would be served. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 598, 1048, subd. (b).) Because Plaintiff’s negligence claims and Water Company’s indemnity cross-claims arise from the same May 23, 2024 collision and the same alleged joint-employment relationship, separate trials would multiply proceedings rather than streamline them. The Court finds the interests of judicial economy are better served by a single trial of all claims. 

Moreover, the two proceedings would substantially overlap: Alvarado’s alleged negligence, his employment and the respective control exercised over him, Plaintiff’s comparative fault, causation, and damages are common to both Plaintiff’s claims and the allocation dispute, and would require the same percipient, corporate, and expert witnesses to testify twice before two juries. (See generally Compl.; Cross-Compl.) That duplication risks inconsistent findings and imposes an unnecessary burden on the Court, the parties, and the witnesses. Severance would also implicate Employment Agency’s right to litigate the underlying issues because binding Employment Agency to first-phase findings would require its meaningful participation in that phase, whereas not binding it would force those same issues to be retried. Severance of a comparative-indemnity claim “may at times not be an attractive alternative,” precisely because a cross-defendant retains the right to litigate the plaintiff’s proportionate fault. (Am. Motorcycle Assn. v. Superior Ct. (1978) 20 Cal.3d 578, 606.) Those concerns are best avoided by trying all claims together. 

Finally, Plaintiff’s related request to preserve the October 28, 2026 trial date is moot because the Court's August 5, 2026 order vacated that date when it granted leave to file the cross-complaint, and California Rules of Court, rule 3.1332, subdivision (a), does not revive a date the Court has vacated. (Minute Order (8/5/26); see Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1332, subd. (a).) Independent of bifurcation, the matter is not yet ready for trial: Plaintiff’s throat and swallowing complaints require an ear-nose-throat examination not available until November 2026, and expert discovery has not yet commenced. (Vandenberghe Decl., ¶ 4.) The proposed stay of discovery directed “solely” to Water Company–Employment Agency is problematic because evidence of who hired, trained, supervised, and controlled Alvarado bears on both the allocation dispute and the agency and course-and-scope issues in Plaintiff’s own case, and Plaintiff has in any event withdrawn his objection to parallel cross-complaint discovery. (Reply, 6:6-12.) Permitting all parties, including Employment Agency, to complete discovery and mediate together preserves the prospect of resolving the entire action in a single proceeding. 

Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES the motion without prejudice to the trial being conducted in phases. A new trial date and related deadlines will be set at the October 6, 2026 trial-setting conference. 

CONCLUSION 

The Court DENIES Plaintiff Victor Payan’s Motion to Bifurcate Defendant San Gabriel Valley Water Company’s Cross-Complaint Claims without prejudice to the trial being conducted in phases. A new trial date and related deadlines will be set at the October 6, 2026 trial-setting conference. 

            Defendant/Cross-Complainant San Gabriel Valley Water Company is ordered to give notice of the Court’s ruling within five calendar days of this order.

Case Number: 26PSCV00063    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: 6

CASE NAME:  Mandy Marie Zuniga v. General Motors LLC 

Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney Fees, Costs and Expenses 

TENTATIVE RULING 

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff's Motion for Attorney Fees, Costs and Expenses in the amount of $14,624.71, including $14,015.00 in attorney fees and $609.71 in costs. 

             Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of the Court’s ruling within five calendar days of this order. 

BACKGROUND 

This is a Song-Beverly action. On January 7, 2026, plaintiff Mandy Marie Zuniga (Plaintiff) filed this action against defendant General Motors LLC (Defendant) and Does 1-100, alleging causes of action for breach of implied warranty, breach of express warranty, violation of the Song Beverly [sic] Consumer Warranty Act, and violation of California Civil Code section 1793.2(b). 

On August 25, 2026, Plaintiff moved for attorney fees, costs, and expenses. On September 11, 2026, Defendant opposed the motion. On September 14, 2026, Plaintiff replied to Defendant’s opposition. 

LEGAL STANDARD 

            “The following items are allowable as costs under Section 1032:… (10) Attorney's fees, when authorized by any of the following: (A) Contract. (B) Statute. (C) Law.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10).) “If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney's fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).) 

The party seeking fees and costs bears the burden to show "the fees incurred were allowable, were reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation, and were reasonable in amount." (Nightingale v. Hyundai Motor America (1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 99, 104.) To satisfy this burden, evidence and descriptions of billable tasks must be presented in sufficient detail, enabling the court to evaluate whether the case was overstaffed, the time attorneys spent on specific claims, and the reasonableness of the hours expended. (Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 459, 486-487.) 

“A trial court assessing attorney fees begins with a touchstone or lodestar figure, based on the ‘careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney… involved in the presentation of the case.” (Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1321.) “The reasonableness of attorney fees is within the discretion of the trial court, to be determined from a consideration of such factors as the nature of the litigation, the complexity of the issues, the experience and expertise of counsel and the amount of time involved. [Citation.] The court may also consider whether the amount requested is based upon unnecessary or duplicative work. [Citation.]” (Wilkerson v. Sullivan (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.)

“The courts repeatedly have stated that the trial court is in the best position to value the services rendered by the attorneys in his or her courtroom [citation], and this includes the determination of the hourly rate that will be used in the lodestar calculus. [Citation.]” (569 E. Cnty. Boulevard LLC v. Backcountry Against the Dump, Inc. (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 426, 436-437.)

A verified fee bill is prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses, and services listed were necessarily incurred. (Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682.) 

PRELIMINARY ISSUES 

            Defendant’s opposition quotes Missouri v. Jenkins by Agyei (1989) 491 U.S. 274 as stating that “purely clerical or secretarial tasks should not be billed ...regardless of who performs them,” omitting the words “at a paralegal rate.” (Opp., 5:26-28.) The omission makes the quotation sound broader than it is. The Court identified this same omission by the same firm in Baranes v. General Motors LLC, SCLAC No. 26PSCV00003 (8/20/26), and admonished counsel to quote authority accurately. 

DISCUSSION 

Summary of Arguments 

Plaintiff seeks $19,136.50 in attorney fees and $609.71 in costs, for a total of $19,746.21, as the prevailing buyer under Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), following her acceptance of Defendant’s section 998 offer for a $73,000 repurchase that designated her the prevailing party. Plaintiff contends the lodestar of 41.6 hours expended plus 2.8 reasonably anticipated hours across four timekeepers, at rates of $350.00 to $495.00, is reasonable, non-duplicative, and supported by verified contemporaneous billing records that constitute prima facie evidence the time was necessarily incurred. Plaintiff argues the requested rates are within prevailing community rates and have been approved by other California courts, and that the fee need not be proportionate to the recovery. Plaintiff characterizes the matter as genuinely litigated work — vehicle-specific investigation, review of Defendant’s production including 24 recorded calls, deposition preparation, and the section 998 analysis — driven in part by Defendant’s own conduct. Plaintiff seeks the lodestar only, with no multiplier. 

In opposition, Defendant argues the request is patently unreasonable for a straightforward lemon-law case that was filed, negotiated, and settled in roughly five months without formal discovery, depositions taken, motions, expert work, or trial preparation, consistent with the streamlining intent of Assembly Bill 1755. Defendant contends a line-by-line review shows that 28.9 of the billed hours were not reasonably or actually incurred, and identifies specific categories for reduction: anticipated time, excessive intake and pre-litigation tasks, administrative and clerical work, cancelled-deposition preparation, document-production review, the templated fee motion, initial disclosures, review of Defendant’s standard answer, and redundant client communications. Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s counsel’s heavy reliance on templates shows the professed hours were not performed, and that clerical and “cost of doing business” tasks are not compensable. Defendant objects to the 2.8 anticipated hours as speculative and untethered to “actual time expended” under section 1794, subdivision (d). Defendant requests that the award be reduced to no more than $6,452.75 in fees and $609.71 in costs, a total of $7,062.46, and states it does not challenge the costs. 

In reply, Plaintiff argues Defendant failed to carry its burden to identify specific objectionable entries with supporting evidence, noting that Defendant’s opposition repeatedly cites a "Declaration of Ryan Kay" that was never filed. Plaintiff contends Defendant announced a rate challenge in its introduction but never developed one, proposed no alternative rate, and in fact asked that work be compensated at counsel’s own $350 rate. Plaintiff argues Defendant’s largest requested reductions are unsupported — that reviewing 810 pages and 113 minutes of audio in 1.5 hours is an arithmetic impossibility, and that Defendant’s intake reduction counts time marked "NOT CHARGED" and billed at $0. Plaintiff maintains that the work Defendant labels excessive was generated by Defendant’s own conduct, including the deposition Defendant noticed, kept on calendar, and cancelled the night before, and that AB 1755 compressed the timeline but did not narrow section 1794, subdivision (d), or reduce the compensable work. 

Analysis 

                        Prevailing Parties 

            There is no dispute that Plaintiff is the prevailing party and therefore entitled to recover attorney fees and costs. The parties’ settlement agreement provides that such fees and costs may be determined by way of a noticed motion. (Geoulla Decl., Ex. 1, ¶ 5; Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).) Defendant’s Opposition also tacitly acknowledges Plaintiff is the prevailing party. (See generally Opp.; see also D.I. Chadbourne, Inc. v. Superior Court (1964) 60 Cal.2d 723, 728, fn. 4 [where nonmoving party fails to oppose a ground for a motion, “it is assumed that [nonmoving party] concedes” that ground].) 

                        Hourly Rates 

            The Court finds Plaintiff’s counsel’s hourly rates reasonable for the East Judicial District. “The courts repeatedly have stated that the trial court is in the best position to value the services rendered by the attorneys in his or her courtroom [citation], and this includes the determination of the hourly rate that will be used in the lodestar calculus. [Citation.]” (569 E. Cnty. Boulevard LLC v. Backcountry Against the Dump, Inc. (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 426, 436-437.) This determination considers the complexity of the case, the quality of services provided, and the attorneys’ experience. An award at the rates requested comes with the expectation that the attorneys work in an efficient manner that reflects the elevated premium paid for their services. The Court considers this factor in addressing the reasonableness of the hours expended. 

                        Hours Expended 

            Plaintiff also provided a verified fee bill, which is prima facie evidence that Plaintiff’s fees and costs listed therein were necessarily incurred. (Geoulla Decl., Ex. 2; Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682.) Defendant therefore bears the burden of challenging the specific portions of the fee bill it contends are improper or unreasonable. (Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc. v. California Ins. Guarantee Assn. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 564 (Premier [burden is on challenging party to point to specific items challenged with sufficient argument and citations to the evidence, and failure to raise specific challenges in the trial court forfeits the claim on appeal].) Defendant’s opposition disputes various charges. 

            The Court finds Plaintiff’s claimed fees somewhat excessive. This case settled within approximately six months of it being initiated and contains no motion practice other than this motion. Moreover, there was nothing particularly complex or unique about this case. The issues involved were applicable to other consumers’ vehicles, thereby triggering economies of scale in terms of Plaintiff’s counsel’s efficiency in litigating this type of lemon law case. Litigating this matter “’should not have required anything more than [a] slight factual modification to [an] existing boilerplate.’” (See Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of N. Am., LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 250.) 

In light of the foregoing discussion and in view of the totality of the circumstances, the Court finds that the total amount of reasonable attorney fees in this case, using a lodestar methodology, is as follows: 

1.      Ryan A. Youabian = 13.0 hours at $495.00 an hour for a subtotal of $6,435.00;

2.      Bryan Geoulla = 6.5 hours, including 2.5 hours for reviewing and responding to Defendant’s opposition and appearing at the hearing, at $450.00 an hour for a subtotal of $2,925.00;

3.      Michael Josephson = .7 hours at $400.00 an hour for a subtotal of $280.00;

4.      Michael Josephson = 3.5 hours at $350.00 an hour for a subtotal of $1,225.00; and

5.      Erik Khovorsky = 9.0 hours at $350.00 an hour for a subtotal of $3,150.00

6.      Total Attorney Fees = $14,015.00 

The Court finds this to be the reasonable amount billed for all tasks in this matter. 

Costs and Expenses 

The Court finds Plaintiff’s claimed costs in the amount of $609.71 to be proper. (Memorandum of Costs (8/25/26).) Defendant did not move to strike or otherwise dispute Plaintiff’s claimed costs, and in fact requested the Court include those costs in Plaintiff’s award. (Opp., 9:2-4; Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700.) 

CONCLUSION 

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff's Motion for Attorney Fees, Costs and Expenses in the amount of $14,624.71, including $14,015.00 in attorney fees and $609.71 in costs.
