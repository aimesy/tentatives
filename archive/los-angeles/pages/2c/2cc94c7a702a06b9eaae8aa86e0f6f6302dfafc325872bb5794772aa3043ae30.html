DEPARTMENT 406 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV18114    Hearing Date: September 28, 2026    Dept: 406

 

KRISTOPHER J. FARM,

 

                        Plaintiff,

 

            v.

 

FCA US LLC, et al.,

 

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV18114

 

  Hearing Date: September 28, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] order RE:

Defendant fca us llc’s motion for summary judgment

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On June 23, 2025, Plaintiff Kristopher J. Farm (“Plaintiff” or “Farm”) filed an action against FCA US LLC (“FCA”), JRDTSP LLC dba Scott Robinson Chrysler Dodge Jeep Ram (erroneously sued as Scott Robinson Chrysler Dodge Jeep Ram) (“JRDTSP”) and Does 1 through 10 (collectively “Defendants”) asserting causes of action for (1) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (d), (2) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b), (3) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3), (4) breach of implied warranty of merchantability (Civil Code sections 1791.1, 1794, 1795.5), (5) negligent repair, and (6) fraudulent inducement concealment.  This action arises out of Plaintiff’s purchase of a 2018 Chrysler Pacifica (“Vehicle”) allegedly manufactured and/or distributed by Defendant FCA.

            Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) on October 23, 2025.

            JRDTSP filed an Answer on November 19, 2025, and FCA filed an Answer on January 22, 2026.

            On June 29, 2026, Defendant FCA filed the instant Motion for Summary Judgment (“MSJ”).  No opposition has been filed.

LEGAL STANDARD

A party seeking summary judgment has the burden of producing evidentiary facts sufficient to entitle him/her to judgment as a matter of law.  (Code Civ. Proc., §437c, subd. (c).)  The moving party must make an affirmative showing that he/she is entitled to judgment irrespective of whether or not the opposing party files an opposition.  (Villa v. McFerren (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 733, 742-743.)  Thus, “the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.”  (Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519 (citing Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.).)

When a plaintiff or cross-complainant seeks summary judgment, he/she must produce admissible evidence on each element of each cause of action on which judgment is sought.  (Code Civ. Proc., §437c, subd. (p)(1).)  When a defendant or cross-defendant seeks summary judgment, he/she must produce admissible evidence showing that one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.  (Id. at §437c, subd. (p)(2).)  Once the initial movant’s burden is met, then the burden shifts to the opposing party to show, with admissible evidence, that there is a triable issue requiring the weighing procedures of trial.  (Id. at §437c, subd. (p).)  The opposing party may not simply rely on his/her allegations to show a triable issue but must present evidentiary facts that are substantial in nature and rise beyond mere speculation.  (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 162.)  Summary judgment must be granted “if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

DISCUSSION

            On October 23, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) asserting the following causes of action against Defendant FCA: violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (d), violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b), violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3), breach of implied warranty of merchantability (Civil Code sections 1791.1, 1794, 1795.5), and fraudulent inducement concealment.

            On January 5, 2026, the Court sustained FCA’s demurrer to the first, second, third, and sixth causes of action without leave to amend.  Thus, the only remaining cause of action asserted against FAC is the breached of implied warranty of merchantability pursuant to Civil Code sections 1791.1, 1794, and 1795.5.

            FCA argues that there is no triable issue of material fact as to the remaining cause of action because (1) Plaintiff does not have any evidence that the Vehicle purchased on April 13, 2018, was unmerchantable during the one-year implied warranty period and (2) there is no privity between Plaintiff and FAC with respect to the sale of the Vehicle.

            FCA presents the following evidence in support of its MSJ: (a) Retail Installment Sale Contract between Plaintiff and Defendant JRDTSP, (b) FAC filed on October 23, 2025, (c) Plaintiff’s initial disclosures dated November 11, 2025, and (d) Warranty Detail Report, produced by FCA.

            Based on this evidence, FCA states that on April 13, 2018, Plaintiff purchased the Vehicle from Defendant JRDTSP.  (Undisputed Material Facts (“UMF”), No. 1.)  The implied warranty of merchantability and other express warranties commenced on the date of purchase on April 13, 2018.  (UMF, No. 2.)  Plaintiff’s initial disclosures identify no repair order or other evidence establishing an alleged defect rendered the Vehicle unmerchantable during the implied warranty period, which expired on April 13, 2019.  (UMF, No. 3.)  FCA’s Warranty Detail Report for the Vehicle reflects no warranty repairs occurring between April 13, 2018 and April 13, 2019.  (UMF, No. 4.)

            FCA argues that Plaintiff cannot produce any evidence to demonstrate that the Vehicle was unmerchantable during the implied warranty period, which extended from April 13, 2018, through April 13, 2019, as confirmed in the FAC (“the duration is not to exceed one-year”).  (FAC ¶81.)  Furthermore, Plaintiff’s claim also fails because there is no privity of contract between Plaintiff and FCA as Plaintiff purchased the Vehicle from a third-party dealership and FCA was not a party to this sales contract.

Pursuant to the implied merchantability warranty, “every sale of consumer goods that are sold at retail in this state shall be accompanied by the manufacturer's and the retail seller's implied warranty that the goods are merchantable.”  (Civ. Code, §1792; Brand v. Hyundai Motor America (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1538, 1545.)  Merchantability means that the goods “‘(1) [p]ass without objection in the trade under the contract description. (2) [a]re fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used. (3) [a]re adequately contained, packaged, and labeled. [and] (4) [c]onform to the promises or affirmations of fact made on the container or label.”  (Civ. Code, §1791.1; Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1297, 1303.)  “The implied warranty of merchantability may be breached by a latent defect undiscoverable at the time of sale.”  (Mexia, 174 Cal.App.4th at 1305.)  “In the case of a latent defect, a product is rendered unmerchantable, and the warranty of merchantability is breached, by the existence of the unseen defect, not by its subsequent discovery…Thus, although a defect may not be discovered for months or years after a sale, merchantability is evaluated as if the defect were known.”  (Ibid.)

The relevant question is whether evidence supports a finding that a defect rendering the product unmerchantable existed while the implied warranty was in effect.  The Court finds that FCA has satisfied its initial evidentiary burden of showing that Plaintiff lacks evidence to show that a defect rendering the Vehicle unmerchantable existed during the period when the implied warranty was in effect.  Plaintiff has not presented any evidence to raise a triable issue of material fact supporting an inference that the Vehicle was unmerchantable during the applicable period.  Defendant’s MSJ is granted on this ground.

As to Defendant’s argument that the cause of action fails for lack of contractual privity, Defendant cites to the 9th Circuit Court of Appeals case Clemens v. DaimlerChrysler Corp., in which the Court noted that under the California Commercial Code, vertical contractual privity is required between plaintiff and defendant for a breach of warranty claim.  (Clemens v. DaimlerChrysler Corp. (2008) 534 F.3d 1017, 1023.)  However, in contrast to the California Uniform Commercial Code, there is no privity requirement under the Song-Beverly Act.  (Keegan v. Am. Honda Motor Co. (2012) 838 F.Supp.2d 929, 947 (“California courts reject the proposition that a party who does not alleged vertical privity cannot state a class claim for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability and hold that the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act did away with the vertical privity requirements that apply to implied warranty causes of action”); Davis v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2024) 100 Cal.App.5th 825, 842.)  Thus, summary judgment is not granted on this ground.

CONCLUSION

For the reasons provided herein, Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment is GRANTED.

 


Case Number: 25STCV20914    Hearing Date: September 28, 2026    Dept: 406

 

ANDREA MORA ALCAUTER,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

CHUGH LLP, et al.,

 

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV20914

 

  Hearing Date: September 28, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] order RE:

PLAINTIFF’S MOTIONS TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES (CRS# 8664, 5798, 1654, & 9269)

 

 

BACKGROUND

On July 15, 2025, Plaintiff Andrea Mora Alcauter (“Plaintiff”) filed this employment discrimination action against Defendants Chugh, LLP and Navneet Chugh (collectively, “Defendants”). Plaintiff alleges that she was harassed and discriminated against based on her national origin and disability. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants retaliated against her for reporting this misconduct and for reporting an immigration fraud scheme occurring within the company. Plaintiff alleges that she was compelled to resign due to intolerable conditions.

On March 20, 2026, the Court granted in part Plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses to interrogatories and requests for production. Defendant Chugh, LLP was ordered to provide further responses by April 30, 2026.

On June 26, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant four motions to compel Defendants’ further responses to interrogatories and requests for production that were at issue in this Court’s March 20, 2026 Order. Defendants filed oppositions on September 15, 2026. Plaintiff replied on September 18, 2026.

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Defendants request that the Court take judicial notice of: (1) the “Tentative Ruling dated August 7, 2026, in this same action, ruling on Cross Defendant Andrea Mora Alcauter's motions to compel further responses to discovery propounded in Defendants' cross-complaint against Plaintiff (Los Angeles County Superior Court Case No. 25STCV20914);” and (2) the “Tentative Ruling dated August 10, 2026, in this same action, ruling on Plaintiff's motion to compel a further deposition of Defendant CHUGH, LLP's person most qualified, and Plaintiff's motions to compel further responses to Requests for Production, Set Two, and related written discovery (Los Angeles County Superior Court Case No. 25STCV20914).” (RJN at p. 2; Evid. Code § 452, subd. (d).)

The request is granted. (Evid. Code § 452, subd. (d).) The Court takes judicial notice of these documents only as to “the existence, content and authenticity of public records and other specified documents;” it does not take judicial notice of the truth of any factual matters asserted in those documents. (Dominguez v. Bonta (2022) 87 Cal. App. 5th 389, 400.)

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Upon receiving responses to its discovery requests, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if the responses are incomplete or evasive, or objections are without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a), 2031.310(a).)

DISCUSSION

I.               Special Interrogatories (CRS# 9269)

As an initial matter, Plaintiff’s motion seeks further responses to SROG Nos. 9-11, 13, 14, 19, and 20. Plaintiff, however, does not include No. 20 in her separate statement. The Court will not analyze interrogatories not included in the separate statement.  

a.     SROG Nos. 9 and 13

SROG Nos. 9 and 13 ask Defendants to describe their responses to Plaintiff’s HR complaints. Defendants’ second supplemental responses acknowledge that Plaintiff made complaints or raised concerns and generally state that management and HR internally reviewed and communicated about said concerns without a formal report or disciplinary action.

The Court previously found that the SROGs “require Defendant[s] to describe the steps [they] took, not merely state that [they] ‘took steps’.” Defendants’ general statements of receiving and communicating about the complaints again evade the call of the questions and disregard the Court’s prior order. Defendants’ responses do not offer a single, concrete detail as to the steps taken post-complaints.

The motion is GRANTED as to SROG Nos. 9 and 13.

b.     SROG No. 10

SROG No. 10 asks Defendants to identify all of their communications with the EEOC regarding an EEOC charge filed by Plaintiff. Defendants responded: “Responding Parties communicated with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission regarding Plaintiff's administrative charge. Those communications include submission of a written position statement and related correspondence exchanged with the EEOC during the administrative process. Certain communications are withheld on the basis of attorney-client privilege and/or work product protection and will be identified if a privilege log is ordered.”

This response is almost identical to the one the Court previously found to be “evasive;” Defendants merely repackaged the meat of the response. Thus, the Court reiterates its finding from the March 20th Order: “The interrogatory asks Defendant[s] to identify the communications, not merely acknowledge their existence. Defendant[s] fail[] to demonstrate that the requested information is privileged. (See Behunin v. Superior Court (2017) 9 Cal.App.5th 833, 844 [‘the burden of establishing that a particular matter is privileged is on the party asserting the privilege’].) Communications with the EEOC are not attorney-client communications, and Defendant[s] fail[] to establish that the communications constitute work product. Moreover, the interrogatory asks Defendant[s] to ‘identify’ communications, not to produce their contents. Even if a privilege did apply, Defendant[s] only claim[] that “[certain]” communications are privileged. Thus, privilege would not justify Defendants’ complete refusal to provide any information.”

The motion is GRANTED as to SROG No. 10.

c.     SROG No. 11

SROG No. 11 asks Defendants to describe all modifications made to Plaintiff’s timesheets, including who made the modifications, when they were made, and the reasons for such modifications. Defendants responded: “Plaintiff was required to record her time in the firm's timekeeping system. Time entries were reviewed by supervisory personnel and accounting personnel for billing accuracy and client-related requirements. Responding Parties have not identified any specific instance in which Plaintiff's time entries were modified for the purpose of reducing Plaintiff's compensation. Responding Parties have not identified any specific time-entry modification, including the identity of a person who made such a modification, the date of such modification, or the reason for such modification, that reflects improper or unlawful conduct.”

Defendants impermissibly self-limit the responses to modifications “for the purpose of reducing Plaintiff’s compensation” and reflecting “improper or unlawful conduct.” The response is evasive. Defendants must identify any modification made to Plaintiff’s timesheets and respond to the who, when, and why subcategories.

            The motion is GRANTED as to SROG No. 11.

d.     SROG No. 14

SROG No. 14 asks Defendants to identify all communications they had with Plaintiff regarding her role as a witness in the Merlin Sight litigation. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants retaliated against her for serving as a witness in the Merlin Sight matter. Defendants responded: “Responding Parties communicated with Plaintiff regarding work-related matters, including client matters, case assignments, and internal workplace issues. Responding Parties are not aware of any communications directed to Plaintiff solely for the purpose of her acting as a witness in the Merlin Sight matter that were separate from general workplace and case-related communications.”

Defendants’ “kitchen sink” first sentence does not answer the call of the question and is irrelevant. And just like with the response to SROG No. 11, Defendants impermissibly self-limit this response to communications “solely for the purpose of [Plaintiff’s] acting as a witness,” leaving open and unanswered all other related communications. The response is evasive and incomplete.

The motion is GRANTED as to SROG No. 14.

e.     SROG No. 19

SROG No. 19 asks Defendants to identify all employees who complained about discrimination or harassment during Plaintiff’s employment at the Atlanta office and to describe their response to each complaint. Defendants responded: “Responding Parties are not aware of any substantiated complaints by other employees in the Atlanta office during Plaintiff's employment alleging discrimination or harassment based on national origin or disability that resulted in formal corrective action.”

Defendants’ response once again impermissibly self-limits the requested categories to “substantiated complaints” “that resulted in formal corrective action.” These limits are not present in the request nor in this Court’s prior Order.

The motion is GRANTED as to SROG No. 19.

II.            Form Interrogatories – Employment (CRS# 5798)

a.     FROG No. 217.1

FROG No. 217.1 requests specific information regarding each response to an RFA that is not an unqualified admission. Defendants responded that RFAs Nos. 3-5, 10, 15, 18, 22, 25, and 31 are not unqualified admissions, but fail to properly respond to the FROG’s subparts: Defendants offer generalized factual support (consisting primarily of their position restatements), only offer the names of the identified persons with knowledge, and generally identify communication or personal record documents relating to the admissions. Defendants state in opposition that they do not oppose further responses, but seek to limit further response to RFAs that are currently denied, qualified, or otherwise not unqualified admissions. Defendants, however, fail to identify which RFAs are now considered unqualified admissions based on alleged subsequent amendments.

 The motion is GRANTED as to FROG No. 217.1.

III.          Form Interrogatories – General (CRS# 1654)

a.     FROG No. 4.1

FROG No. 4.1 asks for information on insurance policies covering the claims at issue. Defendants responded with information about a single insurance policy effective from April 2026 to April 2027. This policy was not effective at the time of the INCIDENT: Plaintiff’s employment allegedly began in 2019 and ended in May 2025. Thus, Defendants’ response fails to answer the call of the question. Defendants also fail to state the address of the insurance company and the name, address, and telephone number of each named insured and the custodian of the policy.

The motion is GRANTED as to FROG No. 4.1.

IV.          Requests for Production (CRS# 8664)

As a preliminary issue, the Court notes that Defendants reference a privilege log in their opposition that was prepared after this motion’s filing, but one has not been attached to the supplemental responses or to Defendants’ separate statement.

a.     RFP No. 5

RFP No. 5 requests all communications between HR and management relating to Plaintiff’s accommodations requests. Defendants responded: “Responsive documents relating to Plaintiff's accommodation requests, including ADA documentation, Human Resources records, and communications concerning accommodation and work-from-home arrangements, have been produced. After a reasonable and diligent search, no additional responsive documents have been identified.” Defendants’ response fails to contain proper statements of compliance or inability to comply pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 2031.220 and 2031.230. Defendants also fail to identify a single responsive document and impermissibly rely on generalizations. It is also not clear from the response if all communications were included.

            The motion is GRANTED as to RFP No. 5.

b.     RFP Nos. 16 & 17

RFP Nos. 16 and 17 request all electronic communications between Defendants’ employees/agents and Defendant Navneet Chugh and Defendants’ management concerning Plaintiff from January 1, 2019 to the present. Defendants responded with Bates-stamped documents relating to work assignments, case management, workplace issues, timekeeping, and client matters. “To the extent such communications exist but have not been located, Responding Party is unable to comply because such documents have not been located after reasonable and diligent search, and are not in Responding Party's possession, custody, or control, and Responding Party is not aware whether such documents ever existed or were retained.”

Defendants’ statement of inability to comply maintains deficiencies that the Court identified in its prior Order. Defendants fail to specify “whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of the responding party.” Defendants also fail to state whether they are complying in whole or in part; the categories identified in the responses are not all-consuming.

The motion is GRANTED as to RFP Nos. 16 and 17.

c.     RFP No. 18

RFP No. 18 seeks all documents relating to the deletion of Plaintiff’s 2019 email outbox, including IT logs and deletion records. Defendants produced Bates-stamped documents that include “email regarding deletion issues.” Defendants offer the same inability to comply statement as RFP Nos. 16 and 17. As stated above, Defendants’ statement of inability to comply is deficient. Defendants also fail to state whether they are complying in whole or in part; the identified “email regarding deletion issues” does not encompass the totality of documents requested.

The motion is GRANTED as to RFP No. 18.

d.     RFP No. 22

RFP No. 22 seeks all documents relating to or referencing any document retention request Defendants received in relation to the Merlin Sight Matter between January 1, 2019 to the present. Defendants produced Bates-stamped documents that include “the Merlin Complaint; Merlin Communications; and the deposition transcript.” Defendants offer a similar inability to comply statement as RFP Nos. 16 and 17. First, Defendants’ identified categories of documents do not speak to the heart of the request: requests for document retention/preservation. Second, as analyzed in the prior subsections, Defendants’ statements of compliance or inability to comply are deficient.

The motion is GRANTED as to RFP No. 22.

e.     RFP No. 26

RFP No. 26 seeks all documents relating to any harassment complaint involving Khushbu Shah. Defendants responded with Bates-stamped documents that “include communications reflecting workplace complaints, interpersonal conflicts, and internal discussions involving Plaintiff.” Defendants offer a similar inability to comply statement as RFP Nos. 16 and 17. Defendants’ identified categories of documents relate to Plaintiff, not Khushbu Shah. And as analyzed in the prior subsections, Defendants’ statements of compliance or inability to comply are deficient.

The motion is GRANTED as to RFP No. 26.

f.      RFP Nos. 27 & 28

RFP Nos. 27 and 28 seek all communications between Navneet Chugh and Zoe Mirza/Khushbu Shah relating to Plaintiff between January 1, 2019 to the present. Defendants responded: “Responding Party is unable to comply. After a reasonable and diligent search and inquiry, Responding Party has not identified documents specifically reflecting communications between Navneet Chugh and Khushbu Shah[/Zoe Mirza] relating to Plaintiff. Responding Party's inability to comply is because such documents have not been located after reasonable and diligent search and are not in Responding Party's possession, custody, or control, and Responding Party is not aware whether such documents ever existed or were retained. Responding Party is not aware of any other person or entity currently in possession of such documents.” Defendants’ responses fail to comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.230 and the Court’s prior order.

The motion is GRANTED as to RFP Nos. 27 & 28.

g.     RFP No. 31

RFP No. 31 seeks all documents relating to Plaintiff’s case reassignment on April 7, 2025. Defendants responded: “Responsive documents include communications relating to reassignment of Plaintiff's work and discussions regarding workload and case management. CHUGH 000248-000265; CHUGH 000320-000321; and CHUGH 000982-000983. No additional responsive documents identified after reasonable and diligent search.” Defendants’ response does not address the request for documents relating to “the rationale for reassignment and COMMUNICATIONS regarding the decision.” Defendants’ response also fails to comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.230 and the Court’s prior order.

The motion is GRANTED as to RFP No. 31.

h.     RFP No. 59

RFP No. 59 seeks all documents relating to any insurance policy that can be used to address the claims here. Defendants responded: “Responding Party will produce all insurance policies within its possession, custody, or control that may provide coverage for the claims in this action. CHUGH 001125-001434. After a reasonable and diligent search, Responding Party has not identified documents specifically matching each named individual combination in the requests. To the extent such documents exist but have not been located, Responding Party is unable to comply because such documents have not been located after reasonable and diligent search and are not in Responding Party's possession, custody, or control, and Responding Party is not aware whether such documents ever existed or were retained.”

As found above, Defendants’ response to FROG 4.1 was deficient because Defendants only identified an insurance policy that came into effect in April 2026. In addition, Defendants state they “will produce” all policies, indicating that all relevant documentation has not been produced. It is uncertain whether Defendants have produced the identified document and if it responds to the request.

The motion is GRANTED as to RFP No. 59.

V.             Sanctions

Courts are required to impose a monetary sanction against any individual who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further responses, “unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, subd. (d), 2031.310, subd. (h).)

Defendants unsuccessfully opposed all four motions and have not provided a persuasive justification for their inability to comply with this Court’s prior Order. Defendants point to prior sanctions awarded in this case against them to support their opposition to the requests. This point, however, cuts against their argument and shows a pattern of discovery misuse. Defendants conceded that most of their responses were deficient and noncompliant in opposition. Thus, the Court finds the imposition of sanctions to be justified.

The Court GRANTS sanctions against Defendants and their counsel of record in the sum of $7,200.00 (i.e., 3 hours per motion at a rate of $600/hour).

CONCLUSION

Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses are GRANTED as set forth above.[1] Defendants Chugh, LLP and Navneet Chugh shall provide further responses within 20 days of this order.  Plaintiff’s request for sanctions is also GRANTED in the sum of $7,200.00 against Defendants and their counsel of record, and the sanctions shall be paid within 30 days of this order.


[1] Plaintiff’s separate statement does not reference SROG No. 20. Therefore, the Court does not address SROG No. 20.


Case Number: 25STCV36426    Hearing Date: September 28, 2026    Dept: 406

 

RAHA NABI,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

MERCEDES-BENZ USA LLC, et al.,

                        Defendant.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV36426

  Hearing Date:  April 20, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

 defendant’s motion to compel arbitration

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On April 23, 2026, Plaintiff Raha Nabi filed this action against Defendant Mercedes-Benz USA LLC (MBUSA), alleging violations of the Song-Beverly Act arising from Plaintiff’s purchase of a motor vehicle.

            On June 29, 2026, MBUSA filed the instant motion to compel arbitration. Plaintiff filed an opposition on September 14, 2026.  MBUSA filed a reply on September 18, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

“On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party to the agreement refuses to arbitrate that controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists….” (Code Civ. Proc, § 1281.2.) “The party seeking arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement, and the party opposing arbitration bears the burden of proving any defense, such as unconscionability.” (Pinnacle Museum Tower Assn. v. Pinnacle Market Development (US), LLC (2012) 55 Cal.4th 223, 236.)

Furthermore, the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA) states that “[a] written provision in any . . . contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce to settle by arbitration a controversy thereafter arising out of such contract or transaction . . . shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.” (9 U.S.C. § 2.)

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE AND OBJECTIONS

Requests for judicial notice is GRANTED.

Objections are OVERRULED.

DISCUSSION

I. Arbitration Clause

            “Because the existence of the agreement is a statutory prerequisite to granting the petition, the petitioner bears the burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence.” (Iyere v. Wise Auto Group (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 754.) “The arbitration proponent must first recite verbatim, or provide a copy of, the alleged agreement.” (Id. at p. 755.) “At this step, a movant need not ‘follow the normal procedures of document authentication’ and need only ‘allege the existence of an agreement and support the allegation.’” (Ibid.)

            Here, MBUSA relies on the lease agreement, which provides as follows:

 

Any claim or dispute, whether in contract, tort or otherwise (including any dispute over the interpretation, scope, or validity of this lease, arbitration section or the arbitrability of any issue), between you and us or any of our employees, agents , successors, assigns, or the vehicle distributor, including Mercedes-Benz USA LLC (each a “Third-Party Beneficiary”), which arises out of or relates to a credit application, this lease, or any resulting transaction or relationship arising out of this lease (including any such relationship with third parties who do not sign this contract) shall, at the election of either you, us, or a Third-Party Beneficiary, be resolved by a neutral, binding arbitration and not by a court action.

(Newman Decl., Ex. 2.)

            This evidence satisfies MBUSA’s initial burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement.

 

II. Third-Party Beneficiary

            “The general rule is that only a party to an arbitration agreement may enforce it.” (Ronay Family Limited Partnership v. Tweed (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 830, 837.) However, one exception is that “a third party beneficiary of an arbitration agreement may enforce it.” (Id. at p. 838.) “A contract, made expressly for the benefit of a third person, may be enforced by him at any time before the parties thereto rescind it.” (Civ. Code, § 1559.) This requires a showing that: (1) “the third party would in fact benefit from the contract;” (2) “a motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to provide a benefit to the third party;” and (3) “permitting a third party to bring its own breach of contract action against a contracting party is consistent with the objectives of the contract and the reasonable expectations of the contracting parties.” (Goonewardene v. ADP, LLC (2019) 6 Cal.5th 817, 830.) 

            Here, the arbitration clause expressly names MBUSA as a third-party beneficiary. (Newman Decl., Ex. 2.) Thus, the three requirements are met: (1) the agreement benefits MBUSA by allowing it to pursue arbitration; (2) the express naming of MBUSA as a third-party beneficiary demonstrates that the parties intended to provide a benefit to MBUSA; and (3) permitting MBUSA to enforce the agreement would be consistent with the objectives of the contract and the reasonable expectations of the parties. Thus, MBUSA is entitled to enforce the arbitration agreement as a third-party beneficiary.

            The Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2025) 17 Cal.5th 1122, are distinguishable from the present case.  In Ford, the manufacturer was not expressly named  as a third-party beneficiary.

Plaintiff’s argument—that MBUSA cannot enforce arbitration as a third-party beneficiary despite being expressly named as a third-party beneficiary—would contravene the plain terms of the agreement and the parties’ intent. The intent of an agreement is ascertained solely from its words, if possible; the language of the contract controls if it is clear and explicit. (See Martinez v. BaronHR, Inc. (2020) 51 Cal.App.5th 962, 967.) The language in the arbitration clause here is clear and explicit in naming MBUSA as a third-party beneficiary entitled to enforce arbitration. Plaintiff has provided no legal support for a contrary interpretation.

            In sum, MBUSA has standing to enforce the arbitration agreement as a third-party beneficiary.

III. Unconscionability

Unconscionability has both a procedural and a substantive element. (Aron v. U-Haul Co. of California (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 796, 808.) Both elements must be present for a court to invalidate a contract or clause. (Ibid.) However, the two elements need not be present in the same degree; courts use a sliding scale approach in assessing the two elements. (Carbajal v. CWPSC, Inc. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 227, 242.)

a. Procedural Unconscionability

Procedural unconscionability “focuses on two factors: ‘oppression’ and ‘surprise.’ ‘Oppression’ arises from an inequality of bargaining power which results in no real negotiation and ‘an absence of meaningful choice.’ ‘Surprise’ involves the extent to which the supposedly agreed-upon terms of the bargain are hidden in the prolix printed form drafted by the party seeking to enforce the disputed terms.” (Zullo v. Superior Court (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 477, 484, internal citations omitted.)

            Plaintiff argues that the arbitration agreement is procedurally unconscionable as a contract of adhesion. Plaintiff also argues that he was not given reasonable opportunity or time to review the terms of the alleged arbitration provision.  However, Plaintiff presents no evidence of either fact, thus failing to meet his burden of proving unconscionability. (See Crippen v. Central Valley RV Outlet (2004) 124 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1166 [“nothing in the record shows that plaintiff could not bargain in this case”].) Therefore, the Court finds no procedural unconscionability.

b. Substantive Unconscionability

Substantive unconscionability focuses on the actual terms of the agreement and evaluates whether they create overly harsh or one-sided results as to shock the conscience. (Suh v. Superior Court (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 1504, 1515.)  

            Plaintiff argues that the arbitration agreement is substantively unconscionable because it waives his rights under the Song-Beverly Act. However, Plaintiff fails to identify any provision in the arbitration agreement that waives his statutory rights or remedies. There is no general rule that claims arising under the Song-Beverly Act cannot be arbitrated. Absent a specific showing of substantive unconscionability, Plaintiff has failed to meet his burden of proof. Moreover, due to the complete absence of procedural unconscionability, the contract would be enforceable even if there were some degree of substantive unconscionability. 

            In sum, the Court finds no procedural or substantive unconscionability. As a result, the arbitration agreement remains enforceable.

IV. MBUSA has not Waived its Right to Arbitration.

            MBUSA was served with Plaintiff’s complaint on May 29, 2026.  MBUSA filed this petition to compel arbitration on June 29, 2026. The court finds that MBUSA providing initial

disclosures to Plaintiff on August 28, 2026, was not a waiver of MBUSA’s right to arbitrate.

 V. MBUSA’s petition is not barred by collaterally estopped.

            As set forth above, this court does not agree with the trial courts’ rulings in the two cases cited by Plaintiff.  Each arbitration ruling is specific to each plaintiff's contract and circumstances. Courts have consistently held that where a previous decision rests on a different factual and legal foundation than the issue sought to be adjudicated in the case at bar, collateral estoppel effect should be denied.  (Johnson v. GlaxoSmithKline, Inc., (2008) 166 Cal. App. 4th 1497; Torrecillas v. Fitness Int'l, LLC, (2020) 52 Cal. App. 5th 485.           

CONCLUSION

            Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED. The Court hereby stays the case in its entirety.

BACKGROUND

On February 3, 2026, Plaintiff Shin P. Yang, individually (“Yang”) and Shin P. Yang dba Law Offices of Shin P. Yang (“Law Offices”) filed this action against Defendants KAZN AM 1300 (“KAZN”), Yvonne Liu (“Liu”), and Does 1 through 20 (collectively, “Defendants”) asserting causes of action for (1) equitable enforcement of oral settlement agreement, (2) promissory estoppel, and (3) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.  The dispute originates from an earlier litigation regarding breach of an advertising agreement, Shin Yang, et al. v. KAZN 1300, et al. (19STCV11399).  During the trial in the earlier action, the parties allegedly reached an oral settlement before the court, whereby Defendants were to pay Plaintiff $250,000 and provide Plaintiff with one year of free radio advertising in exchange for dismissal of the lawsuit.  Plaintiff alleges that Defendants have failed to perform their obligations under the settlement agreement.

Defendants KAZN and Liu filed an Answer on May 21, 2026.

On July 13, 2026, the Court, acting on an unopposed record, granted the following motions filed by Plaintiff on June 11, 2026:

(1)  Motion to Deem Requests for Admission Admitted by Defendant KAZN and Request for Monetary Sanctions;

(2)  Motion to Deem Requests for Admission Admitted by Defendant Liu and Request for Monetary Sanctions;

(3)  Motion to Compel Defendant Liu’s Responses to Form Interrogatories and Request for Monetary Sanctions.

On August 24, 2026, KAZN and Liu filed the three instant Motions to Set Aside and Vacate the Court’s July 13, 2026, Order.  On September 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed Oppositions to these Motions.  On September 18, 2026, Defendants filed Replies.

On September 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) adding a cause of action for promissory fraud.

LEGAL STANDARD

Under California law, once a court grants a motion to deem requests for admission admitted, the matters are conclusively established against the nonresponding party for purposes of the pending action.  (Inzunza v. Naranjo (2023) 94 Cal.App.5th 736, 743.)  However, Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.300 provides a mechanism for a party to seek leave of court to withdraw or amend deemed admissions.  (Code Civ Proc., § 2033.300.)  To obtain relief, the moving party must satisfy two statutory requirements: first, the party must demonstrate that the admission was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect; and second, the party must show that the opposing party will not be substantially prejudiced in maintaining its action or defense on the merits.  (New Albertsons, Inc. v. Superior Court (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1418.)  This statutory mechanism eliminates undeserved windfalls obtained through requests for admission and furthers California's policy favoring resolution of lawsuits on the merits.  (Ibid.)

            Furthermore, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b), “[t]he court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect…¿  [The application]¿shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §¿473, subd. (b).)  Moving parties have the initial burden to prove excusable neglect by a preponderance of competent evidence.  (Kendall v. Barker¿(1988) 197 Cal.App.3d 619, 624.)

            Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d), “[t]he court may, upon motion of the injured party, or its own motion, correct clerical mistakes in its judgment or orders as entered, so as to conform to the judgment or order directed, and may, on motion of either party after notice to the other party, set aside any void judgment or order.”

DISCUSSION

Defendants KAZN and Liu move for an order setting aside and vacating the Court’s July 13, 2026, Order that deemed admitted Plaintiffs’ Requests for Admission, Set One (“RFAs”) propounded upon KAZN and Liu, granted Plaintiff’s request to compel Liu’s responses to Form Interrogatories, Set One, and imposed monetary sanctions in the amount of $1,380.  Defendants argue that the underlying RFAs and Form Interrogatories were improperly served on Defendants via a defunct email address associated with Defendants’ counsel’s former law practice: glee@lawlpl.com.  No party consented to receive electronic service at this address, all of Defendants’ filings have identified the correct email address as Ginam.Lee@TrustableLaw.com, and California State Bar records reflect the Ginam.Lee@TrustableLaw.com as Defendants’ counsel’s correct email address.  Even Plaintiff’s proofs of service for the June 11, 2026, motions list Ginam.Lee@TrustableLaw.com as the address for service.  Furthermore, Defendants did not receive Plaintiff’s discovery motions and had no notice of the July 13, 2026, hearing.  Defendants argue that the thirty-day response period never commenced as the discovery was not properly served, and the predicate for the Court’s discovery orders was absent.

Defendants add that, upon receiving the Court’s July 13, 2026, Order via U.S. mail, they immediately and repeatedly sought Plaintiff’s stipulation to set aside the order, but Plaintiff has refused to stipulate.  Finally, Plaintiff will not be substantially prejudiced by the relief—Defendants are prepared to serve verified responses and trial is scheduled for May 2027.  Defendant Liu’s responses to Requests for Admission and Form Interrogatories are attached to Defendants’ counsel’s declarations submitted in support of the Motions.

In opposing these Motions, Plaintiff contends that service to "glee@lawlpl.com" was valid because Defendants’ counsel failed to file a notice of change of electronic service address as required by California Rules of Court, rules 2.200 and 2.251(g), and that the address therefore remained presumptively valid.  Plaintiff argues that Defendants’ counsel’s failure to update his electronic service address and his failure to monitor the court docket for several months constitute inexcusable neglect that precludes discretionary relief under section 473, subdivision (b).  Plaintiff further contends that mandatory relief under section 473, subdivision (b) is unavailable because it applies only to defaults and dismissals, not to discovery orders.  Plaintiff also argues that defense counsel did not file an attorney declaration of fault with the moving papers—an assertion Defendants dispute by reference to the Court's own register of actions.

Plaintiff asserts substantial prejudice on the ground that the deemed admissions conclusively established core elements of his claims—including breach of the oral settlement agreement, fraud intent, and a basis for punitive damages—and that vacating them would require re-litigation of resolved issues and would undermine Plaintiff's first amended complaint and pending asset discovery.  Plaintiff points to the unpaid $1,380 sanction as an additional component of prejudice and as evidence of a pattern of noncompliance.

Finally, Plaintiff also argues that the tardiness and alleged inadequacy of Defendants' proposed responses—submitted on August 24, 2026—confirm a pattern of noncompliance with discovery obligations.

The threshold question is whether Plaintiff's service of the discovery requests and motion papers to "glee@lawlpl.com" was legally effective.  The Court finds it was not.

California Rules of Court, rule 2.251(g)(1), provides: “[a] party or other person whose electronic service address changes while the action or proceeding is pending must promptly file a notice of change of address electronically with the court and must serve this notice electronically on all other parties and all other persons required to be served.”  Furthermore, “[a]n electronic service address is presumed valid for a party or other person if the party or other person files electronic documents with the court from that address and has not filed and served notice that the address is no longer valid.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.251(g)(3).)  Here, the Court's own record establishes that every document Defendants have filed in this action—Demurrer, Answer, motions to set aside, and replies—was filed and electronically served from Ginam.Lee@TrustableLaw.com.  Nothing has ever been filed from "glee@lawlpl.com."  Defendants’ counsel's address of record in this action never changed; the demurrer filed at the inception of the case bore Ginam.Lee@TrustableLaw.com, as has every filing since.  Thus, Defendants’ counsel had no obligation to file a notice with the Court updating his email address.  There cannot be a presumption attached to an email address that was not used in any of the filings.

Furthermore, California Rules of Court, rule 2.251(b)(1) permits consent to electronic service through (A) a filed notice identifying the agreed address, or (B) affirmative consent through the court's electronic filing system.  Plaintiff identifies neither.  A pre-litigation email exchange does not satisfy rule 2.251(b)(1) and does not designate an address for service in a subsequently filed action.  Critically, Plaintiff's own proofs of service for the June 11, 2026 discovery motions, filed and introduced by Plaintiff's counsel, listed Ginam.Lee@TrustableLaw.com as the service address, confirming that Plaintiff was aware of the correct address at the time the motions were served.

Given that the discovery requests were not validly served, the thirty-day response period under Code of Civil Procedure sections 2033.280 and 2030.260 never commenced and the predicate required for the Court’s July 13, 2026, order was not satisfied.

The Court finds that relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.300 is warranted.  First, Defendants have demonstrated that the admissions resulted from mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect.  Second, the Court does not find that setting aside the admissions will be prejudicial to Plaintiff.  Trial is set for May 18, 2027; discovery remains open; no witness has become unavailable; and Defendant Liu has served verified responses. The inability to obtain a procedural windfall does not constitute substantial prejudice under California law.  Moreover, Plaintiff cannot claim substantial prejudice when Defendants’ failure to respond to discovery resulted from Plaintiff’s improper service of the discovery requests.

Relief is independently available under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b), which provides that relief is available from “judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against the party through the party’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.”  Defendants’ non-receipt of the discovery request supplies a satisfactory excuse.  Defendants sought relief within days of learning of the July 13, 2026, Order, and following numerous unsuccessful attempts to avoid the motions through stipulation, filed formal motions on August 24, 2026, well within the six-month statutory period.

Finally, relief is available under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d) as the Court’s July 13, 2026, Order presupposed that the discovery had been properly served.  Given that the discovery was not properly served, the July 13, 2026, Order may be vacated under section 473, subdivision (d).

Because the Court sets aside the underlying orders and finds that Defendants acted with substantial justification in failing to respond to the discovery, the associated monetary sanctions award cannot stand.  The $1,380 sanctions award is vacated.
