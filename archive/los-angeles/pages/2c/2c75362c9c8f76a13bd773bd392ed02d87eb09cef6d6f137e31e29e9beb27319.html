[TENTATIVE] RULING RE: DEFENDANTS’ MOTIONS TO COMPEL PLAINTIFF’S FURTHER RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS AND SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES; REQUEST FOR ATTORNEYS’ FEES

 

Dept. U

8:30 a.m.

July 2, 2026

 

I.              BACKGROUND

This case arises from a motor vehicle accident that occurred on June 26, 2023.

On August 12, 2024, Plaintiff Hayk Harutunyan filed his original complaint against Defendants Vachagan Agavelyan and Anush Avetisyan, alleging: (1) negligence; (2) negligence per se; and (3) negligent entrustment.

On December 12, 2024, Plaintiff filed his first amended complaint (the “FAC”) against Defendants, alleging: (1) negligence; (2) negligence per se; and (3) negligent entrustment.

On January 24, 2025, Defendants filed their answer to the FAC.

On March 31, 2026, Defendants served Plaintiff with supplemental requests for production of documents (the “RFP”) and supplemental special interrogatories (the “SROGs”).  (Minasyan decl., ¶ 2.)

Plaintiff failed to respond to the RFP and the SROGs until May 19, 2026.  (Minasyan decl., ¶¶ 3-5.)  Plaintiff’s failure to timely respond to the RFP and the SROGs within 30 days waived objections.  See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, 2031.300.

On May 22, 2026, and on May 28, 2026, Plaintiff served amended discovery responses.  After repeated correspondence with defense counsel, Plaintiff’s counsel stated that Plaintiff would not be providing further amended responses.  (Minasyan decl., ¶¶ 6-7.)

On June 5, 2026, Defendants filed their motions to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to the RFP and the SROGs.

Also on June 5, 2026, Judge Anthony Mohr granted Defendants’ ex parte application for an order shortening time for notice and hearing on the motions to compel further.

On June 18, 2026, Plaintiff filed his opposition briefs.

As of 5:00 P.M. on June 29, 2026, Defendants had filed no reply brief(s).

II.           LEGAL STANDARD

a.    Motion to Compel Further Production of Documents 

“On receipt of a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, the demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to the demand if the demanding party deems that … [a] statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete[;] … [a] representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive[; or] … [a]n objection in the response is without merit or too general.”¿ Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310 (a).¿ 

b.    Motion to Compel Further Responses to Interrogatories 

Where a demanding party determines that a responding party’s responses to interrogatories are evasive or incomplete, or that said responses state objections that are too general or without merit, the demanding party may file a motion to compel the responding party’s further response.¿ Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300 (a).¿ 

III.        DISCUSSION

Defendants move to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to RFP #1, 3-6 and to SROGs #1-4.

a.    The RFP motion is denied as to RFP #1 but granted as to RFP #3-6.

Defendants move to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to RFP 1, 3-6.

RFP #1 seeks Plaintiff’s tax records.  Defendants present no persuasive argument for why they have good cause to obtain Plaintiff’s tax records in this case.  While Defendants state such a document is “highly relevant as it correlates directly to Plaintiff’s claimed damages in this case” (Motion, p. 4:24-25), Defendants fail to explain how exactly tax records are relevant to proving damages.  Defendants also fail to explain how any probative information gleaned from Plaintiff’s tax records would not be outweighed by Plaintiff’s individual right to keep such information private. 

RFP #3-6 seek documentation concerning repairs to the subject vehicle, Plaintiff’s return of the subject vehicle to the leaseholder and communications related to the incident.  Plaintiff objects on general grounds that the information is equally available to Defendants and that its disclosure would violate attorney-client privilege.  Plaintiff also provides a statement that he has made a diligent search, but no responsive documents were found.

Plaintiff’s objections to RFP #3-6 are without merit.  As an initial matter, Plaintiff waived his objections by failing to timely respond to discovery.  As to the substance of Plaintiff’s objections, the identified categories of documents are not obviously equally available to Defendants, as they are types of documents which Plaintiff is more likely to possess and control.  To the extent that Plaintiff believes complying with RFPs 3-6 would require the disclosure of material covered by attorney-client privilege, Plaintiff has failed to produce a privilege log.  If Plaintiff has a good faith basis for claiming attorney-client privilege, Plaintiff will need to produce a detailed privilege log with his further response.

As to Plaintiff’s statement that he has made a diligent search but no responsive documents were found, Plaintiff’s statement lacks credibility.  The subject documents are not of a type that are likely to be entirely missing and unrecoverable.  Additionally, Plaintiff has failed to state the precise reason for his inability to comply (e.g., were the documents lost or destroyed?).  Plaintiff has also failed to state, where applicable, the name and contact information of individuals who he believes have possession of the documents.  The response is insufficient.

In sum, Defendants fail to show good cause warranting further production as to RFP #1 at this stage of the case.  However, the categories of documents covered by RFP #3-6 are plainly relevant to this case, and Defendants have demonstrated good cause for compelling their further production.  The motion will be denied as to RFP but granted as to RFP 3-6.

b.    The SROGs motion will be granted.

Defendants move to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to SROGs #1-4.

As an initial matter, the Court notes that Defendants’ separate statement pertaining to the SROGs appears not to have made it into the Court file.  While Plaintiff’s opposition brief references such a separate statement (see SROGs Opposition, p. 3:19-22), the Court finds no separate statement addressing the SROGs in the Court file.  Even so, Defendants’ motion to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to the SROGs sufficiently walks the Court through the SROGs, Plaintiff’s responses, and Defendants’ proffered reasons for compelling a further response.  (See SROGs Motion, pp. 4:27-6:6.)  The Court construes the text of the motion as sufficient to satisfy the separate statement requirement.

SROG 1 asks Plaintiff to identify individuals who worked on the vehicle.  While Plaintiff objects that he lacks personal knowledge of who such individuals were, such information should be within Plaintiff’s power to obtain because Plaintiff owns the repair shop where repairs took place.  Additionally, Plaintiff previously represented that “Tony” worked on the vehicle, and Plaintiff has not provided additional information on how to contact “Tony.”  Plaintiff is required to provide a further response to SROG #1.

SROG 2 asks Plaintiff to describe internal damage to the vehicle.  Plaintiff offers the vehicle’s repair estimate without further describing internal damage.  Plaintiff’s response is not responsive to the interrogatory being posed.  Plaintiff is required to provide a further response to SROG 2.

SROGs 3-4 seek dates on which the vehicle was fully repaired and returned to the leaseholder.  Plaintiff has offered approximate dates (“In or around November 2023”).  This is insufficient.  Plaintiff is required to provide a further response to SROGs 3 and 4 by providing exact dates if such dates are, as the Court would expect, are available to Plaintiff through diligent efforts to find and review information in Plaintiff’s possession or control.

c.     Calculating Attorneys’ Fees

The Court shall impose a monetary sanction against any party “who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further response… unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”¿¿ Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.310 (h), 2030.300 (d).

Plaintiff’s discovery responses are incomplete and noncompliant with the Discovery Act.  Despite Defendants’ efforts to meet and confer, Plaintiff failed to sufficiently supplement his production.  The Court has examined Plaintiff’s arguments in his opposition briefs.  Except as to the issue of Plaintiff’s tax records, Plaintiff’s arguments are without merit.  Defendants are entitled to sanctions. 

As a combined total for both of their motions, Defendants request $1,480.00 in attorneys’ fees, representing at least 6.0 hours of attorney time at a rate of $220.00/hour, plus each motion’s $80.00 filing fee.  As to the number of hours billed, Defendants seek 2.0 hours to draft the motions, 2.0 hours to draft reply briefs, and 2.0 hours to attend the motions’ hearing.

Defendants’ request is reasonable as to the hourly rate but inflated as to the number of hours billed.  Two hours is a reasonable amount of time for counsel to have spent drafting both motions.  Defendants filed no reply brief(s).  The Court will award Defendants 1.0 hour to attend the single hearing on both motions.  The lodestar will be adjusted accordingly.

In sum, Defendants will be awarded sanctions in the form of attorneys’ fees against Plaintiff in the total amount of $820.00, representing 3.0 hours of attorney time at $220.00/hour, plus each motion’s $80.00 filing fee.  ($220.00/hour x 3.0 hours + $80.00 filing fee x 2 = $820.00.)

IV.         CONCLUSION

Defendants Vachagan Agavelyan and Anush Avetisyan’s motion to compel further responses to Supplemental Request for Production of Documents from Plaintiff Hayk Harutunyan is DENIED as to RFP #1 but GRANTED as to RFP #3-6.

 

Defendants Vachagan Agavelyan and Anush Avetisyan’s motion to compel further responses to Supplemental Special Interrogatories from Plaintiff Hayk Harutunyan is GRANTED as to SROGs #1-4.

 

Defendants Vachagan Agavelyan and Anush Avetisyan are AWARDED sanctions against Plaintiff Hayk Harutunyan in the total amount of $820.00, to be paid within 20 days..

 

Defendants Vachagan Agavelyan and Anush Avetisyan are ORDERED to give notice.

I.               BACKGROUND

            This case is a breach of contract action filed by Plaintiffs Craig Pfent (“Craig”) and Jennifer B. Pfent (“Jennifer”) against Defendants Nogol Pedram (“Pedram”), Cameron McGarr (“McGarr”), and Evolve Investment Group Inc (“Evolve”).

            On August 12, 2024, Plaintiffs filed their complaint against Defendants, seeking/alleging: (1) breach of contract re: a joint venture agreement; (2) breach of contract re: a loan agreement; (3) conversion – unauthorized use of funds; (4) disassociation/expulsion of partner; (5) accounting; (6) breach of fiduciary duty; (7) restitution and imposition of constructive trust; (8) fraud – misrepresentation and false promise; (9) fraud – concealment; (10) negligent misrepresentation; (11) constructive fraud; (12) action for money had and received; (13) unjust enrichment; and (14) injunctive relief.

            On September 2, 2025, Pedram filed an answer to the complaint.

            On September 5, 2025, McGarr and Evolve filed answers to the complaint.

            Also on September 5, 2025, Pedram filed an amended answer to the complaint.

            On May 4, 2026, the Court granted Defendants’ motions to compel Plaintiffs’ initial responses to discovery.

            On June 22, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their motion for leave to amend their complaint.

            On June 25, 2026, Judge Yolanda Orozco granted Plaintiffs’ ex parte application for an order advancing the hearing date on Plaintiffs’ motion for leave to amend.  Judge Orozco set an abbreviated briefing schedule whereby Defendants would file an opposition brief by June 30, 2026, and Plaintiffs would not be permitted to file a reply brief.  (See Minute Order dated June 25, 2026.)

            On June 30, 2026, Defendants filed their opposition brief.

II.            LEGAL STANDARD

A complainant may obtain leave from the trial court to amend his pleading beyond the number of amendments allowed under Code of Civil Procedure section 472 (a) by filing a noticed motion.¿ Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324.¿ The motion must be accompanied by a declaration stating: (1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) why the request was not made earlier.¿ Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324 (b).

“Any judge, at any time before or after commencement of trial, in the furtherance of justice, and upon such terms as may be proper, may allow the amendment of any pleading or pretrial conference order.”¿ Code Civ. Proc. § 576.¿ In the absence of a showing of prejudice from the opposing side, the trial court ordinarily lacks discretion to deny a motion to amend a pleading.¿ Honig v. Financial Corp. of America (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 960, 965 (Honig).

III.          DISCUSSION

Plaintiffs move for leave to amend their complaint.  For the reasons discussed below, the Court grants the motion. 

a.     Plaintiffs’ motion satisfies the statutory precepts under California Rules of Court Rule 3.1324.

Plaintiffs’ motion is accompanied by an attorney declaration which explains that the proposed first amended complaint (the “FAC”) includes an additional claim for violation of Penal Code section 496 (c).  Plaintiffs contend that such a claim is supported by documents first produced on May 31, 2026.  Plaintiffs say this new evidence shows Pedram submitted documents to the Department of Public Health identifying himself as Evolve’s 100% owner without identifying Plaintiffs in any capacity.  Counsel says the documents also show misconduct regarding the purchase price of certain assets.  (Kim decl., ¶¶ 5-7.)

Plaintiffs’ motion satisfies the statutory precepts under California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324 because Plaintiffs explain the effect of the proposed amendment, why it is necessary and proper, when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered, and why the request was not made earlier.  See Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324 (b).

Defendants argue Plaintiffs have not shown why their motion could not have been filed earlier given that Plaintiffs waited until so close to the trial date (currently set for July 6, 2026) to serve written discovery and to notice Defendants’ depositions.  While Defendants have a point that Plaintiffs could have probably avoided this issue had they obtained necessary discovery earlier in the case, Plaintiffs still conducted discovery before the discovery cut-off. 

As to Defendants’ position that Plaintiffs had sufficient knowledge that Pedram was the sole buyer in the subject stock purchase agreement as early as December 2022, the Court disagrees that the evidence is dispositive.  The Court cannot discern from the parties’ submitted evidence whether the documents in Plaintiffs’ possession in December 2022 were necessarily sufficient to put Plaintiffs on notice as to a possible civil theft claim.  Defendants identify certain documents that were not turned over until May 31, 2026.  Defendants present a colorable argument that their discovery of such documents was the first time they had information sufficient to understand they have a civil theft claim.  For purposes of a motion for leave to amend, this is sufficient.

The motion is appropriately granted absent a showing of prejudice.¿ See Honig, supra, 6 Cal.App.4th at 965.

b.     Defendants’ arguments in opposition to the motion are insufficient to show actual prejudice.

Defendants argue the Court should deny Plaintiffs’ motion because Defendants will be substantially prejudiced if the motion is granted.  Specifically, Defendants complain that Plaintiffs are attempting to add a new defendant and a new cause of action on the eve of trial.  Defendants argue Plaintiffs have engaged in improper gamesmanship to obtain an unfair advantage by surprising Defendants with new claims at the last moment.

The Court is sympathetic to Defendants’ position, as there is a substantial amount of truth to their complaints regarding the way Plaintiffs have litigated this case.  Had Plaintiffs exercised their discovery rights earlier in the process, Defendants would not be in the position of needing to prepare a defense to an additional claim after already having started trial preparation.

Even so, the public policy favoring liberally allowing plaintiffs to amend their pleadings to state a claim where one exists weighs in favor of granting the motion.  If Plaintiffs have discovered new facts which show civil theft, Plaintiffs should have an opportunity to allege such a claim.  The mere fact that Defendants will need to continue litigating this case and conduct additional trial preparation is insufficient to show actual prejudice.

Because Defendants have not shown that they will suffer actual prejudice if the Court grants Plaintiffs’ motion, the motion is granted.¿ See Honig, supra, 6 Cal.App.4th at p. 965; see also Pacific Construction, Inc. v. City of Sunnyvale¿(2007) 155 Cal.App.4th 525, 545 (even on eve of trial, case law directs trial court to¿allow parties to amend pleadings so long as there is no showing of actual prejudice).

c.     The parties are encouraged to stipulate to a trial continuance.

The Court acknowledges that granting Plaintiffs’ motion will mean their July 6 trial date will need to be continued.  The parties are encouraged to meet and confer regarding a new trial date and stipulate to continue the trial to an agreed upon trial date.

IV.          CONCLUSION

Plaintiffs Craig Pfent and Jennifer B. Pfent’s motion for leave to amend their Complaint and file a First Amended Complaint is GRANTED.

 

Plaintiffs Craig Pfent and Jennifer B. Pfent are ORDERED to e-file their First Amended Complaint¿as a¿standalone, e-filed¿document¿in the Court’s electronic filing system within five (5) calendar days of the motion’s hearing.

 

Plaintiffs Craig Pfent and Jennifer B. Pfent are ORDERED to give notice.

Dept. U

8:30 a.m.

I.               BACKGROUND

This case is a lemon law action.

On December 26, 2024, Plaintiffs Teresa Mercado (“Teresa”) and Alfonso Mercado (“Alfonso”) filed their complaint against Defendant Kia America, Inc., alleging: (1) breach of express warranty in violation of the Song-Beverly Act; and (2) breach of implied warranty in violation of the Song-Beverly Act. 

On March 17, 2025, the Court sustained, with leave to amend, Defendant’s demurrer to the complaint.

On March 27, 2025, Plaintiffs filed their first amended complaint (the “FAC”) against Defendant, alleging: (1) breach of express warranty in violation of the Song-Beverly Act; and (2) breach of implied warranty in violation of the Song-Beverly Act.

On April 23, 2025, Defendant filed its answer to the FAC.

On April 7, 2026, Defendant filed its motion for summary judgment or, alternatively, for summary adjudication as to both causes of action in the FAC.

On June 12, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their opposition brief.

On June 22, 2026, Defendant filed its reply brief.

II.            LEGAL STANDARD

The purpose of a motion for summary judgment “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.”  Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843; Code Civ. Proc., § 437c (c).  A plaintiff reaches its burden on summary judgment by showing prima facie evidence for each element of its cause of action.  Code Civ. Proc., § 437c (p); Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.  The burden will then shift to the defendant to show the existence of a triable issue of material fact for at least one element of the cause of action at issue.  Ibid. 

Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment or summary adjudication and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.”  Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.  However, if all inferences reasonably deducible from the submitted evidence are uncontradicted by other inferences and there is no triable issue as to any material fact, the moving party is entitled to summary judgment as a matter of law.  Code Civ. Proc., § 437c (c); Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.

III.          RULINGS ON EVIDENCE

Both sides object to evidence submitted by the other side in support of / in opposition to the motion.

For purposes of the instant motion for summary judgment, both sides’ objections are overruled.¿ See Reid v. Google, Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 512, 531-533 (written evidentiary objections made before hearing avoid waiver regardless of trial court determination as to admissibility of evidence subject to said objections).  The Court likewise rejects Plaintiffs’ request that the Court strike portions of Defendant’s memorandum.

IV.          DISCUSSION

Defendant moves for summary judgment or, alternatively, for summary adjudication as to both causes of action in the FAC: (1) breach of express warranty in violation of the Song-Beverly Act; and (2) breach of implied warranty in violation of the Song-Beverly Act.

For the reasons discussed below, the motion is denied.

a.     Defendant’s Submitted Evidence

Defendant submits the following evidence in support of its motion:

·       The declaration of Kyran Johal, Defendant’s Escalated Case Administrator Manager;

·       The declaration of Jeff Richards, Defendant’s outside consultant;

·       The declaration of David Fernandez, counsel for Defendant;

·       The declaration of Alexander Alarcon, counsel for Defendant;

·       Excerpts from Teresa’s deposition transcript (Motion, Exh. A);

·       Copies of Alfonso’s discovery responses (Motion, Exh. B);

·       Copies of emails between counsel (Reply, Exhs. A, C-E); and

·       A copy of the Court’s order ruling on Defendant’s ex parte application to continue trial (Reply, Exh. B).

Defendant’s submitted evidence shows that on July 14, 2023, Plaintiffs purchased a 2024 Kia Seltos (VIN #KNDER2AA3R7504769) from Van Nuys Kia with 25 miles on the odometer for $27,685.00, plus tax, license, fees, and optional service contracts.  (SUMF #1.)

Plaintiffs presented the vehicle to Van Nuys Kia on December 19, 2023, reporting two (2) illuminated dashboard warning lights for backup collision and blind spot sensors.  A technician found no loose connections or damaged pins, applied a stabilant to connector ICU-A on the fuse box and deactivated the dash lights.  The vehicle was verified as operating as intended, and Plaintiffs paid nothing out of pocket.  (SUMF #2-3.)

Plaintiffs returned on January 31, 2024, again reporting dashboard light issues, though no lights were illuminated upon presentation.  The technician inspected the wiring, found no issues, installed a replacement system and verified the vehicle was operating as intended.  Plaintiffs paid nothing out of pocket, and the warning lights did not reappear after this visit.  (SUMF #4-5.)

On September 30, 2024, Plaintiffs again presented the vehicle, reporting that its radar detection was failing to detect other cars.  The technician could not verify the concern, performed no repair and verified the vehicle was operating as intended.  Plaintiffs paid nothing out of pocket and reported no further radar detection issues thereafter.  (SUMF #6-7.)

Plaintiffs’ three (3) visits to Van Nuys Kia (on December 19, 2023, January 31, 2024, and September 30, 2024) are the only repair presentations Plaintiffs have identified.  The vehicle’s dashboard warning light issue and the vehicle’s radar detection issue were unrelated complaints.  A legal inspection conducted on February 13, 2026 confirmed that the vehicle remained repaired and operating normally.  (SUMF #8-11.)

Defendant is a distributor of new vehicles and did not manufacture the subject vehicle.  (SUMF #12-13.)

b.     Review of Plaintiffs’ Submitted Evidence

Plaintiffs submit the following evidence in opposition to the motion:

·       The declaration of Phil Thomas, counsel for Plaintiffs;

·       The declaration of Teresa;

·       Excerpts from Teresa’s deposition transcript (Exh. 1);

·       Excerpts from the deposition transcript of Jeff Richards, Defendant’s expert (Exh. 2);

·       Copies of emails between counsel (Exh. 3); and

·       Copies of repair orders (Exhs. A-C).

Plaintiffs’ submitted evidence shows that on July 14, 2023, Teresa and her father Alfonso purchased a new 2024 Kia Seltos (VIN #KNDER2AA3R7504769) from Van Nuys Kia in Los Angeles.  Teresa purchased the vehicle in part because it was equipped with a blind spot detector and backup camera, and she reviewed the owner’s manual shortly after purchase to learn how to use these features.  (SUMF #1-3.)

Within a few months of purchase, Teresa noticed the blind spot detector was failing to alert her to vehicles in her blind spot while driving on the freeway at approximately 60 mph, causing her to attempt unsafe lane changes and fear for her safety.  Around the same time, the backup camera also failed to function, including in a grocery store parking lot with pedestrians present, occurring four or five times before she could bring the vehicle in.  (SUMF #4-5.)

Teresa brought the vehicle to Van Nuys Kia on December 19, 2023, reporting both issues.  Teresa does not know what work, if any, was performed, but the repair order indicated that the technician found code C181887, discovered connector ICU-A on the fuse box was not fully connected, applied stabilant 22 and secured the connection,  (SUMF #6-7.)

Both problems recurred within a few days.  Teresa returned to Van Nuys Kia on January 31, 2024, with no dashboard lights illuminated.  The repair order reflects that a technician found the same code on a second visit for the same concern, replaced the left rear radar sensor, performed BSD radar calibration and completed a 21-mile road test verifying repairs.  (SUMF #8-9.)

The blind spot detector problem recurred again after several months, and Teresa returned to Van Nuys Kia on September 30, 2024, again with no dashboard lights illuminated.  The repair order indicates that no active codes were found, the system appeared to be working as designed during an extended road test and no repairs were performed.  (SUMF #10-11.)

Teresa was never provided a rental or loaner vehicle by Kia or Van Nuys Kia during any period the dealership kept the vehicle.  (SUMF #12.)

On May 22, 2026, Plaintiffs noticed the deposition of Defendant’s expert Jeff Richards for May 27, 2026.  The deposition could not proceed because Defendant failed to provide Richards’ expert file with sufficient time for Plaintiffs’ counsel to prepare, and Defendant has since provided no available dates for the deposition.  (SUMF #13.)

c.     The Court will not deny the motion on procedural grounds.

Plaintiffs argue the Court must deny the motion on procedural grounds because Defendant filed its motion on April 7, 2026, about 73 days before the then-trial date of June 19, 2026.  Plaintiffs cite statutes requiring that a motion for summary judgment be served on all parties 81 days before the hearing date and must be heard no later than 30 days before trial.  See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c (a)(2)-(a)(3).

Plaintiffs are conflating two (2) separate statutory requirements.  The statute requires that a motion for summary judgment must be served on all parties 81 days before the hearing date.  Here, the motion was served via email on April 7, 2026.  The hearing date is July 2, 2026, which is 86 days after April 7, 2026.  This satisfies the requirement that the motion be served 81 days before the hearing date, plus at least two (2) additional days for electronic service.  See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 437c (a)(2), 1005.

Separately, a motion for summary judgment may not be heard within 30 days of trial unless the Court finds good cause to do so.  Whereas other statutes tie deadlines to the date originally set for trial (e.g., discovery cut-offs), the summary judgment statute does not specify that the deadline is tied to the original trial date.  The statute merely states that the motion shall be heard 30 days before trial.  See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c (a)(3).  Here, the motion is set for hearing on July 2, 2026, which is at least 30 days before the currently-set trial date of August 3, 2026. 

d.     The motion is denied as to Plaintiffs’ first cause of action for breach of express warranty in the FAC.

Defendant moves for summary adjudication as to Plaintiffs’ first cause of action for breach of express warranty in the FAC.

“A plaintiff pursuing an action under the Song-Beverly Act has the burden to prove the following elements: (1) the product had a defect or nonconformity covered by the express warranty; (2) the product was presented to an authorized representative of the manufacturer for repair; and (3) the manufacturer or its representative did not repair the defect or nonconformity after a reasonable number of repair attempts.”  Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 798-799 (Robertson); see also Civ. Code, § 1793.2(d)(1) (“if the manufacturer or its representative in this state does not service or repair the goods to conform to the applicable express warranties after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer shall either replace the goods or reimburse the buyer”).

Per the statute’s requirement that a manufacturer was unable to repair a defect or nonconformity to warranty after a reasonable number of repair attempts, “‘[a]ttempts’ is plural. The statute does not require the manufacturer to make restitution or replace a vehicle if it has had only one opportunity to repair that vehicle.”  Silvio v. Ford Motor Co. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1208 (Silvio).  “[A]t a minimum there must be more than one opportunity to fix the nonconformity.”  Robertson, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at p. 799.

Defendant argues Plaintiffs’ breach of express warranty claim fails because Plaintiffs presented their vehicle just once as to each issue for which the vehicle was presented.  Although undisputed evidence shows that Plaintiffs presented the vehicle to Van Nuys Kia on three (3) separate occasions (December 19, 2023, January 31, 2024, and September 30, 2024), Defendant argues that none of those three (3) visits were to address the same nonconformity.  In support of its position, Defendant cites the fact that Plaintiffs presented the vehicle for different dashboard alerts on those three different dates.

In opposition to the motion, Plaintiffs present evidence that on all three (3) occasions they complained to Van Nuys Kia that the vehicle’s blind spot lights were not working.  On at least the December 19, 2023 and January 31, 2024 visits, Van Nuys Kia verified the concern and found error code C181887.  Per repair orders produced on those first two visits, Van Nuys Kia was able to correct the issue before returning the vehicle to Plaintiff.  On the third visit (completed on October 23, 2024), Plaintiff presented the vehicle to Van Nuys Kia with a similar complaint regarding blind spot radar, but Van Nuys Kia was unable to verify the problem.  (See Teresa decl., ¶¶ 8-16.)

The Court rejects Defendant’s position that Plaintiffs’ breach of express warranty claim fails merely because Plaintiffs’ several visits addressed different components of the blind spot detector system.  On the first, second, and third visits, Plaintiffs complained of malfunctioning blind spot light and radar systems.  That Plaintiffs characterized the issue slightly differently on the first and second visits (“blind spot lights aren’t working” on December 19, 2023, vs. “blind spot lights aren’t working. Also back up collision warning sensor is not working” on January 31, 2024) does not show that the blind spot detector issues are entirely distinct.

Plainly, Plaintiffs presented their vehicle for a blind spot light malfunction on three (3) separate occasions.  Regardless of whether the issue was illuminating different dashboard alert lights on the different dates on which Plaintiffs presented the vehicle to Van Nuys Kia, Plaintiffs were clearly re-presenting the vehicle due to the same malfunctioning system.  That Plaintiffs attest Defendant was allegedly unable to fix the blind spot system after at least two (2) attempts is sufficient to at least raise a triable fact issue as to whether Plaintiffs have a breach of express warranty claim.  See Silvio, supra, 109 Cal.App.4th at p. 1208 (statute requires that manufacturer have at least 2 opportunities to conform vehicle to warranty).

As to Defendant’s position that Plaintiffs have no breach of express warranty claim because the repair order dated October 23, 2024 (for a service appointment that originated on September 30, 2024) indicates that Van Nuys Kia could not verify an ongoing issue with the blind spot radar system, Teresa’s testimony indicates that the issue persisted after Plaintiffs previously received the vehicle back from Van Nuys Kia on January 31, 2024.  (See Teresa decl., ¶ 14.)  The conflicting accounts between Teresa and Van Nuys Kia as to whether the blind spot system error persisted on October 23, 2024, is sufficient to raise a triable fact issue as to Plaintiffs’ breach of express warranty claim.

The motion is denied as to Plaintiffs’ first cause of action for breach of express warranty in the FAC.

e.     The motion is denied as to Plaintiffs’ second cause of action for breach of implied warranty in the FAC.

Defendant moves for summary adjudication as to Plaintiffs’ second cause of action for breach of implied warranty in the FAC. 

“[A] breach of the implied warranty of merchantability means the product did not possess even the most basic degree of fitness for ordinary use.”  Mocek v. Alfa Leisure, Inc. (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 402, 406.  The necessary elements for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability in violation of the Song-Beverly Act are: (1) the plaintiff bought a new or used vehicle from the defendant; (2) at the time of the purchase the defendant was in the business of selling consumer goods to retail buyers or manufacturing consumer goods; (3) the vehicle was not of the same quality as those generally acceptable in the trade or was not fit for the ordinary purposes for which the goods are used; (4) plaintiff was harmed; and (5) the defendant’s breach of implied warranty was a substantial factor in causing plaintiff’s harm.¿ CACI #3210.¿ 

Defendant argues that it cannot be liable for breach of implied warranty because Defendant is a vehicle distributor, not a vehicle manufacturer.  The Court disagrees.

“If express warranties are made by persons other than the manufacturer of the goods, the obligation of the person making such warranties shall be the same as that imposed on the manufacturer under this chapter.”  Civ. Code, § 1795.  An express warrantor who is not a manufacturer opts into the Song-Beverly Act, including its implied warranty provisions, by making an express warranty.  See Mega RV Corp. v. HWH Corp. (2014) 225 Cal.App.4th 1318, 1331 (Mega RV).

The Court agrees with Plaintiffs that the analysis in Mega RV, supra, 225 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1334-1335 regarding how component manufacturers cannot avoid a breach of implied warranty claim merely because they did not “manufacture” the final product when they also issued an express warranty compels the same conclusion with respect to vehicle distributors who also issue an express warranty.  Given that Defendant’s sale of the vehicle to Plaintiffs was accompanied by an express warranty, the sale was also accompanied by an implied warranty.  Defendant cannot defeat Plaintiffs’ breach of implied warranty claim merely because Defendant is in the business of distributing rather than manufacturing vehicles.

Defendant makes no other arguments in support of its motion to summarily adjudicate Plaintiffs’ second cause of action for breach of implied warranty.  The motion is denied as to Plaintiffs’ second cause of action for breach of implied warranty in the FAC.

V.             CONCLUSION

Defendant Kia America, Inc.’s motion for summary judgment or, alternatively, for summary adjudication as to issues in Plaintiffs’ First Amended Complaint is DENIED.
