LEGAL STANDARD

California Rules of Court, rule 3.350(a) states:

(1) A notice of motion to consolidate must:

(A) List all named parties in each case, the names of those who have appeared, and the names of their respective attorneys of record;

(B) Contain the captions of all the cases sought to be consolidated, with the lowest-numbered case shown first; and

(C) Be filed in each case sought to be consolidated.

(2) The motion to consolidate:

(A) Is deemed a single motion for the purpose of determining the appropriate filing fee, but memorandums, declarations, and other supporting papers must be filed only in the lowest-numbered case;

(B) Must be served on all attorneys of record and all non-represented parties in all of the cases sought to be consolidated; and

(C) Must have a proof of service filed as part of the motion.

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.350(a).)

Additionally, Code of Civil Procedure section 1048, subdivision (a), states: “When actions involving a common question of law or fact are pending before the court, it may order a joint hearing or trial of any or all the matters in issue in the actions; it may order all the actions consolidated and it may make such orders concerning proceedings therein as may tend to avoid unnecessary costs or delay.”

The decision to grant or deny a motion to consolidate is within the trial court’s sound discretion and will only be reversed if there is a clear showing of abuse of that discretion.  (See Fellner v. Steinbaum (1955) 132 Cal.App.2d 509, 511.)

DISCUSSION

In accordance with California Rules of Court, rule 3.350(a)(1), Defendants have: (A) identified all named parties and their respective attorneys of record in both cases; and (B) provided the captions of both cases to be consolidated, listing the lowest-numbered case first.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.350(a)(1)(A)-(B).)

Furthermore, in accordance with California Rules of Court, rule 3.350(a)(2), Defendants’ motion complied with the following conditions: (A) the memorandums, declarations, and other supporting documents were filed in the lowest-numbered case; (B) the motion has been served on all attorneys of record; and (C) a proof of service is attached and has been filed as part of the motion.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.350(a)(2)(A)-(C).)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1048, this Court finds that Defendants’ motion demonstrates a common question of law or fact that is before the Court.  Both cases arise from the same incident that occurred on December 3, 2022, and both are filed against Carolyn Ihrig, as trustee of the Zicaro Survivor’s Trust and the Zicaro Exemption Trust.  (Motion, p. 4:5-10; Declaration of El Mahdi Young, ¶¶ 2-5.)

However, Defendants failed to file the notice of this motion under Case No. 24TRCV04059.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.350(a)(1)(C).)

Accordingly, Defendants’ motion is DENIED.

ORDERS

1)      Defendants’ Motion to Consolidate Actions is DENIED.

2)      Defendants are ordered to provide notice of this Court’s ruling in both Case Nos. 24TRCV04039 and 24TRCV04059.

LEGAL STANDARD

“A party may move for summary judgment in an action or proceeding if it is contended that the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(1).)  “A cause of action has no merit if either of the following exists: (1) One or more of the elements of the cause of action cannot be separately established, even if that element is separately pleaded.  (2) A defendant establishes an affirmative defense to that cause of action.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (o).)

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing[.]”  (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.)  “A plaintiff or cross-complainant has met that party’s burden of showing that there is no defense to a cause of action if that party has proved each element of the cause of action entitling the party to judgment on the cause of action.  Once the plaintiff or cross-complainant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the defendant or cross-defendant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.  The defendant or cross-defendant shall not rely upon the allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists but, instead, shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).)

“When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.”  (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467; Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).)

DISCUSSION

Plaintiff moves for summary adjudication on two distinct claims of liability presented under the singular cause of action for Violation of Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act, Civil Code § 1790 et seq.: (1) Breach of Express Warranty and (2) Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability.  (Notice of MSJ, pp. 1:28-2:16.) 

Breach of Express Warranty

“A plaintiff pursuing an action under the Song–Beverly Act has the burden to prove the following elements: (1) the product had a defect or nonconformity covered by the express warranty; (2) the product was presented to an authorized representative of the manufacturer for repair; and (3) the manufacturer or its representative did not repair the defect or nonconformity after a reasonable number of repair attempts.”  (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 7798-799, citing Oregel v. American Isuzu Motors, Inc. (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 1094, 1101.)

Plaintiff contends that his 2024 Honda Prologue, an all-electric vehicle, exhibits a covered nonconformity within the high-voltage battery (HV battery) system or its charging components.  (Plaintiff’s Undisputed Material Facts (“UMFs”), ¶¶ 1, 6; Declaration of Timothy Hill (“Hill Decl.”), ¶¶ 3-4, Exh. A.)  [However, the Court observes that the evidence provided does not demonstrate that there is no triable issue of material fact concerning the existence of this nonconformity.]

On July 8, 2024, Plaintiff entered into a lease agreement for the vehicle.  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 1; Hill Decl., ¶¶ 3-4, Exh. A.)  Defendant issued a limited express warranty for the new vehicle, which encompasses all systems for a duration of three years or 36,000 miles.  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 4; Hill Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. B.)  Additionally, specific powertrain components are covered for five years or 60,000 miles, while the HV battery system is warranted for eight years or 100,000 miles.  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 4; Hill Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. B.)

On January 31, 2025, Plaintiff presented his vehicle to Airport Marina Honda for warranty repairs, indicating that “when connecting vehicle to charge port, vehicle is not recognized by good known charger [and] also disconnects in the middle of charging.”  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 6; Hill Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. C, p. 1.)  However, the technician’s report indicates an inability to “replicate charging station not recognizing vehicle.”  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 7; Hill Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. C, p. 1.)  The vehicle’s computer did not register any malfunction codes, and it successfully charged to 94% when connected to the charging station at Airport Marina Honda.  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 7; Hill Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. C, p. 1.)  Additionally, the report states that the “charging system operating normal.”  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 7; Hill Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. C, p. 1.)

On February 17, 2025, Plaintiff brought his vehicle to Scott Robinson Honda for warranty repairs, reporting that “when trying to charge vehicle it will not recognize vehicle and may shut off mid cycle when charging.”  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 8; Hill Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. D, p. 1.)  However, the technician noted that they “could not duplicate concern.”  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 9; Hill Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. D, p. 1.)  The report further states that the technician “[c]harged vehicle to 95% (max charge) and vehicle did not disconnect, also had no problem charging or connecting.”  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 9; Hill Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. C, p. 1.)

Plaintiff alleges that “[f]or the next months, the vehicle continued to have intermittent charging problems and would not charge fully[,]” including at various “EvGo” locations recommended by Defendant for Prologue owners to charge their vehicles.  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 10; Hill Decl., ¶ 8.)

On April 11, 2025, Plaintiff brought his vehicle back to Airport Marina Honda for warranty repairs, reporting a “detection error code 50 present when plugging into certain charging stations.”  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 11; Hill Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. E, p. 2.)  He reported that “[t]here does not seem to be consistency as the type of station when this occurs[.]”  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 11; Hill Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. E, p. 2.)  Yet, the technician noted that the “FTS was unable to replicate customers’ concern at this time.”  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 12; Hill Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. E, p. 2.)  Moreover, it was documented that Plaintiff’s vehicle successfully charged at both Level 1 and Level 2 chargers at the dealership without encountering any issues.  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 12; Hill Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. E, p. 2.)  The assessment also indicated that the “system is operating to manufacture standards.”  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 7; Hill Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. C, p. 1.)

Plaintiff also contend that when utilizing the fast charging options available in the settings, “vehicle is not actually charging to the fast capabilities that customer was promised[.]”  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 11; Hill Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. E, p. 2.)  He reported witnessing a maximum charging rate of 71 kW.  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 11; Hill Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. E, p. 2.)  Time-stamped photographs provided by the Plaintiff demonstrated that upon plugging in the charger, the rate was initially 71 kW; however, after approximately 44 minutes of charging, it decreased to 35 kW.  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 11; Hill Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. E, p. 2.)

Despite these observations, the technician stated that “FTS was not able to verify customer concerns at this time.”  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 12; Hill Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. E, p. 2.)  The technician further explained that this reduction in charging rate is typical as the vehicle nears its full charge, as it transitions into “top off mode,” resulting in a decrease in kilowatt (kW) output.  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 12; Hill Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. E, p. 2.)  The report confirmed that the “system is operating to manufacture standards.”  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 12; Hill Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. E, p. 2.)

Plaintiff indicates that he is experiencing persistent challenges while attempting to charge his vehicle, whether at public charging stations or with a charger borrowed from a neighbor.  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 13; Hill Decl., ¶¶ 10-11.)  Consequently, he has opted to leave the vehicle permanently parked at his residence since September 2025.  (Plaintiff’s UMFs, ¶ 15; Hill Decl., ¶ 11.)  While Plaintiff presents evidence indicating the experience of a nonconformity, the evidence also shows that the vehicle successfully held a charge, attaining levels of 94% and 95% during two separate visits to the dealership. Ergo, a triable issue of a material fact. 

Furthermore, the technicians were unable to replicate the issues that Plaintiff alleges he has encountered with the vehicle.  On each occasion, the technicians confirmed that the vehicle was operating in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications.  Thus, Plaintiff is unable to demonstrate that there is no triable issue of material fact concerning the existence of this nonconformity.

In an attempt to refute this conclusion, Plaintiff argues, “A manufacturer’s inability to duplicate a defect is itself a failure to repair: ‘a failure to repair is no less a failure simply because the defect could not be duplicated on every attempt.’  (Oregel v. American Isuzu Motors, Inc. (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 1094, 1103.)  [Defendant’s] apparent theory that repeatedly telling a customer ‘no problem found’ insulates it from liability is precisely the argument Oregel rejects.”  (Reply, p. 2:9-13.)  Such an argument would be persuasive if the Court of Appeal in Oregel had actually made that statement.  However, the Court points out that Plaintiff’s quote is fictional, as the Court of Appeal did not make such a statement.  

As such, the plaintiff’s motion for summary adjudication is DENIED with respect to this claim. There is a triable issue of material fact with respect to the existence of a nonconformity. Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability

“ ‘Unless specific disclaimer methods are followed, an implied warranty of merchantability accompanies every retail sale of consumer goods in the state.  (§ 1792.)  This warranty includes a guarantee that the particular item is “fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used.”  (§ 1791.1, subd. (a)(2).)’  [Citation.]  Breach of the implied warranty of merchantability ‘means the product did not possess even the most basic degree of fitness for ordinary use.  (Com. Code, § 2314, subd. (2).)’  [Citation.]”  (Carver v. Volkswagen Group of America, Inc. (2024) 107 Cal.App.5th 864, 878, internal citations omitted.)

“The duration of the implied warranty of merchantability and where present the implied warranty of fitness shall be coextensive in duration with an express warranty which accompanies the consumer goods, provided the duration of the express warranty is reasonable; but in no event shall such implied warranty have a duration of less than 60 days nor more than one year following the sale of new consumer goods to a retail buyer.”  (Civ. Code, § 1791.1, subd. (c).)

Here, Plaintiff has failed to demonstrate that there is no triable issue of material fact related to the existence of a defect within the first year that would impact on the vehicle’s ability to be driven as discussed above.  As such, the plaintiff’s motion for summary adjudication is DENIED with respect to this claim.

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion for summary adjudication and the request for interlocutory judgment are DENIED.

LEGAL STANDARD

A defendant may serve and file a notice of motion to quash service of summons on the grounds of lack of personal jurisdiction.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a).)  Code of Civil Procedure section 418.10 provides the exclusive procedure for challenging personal jurisdiction at the outset.  (Roy v. Superior Court (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 337, 342.)  The court may dismiss without prejudice the complaint in whole, or as to that defendant, when dismissal is made pursuant to section 418.10.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 581, subd. (h).)

“A court of this state may exercise jurisdiction on any basis not inconsistent with the Constitution of this state or of the United States.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 410.10.)  “The Due Process Clause protects an individual’s liberty interest in not being subject to the binding judgments of a forum with which he has established no meaningful ‘contacts, ties, or relations.’” (Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz (1985) 471 U.S. 462, 471-472.)  A state court may not exercise personal jurisdiction over a party under circumstances that would offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.”  (Asahi Metal Industry Co., Ltd., v. Superior Court of California, Solano County (1987) 480 U.S. 102, 113.)

When a defendant moves to quash service of the summons and complaint, the plaintiff has “the burden of proving the facts that did give the court jurisdiction, that is the facts requisite to an effective service.”  (Coulston v. Cooper (1966) 245 Cal.App.2d 866, 868.)  The plaintiff must establish the facts of jurisdiction by a preponderance of the evidence.  (Aquila, Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 556, 568.)  “When a nonresident defendant challenges personal jurisdiction the burden shifts to the plaintiff to demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence that all necessary jurisdictional criteria are met.  [Citation.]  This burden must be met by competent evidence in affidavits and authenticated documentary evidence.  An unverified complaint may not be considered as an affidavit supplying necessary facts.”  (Jewish Defense Organization, Inc. v. Superior Court (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 1045, 1054-1055.)

“Personal jurisdiction may be either general or specific.”  (Vons Companies, Inc. v. Seabest Foods, Inc. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 434, 445 (Vons).)  “A defendant that has substantial, continuous, and systematic contacts with the forum state is subject to general jurisdiction in the state, meaning jurisdiction on any cause of action.”  (HealthMarkets, Inc. v. Superior Court (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1160, 1167.)  “General jurisdiction, as its name implies, extends to any and all claims brought against a defendant.  Those claims need not relate to the forum State or the defendant’s activity there; they may concern events and conduct anywhere in the world.  But that breadth imposes a correlative limit: Only a select set of affiliations with a forum will expose a defendant to such sweeping jurisdiction.  In what [the Supreme Court] ha[s] called the paradigm case, an individual is subject to general jurisdiction in her place of domicile.  And the equivalent forums for a corporation are its place of incorporation and principal place of business.” (Preciado v. Freightliner Custom Chassis Corp.¿(2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 964, 976, internal citation omitted.)

A nonresident may be subject to specific jurisdiction if a three-prong test is met.  “First, the defendant must have purposefully availed itself of the state’s benefits.  Second, the controversy must be related to or arise out of the defendant’s contacts with the state.  Third, considering the defendant’s contacts with the state and other factors, California’s exercise of jurisdiction over the defendant must comport with fair play and substantial justice.”  (Gilmore Bank v. AsiaTrust New Zealand Ltd. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 1558, 1568, internal citations and emphasis omitted.)  “When a defendant moves to quash service of process on jurisdictional grounds, the plaintiff has the initial burden of demonstrating facts justifying the exercise of jurisdiction.  Once facts showing minimum contacts with the forum state are established, however, it becomes the defendant’s burden to demonstrate that the exercise of jurisdiction would be unreasonable.”  (Vons, supra, 14 Cal.4th at p. 449, internal citations omitted.)

DISCUSSION

Defendants are seeking to quash the service of the summons, contending that the Court lacks the authority to exercise personal jurisdiction.  (PH Motion, p. 1:2-9; PHRI Motion, p. 1:2-10.)   Defendants assert that (1) they were not incorporated in California; (2) their principal places of business are not in California; and (3) any contacts they may have with California do not give rise to Plaintiffs’ claims.  (PH Motion, p. 1:5-8; PHRI Motion, p. 1:5-8.)

Conversely, Plaintiffs assert both general and specific personal jurisdiction over Defendants.  (Opp., pp. 6:15-13:26.)  In the alternative, should the Court determine that additional factual information is necessary, Plaintiffs request that the Court continue the motions and allow for jurisdictional discovery.  (Opp., pp. 14:1-15:19.)  However, the Court concurs with Defendants’ arguments and finds that jurisdictional discovery is not warranted in this case.

Specific Jurisdiction

First Prong: Purposefully Availed

With respect to the first prong, Plaintiffs maintain that Defendants purposefully availed themselves by (1) intentionally engaging in commercial activities aimed at California through their websites and reservation system, and (2) deliberately establishing a substantial contractual relationship, the anticipated benefits and consequences of which were expected to extend to California.  (Opp., pp. 6:24-8:13.) 

Website

“To determine whether a Web site is sufficient to establish purposeful availment, we first look to the sliding scale analysis described in Zippo Mfg. Co. v. Zippo Dot Com, Inc. (W.D.Pa.1997) 952 F.Supp. 1119  (Zippo ).  (See Pavlovich, supra, 29 Cal.4th at p. 274, 127 Cal.Rptr.2d 329, 58 P.3d 2.)  ‘At one end of the spectrum are situations where a defendant clearly does business over the Internet.  If the defendant enters into contracts with residents of a foreign jurisdiction that involve the knowing and repeated transmission of computer files over the Internet, personal jurisdiction is proper.  [Citation.]  At the opposite end are situations where a defendant has simply posted information on an Internet Web site which is accessible to users in foreign jurisdictions.  A passive Web site that does little more than make information available to those who are interested in it is not grounds for the exercise [of] personal jurisdiction.  [Citation.]  The middle ground is occupied by interactive Web sites where a user can exchange information with the host computer.  In these cases, the exercise of jurisdiction is determined by examining the level of interactivity and commercial nature of the exchange of information that occurs on the Web site.’ (Zippo, at p. 1124.)”  (Snowney v. Harrah’s Entertainment, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1054, 1063.)

Here, Plaintiffs reference Exhibits 3 to 5, asserting that these websites, which feature the Planet Hollywood brand and include a California-specific consumer privacy page, are indicative of purposeful availment.  (Opp., p. 7:1-2.)  However, the Court notes these websites appear to be operated by Planet Hollywood International, as evidenced by the copyright notice found in the bottom-right corner of each exhibit.  (Declaration of Adam Reis (“Reis Decl.”), Exhs. 3-5.)  Since neither defendant is Planet Hollywood International, these exhibits do not substantiate the claim that Defendants operate these websites.

A similar predicament arises with Exhibits 8 and 9, which Plaintiffs assert are “the official booking websites, available to California residents” and “advertised the Resort’s amenities, including a ‘SoCal Dining’ option marketed to Southern California consumers[.]”  (Opp., p. 7:2-4.)  However, Exhibit 8 does not provide any pertinent information about the relevant defendants and is copyrighted by Planet Hollywood Costa Rica.  (Reis Decl., Exh. 8, p. 4.)  Likewise, Exhibit 9 fails to present pertinent information about the relevant defendants and is copyrighted by Marriott International, Inc.  (Reis Decl., Exh. 9, p. 3.)

Thus, Plaintiffs’ argument that Defendants purposefully availed themselves through their websites is unfounded.  The Court will not exercise jurisdiction over Defendants solely on the basis that some of these websites feature the “Planet Hollywood” brand.

As such, the Court lacks personal jurisdiction based on this theory.

Contract

Plaintiffs contend that “[t]he evidence further establishes that Defendants’ representations regarding the Resort — including the express assurance of backup generator capacity — were reported to and relied upon by Sabio’s Chief Executive Officer in Los Angeles County in approving the booking; that the contract itself designates that California-based CEO by name as the recipient of Marriott Bonvoy Events benefits (Cmpl., Ex. A, Annex A); that Defendants accepted payments for the event made from Sabio Inc. accounts at a California bank, processed from Sabio’s California office; and that Defendants knew their performance failures would cause economic injury to Sabio Inc., a California business.  (Rahimtoola Decl., ¶¶ 7–9[].)”  (Opp., p. 7:5-12.)  However, the Court observes that none of these alleged actions were undertaken by any relevant defendant.

It is crucial to clarify that the contract central to this dispute is between Sabio Holdings and Planet Hollywood Costa Rica, An Autograph Collection All-Inclusive Resort.  (Compl., Exh. A.)  Plaintiffs fail to provide any evidence demonstrating that Defendants own, operate, or control Planet Hollywood Costa Rica, An Autograph Collection All-Inclusive Resort, or that they were otherwise engaged in the contract.  In fact, the evidence submitted by Defendants indicates that they had no involvement with Planet Hollywood Costa Rica, An Autograph Collection All-Inclusive Resort, other than Planet Hollywood LLC owning the Planet Hollywood brand, while Planet Hollywood Resorts International, LLC functions as a licensor of that brand.  (Declaration of Jeffrey C. Sirolly re: Planet Hollywood LLC, ¶¶ 2-7; Declaration of Jeffrey C. Sirolly re: Planet Hollywood Resorts International, LLC, ¶¶ 2-8.)

Therefore, Plaintiffs’ assertion that “the express assurance of backup generator capacity — were reported to and relied upon by Sabio’s Chief Executive Officer in Los Angeles County in approving the booking; that the contract itself designates that California-based CEO by name as the recipient of Marriott Bonvoy Events benefits (Cmpl., Ex. A, Annex A); … and that Defendants knew their performance failures would cause economic injury to Sabio Inc., a California business” is deemed irrelevant, as Defendants had no connection to the underlying contract.

Moreover, the claim that “Defendants accepted payments for the event made from Sabio Inc. accounts at a California bank, processed from Sabio’s California office” is incorrect, as Plaintiffs’ own Complaint informs the Court that Defendants were not implicated in this payment process.  Plaintiffs’ Exhibit A in the Complaint clearly demonstrates that all funds were directed to Planet Hollywood Costa Rica, An Autograph Collection All-Inclusive Resort.  (Compl., Exh. A, p. 7.)  As such, the Court lacks personal jurisdiction based on this theory.  

Consequently, Plaintiffs failed to establish specific jurisdiction. There is no evidence to satisfy the first prong: purposeful availment by Defendants within the meaning of Gilmore Bank (Supra).

General Jurisdiction

Plaintiffs contend that Defendants’ connections to California were both continuous and systematic, substantiated by two primary arguments: (1) the marketing and sale of services to California residents, and (2) the operation of a Planet Hollywood restaurant in California at the Los Angeles International Airport by Defendants or their subsidiaries or agents.  (Opp., pp. 12:3-13:26.) 

“General (also called all-purpose) jurisdiction means that a defendant’s contacts with a state are sufficiently extensive that the ‘defendant is “essentially at home,” ’ and the court may exercise jurisdiction over the defendant regardless of whether the claims relate to the forum state.”  (Doe v. Dameron (2021) 70 Cal.App.5th 684, 689-690.)  “[T]he proper inquiry, this Court has explained, is whether a foreign corporation’s ‘affiliations with the State are so “continuous and systematic” as to render [it] essentially at home in the forum State.  [Citation.]”  (Daimler AG v. Bauman (2014) 571 U.S. 117, 119, internal citation omitted.)

As discussed above, Plaintiffs have not successfully demonstrated that Defendants marketed and sold services to California residents.

Furthermore, the Court finds that the existence of a single business location is inadequate to establish that Defendants’ presence in California is both continuous and systematic, particularly in light of the absence of evidence indicating that Defendants own, operate, or otherwise control the restaurant in question.  Plaintiffs concede that the restaurant is operated by Planet Express (LAX), LLC, with Planet Hollywood Restaurant Group, LLC, and PB Restaurants, LLC, identified as the alleged managers or members.  (Opp., pp. 12:22-13:1.)  Clearly, Defendants are not these companies.

Moreover, Plaintiffs contend that “[a]ll of these entities operate(d) out of the same offices, which is also the principal place of business of Defendants.”  (Opp., p. 13:1-3.)  However, this assertion is irrelevant, as Plaintiffs do not provide admissible evidence to substantiate these claims and do not offer a legal analysis to support the claim that any of these limited liability companies function as the alter ego of another.

Lastly, Plaintiffs contend that “the terms and conditions of Defendants’ public website provides that the website ‘is owned, operated, licensed, or controlled by PB Restaurants, LLC (“Planet Hollywood”),” but the privacy policy for that same website states ‘[y]our privacy is very important to Planet Hollywood, LLC (“Planet Hollywood”, “we” or “us”).’  (Reis Decl. Exs. 6, 7.)”  (Opp., p. 13:3-7.)  However, it remains unclear whether the restaurant’s website features this privacy policy or if it pertains to a separate page managed by Planet Hollywood, LLC.  In either case, it is unclear how this information could substantiate a claim for general jurisdiction; a privacy policy does not demonstrate that Planet Hollywood, LLC owned, operated, or controlled the restaurant.

As such, the Court lacks personal jurisdiction based on these theories.

 

 

Jurisdictional Discovery

“A plaintiff attempting to assert jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant is entitled to an opportunity to conduct discovery of the jurisdictional facts necessary to sustain its burden of proof.  [Citation.]  In order to prevail on a motion for a continuance for jurisdictional discovery, the plaintiff should demonstrate that discovery is likely to lead to the production of evidence of facts establishing jurisdiction.  [Citation.]  …  A ruling on a motion to continue in order to allow additional time to discover jurisdictional facts lies in the trial court’s discretion.”  (In re Automobile Antitrust Cases I & II (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 100, 127, internal citations omitted.)

Here, Plaintiffs contend that the following discovery requests, propounded on July 10, 2026, will facilitate their evaluation of the jurisdictional questions presented by the motions: (1) “the franchise, licensing, sublicensing, and intercompany agreements governing the Planet Hollywood brand and the operation, branding, marketing, or managing the Resort;” (2) “documents identifying the respective rights and responsibilities of each Planet Hollywood entity with respect to quality control, inspections, operational standards, marketing, reservations, and guest experience; (3) “insurance policies, claims correspondence, and communications concerning Plaintiffs’ event, and the handling of Plaintiffs’ claims and responsibility therefore;” and (4) “documents supporting the factual assertions contained in Defendants’ declarations.”  (Opp., pp. 14:23-15:6.)  However, Plaintiffs fail to articulate how these specific discovery requests are likely to lead to the production of evidence of facts establishing jurisdiction. 

As such, Plaintiffs request that the Court continue the hearing and grant permission for jurisdictional discovery is DENIED. The Court lacks personal jurisdiction as discussed above.  (Opp., pp. 14:1-15:19.)  

ORDERS

1)      Specially Appearing Defendant Planet Hollywood LLC’s Motion to Quash Plaintiffs’ Service of Summons and Complaint for Lack of Personal Jurisdiction is GRANTED.

2)      Specially Appearing Defendant Planet Hollywood Resorts International, LLC’s Motion to Quash Plaintiffs’ Service of Summons and Complaint for Lack of Personal Jurisdiction is GRANTED.

3)      Plaintiffs Sabio Holdings Inc. and Sabio Inc.’s Requests to Continue the Hearing and Permit Jurisdictional Discovery are DENIED.

4)      Defendants are ordered to provide notice of this Court’s ruling.
