Case Number: 22STCV21977    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: 414

22STCV21977 Marc Alan Corbin, et al. v. 720 Normandie, LP et al.

Friday, July 30,  2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING PLAINTIFFS’ JOINT MOTION TO COMPEL DEFENDANT 709 S. MARIPOSE AVE, LLC’S  FURTHER RESPONSE TO REQUEST FOR PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS, SETS TWO AND THREE; AND FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS (3628)

 

 

I.       BACKGROUND

      The first amended complaint asserts claims arising from alleged uninhabitable conditions on property owned, managed, or controlled by Defendants. Plaintiffs allege eight causes of action arising from Defendants’ alleged failure to remedy the conditions. Plaintiffs substituted themselves in place of prior counsel. (Subst. 12.29.25.)

II.     DISCUSSION

      A motion to compel further responses to a document request is proper where the requesting party believes the statement of compliance is incomplete, or a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive and/or an objection in the response is without merit or too general.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310.)

      Plaintiff served a second set of document requests on August 14, 2025 and a third request on September 11, 2025.  Defendant served written responses to both sets on May 1, 2026, which are the subject of this motion. After Plaintiffs filed this motion on June 17, 2026, Defendant supplemented its responses on June 24, 2026, stating “unqualified compliance” with statute, and producing Bates numbered documents. While Defendant submits its June 24, 2026 responses, they are unverified. (Opp.  .pdf 158-158; .pdf 172-173.)  As such, they are non-compliant. Unsworn responses are “tantamount” to no responses at all. (Appleton v. Superior Court (1988) 206 Cal. App. 3d 632, 636.)  Plaintiff’s reply brief maintains the responses are defective.

      Plaintiffs’ supplemental declaration states that Defendant served a second supplemental response on September 22, 2026, that included a September 11, 2026, verification, which Plaintiffs contend constitutes a fraud because Defendant cannot verify a response that did not exist on September 11, 2026.

      What appears clear is that Defendant did not attempt to cure the defects identified by Plaintiffs until after Plaintiffs incurred the cost and burden of filing this motion. After Plaintiff filed the motion, Defendant attempted to supplement twice with defective verifications.

      Defendant’s further responses shall comply with the requirements of statute by stating it will comply in whole or in part "and that all documents or things in the demanded category that are in the possession, custody, or control of that party and to which no objection is being made will be included in the production." (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.220.)

      A statement of inability to comply "shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand. This statement shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of the responding party. The statement shall set forth the name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item." (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.230.)

      Defendant shall comply with the statutory requirements for production which requires that documents produced "shall be identified with the specific request number to which the documents respond." (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.280.) An unidentified “data dump” as characterized by Plaintiffs is not permitted. (Pollock v. Superior Court (2023) 93 Cal.App.5th 1348, 1358 ("As recently amended, it requires that a document ‘be identified with the specific request number to which the documents respond.’ (Id., subd. (a).) This replaces the prior requirement that documents “either be produced as they are kept in the usual course of business, or be organized and labeled to correspond with the categories in the demand.” (Former § 2031.280, subd. (a).).”].)

      The production itself need not be verified, only the response. (Id.)  While Pollock states that produced documents need not be Bates numbered, it appears that Defendant complied with the Bates request in at least one of its defective supplemental responsive production. The Court orders that the production be Bates numbered in the interest of efficiency and to avoid confusion, given the volume of documents produced.

      Defendant has not shown substantial justification for failing to respond to document requests served in August and September of 2025, and subsequently attempting to respond only when prodded by Plaintiffs with this motion.  Plaintiffs also request sanctions in the amount of $2741.00. Plaintiffs are self-represented. “A pro se litigant cannot recover attorney's fees as a discovery sanction, but he can recover the ‘reasonable expenses’ he has ‘incurred,’ including photocopying, computer-assisted legal research, and other identifiable and allocable costs.” (Kravitz v. Superior Court (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 1015, 1017.) Although a self-represented litigant may recover attorney’s fees actually incurred for legal assistance provided in connection with a discovery dispute, the litigant must establish that such fees were in fact incurred. (Dalessandro v. Mitchell (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 1088, 1093; Argaman v. Ratan (1999) 73 Cal.App.4th 1173, 1181.)

      Here, Plaintiffs seek $2000.00 based upon four hours of “Legal research and assistance $250 per hour.” (Motion, Megan Corbin Decl., ¶ 8.) The declaration, however, does not identify an attorney who provided the claimed assistance, state that Plaintiffs retained or paid an attorney, attach an invoice or billing record, or otherwise establish that Plaintiffs actually incurred an obligation to pay $250 per hour for legal services. Nor is there evidence establishing the source or basis for the asserted $250 hourly rate.  The bare characterization of time spent on “legal research and assistance,” without evidence that the claimed expense was actually incurred, is insufficient to support an award of attorney’s fees or other legal-assistance expenses. The Court therefore declines to award the requested $2,000. Plaintiffs have, however, established $741 in recoverable costs actually incurred in connection with the discovery dispute. Accordingly, the Court awards Plaintiffs monetary sanctions consisting solely of costs in the amount of $741.

 

IV.  CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff’s motion is GRANTED. Defendant is ordered to serve verified responses and production without objection in compliance with statute as described above within 20 days. The Court imposes monetary sanctions totaling $741.00 against Defendant, and its counsel of record, jointly and severally, payable to Plaintiffs within 20 days.

 

 

 

     


Case Number: 24STCV21891    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: 414

24STCV21891 Jenny Song v. Kevin Song, et al.

Thursday, September 24, 2026

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING MOTION OF U.S. BANK, NATIONAL ASSOCIATION, FOR (1) OSC RE CONTEMPT AGAINST KEVIN SONG, (2) ISSUANCE OF A BENCH WARRANT AGAINST KEVIN SONG, (3) ORDER TO COMPEL COMPLIANCE WITH ORDER AND DEPOSITION SUBPOENA, AND (4) FOR SANCTIONS (0326)

 

I.         BACKGROUND

      The second amended complaint arises from a dispute over the validity of a deed of trust recorded against real property located at 606-608 N. Fuller Avenue in Los Angeles, alleged to be owned by Plaintiff. Plaintiff alleges that her estranged father, Kevin Song, (“Mr. Song”) used the property as security for a loan obtained from Velocity Commercial Capital LLC (“Velocity”). Mr. Song allegedly incorporated a company with the same name as Plaintiff’s previously dissolved company, Lioness Investment, Inc. Mr. Song allegedly procured the loan by falsely claiming that his company with the same name owned the Fuller Avenue real property, which served as security for the $1,295,000 loan. Velocity subsequently recorded a Deed of Trust (DOT), which Plaintiff alleges is void.

      Defendant, US Bank National Association (“USB”) (transferee of the Velocity note), filed a cross-complaint against Mr. Song, Angela Song, and Lioness Investment, Inc. for breach of contract, fraud, negligent misrepresentation, indemnity, and related claims.

      The clerk entered default against Mr. Song in the Complaint and Cross-Complaint.     

      USB moves for an Order to Show Cause re Contempt for Mr. Song’s failure to appear for his deposition. USB personally served Mr. Song with the subpoena on May 1, 2026. Mr. Song threw the subpoena under his car and failed to appear at the deposition on May 21, 2026.

      The motion was served on the deponent, who is precluded from filing an opposition given his default status. (Rios v. Singh (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 871, 887 ["After a default, a defendant is ‘out of court’ and cannot take any further steps in the cause affecting the plaintiff's right of action until the default is set aside in a proper proceeding."].)

 

 

 

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARDS

     

            After notice to the opposing party’s counsel, and if the court is satisfied with the sufficiency of the affidavit, the court “must sign an order to show cause re contempt in which the date and time for a hearing are set forth. (§ 1212; Arthur v. Superior Court (1965) 62 Cal.2d 404, 408,  [‘an order to show cause must be issued’]; Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 1999) § 9:715, p. 9(II)–47.) The order to show cause acts as a summons to appear in court on a certain day and, as its name suggests, to show cause why a certain thing should not be done." (Cedars-Sinai Imaging Medical Group v. Superior Court (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 1281, 1286.)

      Disobedience of any lawful judgment, order, or process of the court is punishable by contempt. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1209 (a)(5).) The facts essential to a court’s jurisdiction over contempt proceedings are “(1) the making of the order; (2) knowledge of the order; (3) ability of the respondent to render compliance, (4) willful disobedience of the order." (Van v. LanguageLine Solutions (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 73, 81; Conn v. Superior Court (1987) 196 Cal.App.3d 774, 784.)

      Moving party must prove the existence of a “valid court order, the alleged contemner's knowledge of the order, and noncompliance. If the petitioner proves those elements beyond a reasonable doubt the violation is established. He or she need go no farther.” (Moss v. Superior Court (Ortiz) (1998) 17 Cal.4th 396, 428.)

III.  DISCUSSION

      On February 20, 2026, the Court granted USB’s motion to compel Mr. Song to appear for deposition. (M.O. 2.20.26.) USB submits the declaration of a private investigator who personally served Mr. Song with the amended deposition subpoena. (Barocio decl.) Mr. Barocio effected service at a Chick & Burger location where he observed a black Porsche. He verified that the vehicle was leased to Kevin Hee Song, who unlocked the restaurant and entered. (Barocio decl.) Mr. Barocio personally served Mr. Song when he returned to his vehicle. (Id.) Despite proper service, Mr. Song did not appear at his deposition and refused to comply with this Court’s order. (Guilani decl., ¶ 6.)

      USB is entitled to an award of sanctions consisting of attorney’s fees and costs. (Cal. Code Civ. Procedure § 1218.) Counsel incurred filing fees, interpreter fees, no-show fees and costs for work undertaken by the private investigator to locate and serve Mr. Song. (Guilani decl., ¶8-9, 12.) The Court awards sanctions of $16,519.25.

      U.S. Bank requests issuance of a bench warrant. Although its Notice of Motion identifies Code of Civil Procedure section 1212 as the statutory basis for the requested warrant, its memorandum also expressly invokes section 1993, arguing that a warrant may issue for “the failure of a witness to attend in response to a duly served subpoena.” U.S. Bank therefore seeks a warrant both to compel Mr. Song’s attendance at the contemplated contempt proceeding under section 1212 and based upon his failure to appear for deposition pursuant to the personally served subpoena under section 1993.

      The Court declines to issue a warrant under section 1212 for the purpose of requiring Mr. Song’s personal attendance at the Order to Show Cause hearing. Although section 1212 authorizes a warrant of attachment in an indirect contempt proceeding, an alleged contemnor summoned by an order to show cause may appear through counsel, and a bench warrant may not be used merely to compel his personal presence where an appearance through counsel is sufficient. (Morelli v. Superior Court (1968) 262 Cal.App.2d 262, 268–270; Silvagni v. Superior Court (1958) 157 Cal.App.2d 287, 291–292.)

      The Court nevertheless concludes that a bench warrant is authorized under Code of Civil Procedure section 1993. U.S. Bank expressly raised section 1993 as an alternative statutory basis for the requested warrant. The evidence establishes that Mr. Song was personally served with the amended deposition subpoena requiring his appearance on May 21, 2026, and nevertheless failed to appear. (Barocio decl.; Guilani decl., ¶ 5.) Section 1993 authorizes issuance of a warrant upon proof that a witness failed to appear in response to a duly served subpoena. (Silvagni, supra, 157 Cal.App.2d at p. 290.) Accordingly, the bench warrant issues under section 1993 based upon Mr. Song’s failure to appear pursuant to the duly served deposition subpoena, and not for the purpose of compelling his personal attendance at the Order to Show Cause hearing.

 

IV.  CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing, USB’s motion is GRANTED. The Court (1) sets an order to show cause as to why Mr. Song should not be held in contempt of the Court’s February 20 order, scheduled for December 2, 2026, (2) Mr. Song is ordered to comply with the Court’s order and deposition subpoena and appear at a deposition set by USB (3) The Court issues a bench warrant for Mr. Song’s arrest.  Bail is set at $15, 000.  (4)The Court imposes sanctions in connection with the motion to compel, service attempts upon Kevin, and contempt proceedings in the amount of $16,519.25 payable to USB and its counsel on or before the date of the OSC hearing.

Case Number: 25STCV32164    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: 414

25STCV32164 Wasim Wisa Wasef v. Universal Protection Service, LP

Thursday, September 24 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANT’S MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION AND STAY ACTION (9960)

 

I.          BACKGROUND

       The complaint alleges that Defendant employed Plaintiff for approximately 25 years. Defendant allegedly retaliated against Plaintiff after Plaintiff took protected family leave to care for his mother. Plaintiff alleges claims in violation of THE Fair Employment and Housing Act, the California Family Rights Act, and tort claims for wrongful termination in violation of public policy and intentional infliction of emotional distress.

       Defendant previously moved to compel arbitration which the Court denied on May 19, 2026, on grounds Defendant did not establish that Plaintiff signed the agreement during the onboarding process. Additionally, Defendant sought to introduce a different physical agreement purportedly signed by the Plaintiff, which the Court declined to consider because it was new evidence filed with the reply brief and shifted Defendant’s theory.

       Defendant now moves to compel arbitration based on the 2016 agreement with the “wet signature.”  Plaintiff argues that the agreement is procedurally and substantively unconscionable.

II.         LEGAL STANDARDS

       The court can order the parties to arbitrate the matter on petition of a party to an arbitration agreement. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2). The petitioner’s burden is to establish that a valid arbitration agreement exists. The opposing party’s burden is to establish a defense to enforcement based on a preponderance of evidence.  (Molecular Analytical Systems v. Ciphergen Biosystems, Inc. (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 696, 705).

       Courts apply state law to determine who is bound by an arbitration agreement and who may enforce it. (Thomas v. Westlake (2012) 204 Cal.App.4th 605, 614, fn. 7); Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin. Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 410 ["Because the California procedure for deciding motions to compel [arbitration] serves to further, rather than defeat, full and uniform effectuation of the federal law's objectives, the California law, rather than section 4 of the USAA, is to be followed in California courts."].)

       Under Section 2 of the FAA, written arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable “save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of a contract.” (Arthur Andersen LLP v. Carlisle (2009) 556 U.S. 624, 629–630; 9 U.S.C.A. § 2.) Section 3 of the FAA requires the court to stay the action if it involves issues referable to arbitration. (9 U.S.C.A. § 3.).

III.        DISCUSSION

A.      Existence of an agreement to arbitrate

       Defendant need only show the existence of an agreement by a preponderance of evidence. (Espejo v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1058 ["as a preliminary matter the [trial] court is only required to make a finding of the agreement's existence, not an evidentiary determination of its validity.”].)  The moving party need only attach a copy of the agreement to the petition and incorporate it by reference. (Id. at 1058; Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330 [“The provisions must be stated verbatim or a copy must be physically or electronically attached to the petition and incorporated by reference."].)

       Defendant provides three pages of a six-page agreement. (Elliot decl., Ex. A.) The fourth page is the signature page showing that Plaintiff and Defendant signed the agreement. (Eliot decl, Ex. A, page 4.)  Plaintiff submits page five, which is an acknowledgement of receipt of the mutual agreement to arbitrate separately signed by Plaintiff on August 18, 2026. (Galindo decl.,  at p. 5.) Page 6 is an “acknowledgement of execution of mutual agreement to arbitrate” wherein Plaintiff separately initialed provisions of his agreement and understanding, including that he freely and voluntarily waived his right to have any employment-related claims decided by a judge and/or jury. (Id. p. 6)

       Defendant has established that an agreement to arbitrate exists. Plaintiff does not deny signing the agreement including the last three pages of the six-page agreement.

B.      Procedural and Substantive unconscionability

       Unconscionability analysis has two elements, procedural and substantive, and both elements must be present in order for the Court to exercise its discretion to refuse to enforce the arbitration provision. (De La Torre v. CashCall, Inc. (2018) 5 Cal.5th 966, 982. In determining whether an agreement is unconscionable, the Court examines the entire bargaining process and the degree to which each element exists . No one term of the contract determines its application. (Id. 982-983.)

       “Procedural unconscionability” exists where there are circumstances of oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power exist. The court “focuses on the oppression that arises from unequal bargaining power and the surprise to the weaker party that results from hidden terms or the lack of informed choice.” (Ajamian v. CantorCO2e, L.P. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 771, 795.) While both procedural and substantive elements must be present, they do not have to be present in the same degree. (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114 [“the more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa."].)

       "[T]here are degrees of procedural unconscionability. At one end of the spectrum are contracts that have been freely negotiated by roughly equal parties, in which there is no procedural unconscionability.... Contracts of adhesion that involve surprise or other sharp practices lie on the other end of the spectrum. [Citation.] Ordinary contracts of adhesion, although they are indispensable facts of modern life, are generally enforced.” (Baltazar v. Forever 21, Inc. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 1237, 1244.)

1)      Procedural unconscionability

       Plaintiff declares that the agreement was a contract of adhesion, in that it was offered to him by the employer on a take-it-or-leave-it basis. It was a condition of employment. An arbitration agreement is adhesive where the provision is imposed on the employee, and there is no opportunity to negotiate. (Armendariz at 115.)

       The agreement to arbitrate is a contract of adhesion. The Acknowledgement of Receipt that Plaintiff signed states: “I further understand that I must sign the Mutual Agreement to Arbitrate and sign and initial the Acknowledgment of Execution of Mutual Agreement to Arbitrate and return both documents before I may begin or continue my employment with the Company.” (Galindo decl., Ex. A, p. 5.) Even if the contract is adhesive in nature, there is no element of surprise such that the contract is not subject to the “same degree of scrutiny” as contracts that involve “sharp practices.” (Baltazar v. Forever 21, Inc. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 1237, 1245.”

       Here, Plaintiff was given the mutual agreement to arbitrate, captioned in bold, capitalized letters; the signature line was preceded by bold capitalized terms acknowledging that the employee has read and understood the terms.  Plaintiff was given a separate acknowledgement of receipt permitting Plaintiff to take the documents for review with an attorney if the employee so desired; and an “Acknowledgement of Execution” separately affirming, among many other things, that Plaintiff understood the agreement, that he was given a reasonable opportunity to consider its terms before signing, and that he freely and voluntarily waived the right to a jury. (Galindo decl., Ex. A.)

2)      Substantive unconscionability

       The second element, “substantive unconscionability,” is defined as the presence of overly harsh terms that are so one-sided as to “‘shock the conscience’ [citation]”, (Prima Donna Development Corp. v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2019) 42 Cal.App.5th 22, 38.) The court considers whether the terms are unreasonably favorable to the more powerful party. (Id.) The “paramount” consideration in assessing [substantive] conscionability is mutuality.” (Carmona v. Lincoln Millennium Car Wash, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 74, 85) There must be a reasonable justification for a lack of mutuality. (Id.)

       Plaintiff contends that the agreement is signed only by Plaintiff and Defendant’s representatives, requiring Plaintiff to arbitrate claims; the employee would be unlikely to succeed in enforcing the agreement against an agent who did not sign the agreement. (Opp.  11:17-18.) This does not demonstrate a lack of mutuality.

       Plaintiff cites Cook v. University of Southern California (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 312 which found lack of mutuality where the employee was required to arbitrate any and all claims against the university, including officers, trustees and agents, but did not require the university and its related entities to arbitrate. (Id. 326.) As Defendant points out, the agreement to arbitrate is mutual, requiring “you and the Company” to arbitrate. (Galindo decl., Ex. A, p. 1.)

       “Company” is broadly defined as Universal Protection Service, LP and any of its parents, subsidiaries, or affiliated companies, and its current and former officers, directors, agents, employees, representatives, owners, shareholders, equity sponsors, and all successors and assigns of any of them.” (Id. p. 1, sect. 3) That the agreement was signed by the Defendant’s representative does not negate the clear intent that all of Defendant’s agents, subsidiaries and affiliates would be equally bound.

       Plaintiff argues that the purported agreement here is “overbroad in duration as it survives his employment and is presumably of indefinite duration since it is silent as to length of the agreement.” (Opp.  11:21-23.) This provision is not comparable to the one in Cook, which the Court found substantively unconscionable. The provision in Cook stated it shall survive the termination of Employee's employment and may only be revoked or modified in a written document that expressly refers to the ‘Agreement to Arbitrate Claims’ and is signed by the President of the University.” (Cook at 325.)

       Here, the duration of the agreement is tied to employment and termination of employment. It is limited in scope because it governs only employment-related claims. (Galindo decl., Ex. A, p.1.)

       Plaintiff argues the confidentiality agreement is one-sided because “it carves enforcement of the agreement out of the purported arbitration agreement.” (Opp.  12:23-24.) The confidentiality agreement is a separate, stand-alone agreement applicable to security professionals. It is not incorporated in the arbitration agreement, and none of its terms refer to the arbitration agreement. Whether it is unconscionable standing alone is not relevant to whether the arbitration agreement is enforceable by its own terms.

       Plaintiff argues the provision in the arbitration agreement requiring arbitration of sexual harassment claims is illegal. First, Plaintiff did not assert a claim for sexual harassment so it does not affect the enforceability of arbitration of Plaintiff’s other FEHA claims. Secondly, the statute on which Plaintiff relies states that a predispute arbitration agreement concerning a sexual harassment dispute or sexual assault dispute is not valid or enforceable. (9 U.S.C.A. § 402 (West)) That statute became effective on March 3, 2022. Plaintiff signed the arbitration agreement on August 18, 2016.

IV.  CONCLUSION

       Plaintiff has not met the burden of establishing that the arbitration agreement is not enforceable. Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED. This action is stayed until the completion of arbitration. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.)

 


Case Number: 25STCV35554    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: 414

25STCV35554 Michele Nasatir v. David Arakelyn et al.

Thursday, September 24, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING IN PART AND OVERRULING IN PART DEMURRER TO PLAINTIFF’S FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT BY DEFENDANT, FIRST CITIZENS BANK AND TRUST COMPANY (0619)

 

I.       BACKGROUND

      The first amended complaint alleges that Plaintiff wrote and signed a check for $118,600 to make a quarterly tax payment to the United States Treasury (“the Original Check”) through her account at First Citizens Bank & Trust Company, (“FCB”). Plaintiff alleges that Oscar Castillo (“Castillo”) and David Arakelyan (“Arakelyan”), are members of OD Exotic Rentals, LLC, (“ODE”). Plaintiff alleges that Arakelyan and Castillo are signatories of a Bank America (“BOA”) account. Arakelyan fraudulently obtained the original FCB check from the United States Post Office and altered it to make himself and ODE the payees (the “Washed Check”), but did not alter the memo line indicating the check was for payment of quarterly taxes. Arakelyan then deposited the check into the BOA account.

       The first amended complaint alleges that BOA accepted the Washed Check despite clear signs of the fraudulent nature of the check. Plaintiff alleges three causes of action against Castillo Arakelyan and ODE.  The fourth cause of action is one for negligence against BOA and FCB.

      FCB demurs to the negligence claim for failure to state a cause of action for the following reasons: the negligence claim is displaced by the California Uniform Commercial Code (“UCC”) which precludes plaintiff from asserting fraud or forgery against FCB and FBC has no recognized duty owed to Plaintiff. Additionally, the claim is barred by the statute of limitations.

II.     LEGAL STANDARDS

      A demurrer tests the sufficiency of a complaint as a matter of law and raises only questions of law. (Schmidt v. Foundation Health (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1702, 1706.) In testing the complaint’s sufficiency, the court must assume the truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations as well as facts that can be reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded facts. The court may also consider matters properly subject to judicial notice. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

      The court may not consider contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law. (Moore v. Conliffe (1994) 7 Cal.4th 634, 638.) Plaintiff is required to allege facts sufficient to establish every element of each cause of action. (Rakestraw v. California Physicians Service (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 39, 43.) Where the complaint fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, courts should sustain the demurrer. Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(e); (Zelig v. County of Los Angeles (2002) 27 Cal.4th 1112, 1126.)

      Sufficient facts are the essential facts of the case “with reasonable precision and with particularity that is sufficiently specific to acquaint the defendant with the nature, source, and extent of his cause of action.” (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 643-644.)  Whether the Plaintiff will be able to prove the pleaded facts is irrelevant. (Stevens v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 605, 609–610.)

      A demurrer may also be sustained if a complaint is “uncertain.” Uncertainty exists where a complaint’s factual allegations are so confusing, they do not sufficiently apprise a defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2; Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(f).)

      A pleading is required to assert general allegations of ultimate fact. Evidentiary facts are not required. (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal. 4th 26, 47; Lim v. The.TV Corp. Internat. (2002) 99 Cal. App. 4th 684, 690.)

III.    DISCUSSION

A.     The demurrer is not procedurally defective as Plaintiff contends.

      Code of Civil Procedure § 430.41 states:  "A party demurring to a pleading that has been amended after a demurrer to an earlier version of the pleading was sustained shall not demur to any portion of the amended complaint, cross-complaint, or answer on grounds that could have been raised by demurrer to the earlier version of the complaint, cross-complaint, or answer." (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41.)

      BOA was the demurring party to the claim. The Court granted leave to amend solely as to the negligence claim. (M.O. p. 5.) The statute does not apply to a party demurring for the first time.

B.     Defendant has demonstrated that the common-law negligence claim is displaced by the UCC.

      The negligence claim alleges that Defendant FCB, as Plaintiff’s drawee and depository bank, owed Plaintiff a duty to exercise ordinary care in connection with the handling and payment

of items drawn on Plaintiff’s account, including the duty to pay only those items that were

properly payable and not materially altered. (FAC ¶ 48.) Plaintiff alleges that the Washed Check bore obvious and material indicia of alteration and irregularity because the payee was altered from U.S. Treasury to “Arakelian-Od Exotic Rental LLC” and the memo line indicated it was for a tax payment. Plaintiff alleges the amount of the check was inconsistent with Plaintiff’s longstanding account history. (FAC ¶ 50.)

      FCB breached its duty by honoring the Washed Check although it was not properly payable. (FAC ¶ 55.)

      Defendant contends that the UCC displaces a common law claim for negligence. Commercial Code 4401 states that "[a] bank may charge against the account of a customer an item that is properly payable from that account even though the charge creates an overdraft. An item is properly payable if it is authorized by the customer and is in accordance with any agreement between the customer and bank." (Cal. U. Com. Code, § 4401 (a); Chino Commercial Bank, N.A. v. Peters (2010) 190 Cal.App.4th 1163, 1173 ["This provision displaces a common law claim for negligent payment of an ‘item’ on a customer's account."].)

      However, Plaintiff cites Kurtz-Ahlers, LLC v. Bank of America, N.A. (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 952 which states that it is “well established that a bank has ‘a duty to act with reasonable care in its transactions with its depositors … which duty is an implied term in the contract between the bank and its depositor. (Kurtz-Ahlers at 956.)

      Defendant argues that Kurtz narrows the duty that banks owe, and that a bank does not owe a common law negligence duty to police depositors’ accounts for fraud. (Id.) 956.) As Defendant recognizes, the case does not discuss displacement by the UCC, however. Under Chino, the claim that FCB breached its duty of care by honoring the check appears precluded by the UCC. Other cases cited by Defendant are in accord:

·       Joffe v. United California Bank (1983) 141 Cal.App.3d 541, 557–558 ["Section 4401, subdivision (1) explicitly permits banks to charge a depositor's account for ‘properly payable’ items drawn from that account. Thus, if Continental's signature was authorized and the check was “properly payable,” UCB comes within the protection of the statutes. Similarly, if Continental's signature was not authorized and the check was not ‘properly payable,’ the Joffes may recover under the Code. The principles of tort law are therefore displaced by the particular provisions of the Code in this instance, and the demurrer will stand. (Cal. U. Com. Code, § 1103.”].

 

·       A.B. Concrete Coating Inc. v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (E.D. Cal. 2020) 509 F.Supp.3d 1217, 1224 ["Section 4406, which provides particular rules governing the duties between a customer and a bank with regard to forged checks, displaces common-law rules governing the duties between those parties in that situation, and thus displaces plaintiff's negligence claims against defendant here."]

      Plaintiff relies on Sun 'n Sand, Inc. v. United California Bank (1978) 21 Cal.3d 671  for the proposition that the negligence claim is not displaced by the UCC. That case is distinguishable as it involved a check made out to the bank who allowed an employee to deposit the check into her own account. (Id. at 704 ["When a bank named as payee on a check receives and negotiates the check, it holds the funds for the use of the drawer, and if the bank diverts the funds to an unauthorized third party, including the drawer's employee, it does so at its own risk."].)

      Moreover, Sun ‘n Sand’s precedential value has been questioned as it was "not determined by the normal complement of seven justices but was heard before but five. Of the five justices who participated in the decision, the first or lead opinion had the support of but two. It is well established that an opinion that expresses the views of less than a majority of the members of the court is not precedent." (Roy Supply, Inc. v. Wells Fargo Bank (1995) 39 Cal.App.4th 1051, 1067.)

      C.   Whether the claim is barred by the one-year limitations period.

      Defendant relies on Cal. U. Com. Code, § 4406, (f) which states:

"Without regard to care or lack of care of either the customer or the bank, a customer who does not within one year after the statement or items are made available to the customer (subdivision (a) discover and report the customer's unauthorized signature on or any alteration on the item is precluded from asserting against the bank the unauthorized signature or alteration. If there is a preclusion under this subdivision, the payer bank may not recover for breach of warranty under Section 4208 with respect to the unauthorized signature or alteration to which the preclusion applies."

      This provision "is not per se a statute of limitation but instead is an issue-preclusion statute. Unlike a statute of limitations, it does not purport to bar an action against a bank; rather, it simply precludes a customer from asserting a forgery or alteration against the bank if the customer has failed to discover and report the forgery or alteration to the bank." (Roy Supply, Inc. v. Wells Fargo Bank (1995) 39 Cal.App.4th 1051, 1065–1066.) The preclusion applies if the customer must prove a forgery in order to establish the claim. However, "any claim that is not dependent upon proof of the forgery will not be precluded by section 4406, subdivision (4), although the customer will still be precluded from asserting the forgery in pursuing that claim." (Id.)

      Plaintiff relies chiefly on policy reasons articulated by Sun ‘n Sand that the one-year period applies in actions seeking to establish warranty liability under an implied contractual warranty or the UCC’s statutory warranty. (Sun ‘n Sand at 698.) Plaintiff argues that for reasons of policy, a three-year statute of limitations should apply. As previously discussed, this case does not have precedential value.

      The statute requires the customer to discover and report the alteration within one year after the statement or items are made available. (UCC § 4406.) Plaintiff alleges that she wrote the check on September 1, 2024. (FAC ¶ 10.) Plaintiff does not allege when the check was deposited into Arakelyan’s account or when FCB honored the check. (FAC ¶ 17, 21.) Plaintiff did not discover the fraudulent check until October of 2025 when the U.S. Treasury notified her that Plaintiff did not make her quarterly tax payment. (UF 22.)  Plaintiff alleges she sent a demand letter to both BOA and FCB on November 13, 2025.

      It is not clear whether this claim is precluded because Plaintiff does not allege when the “statement or items” were made available to her to establish that her notification to the bank was within the one-year period. (UCC § 4406(f). In order for the court to sustain demurrer to the complaint based on a statute of limitations defect, “the defect must clearly and affirmatively appear on the face of the complaint… . A demurrer will not lie where the action may be but is not necessarily barred.” (Citizens for a Responsible Caltrans Decision v. Department of Transportation (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 1103, 1117; Marshall v. Gibson, Dunn & Crutcher (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1397, 1398.)

      Accordingly, demurrer is overruled on this ground.

IV.  CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing the Court SUSTAINS demurrer on grounds the negligence claim is precluded by the UCC. The demurrer is OVERRULED on grounds of the statute of limitations.
