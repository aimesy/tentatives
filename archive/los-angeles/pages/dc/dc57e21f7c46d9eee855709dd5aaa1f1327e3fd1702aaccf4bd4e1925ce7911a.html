Petitioner County of Santa Cruz Health Agency (the “County”) petitions the Court for a writ of mandate to set aside and reverse an audit adjustment by respondents Department of Health Care Services (the “Department”) and its Director, Michele Baass (collectively, “Respondents”), which reduced the County’s Mental Health Plan program’s entitlement to federal funds.

 

The Court GRANTS the Petition. The Court sets aside the decision of the Administrative Law Judge (the “ALJ”) and remands the matter to the ALJ to make findings consistent with this Order and relevant statutes and regulations.

 

The ALJ, the Department, and the County face a challenging task of applying a complex series of federal and state regulations and guidance materials that are not a model of clarity. The federal regulations and guidance documents apply a confusing set of overlapping and shifting terminologies and definitions. At the highest level, the Court concludes that the finding by the ALJ that the costs at issue benefited multiple cost objectives requires a different analysis to properly allocate those costs. The Court remands the case to the ALJ to perform the analysis based on the facts before it.

 

 

Background

 

The County filed its petition for a writ of administrative mandate on October 7, 2025 against the Department and its director. As noted above, the petitioner here, which this ruling refers to as the “County,” is more specifically a local county agency that administers the Mental Health Plan for the County of Santa Cruz. (Pet., ¶¶ 1, 4.) The Department, the primary respondent here, is a California State-level department that, among other things, reviews and adjusts annual cost reports from locally-administered Mental Health Plans before costs in those reports are approved for reimbursement from federal funding programs (“federal reimbursement”).

 

The Department is the California agency responsible for administering California’s Medi-Cal program, which is a public health insurance system assisting low-income individuals and others. (1 AR 15 [ALJ Opinion (“ALJ Opn.”), 3:1-3].) The Department contracts with certain counties (like Santa Cruz) to provide mental health services to eligible Medi-Cal beneficiaries. (1 AR 15 [ALJ Opn., 3:4-10].) To obtain reimbursement, the County files an annual cost reporting and data collection report with the Department, which sets forth the County’s  costs in providing mental health services to Medi-Cal beneficiaries. (1 AR 16-17 [ALJ Opn., 4:12-5:3].)

 

The County disputes one of the Department’s adjustments to the cost report that the County submitted for its Mental Health Plan, covering the fiscal year ending in June 2012, which is the financial year end for 2012 (the “Cost Report”). The County contends that the Department erroneously adjusted the Cost Report in a manner that unlawfully reduces the Mental Health Plan’s entitlement to federal reimbursement. The Department stands by the adjustment.

 

According to applicable statutes and regulations, the Mental Health Plan’s entitlement to federal reimbursement depends primarily on the identification of “direct costs” and “indirect costs” and the assignment and allocation of costs to one of three “cost objectives” identified in federal awards: (1) mental health services or patient care, (2) utilization review/quality assurance, and (3) plan administration. Once costs are characterized as direct or indirect, different rules govern their allocation across cost objectives. For purposes of reimbursement, “direct costs” are generally assigned to the objectives with which the costs are specifically identified. “Indirect costs,” however, which are not easily identified with a specific objective, are equitably distributed among the three objectives.

 

Crucially for the parties’ dispute, state law caps federal reimbursement of “administrative costs” at “15 percent of the total actual cost of direct client services.” (Welf. & Inst. Code, § 14711(c).) Costs associated with other cost objectives are not similarly limited. (See County’s Opening Brief (“Opening Brief”), 4:28-5:4.)

 

The County contends the Department’s audit of its Cost Report over-assigned Mental Health Plan costs to the “plan administration” objective, which limited the Mental Health Plan’s entitlement to federal reimbursement because “plan administration” costs are subject to the 15% cap. If certain costs had been treated as mental health services or quality assurance costs, they would have been entitled to federal reimbursement; characterized as plan administration costs, they are not. (See ibid.)

 

The County submitted the Cost Report on September 16, 2016. Within the Report, the County included $2,592,966.00 in costs (the “Disputed Costs”) which it characterized as “ ‘Legal and Accounting’ expenses” (Pet., ¶ 35.) The parties also refer to the Disputed Costs as “costs ... associated with patient data and patient accounting”. (Opening Brief, 5:20; see also 1 AR 17 [ALJ Opinion, 5:4-11].) On September 20, 2019, the Department issued its audit report for the County’s Cost Report. (Pet., ¶ 36.) In the audit report, the Department “reclassified [the County’s] reported patient data and patient accounting costs to the administrative cost center”, which made a large portion of those costs ineligible for federal reimbursement. (Id., ¶ 36.)

 

The County informally appealed, then formally appealed. (Id., ¶¶ 37-40.) An ALJ conducted a formal appeal hearing on June 24-25, 2024 and issued a decision on April 28, 2025 that upheld the determination in the Department’s audit without modification. (Id., ¶¶ 41-43; 1 AR 13-40 [ALJ Opn.].) The written decision became final on August 12, 2025. (1 AR 9.) This petition followed.

 

The Court held a trial setting conference on January 13, 2026 and set oral argument on the petition for July 1, 2026. On April 28, 2026, the parties stipulated to augment the administrative record to include transcripts of hearings before the ALJ and certain exhibits (the County’s Exhibits N through V) that were admitted at the hearing but not included in the administrative record.

 

The County filed its opening brief on May 1, 2026. On the same date, it lodged the administrative record with the Court in the form agreed upon by the parties. On May 28, 2026, Respondents filed their brief in opposition. On June 16, 2026, the County filed its reply brief and lodged the parties’ Joint Appendix to the Administrative Record. After a July 1, 2026 hearing on the petition, the Court called for further briefing. The parties filed their supplemental brief, opposition, and reply on August 17, August 24, and September 4, 2026, respectively. The parties returned for a second hearing on September 23, 2026.

 

Legal Standard

 

The Superior Court may issue a writ of mandate “for the purpose of inquiring into the validity of any final administrative order or decision made as the result of a proceeding in which by law a hearing is required to be given, evidence is required to be taken, and discretion in the determination of facts is vested in the inferior tribunal, corporation, board, or officer[.]” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1094.5(a).)

 

“The inquiry in such a case shall extend to the questions whether the respondent has proceeded without, or in excess of, jurisdiction; whether there was a fair trial; and whether there was any prejudicial abuse of discretion. Abuse of discretion is established if the respondent has not proceeded in the manner required by law, the order or decision is not supported by the findings, or the findings are not supported by the evidence.” (Id., subd. (b).)

 

“In an administrative writ proceeding that ... does not involve a fundamental vested right, the trial court reviews the agency's factual findings for substantial evidence and its legal conclusions de novo.”  (Reznitskiy v. County of Marin (2022) 79 Cal.App.5th 1016, 1023; accord E.W.A.P., Inc. v. City of Los Angeles (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 310, 325.)  Substantial evidence is relevant evidence that a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion (California Youth Authority v. State Personnel Board (2002) 104 Cal. App. 4th 575, 584-85), or evidence of ponderable legal significance which is reasonable in nature, credible, and of solid value. (Mohilef v. Janovici (1996) 51 Cal. App. 4th 267, 305 n. 28.)  “Courts may reverse an [administrative] decision only if, based on the evidence …, a reasonable person could not reach the conclusion reached by the agency.”  (Sierra Club v. California Coastal Com. (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 602, 610.)  

 

However, “[o]n questions of law arising in mandate proceedings, [the court] exercise[s] independent judgment.’”  (Christensen v. Lightbourne (2017) 15 Cal.App.5th 1239, 1251.)  The interpretation of statute or regulation is a question of law.  (See State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Quackenbush (1999) 77 Cal.App.4th 65, 77.)

 

Discussion

 

1. Standard of Review

 

            As a threshold matter, the parties dispute the standard of review the Court should apply in this case. Respondents contend the Court should review the ALJ’s decision for substantial evidence. (Respondent’s Opposition Brief (“Opposition Brief”), 10:18-21.) The County argues it relies entirely on the ALJ’s purported error of law, such that the Court should apply its independent judgment, functionally reviewing the ALJ’s decision de novo. (County’s Reply Brief (“Reply Brief”), 4:26-5:3.)

 

            The County’s petition depends on a question of law. Pure questions of law in mandate proceedings are subject to the Court’s independent judgment. (Christensen v. Lightbourne, supra, 15 Cal.App.5th, at p. 1251.) Rather than conducting a substantial-evidence review based on only part of a voluminous administrative record, the Court remands the dispute to the ALJ for the ALJ to correctly apply the law.

 

            2. Applicable Laws and Regulations[1]

 

            The reimbursement process for government-funded, locally-administered health programs like the Mental Health Plan is governed by a host of interlocking federal and state laws and regulations.

 

The Mental Health Plan obtains reimbursement from the federal government’s low-income Medicaid program via the state’s analogous Medi-Cal program. The federal funds are disbursed from the federal Department of Health and Human Services through its responsible subdivision, the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS). CMS makes arrangements with state-level agencies, like the Department, for reimbursement. CMS’s arrangements with state agencies govern federal reimbursement for individual programs. Here, the governing arrangement is the “Section 1971.5 Waiver” approved by CMS for the Department. (See 1 AR 19-20 [ALJ Opn. 7:6-8:5].)

 

            The Section 1971.5 Waiver (named for a particular portion of the federal Social Security Act) binds the Department to follow federal regulations and guidance in administering federal reimbursement for state and local programs. (See 1 AR 24-34 [ALJ Opn. 12:12-22:8.) Those regulations lay out a host of cost-reporting procedures that local agencies and the Department must follow in order to obtain federal reimbursement. (Ibid.) “The Department’s relationship with providers [like the Mental Health Plan] is similar to a contract, the terms of which include and are limited by the laws and valid regulations applicable to the Medi-Cal program.” (1 AR 34 [ALJ Opn. 22:3-8].)

 

State law requires the Department to “develop ... a reimbursement methodology for use in the Medi-Cal claims processing and interim payment system that maximizes federal funding and utilizes, as much as practicable, federal Medicaid and Medicare reimbursement principles.” (Welf. & Inst. Code, § 14711(a).)

 

            The parties’ dispute here turns on the interpretation and application of two sets of terms that recur throughout the various laws, regulations, and agreements:

 

(1)       The allocation of costs between the three “cost objectives” that all reimbursable costs must serve in order to be eligible for federal reimbursement; and

 

(2)       The characterization of the costs as “direct” or “indirect” and the effect, if any, the characterization has on their allocation between the three cost objectives.

 

                        a. Cost Objectives

 

            A “[c]ost objective means a program, ... award, ... contract, [etc.] for which cost data are desired and for which provision is made to accumulate and measure [certain relevant costs]. A cost objective may be a major function of [a] non-Federal entity, a particular service or project, a Federal award, or an indirect (Facilities & Administrative) cost activity ... .” (10 AR 8489 [2 C.F.R. § 200.1].)

 

            For programs like the Mental Health Plan, the parties agree there are three relevant cost objectives, sometimes called “cost centers.” As described in DMH regulatory guidance, the three objectives are:

 

(1)       “[T]he provision of mental health services”, also called the “medical assistance (i.e., direct client services)” (“mental health services”);

(2)       “[U]tilization review/quality assurance activities” or “UR/QA activities” (“quality assurance”); and

            (3)       “Administration of the mental health plan” (“plan administration”).

 

(10 AR 7966 [DMH Letter 11-01, p. 2.]; see also 10 AR 8080 [BHIN No. 21-027, p. 2].)

 

            The relevant regulations and regulatory guidance refer to the cost objectives with a confusing variety of terms. [2] The Court adopts the terms defined in the parentheticals above to describe these three objectives: (1) mental health services, (2) quality assurance, and (3) plan administration.

 

            The “mental health services” cost objective refers, in a general sense, to the actual provision of healthcare to patients. Costs allocable to mental health services include, for example, “the costs for direct practitioners, medical equipment, medical supplies, [or] psychiatric inpatient hospital services ... .” (10 AR 8081 [BHIN No. 21-027, p. 3].)[3]

 

            The second objective, “quality assurance,” refers to activities such as “monitoring and measuring performance, designing and implementing quality standards, developing policies and procedures for Quality Assurance, reporting and disseminating guidance, [and] authorizing and coordinating service delivery ... .” (10 AR 8083 [BHIN No. 21-027, p. 5].)

 

            The third objective, “plan administration”, refers to activities that support, for example, “Management Information Systems”, “Access and Availability of Services”, “Beneficiary Problem Resolution”, or “Reporting Requirements”, and “for developing policies and procedures, implementing and managing processes, and monitoring compliance to administrative requirements.” (10 AR 8084 [BHIN No. 21-027, p. 6].) DMH Guidance also refers to the plan administration objective under the heading “Administrative Costs” and describes relevant costs as “expenditures incurred for activities necessary for the proper and efficient administration of community mental health programs. Proper and efficient planning and administration includes outreach, eligibility intake, contract administration, program planning and policy development, administrative case management, program coordination and claims administration.” (10 AR 7986-7987 [Cost Reporting Instructions, pp. 17-18].)

 

            As is apparent from the definitions themselves, the precise distinction between these three objectives is not always clear.  For example, the guidance defines the latter two objectives, quality assurance and plan administration, by reference to eleven of more than a dozen attachments to Exhibit A to the DHCS/CMS Waiver, with each attachment describing a different subset of local agencies’ Scope of Work. (See 10 AR 8083-8084 [BHIN No. 21-027, pp. 5-6].)

 

                        b. Direct and Indirect Costs

 

            The process for allocating costs to appropriate objectives is partly governed by their character as “direct” or “indirect” costs.

 

“Direct costs are those costs that can be identified specifically with a particular final cost objective ... or that can be directly assigned to such activities relatively easily with a high degree of accuracy.” (10 AR 8501 [2 C.F.R. § 200.413.].)

 

“Indirect ... costs means those costs incurred for a common or joint purpose benefitting more than one cost objective, and not readily assignable to the cost objectives specifically benefitted, without effort disproportionate to the results achieved.” (10 AR 8494 [2 C.F.R. § 200.1].)[4]

 

All costs are either direct or indirect; “[t]he total cost of a Federal award is the sum of the allowable direct and allocable indirect costs minus any applicable credits.” (2 C.F.R. § 200.402.)[5]  “There is no universal rule for classifying certain costs as direct or indirect costs. A cost may be direct for some specific service or function but indirect for the Federal award or other final cost objective.” (2 C.F.R. § 200.412.)[6] However, “[i]dentification with the Federal award rather than the nature of the goods and services involved is the determining factor in distinguishing direct from indirect ... costs of Federal awards.” (10 AR 8501 [2 C.F.R. § 200.413(b)].)

           

3. Administrative Law Judge Ruling

 

With all the above in mind, the Court turns to the ALJ’s opinion issued April 28, 2025 and finally adopted August 12, 2025. (See 1 AR 9, 13-40 [ALJ Opn.].) The Court quotes the ALJ’s relevant findings with internal footnotes omitted:

           

“Provider is entitled to reimbursement for costs incurred performing three categories of activities. [Footnote.] ... [¶] The sole issue in this appeal is whether the Department correctly made adjustment 6 that allocated [the Disputed Funds] to administrative costs rather than direct service or indirect costs as [the County] maintains.”

 

(1 AR 35-36 [ALN Opn. 23:13-14, 23:19-24:2].)

 

“The [Disputed Costs] benefit all three final cost objectives ... .”

 

(1 AR 36 [ALJ Opn., 24:2-3].)

 

“The description of the activities of the staff involved here[ ] establish that they supported the administration of the mental health plan. The Department correctly determined ... the costs were administrative in nature. As a result, the Department reclassified these costs from direct services to the administrative cost objective.”

 

(1 AR 36 [ALJ Opn., 24:16-17].)

 

“There is confusion in this case regarding the designation of costs as direct or indirect as well as the assignment of costs with the direct services and administrative cost centers at issue in this appeal. The [Disputed Costs] are not direct services costs . . . . They may be costs that support the clinical personnel providing mental health services, but that very description makes them administrative in nature as these personnel are not, themselves, delivering a mental health service.

 

The activities of staff in this case were central and necessary to administer the mental health plan and benefitted multiple cost objectives. ... [W]hen there are costs that benefit multiple cost objectives but can be assigned, the costs will be categorized as direct costs. In this case, Provider was readily able to identify its costs.

 

Whether the costs at issue are direct or indirect they still must be placed in the appropriate cost center. Here, the Department reclassified [the Disputed Costs] to the administrative cost center. As described above, treatment of these costs as administrative is consistent with [relevant regulations].”

 

(1 AR 39 [ALJ Opn., 27:1-9].)

 

The ALJ was not explicit as to whether she determined the Disputed Costs were direct or indirect. In the second-to-last paragraph, it appears that the ALJ is treating the Disputed Costs as direct costs because they “can be assigned.” But in the last paragraph, the ALJ appears to say that it is appropriate to assign the costs to plan administration  whether they are direct or indirect costs. The rationale used for assigning them to plan administration is the ALJ’s conclusion that they were by description “administrative in nature.”  

 

            4. Application of Regulations to ALJ Decision

 

The Court begins with the ALJ’s finding that “[t]he [Disputed Costs] benefitted all three cost objectives ... ." (1 AR 36 [ALJ Opn., 24:2-3].)  The ALJ mentioned this finding several times, stating that the activities of staff which were the subject of the Disputed Costs “ were central and necessary to administer the mental health plan and benefitted multiple cost objectives.” (1 AR 39 [ALJ Opn., 27:0-11].)

 

According to the County, because the ALJ found the Disputed Costs are “ ‘multi-benefit cost[s]’, ” the Disputed Costs must either (1) be treated as direct costs and assigned proportionally to the objectives benefitted, or (2) be treated as indirect costs and distributed equitably across all objectives. (Opening Brief 11:1-4.) The County contends the ALJ found the Disputed Costs to be “multi-benefit,” then assigned them wholesale to plan administration, which is not permitted under relevant regulations and violates state law requiring the Department to maximize federal reimbursement.

 

In opposition, Respondents argue that the ALJ found the Disputed Costs are direct costs, not indirect costs. (Opposition Brief, 15:1-14.) The definition of indirect costs is conjunctive: indirect costs must (1) benefit more than one objective, and (2) not be readily assignable to a specific objective. The ALJ found the former element satisfied, but not the latter. The ALJ found that (1) the Disputed Costs can be identified with a final cost objective (plan administration) and (2) can be directly assigned to plan administration relatively easily with a high degree of accuracy. (Opposition Brief, 14:3-15.)

 

The Court concludes that in determining whether the cost allocation is appropriate, the proper analysis must (i) determine whether the costs were direct or indirect; (ii) depending on that determination, use the proper criteria to assign costs to particular cost objectives; and (iii) make this determination in light of the objectives served by the cost objectives, not the way the task appears when it is performed.  On this latter point, the question is not whether the task appears “administrative in nature” in the colloquial sense but which of the three plan objectives it most closely serves. For example, the fact that an executive assistant working on a computer is the one being paid does not alone answer the question of how to allocate the funds; the question is whether that executive assistant is serving one of the three cost objectives.

 

Because the ALJ’s analysis was not consistent with this framework, the Court grants the writ and remands the matter to the ALJ for further proceedings.

 

 

A.    Allocation of costs benefiting multiple cost objectives

 

The County argues that costs benefitting multiple cost objectives are by definition indirect costs. The Court cannot agree that this is universally true. The definition of direct costs is:  “those costs that can be identified specifically with a particular final cost objective ... or that can be directly assigned to such activities relatively easily with a high degree of accuracy.” (10 AR 8501 [2 C.F.R. § 200.413.], emphasis added.)  It is theoretically possible that costs cannot be “identified specifically” with only one cost objective but nevertheless may be ‘directly assigned to such activities relatively easily.” If, upon remand, the Court determines that the Disputed Costs are in whole or in part direct costs because they can be assigned to particular cost objectives “relatively easily,” it may make that finding and explain why.

 

Even if these are direct costs, however, that does not mean that it is permissible to simply allocate all those costs to just one of the three objectives without further analysis other than by reference to the nature of the work. Instead, if the ALJ determines that the Disputed Costs benefitted multiple objectives, the regulations state  that the ALJ was next required to question whether the Costs’ proportional benefit to each objective could be determined without undue effort or cost.

 

This requirement appears at 2 C.F.R. § 200.405(d), under the heading “Direct cost allocation principles”:

 

“If a cost benefits two or more projects or activities in proportions that can be determined without undue effort or cost, the cost must be allocated to the projects based on the proportional benefit. However, when those proportions cannot be determined because of the interrelationship of the work involved, then ... the costs may be allocated or transferred to benefitted projects on any reasonable documented basis.”

 

(2 C.F.R. § 200.405, subd. (d).)

 

This regulation gives two options for allocating direct costs that benefit more than one cost objective: (a) proportionally, if that can be done without undue effort, or if not, (b) “any reasonable documented basis.” (Ibid.) If neither method works, the costs are necessarily indirect costs because they are costs “benefitting more than one cost objective, and not readily assignable to the cost objectives specifically benefitted, without effort disproportionate to the results achieved.” (10 AR 8494 [2 C.F.R. § 200.1].)

 

Thus, the regulations provide three ways to distribute costs which benefit more than one cost objective. The more difficult it is to ascertain the objectives served, the more latitude allowed to the Department:

 

First, if direct costs can be apportioned without undue effort, they should simply be apportioned. (2 C.F.R. § 200.405, subd. (d).)

 

Second, if apportioning direct costs would require undue effort, the Department may resort to a “reasonable documented basis.” (2 C.F.R. § 200.405, subd. (d).)

 

Third, if apportionment requires undue effort and no reasonable documented basis is available, then they may be treated as indirect costs and apportioned equitably. (2 C.F.R. § 200.405, subd. (b) [all activities benefiting from an indirect costs “will receive an appropriate allocation of indirect costs].)

 

The ALJ’s analysis must consider whether the Disputed Costs are direct costs or indirect costs, and allocate them accordingly. The ALJ’s opinion seemed to determine that the costs were direct costs, but did not state this clearly or give the reasons. This is an important first step to determining the proper allocation.

 

B.    “Administrative in nature” is not a recognized allocation criterion

 

Moreover, the ALJ did not make findings that matched the Disputed Costs with the objectives served by the Disputed costs. To support the conclusions that the Disputed Costs are direct and should all be assigned to plan administration, the ALJ made the following findings:

 

(1)       The Disputed Costs arose from “activities ... that [ ] supported the administration of the mental health plan”;

 

(2)       That the Costs “were administrative in nature”;

 

(3)       That the Costs were “administrative in nature” because they relate to “personnel [who] are not, themselves, delivering a mental health service”; and

 

(4)       That the activities in question were “central and necessary to administer the mental health plan”.

 

(1 AR 35-36, 39.)

 

The ALJ’s term “administrative in nature” seems to use the term administrative in the ordinary, colloquial sense, rather than determining whether the cost benefited plan administration, the third cost objective. In this analysis, the salary of any administrative assistant whose job involved primarily using a computer could be allocated to plan administration, even if the person’s efforts were directed solely to quality assurance,

 

No party has cited any law which allows the way the work is physically conducted to govern which cost objective it should be allocated to. The regulations indicates that it is the cost objective served, not the nature of the work, that governs. The key regulation which the parties agree apply states: “A cost is allocable to a Federal award or other cost objective if the cost is assignable to that Federal award or other cost objective in accordance with the relative benefits received.” (2 C.F.R. § 200.405(a), emphasis added.) This makes the relative benefits received, rather than the nature of the work, the key criteria. Moreover, in a  version of the regulation cited by the parties in the Administrative Record, the regulations expressly provide:  “Identification with the Federal award rather than the nature of the goods and services involved is the determining factor in distinguishing direct from indirect ... costs of Federal awards.” (10 AR 8501 [2 C.F.R. § 200.413(b), emphasis added].)[7]       

 

            It does not appear appropriate to simply concludes that work is generally “administrative” and therefore should be assigned to plan administration. Consider, for example, the salary and benefits related to the position of a “Mental Health Client Specialist I & II” (“MHCS”), which appears in the Administrative Record at 10 AR 5315-5320 and the County includes among the “concrete examples of why [the Disputed Costs] were shared” between different objectives. (Opening Brief, 5:25-26.)

 

            An MHCS’ “Job Specifications” state that she will “[u]nder general supervision, ... provide a variety of professional mental health and/or substance use disorder services”. (10 AR 5315.) It is difficult to see how this could not be regarded as a mental health services cost, allocable to the first cost objective. The specifications also require her to perform “related administrative functions in support of clients and their families ... .” (Ibid.) These costs, one assumes, would be allocable to plan administration. The specifications describe “short term crisis intervention” and “counseling” among an MHCS’ duties, which again appear to be mental health services costs, but they also describe “care coordination”, “evaluation”, and “assisting in ... implementation” of care plans, which could feasibly be allocated to plan administration or quality assurance. The specifications refer to providing “crisis intervention services ... [and] walk-in crisis and/or street medicine” – again, services – while also stating the MHCS II performs “outreach”, which is expressly enumerated as a plan administration cost in the regulatory guidance. (See 10 AR 7986-7987 [Cost Reporting Instructions, pp. 17-18].) Also, the whole job description is qualified with a catch-all provision requiring the MHCS “to do other work as required”. Taken as a whole, the job description incorporates elements of all three cost objectives.  Neither the Department nor the ALJ has supported allocating them entirely to plan administration.

 

The ALJ was correct to determine that the costs in question served multiple objectives. But at least based on the job descriptions in the administrative record, not all of the work these employees performed could be specifically identified with plan administration, nor could the work be readily assigned to plan administration with a high degree of accuracy.

 

The ALJ has determined that the Disputed Costs benefit multiple costs objectives. To allocate these disputed costs, the ALJ must do one of the following:

 

(1)       Find the costs benefit the multiple objectives in proportions that can be determined without undue effort or cost, and allocate the costs to objectives based on that proportional benefit; or

 

(2)       Find the costs’ proportional benefit cannot be determined without undue effort or cost, then allocate them among the benefited cost objectives based on a reasonable documented basis; or

 

(3)       Find the costs cannot be allocated among the multiple cost objectives without undue effort or cost and they cannot be apportioned on a reasonable documented basis, treat the costs as indirect, and allocate them equitably.

 

In making this determination, the ALJ should make clear whether she is determining the costs to be direct and applying options (1) or (2), or whether she is determining the costs to be indirect and applying option (3).

 

5. Supplemental Briefing

 

After the initial hearing, the Court called for further briefing.  The important questions from this briefing are twofold:  (1) Does 2 C.F.R. § 200.405(d), which describes “Direct cost allocation principles” and which the Court has discussed above, apply to this case, and (2) do any federal cases bear on the correct application of the regulations?

 

a.      Application of subdivision (d)

 

In its supplemental briefing, the Department argued that 2 C.F.R. § 200.405(d) applies only to “projects and activities” and not to a “Federal Award” as referenced in subdivision (a) of the same regulation.  (Resp. Supp. Br. 8:4-14.) According to the Department, this case involves Federal Awards, but not projects or activities. (Ibid.) The Court does not agree with this argument.

 

Subdivision (a) reads:

 

“Allocable costs in general. A cost is allocable to a Federal award or other cost objective if the cost is assignable to that Federal award or other cost objective in accordance with the relative benefits received. ... .”

 

Subdivision (d) – quoted here for reference a second time – reads:

 

“Direct cost allocation principles. If a cost benefits two or more projects or activities in proportions that can be determined without undue effort or cost, the cost must be allocated to the projects based on the proportional benefit. However, when those proportions cannot be determined because of the interrelationship of the work involved, then ... the costs may be allocated or transferred to benefitted projects on any reasonable documented basis.  Where the purchase of equipment or other capital asset is specifically authorized under a Federal award, the costs are assignable to the Federal award regardless of the use that may be made of the equipment or other capital asset involved, when no longer needed for the purpose for which it was originally required.”

 

(2 C.F.R. § 200.405.)

 

Subdivision (a) includes the term “cost objectives.” The definition of “cost objective” appears in 2 C.F.R. § 200.1:

 

“Cost objective means a program, function, activity, award, organizational subdivision, contract, or work unit for which cost data are desired and for which provision is made to accumulate and measure the cost of processes, products, jobs, and capital projects. A cost objective may be a major function of the recipient or subrecipient, a particular service or project, a Federal award, or an indirect cost activity ... .”

 

(10 AR 8489 [2 C.F.R. § 200.1], italics added.)  “Federal award” is defined separately. It refers to:

 

“(1)(i) Federal financical assistance received directly from a Federal agency or indirectly through a pass-through entity ... .”

 

(Ibid.)  Neither “project” nor “activity” is separately defined in 2 C.F.R. § 200.1.

 

A reader is left with three separate relevant terms – “Federal awards”, “projects”, and “activities” – all of which fall under the definition of “cost objective.” Subdivision (a) refers separately to a “Federal award” or “other cost objective,” implicitly acknowledging that a Federal award is among the subcategories of the broader term “cost objective,” which also includes a “program, function, activity.”

 

Respondents’ argument on the point begs the question. Respondents assume that the Disputed Costs fall into the definition only of a Federal award, and not “projects” or “activities.”  Respondent does not support that argument. “Federal award” appears to mean, in broad strokes, (1) actual disbursed funds or (2) the contracts governing their disbursement. It seems like the various uses for those funds – patient services, quality assurance, or administration – would better be categorized within the broader categories of “projects” or “activities.” Given the continual conflation of the terms “Federal award” and “cost objectives” (including projects and activities) throughout the regulations, Respondents’ attempt to keep their definitions rigorously separate is not well taken.

 

The immateriality of the distinction is emphasized by both parties’ interchangeable use of the terms “cost objective” and “Federal award” in their papers (before the Court expressly asked them to distinguish between 2 C.F.R. § 200.405(a) and (d) via supplemental briefing). For Respondents particularly: at Opposition Brief 5:28, Respondents refer to “a specific Federal award or final cost objective”; at 6:4 it refers to “three federal awards or cost objectives”; at 11:14-15, to “identification with the Federal award ..., or final cost objective”; 15:24, to a “final cost objective/federal award”; at 16:5, to “a specific Federal award or final cost objective”; at 16:7, to a “Federal award or cost objective”; at 16:21, to a “cost object[ive]/Federal Award”. The Court acknowledges there are also several places in Respondents’ brief where it refers to the three objectives specifically as “Federal award[s]”. (See Opposition Brief, 12:23, 16:3, 16:14.) But the bulk of Respondents’ analysis refers to the two terms together.

 

Respondents also cite 2 C.F.R. § 200.413 – rather than 2 C.F.R. § 200.1 – for the definition of “cost objective”, focusing on that section’s reference to Federal awards. But quoted in full, section 200.413(a) reads:

 

“General. Direct costs are those costs that can be identified specifically with a particular final cost objective, such as a Federal award, or other internally or externally funded activity, or that can be directly assigned to such activities relatively easily with a high degree of accuracy. ... .”

 

(10 AR 8501 [2 C.F.R. § 200.413(a)], italics added.)

 

Section 200.413(a), quoted in its entirety, further implies that a Federal award may be treated as an “activity”, at least for purposes of the relevant regulations. Fundamentally, the Court concludes that the terms “Federal award”, “projects”, and “activities” may be treated interchangeably for the purposes of this dispute.

 

b. Application of Relevant Case Law

 

The few federal cases the parties have located are only tenuously on point.

 

In Arizona v. Thompson (D.C. Cir. 2002) 281 F.3d 248 (Thompson), the D.C. Circuit examined whether states could “use their TANF funds to pay for all of the costs that are common to the administration of TANF, Medicaid, and Food Stamps.” (Thompson, supra, at p. 251.) The issue is distinguishable because it involved entirely separate federal programs, rather than objectives within a single program, as here.

 

The County’s federal district court case endorses a principle that “OMB Circular A–87 provides that common costs should ordinarily be allocated among benefitting programs,” citing Thompson. (Nebraska Dept. of Health & Human Services v. U.S. Dept. of Health and Human Services (D.D.C. 2004) 340 F.Supp.2d 1, 4 (Nebraska v. HHS).) Respondents point to the final clause in the sentence quoted above: “... common costs should ordinarily be allocated among benefitting programs, but OMB Circular A–87 does not require it.” (Ibid., italics added.) Although OMB guidance might not require that allocation here, California law independently requires DHCS to maximize federal reimbursement. And the Nebraska v. HHS decision goes on to apply principles of equitable distribution, based on the assumption that the common costs are indirect and must be so distributed.[8]

 

In sum, the reasoning of the federal cases cited are not dispositive of the issues before the Court.

 

Conclusion

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court GRANTS the Petition for Writ of Administrative Mandate.

 

            The Court sets aside the ALJ’s decision and orders the matter remanded for the decision-maker to make findings consistent with this order, including by holding further fact-finding proceedings as necessary.

INTRODUCTION  

 

            The Center for Biological Diversity (“Petitioner”) filed a petition for writ of mandate challenging the November 7, 2025 decision of the Governing Board of the South Coast Air Quality Management District (the “Air District”) enacting a voluntary compliance mechanism (the “Cooperative Agreement”) to reduce air pollution at the Ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach. Petitioner asserts three causes of action: (1) A violation of the California Environmental Quality Act (“CEQA”), (2) Unlawful Abrogation of Police Powers, and (3) Violations of the California Clean Air Act.

The Court DENIES the petition.

 

BACKGROUND  

 

A.     Attainment Plans Under the Federal and California Clean Air Acts

 

The federal Clean Air Act is designed to attain and maintain compliance with the National Ambient Air Quality Standards (“NAAQS”) within specified air quality control “regions.” (42 U.S.C. §§ 7407, 7409; 40 C.F.R. Part 50; 40 C.F.R. Part 81.) The California Clean Air Act is designed to attain and maintain compliance with the California Ambient Air Quality Standards (“CAAQS”) within specified air quality “basins.” (Health & Safety Code, § 39606.)

 

Under the federal Clean Air Act, the Environmental Protection Agency  must determine whether each air quality region is “attainment” or “nonattainment” of the NAAQS for each criteria pollutant. (42 U.S.C. § 7407, subd. (d)(4); 42 U.S.C. § 7501(2).) Once a region is designated nonattainment, the Clean Air Act requires states to prepare a “state implementation plan” (“SIP”). (42 U.S.C. § 7410.) Each SIP must provide for (1) “implementation of all reasonably available control measures as expeditiously as practicable,” and (2) the attainment of the NAAQS by specified deadlines. (See 42 U.S.C. §§ 7502, subd. (a)(2), 7511, subd. (a).) Nonattainment SIPs must also provide for “reasonable further progress” toward attainment by showing the annual incremental emission reductions of the relevant pollutant in the interim needed to ensure timely attainment of the NAAQS by the applicable deadline. (42 U.S.C. §§ 7501(1), 7502, subd. (c)(2).) The Environmental Protection Agency  must review and approve each proposed SIP. (42 U.S.C. § 7410, subd. (a)(1).)

 

Similarly, under the California Clean Air Act, the California Air Resources Board must determine whether each air quality basin is in attainment or nonattainment of the CAAQS, an “attainment plan” must be prepared for each nonattainment region, and the California Air Resources Board must review and approve each attainment plan. (Health & Safety Code, §§ 39608, 40911, 40913.) Attainment plans must be designed to achieve the CAAQS by “the earliest practicable date” and to reduce district–wide emissions of each nonattainment pollutant or its precursors by at least 5% per year (averaged over consecutive three–year periods), unless the district cannot meet that target even with every feasible measure adopted on an expeditious schedule. (Health & Safety Code, §§ 40913, 40914.)

 

B.     Background to the Air District

 

The “South Coast Air Basin” (the “Basin”) is a designated air quality control region under the federal Clean Air Act and an air quality basin under the California Clean Air Act. The Basin consists of the urban portions of Los Angeles, Riverside, and San Bernardino counties, and all of Orange County. (40 C.F.R. § 81.305; Health & Safety Code, § 40410.) The Air District is responsible for preparing the Basin's “Air Quality Management Plans” (“AQMP”.) (Health & Safety Code, §§ 40408, 40412.) Each AQMP serves as both the SIP under the Clean Air Act and the attainment plan under the California Clean Air Act for the Basin for specified pollutants. (Health & Safety Code, § 40460.) AQMPs set forth general “control measures” designed to attain and maintain the NAAQS and the CAAQS within the Basin. (Health & Safety Code, § 40913.)

 

The Basin is known to have critical air pollution problems caused by the operation of millions of motor vehicles, stationary sources of pollution, frequent atmospheric inversions that trap aerial contaminants, and the large amount of sunshine that transforms vehicular and nonvehicular emissions into a variety of deleterious chemicals. (Health & Safety Code, § 40402, subd. (b).)

 

According to the Air District, the Basin is in an “extreme” non-attainment area for the NAAQS for ozone. (AR 11527.) Ozone is formed when volatile organic compounds and nitrogen oxides react in the presence of sunlight. (AR 6720.) The key to reducing ozone is to reduce nitrogen oxides. (AR 6720.) Breathing high levels of ozone can cause a variety of negative health impacts such as asthma, chronic bronchitis, and emphysema, and increased susceptibility to lung infection. (AR 6719.)

 

C.     California Environmental Quality Act

 

CEQA was designed to inform agency decision makers and the public about (1) the potentially significant impacts that might result from proposed projects, (2) ways that those impacts can be reduced or avoided through alternatives and/or mitigation measures, (3) prevent significant, avoidable damage to the environment by requiring changes in projects through the use of alternatives or mitigation measures when the governmental agency finds the changes to be feasible; (4) disclose to the public the reasons why a governmental agency approved the project in the manner the agency chose if significant environmental effects are involved. (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, § 15002, subd. (a); Save Our Access v. City of San Diego (2025) 115 Cal.App.5th 388, 396-397.) Under CEQA, an agency can approve a project notwithstanding significant impacts if the agency finds that overriding considerations outweigh the impacts. (Public Resources Code, § 21081, subd. (b); Mission Bay Alliance v. Office of Community Investment & Infrastructure (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 160, 183.) Thus, CEQA does not necessarily “dictat[e] pro–environmental outcomes,” but does ensure that decisions are made with full knowledge of environmental consequences. (Gooden v. County of Los Angeles (2024) 106 Cal.App.5th 1, 14–15.)

 

A public agency must generally prepare an environmental impact report (“EIR”) whenever the agency proposes to approve or to carry out a project that may have a significant effect on the environment. (Public Resources Code, § 21151.) The term “project” is broadly defined to include any activity that may cause a direct or reasonably foreseeable indirect physical change in the environment. (Public Resources Code, § 21065.) The term “significant effect on the environment” is defined as “substantial, or potentially substantial, adverse changes in the physical conditions” which exist within the project area, including changes in air quality. (Public Resources Code, § 21151, subd. (b); Public Resources Code, § 21060.5; Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, § 15382.)

 

An EIR must identify and describe “[d]irect and indirect significant effects of the project on the environment” (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, § 15126.2, subd. (a)) and must also describe feasible mitigation measures and alternatives to the project that could avoid or minimize significant environmental impacts. (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, §§ 15126.4, 15126.6.)

 

A program EIR can be prepared when there are a series of actions that are characterized as one large project and related by (1) geography; (2) as logical parts in the chain of contemplated actions; (3) in connection with the issuance of rules, regulations, plans, or other general criteria to govern the conduct of a continuing program, or (4) as individual activities carried out under the same authorizing statutory or regulatory authority and having generally similar environmental effects which can be mitigated in similar ways. (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, § 15168, subd. (a).) An agency shall incorporate feasible mitigation measures and alternatives developed in a program EIR into later activities in the program. (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, § 15168, subd. (c)(3).) However, when a later activity would have effects that were not examined in the program EIR, a new initial study [must] be prepared leading to either an EIR or a negative declaration.” (Id. at subd. (c)(1).)

 

D.    The Port of Los Angeles and the Port of Long Beach

 

The City of Los Angeles and the City of Long Beach own the Port of Los Angeles and the Port of Long Beach (the “Ports”). These Ports are the largest in the nation in terms of container throughput. (AR 8057.) At the Ports, equipment is used to move cargo, rail cars, and trucks, with the three greatest contribution to emissions being rubber-tired gantry cranes, yard hostlers, and top handlers. (AR 6209.)

 

The Clean Air Act expressly preempts state and local standards relating to emissions from new motor vehicles and engines, new nonroad vehicles and engines, and new locomotives and locomotive engines. (42 U.S.C. § 7543, subd. (a), (e).) While the Air District does have authority to address Port–related mobile emissions through an “indirect source rule,” that authority only allows the Air District to impose requirements on the Ports or their tenants – the Air District cannot impose requirements directly on mobile sources operated by tenants. (42 U.S.C. § 7410, subd. (a)(5); Health & Safety Code, § 40716, subd. (a)(1).)

 

The Ports have imposed such requirements in leases with terminal operators and “concession agreements” with companies that transport cargo at the Ports, such as trucking companies. (See American Trucking Assns., Inc. v. City of Los Angeles (2013) 569 U.S. 641, 645–646.) In 2006, the Ports adopted the “San Pedro Bay Ports Clean Air Action Plan” (“CAAP”) a joint program of emission–reduction strategies. (AR 3126.) Many of the actions connected with CAAP are voluntary in nature or occur as a result of grants or incentive programs. (AR 3126.) Other reduction targets are a result of implementation of federal and state regulations. (AR 4023.) A draft of the Cooperative Agreement (described in more detail below) states that CAAP-related programs since 2005 have resulted in a 91% reduction in diesel particulate matter, 72% reduction in nitrogen oxides, and 98% reduction in sulfur oxides. (AR 12107.) 

 

E.     Adoption and Implementation of the Port-Related Control Measures

 

The 2016 AQMP contained Control Measure MOB–01 entitled “Emission Reductions at Commercial Marine Ports.” The measure sought to “quantify the emission reductions realized from the CAAP and credit the reductions into the SIP to the extent that these actions are real and surplus to the existing SIP.” (AR 161, 3126.) The AQMP itself did not attempt to estimate the emission reductions to be achieved by MOB–01. (AR 3123.) Nor were the reductions used in the attainment demonstration. (AR 3108.) Instead, the 2016 AQMP established a process for crediting emissions reductions achieved by MOB–01 to the SIP after the emissions reductions occur. (AR 3126; 4023.)

 

Before any reduction can be credited, the Air District would have to demonstrate to the federal Environmental Protection Agency that the reduction in emissions would be real, quantifiable, surplus, enforceable, and permanent. (AR 150-151; 4023.) The Air District analyzed the environmental impacts of the 2016 MOB–01 control measure in a PEIR prepared for the 2016 AQMP. (AR 66–732.)

 

After the Air District Governing Board adopted the 2016 AQMP in March of 2017, Air District staff convened working groups to discuss implementation of MOB–01. (AR 6136–6155.) Through 2021, the Air District negotiated draft MOUs with both Ports. (AR 6353–6382; AR 6443–6476.) According to a slide deck of the Board of the Air District, the negotiations failed due to an impasse on enforceability. (AR 6552; 610508.)

 

In February 2022, the Board began work on a new indirect source rule known as Proposed Rule 2304 (“PR 2304”). The 2022 AQMP, adopted in December of that year, sought to reduce emissions related to on-road heavy duty vehicles, ocean going vessels, cargo handling equipment, locomotives, and harbor craft that go to and from the ports. (AR 6893.) The Air District analyzed the environmental impacts of the 2022 control measure in a PEIR prepared for the 2022 AQMP (the “2022 PEIR”). (AR 1237.)

 

Between 2022 and 2025, Air District staff conducted a series of working group meetings, community meetings, site visits, and committee briefings on PR 2304. (AR 6674, 10450, 11527–11528, AR 11849–11850.) According to a summary prepared for the Board of the Air District, a consistent message from stakeholder engagement was that a lack of infrastructure was preventing deployment of zero-emission technologies. (AR 11527-11528.) According to a letter from environmental groups, Air District staff allegedly committed to adopt an indirect source rule by the first quarter of 2025. (AR 10451.)

 

Staff released draft language for PR 2304 for consideration by the Air District Board on August 1, 2025. (AR 10516-10535.) However, on July 18, 2025, the ports submitted a draft Cooperative Agreement (“Cooperative Agreement”) as an alternative to PR 2304. (AR 11528; AR 10412–10443.) As explained on an Air District agenda, the Cooperative Agreement is an enforceable contractual agreement that is consistent with the objectives of AQMP control measure MOB-01 and closely mirrors the requirements proposed in PR 2304. (AR 11528.)

 

On August 1, 2025, after public testimony, the Board directed staff to negotiate the terms of a Cooperative Agreement with the Ports. (AR 10791; 11853.) Air District held public meetings on August 28, 2025 and October 15, 2025 and five virtual office hour sessions to discuss the Cooperative Agreement. (AR 10795, 11530.)

 

On November 5, 2025, Petitioner and others, submitted a comment letter opposing the Cooperative Agreement. (AR 11206-011208.) The letter argued that the Cooperative Agreement is inadequate because it only addresses infrastructure planning and does not include binding emission reduction commitments. (AR 11207.) The letter also argued that the Cooperative Agreement does not fall within the scope of previously conducted CEQA review. (AR 11207-11208.)

 

The Board of the Air District approved the final version of the Cooperative Agreement on November 7, 2025, under Resolution 25-22. (AR 2914-2915.)

 

The Cooperative Agreement as approved obligates each Port to prepare zero–emission charging and fueling infrastructure plans (“ZE Plans”) in three phases, with fixed approval deadlines: December 31, 2027 for container–terminal cargo handling equipment and drayage trucks; December 31, 2028 for remaining cargo handling equipment, locomotives, and harbor craft; and December 31, 2029 for ocean–going vessels. (AR 12124, 12131.) Each ZE Plan must include a baseline infrastructure inventory; planning targets; project–level details, including locations, technologies, costs, funding sources, schedules, permits, and CEQA documentation; and energy–supply and construction–workforce analyses. (AR 12125–12127.) The Agreement requires the Ports to “work with their Tenants, Port Source operators, and/or other Project Delivery Entities to implement the ZE Plans,” “consistent with CEQA and other applicable laws and regulations.” (AR 12133.) The Agreement sets forth penalties for noncompliance. For each default within its control, each Port must pay $50,000 to $200,000 into a Clean Air Mitigation Fund. (AR 12114-12116.) The Agreement has a five–year term which expires on December 31, 2030. (AR 12112.) Any party, however, may terminate the Agreement, for any reason, on 45 days’ written notice. (Ibid.)

 

In Resolution No. 25–22, the Board directed staff to “not pursue any new rulemaking that meets the objective of AQMP Control Measure MOB–01” during the term of the Cooperative Agreement, unless the Agreement is terminated before the end of the five–year term. (AR 65.)

 

According to notes provided at the Air District board meeting, the Cooperative Agreement imposes essentially the same substantive obligations on the Ports as would have been required by PR 2304: the Ports must prepare port–wide infrastructure plans, set emissions reduction targets, meet specified milestones, and prepare annual reports. (AR 11532, 11528, 10858, 10859.) The only difference is as to enforcement: Proposed Rule 2304 would have been enforced under the California Health & Safety Code and would include statutory penalties for violations while the Cooperative Agreement is enforceable under contract terms and conditions, including payments into a mitigation fund for violations. (AR 10859.)

 

As required by CEQA, Air District staff considered whether the Cooperative Agreement was “within the scope” of the activities analyzed in the 2016 and 2022 PEIRs as provided for in the CEQA Guidelines. (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, § 15168, subd. (c).) Staff set forth this analysis in Attachment G to the November 7, 2025 Board package, a 101–page document entitled “CEQA Analysis of the Proposed Cooperative Agreement” (“Attachment G”). (AR 11960–12060.) The Air District Governing Board adopted the determinations set forth in Attachment G (AR 61–65), and the Air District filed a notice of determination the same day. (AR 1–4.)

STANDARD OF REVIEW

 

The petition includes three causes of action for writ of mandate under either Code of Civil Procedure section 1085 or 1094.5.  The first cause of action alleges violations of CEQA.  The second cause of action alleges an abrogation of police powers. The third cause of action alleges a violation of the California Clean Air Act.

 

A.     CEQA

 

 In an action challenging an agency’s decision under CEQA, the trial court reviews the agency’s decision for a prejudicial abuse of discretion.  (Pub. Res. Code, § 21168.5.)  “Abuse of discretion is established if the agency has not proceeded in a manner required by law or if the determination or decision is not supported by substantial evidence.”  (Ibid.; see also Vineyard Area Citizens for Responsible Growth, Inc. v. City of Rancho Cordova (2007) 40 Cal.4th 412, 435.)  Challenges to an agency’s failure to proceed in a manner required by CEQA are subject to a less deferential standard than challenges to an agency’s factual conclusions.  (Vineyard, supra at p. 435.)  In reviewing these claims, the court must “determine de novo whether the agency has employed the correct procedures.”  (Ibid.; see Dry Creek Citizens Coalition v. County of Tulare (1999) 70 Cal.App.4th 20, 26.) 

 

 “Substantial evidence includes fact, a reasonable assumption predicated upon fact, or expert opinion supported by fact…. Substantial evidence is not argument, speculation, unsubstantiated opinion or narrative, evidence that is clearly inaccurate or erroneous, or evidence of social or economic impacts that do not contribute to, or are not caused by, physical impacts on the environment.”  (Public Resources Code § 21080, subd. (e).)  Under the substantial evidence test, the court review “the administrative record to see if it contains evidence of ponderable legal significance that is reasonable in nature, credible, and of solid value, to support the agency's decision.”  (Protect Tustin Ranch, supra, 70 Cal.App.5th at p. 960.) A court may not set aside an agency’s approval of an EIR on the round that an opposite conclusion would have been equally or more reasonable. (Laurel Heights Improvement Assn. v. Regents of University of California (1988) 47 Cal.3d 376, 393 (Laurel Heights).)

 

An agency is presumed to have regularly performed its official duties.  (Evidence Code § 664.)  When an appellant challenges “the sufficiency of the evidence, all material evidence on the point must be set forth and not merely [its] own evidence.” (Toigo v. Town of Ross (1998) 70 Cal.App.4th 309, 317.)  The petitioner “must lay out the evidence favorable to the other side and show why it is lacking.” (Defend the Bay v. City of Irvine (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 1261, 1266.)  A reviewing court “will not act as counsel for either party to an appeal and will not assume the task of initiating and prosecuting a search of the record for any purpose of discovering errors not pointed out in the briefs.” (Fox v. Erickson (1950) 99 Cal.App.2d 740, 742.)  “When an appellant fails to raise a point, or asserts it but fails to support it with reasoned argument and citations to authority, we treat the point as waived.”  (Nelson v. Avondale HOA (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 857, 862-863.)

 

B.     Abrogation of Police Powers and California Clean Air Act

 

 

For the second and third causes of action, it is petitioner’s burden to establish that an agency’s decision was “arbitrary, capricious, entirely lacking in evidentiary support, unlawful, or procedurally unfair.” (American Coatings Assn. v. South Coast Air Quality Management Dist. (2012) 54 Cal.4th 446, 460.) This is an extremely deferential test and requires a court to ensure an agency has considered all relevant factors and has demonstrated a rational connection between these factors, the choice made, and the purposes of the enabling statute. (County of Los Angeles v. City of Los Angeles (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 643, 654.)

 

“On questions of law arising in mandate proceedings, [the court] exercise[s] independent judgment.’”  (Christensen v. Lightbourne (2017) 15 Cal.App.5th 1239, 1251.)  The interpretation of a statute or regulation is a question of law.  (See State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Quackenbush (1999) 77 Cal.App.4th 65, 77.)  To the extent “purely legal issues involve the interpretation of a statute [or regulation] an administrative agency is responsible for enforcing, [the court] exercise[s] [its] independent judgment, ‘taking into account and respecting the agency's interpretation of its meaning.’” (Housing Partners I, Inc. v. Duncan (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 1335, 1343; see also Yamaha Corp. of America v. State Bd. Of Equalization (1998) 19 Cal.4th 1, 11.)  How much weight to accord an agency's construction is “situational,” and depends on the circumstances.  (See American Coatings Assn. v. South Coast Air Quality Management Dist. (2012) 54 Cal.4th 446, 461-462.)

 

JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

The Court denies Petitioner’s request for judicial notice.

 

Petitioner requested judicial notice of a report for the Warehouse Actions and Investments to Reduce Emissions (WAIRE) Program from January 2026 and a description of The Public Trust Doctrine, available on the California Coastal Commission’s website. These documents are all extra-record evidence.  “As a general rule, a hearing on a writ of administrative mandamus is conducted solely on the record of the proceeding before the administrative agency.”  (Richardson v. City and County of San Francisco (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 671, 702.)  There is a narrow exception under which the court may augment the administrative record:

 

Where the court finds that there is relevant evidence that, in the exercise of reasonable diligence, could not have been produced or that was improperly excluded at the hearing before respondent, it may enter judgment as provided in subdivision (f) remanding the case to be reconsidered in the light of that evidence; or, in cases in which the court is authorized by law to exercise its independent judgment on the evidence, the court may admit the evidence at the hearing on the writ without remanding the case.

 

(Code Civ. Pro. § 1094.5, subd. (e).)  “Augmentation of the administrative record is permitted only within the strict limits set forth in the statute.”  (Armondo v. Department of Motor Vehicles (1993) 15 Cal.App.4th 1174, 1180.)  A request for judicial notice cannot be used to circumvent the rules constraining the admission of extra-record evidence. (Ballona Wetlands Land Trust v. City of Los Angeles (2011) 201 Cal.App.4th 455, 475, fn. 10.)  The Los Angeles Superior Court Local Rules provide: “Any motion to augment the record under Section 1094.5, subd. (e) must be filed as a noticed motion. A party considering filing a motion to augment should discuss the timing of the motion with the court at the trial setting conference.”  (LASC Local Rules 3.231(g)(3) [emphasis added].)  Neither party moved to augment the administrative record with this extra-record evidence.  The parties did not show that the requirements of section 1094.5(e) are met to augment the record.  Petitioner does not show that, with the exercise of reasonable diligence, the description of the public trust doctrine could not have been submitted during the administrative proceedings. The WAIRE report appears to postdate the administrative decisions at issue, and the Court declines to consider evidence that is new at this point.

 

            The Court also denies the Real Parties Harbor Commission of the Port of Los Angeles and Harbor Commission of the Port of Long Beach (“Real Parties”) request for judicial notice. The Real Parties request judicial notice of various government documents that predate the filing of the petition, and there is no explanation for why they are not already part of the administrative record.

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.     What is the proper standard of review?

 

The parties dispute which standard of review applies under CEQA for Petitioner’s claim that the Air District failed to analyze the environmental impacts of the decision to proceed with a Cooperative Agreement.

 

Petitioner contends that the fair argument standard applies to review an agency's determination about whether to prepare a new or supplemental EIR in a later new project following certification of a program EIR. (Reply, 1:20-21.) The Air District contends that the fair argument standard applies only when the agency approves an activity that was not within the scope of the prior PEIR, and because this case involves a project that was in the scope of a prior PEIR. (Opposition, 10:4-28.)

 

The Air District is correct; the substantial evidence standard should be used. As the Court lays out below, the Air District has shown that the Cooperative Agreement is within the scope of the previous PEIR and that all environmental impacts from the Cooperative Agreement have been analyzed in the previous PEIR.  (IBC Business Owners for Sensible Development v. City of Irvine (2023) 88 Cal.App.5th 100, 118-119; Citizens for Responsible Equitable Environmental Development v. City of San Diego Redevelopment Agency (2005) 134 Cal.App.4th 598, 608 [California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA) statute does not require an agency to prepare an environmental impact report (EIR) for a project whose environmental impacts have been sufficiently analyzed in a prior program EIR or master EIR].)

 

B.     Was the Air District required to prepare a new CEQA document for the Cooperative Agreement?

 

After certifying a PEIR, when an agency proposes to approve a “later activity” within the program, the agency must determine (1) whether the later activity would have effects that were not examined in the PEIR (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, § 15168, subd. (c)(1)), and (2) whether the later activity will have new or substantially increased significant effects based on changes in the program or new information. (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, § 15162.) If neither of these conditions is present, then the later activity is “within the scope” of the prior PEIR, and the agency need not prepare any new CEQA document. (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, § 15168, subd. (c)(1).)

 

Here, the Cooperative Agreement implements the Port–related MOB–01 control measures adopted in the AQMPs. (AR 11966.) Therefore, the Air District evaluated whether the Cooperative Agreement qualify as a “later activity” within the scope of the previous analyses conducted in the PEIRs for the 2022 AQMP and the 2016 AQMP. (AR 11966-11967.) As required by CEQA, the Air District evaluated whether the Cooperative Agreement was within the scope of the PEIRs. The Air District’s evaluation and evidence is set forth in Attachment G, a 101–page document submitted to the Air District Governing Board when it approved the Cooperative Agreement. Attachment G concludes that the “environmental impacts associated with implementing the proposed Cooperative Agreement are within the scope of what was previously analyzed in the Final Program EIR for the 2022 AQMP for Control Measures MOB-01 and MOB-15, and Final Program EIR for the 2016 AQMP for Control Measure MOB-01. (AR 12053.) Accordingly, no EIR or other CEQA document was required. (AR 12053.)  

 

1.      Petitioner’s argument that there was no substantial evidence to indicate the Agreement fell within the scope of the PEIRs

 

Petitioner’s first argument is that the record does not contain “substantial evidence” to claim that the “Agreement falls within the scope of the PEIRs.” (Opening Brief, 11:24-13:28.)

 

When an appellant changing an EIR for insufficient evidence fails to lay out the evidence favorable to the other side and show why it is lacking, that is fatal to the appellant. (Defend the Bay v. City of Irvine (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 1261, 1266.)

 

 Petitioner’s brief includes very few citations to Attachment G, which includes the Air District’s reasons for arguing that the Agreement fell within the scope of the PEIRs. Petitioner does cite AR 11961 for the argument that the Cooperative Agreement is not “SIP-credible because it merely requires the Ports to prepare zero-emission infrastructure plans by 2028, unless extended, and then work with tenants and other entities to implement those plans or provide notice if it anticipates it is unable to do so.” (Opening Brief, 12:11-14.) Petitioner explains this is concerning because the Cooperative Agreement deviates from the 2016 PEIR, which assumed that MOB-01 emission reduction measures would be real, quantifiable, enforceable, and permanent. (Ibid.)

 

This argument is flawed for several reasons. First, whether emissions reductions from the Cooperative Agreement can be credited to the SIP has no bearing on whether the activities described in the Cooperative Agreement were within the scope of the activities contemplated in the Port–related control measures and analyzed in the Program EIRs.

 

Second, this argument misstates the record, as neither MOB-01 measure presumed that emission reduction measures at the port would be SIP creditable.  The 2016 version of MOB–01 stated that “to the extent that” the emissions reduction measures resulting therefrom turned out to be SIP–creditable, the Air District will credit them to the SIP after they occur through rate–of–progress reporting or revised baseline inventories in future plan revisions. (AR 3126.) The 2022 version of MOB–01 did not address SIP creditability requirements in any way. (AR 6893.)

 

The Cooperative Agreement also does require the Ports to implement the infrastructure plans. The Agreement provides that, after the Air District approves the infrastructure plan, “the Ports shall work with their Tenants, Port Source operators, and/or other Project Delivery Entities to implement the ZE Plans through the term of this agreement, including seeking all project–specific approvals and local development permits.” (AR 12133.) Having signed the Agreement, the Ports are now contractually obligated to prepare and implement the zero–emission infrastructure plans, and the Air District can legally enforce that obligation through the Agreement’s penalty provisions or otherwise. (AR 12113 [“Nothing contained in this paragraph is intended to limit any rights or remedies that the Parties may have under law”].)

 

Petitioner also states that the Air District’s reliance on MOB-15 fails. (Opening Brief, 13:4.) In Attachment G, the Air District stated that the previous analyses of environmental impacts for Control Measure MOB-15 of the 2022 AQMP Final Program EIR covered the breadth of impacts that are expected to result from the development and implementation of infrastructure plans. (AR 11977.) While Petitioner contends that the Air District should have been more specific, legally what the Air District is required to do is conduct “a thorough investigation” to determine that “a particular impact is too speculative for evaluation.” (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, § 15145.)  If an impact is too speculative for evaluation, the agency must make that note and terminate discussion of the impact. (Ibid.) The Court concludes that the Air District has properly applied Section 15145 in its analysis at AR 11977.

 

2.      Petitioner’s argument that the Cooperative Agreement has environmental effects not examined in the PEIR

 

Next, Petitioner asserts that the Cooperative Agreement has environmental effects not examined in the PEIR. (Opening Brief, 13:19-14:21.) Petitioner does not even come close to meeting their burden to present evidence favorable to the other side.

 

There is not a single citation to Attachment G on Page 13 or Page 14 of the opening brief. Attachment G compared, topic–by–topic and in detail, the impacts considered in the Program EIRs with the impacts expected to result from the Cooperative Agreement. (AR 11968–11980.) Attachment G explained that while there was still speculation about the exact scope of future infrastructure plans, the development and implementation of infrastructure plans as required by the Cooperative Agreement could have the same or less significant impacts than the 2022-MOB-01. (AR 11974.) Attachment G also explained that 2022 PEIR mitigation measures “are expected to apply” to development and implementation of the infrastructure plans under the Cooperative Agreement. (AR 11974.)

 

With no citation to the record, Petitioner states that the Air District’s “enactment of the Agreement and abandonment of an enforceable emissions rulemaking will result in new, or more severe, unassessed environment effects, necessitating an Initial Study and EIR for the Agreement.” (Opening Brief, 14:1-14:3.) As the Court addressed above, the notion that the plan lacks enforceable emission rulemaking is incorrect. The Ports are contractually obligated to implement zero-emission infrastructure plans and the Air District can legally enforce that obligation through the penalty provision of the Cooperative Agreement. (AR 12113.) Petitioner may not like the way enforcement has been setup, but Petitioner’s preferences are not a reason for the Court to grant this petition for writ of mandate.

 

Petitioner cites Sierra Club v. County of Sonoma (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 1307, 1319 for the proposition that as long as the Cooperative Agreement may have a significant adverse effect on the environment which was not examined in the prior program EIR, a new EIR must be created.

 

The law has defined this Sierra Club test as the “reverse substantial evidence” test, which applies only in limited circumstances – where the initial EIR is a “program EIR” and a subsequent project is proposed which is not the same or within the scope of the plan described in the program EIR. (Olen Properties Corp. v. City of Newport Beach (2023) 93 Cal.App.5th 270, 279-280.) Substantial evidence in the record indicates that the Draft Cooperative Agreement covers on-port infrastructure for all port sources, just like Proposed Rule 2304. (AR 10859.) Therefore, substantial evidence indicates that the scope of both projects is similar. (Ibid.) The reverse substantial evidence test should not be applied here.

 

Petitioner also cites Center for Sierra Nevada Conservation v. County of El Dorado (2012) 20 Cal.App.4th 1156, 1185 for the proposition that the Air District cannot shield subsequent projects affecting the environment simply on the grounds that it has previously  recognized that the Ports are subject to adverse air quality.

 

However, the facts of that case differed significantly from the facts of the case before this Court. In Center for Sierra Nevada Conservation, a 2004 program EIR Noticed that even with the anticipated mitigation measures of Options A and B and other proposed mitigation measures in place, impacts would not be reduced to a less-than-significant level. (Id., at p. 1182.) By contrast, the subsequent negative declaration for the oak woodland management plan concluded that cumulative impacts would be less than significant. (Ibid.)

 

This is not a situation where a past program EIR and a present CEQA document have come to diametrically opposed conclusions. Attachment G explained that while there was still speculation about the exact scope of future infrastructure plans, the development and implementation of infrastructure plans as required by the Cooperative Agreement could have the same or less significant impacts than the 2022-MOB-01. (AR 11974.)

 

Ultimately, though, a court’s job is not to review a record to determine whether there is a possibility of an environmental impact that a government agency did not mention. (Latinos Unidos de Napa v. City of Napa (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 192, 205.) A court’s job is to view the evidence in the light most favorable to the agency’s decision to determine whether substantial evidence supports the decision not to require additional review. (Ibid.)

 

And here, the Court finds that there was substantial evidence not to require additional review.

 

Attachment G explained that the activity under the Cooperative Agreement will occur at the same or smaller scale, and there would be no new rail or truck routes. (AR 12013, AR 12031.) The same regulations reviewed in past CEQA documents are still in effect: those governing spill-containment, water quality, battery-recycling, and building codes. (AR 12042-12043.) As Attachment G stated, activity under the Cooperative Agreement “is not expected to result in new or modified physical changes or impacts that were not previously analyzed.” (AR 12052.) Consistent with the previous PEIR, Attachment G noted that some impacts remain unclear until such time as specific projects are proposed. (AR 11974-11977, 12014, 12024.) As the Court said above, the law does not require an agency to expend resources on speculative impacts. (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, § 15145.) 

 

The Cooperative Agreement will ultimately result in the construction and operation of zero-emission infrastructure plans. (AR 11961.) The Agreement is likely to result in increased demand for electricity and alternate fuels. The 2022 PEIR considered the environmental impacts of these changes, including air quality and greenhouse gases (AR 1536, 1548, 1565, 1576-1577, 1581, 1585), energy (AR 1612, 1622-1623), hazards and hazardous waste materials (AR 1654), noise (AR 1729, 1732, 1735, 1739) and solid and hazardous waste. (AR 1750, 1754, 1762, 1764.)

 

The 2022 PEIR concluded that five impacts were significant: the impact of construction on air quality, even after mitigation (AR 1262); the increase in demand for energy from operation of infrastructure (AR 1264); the increased transport of hazardous materials (AR 1264-1265); noise from construction (AR 1267); and increases in waste from construction and operation (AR 1268.) Consistent with a “first–tier” level of review, the 2022 PEIR identified many impacts that cannot be analyzed in detail until such time as specific projects are proposed. (See, e.g., AR 1525, 1548, 1551, 1599, 1262, 1722, 1736.)

 

Thus, substantial evidence shows (1) the charging and fueling infrastructure as contemplated in the Port–related control measures and the Cooperative Agreement remains the same; (2) the impacts are the same or less; (3) the mitigation measures are equally applicable; and (4) there is no new information that changes any prior analysis.

 

3.      Petitioner’s argument that the Air District did not incorporate feasible mitigation measures and alternatives developed in the program EIR into later activities in the program

 

 

Next, Petitioner indicates that the Air District did not incorporate feasible mitigation measures and alternatives developed in the program EIR into later activities in the program, in violation of CEQA Guidelines Section 15168, subd. (c)(3). (Opening Brief, 14:23-15:12; Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, § 15168, subd. (c).)

 

According to Petitioner, Section 15168, subd. (c) required the Air District to “continue with PR 2304 or adopt[] another enforceable indirect source rule” rather than the Cooperative Agreement purportedly because a rule, unlike the Cooperative Agreement, would “guarantee timely or measurable emission reductions.” (Opening Brief, 14:27-15:2.)

 

This claim is not correct. First, Petitioner cites no part of the record which indicates Proposed Rule 2304 was actually adopted as a mitigation measure in either Program EIR. This means CEQA Guidelines Section 15168, subd. (c)(3) does not govern this case.

 

Petitioner also identifies no part of the record which indicates Proposed Rule 2304 guarantees timely or measurable emission reductions.

 

Petitioner then states that the Air District “unilaterally decided to enact an Agreement relying on voluntary infrastructure plans, future negotiations, and mitigation payments that do not guarantee timely or measurable emission reductions.” (Opening Brief, 15:2-4.)

 

Substantial evidence suggests that the Air District did not unilaterally decide to enact an Agreement. On August 1, 2025, after public testimony, the Board directed staff to negotiate the terms of a Cooperative Agreement with the Ports. (AR 10791; 11853.) the Air District held public meetings on August 28, 2025 and October 15, 2025 and five virtual office hour sessions to discuss the Cooperative Agreement. (AR 10795, 11530.)

 

4.      Petitioner’s argument that the Air District failed to prepare sufficient environmental documentation and assessment for the agreement

 

Finally, Petitioner suggests that broadly speaking, the Air District failed to prepare sufficient environmental documentation and assessment for the agreement. (Opening Brief, 15:20-16:16.)

 

This argument misapplies CEQA law. The Air District was not obligated to prepare an initial study, an EIR or negative declaration, or a notice of preparation prior to approving the Cooperative Agreement. (Opening Brief, 15:13–16:16.) Those obligations only arise when a later activity falls outside the scope of the program EIR or a Section 21166 trigger exists. (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, §§ 15162, 15168, subds. (c)(1), (c)(2).) Where, as here, the agency finds based on substantial evidence that the activity is within the scope of the activity previously studied, “no new environmental document” is required. (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 14, § 15168, subd. (c)(2).)

 

Petitioner asserts that a new CEQA document  “would have assessed whether the agreement will produce measurable, enforceable, emission reductions” and would have assessed whether the Cooperative Agreement “will close the gap between the expected 2 to 3 tons per year of Nox reductions by 2031 and the Ports’ approximately 19-ton-per-day fair-share need identified in the 2022 AQMP.” (Opening Brief, 16:5-8.) This argument is unpersuasive.

 

A new document prepared for a Cooperative Agreement would have evaluated the same things evaluated in the Program EIRs: changes to the environment that would have resulted from the construction and operation of zero–emission infrastructure at the ports. Contrary to what Petitioner suggests, CEQA does not require the Air District to consider whether emission reductions would help the region attain the NAAQS or the CAAQS or otherwise improve existing air quality. It is the Clean Air Acts, not CEQA, that requires the Air District to engage in attainment planning.

 

Petitioner  further suggests that the Air District violated CEQA by “fail[ing] to adequately include and consider the public’s comments and input in the decision–making process” that led to the Cooperative Agreement. (Opening Brief, 16:18–19.) All of the authorities that Petitioner cites in support of this statement, however, apply to the preparation of new CEQA documents. Petitioner does not cite any legal authority which requires a certain level of public participation when an agency determines that a later activity is within the scope of an activity previously studied in a Program EIR.

 

The Air District also did engage in a public process, and Petitioner does not explain what was wrong with that process. the Air District held public meetings on August 28, 2025 and October 15, 2025 and five virtual office hour sessions to discuss the Cooperative Agreement. (AR 10795, 11530.) Public comments produced material changes. Enforcement triggers with escalating financial consequences were added; default payments were doubled; the Ports’ proposed ten–year rulemaking prohibition was removed from the contract and replaced with a five–year direction housed in a Board resolution; and the termination notice period was halved to 45 days. (AR 11851-11852; AR 11855–11856; AR 11858.)  Hundreds of businesses and organizations wrote letters or shared feedback explaining why a Cooperative Agreement would  offer benefits, explaining that it would allow parties to adapt, make corrections, and accelerate progress, rather than be stuck with rigid mandates that might halt progress or shift investment elsewhere. (AR 11062, 11197, 11209, 11213 – 11216.)

 

There is substantial evidence in the record to suggest that the Air District adequately considered public comments and input in its decision-making.

 

C.     Did the Air District violate the California Clean Air Act?

 

Petitioner alleges that the Air District violated its duties under the California Clean Air Act by acting arbitrarily, capriciously, and unlawfully, when executing a Voluntary Agreement tailored solely to infrastructure planning without enforceable emission reduction commitments in lieu of a binding indirect source rule. (Opening Brief, 18:26-19:2.)

 

According to the California Clean Air Act, the Air District must adopt rules and regulations which conform to its attainment plans and which “[r]equire the use of best available control technology for new and modified sources and the use of best available retrofit control technology for existing sources,” “provide for indirect source controls in those areas of the south coast district in which there are high-level, localized concentrations of pollutants,” and “provide for transportation control measures.” (Health & Safety Code § 40440, subd. (b)(1), (b)(3), (b)(4).)

 

As the Air District admits, the Cooperative Agreement is not a rule or regulation. (Opposition, 19:7-8.) Because the above Health & Safety Code statutes only pertain to rules and regulations, the Cooperative Agreement cannot be said to have violated any of the statutes mentioned in Petitioner’s brief.

 

As a general matter, based on the existing record, the Court cannot say that the Air District has violated the California Clean Air Act by adopting the Cooperative Agreement. The record before the Court does not demonstrate that the Air District lacked other projects or initiatives that satisfy the Health & Safety Code statutes mentioned in Petitioner’s brief.

 

The California Clean Air Act does not prohibit the Air District from engaging in other efforts to reduce emissions beyond rules and regulations. Indeed, another part of the Health and Safety Code (not part of the California Clean Air Act) authorizes the Air District to “contract with any federal, state, or local governmental agencies…” to reduce emissions. (Health & Safety Code, § 40701, subd. (f).)

 

Petitioner argues that because it is feasible to have a binding indirect source rule, a binding indirect source rule should have been created by the Air District. (Opening Brief, 20:22-23.) However, this is Petitioner’s policy preference and not a ground that the Court can use to grant a petition for writ of mandate.

 

There is also one other problem: Petitioner’s conviction that the Cooperative Agreement is voluntary. (Opening Brief, 20:8-21.) However, the Court reads the Cooperative Agreement to have specific requirements: If a party fails to meet its commitment or anticipates an inability to meet its commitment, the party must provide notice and seek to negotiate a mutually agreeable solution within 90 days of the date of the Notice. (AR 12112.) Any dispute not resolved in the normal course of business can be submitted for mediation. (AR 12113.) If a party is still in breach of a Cooperative Agreement, the aggrieved party can seek relief from a federal or state court. (AR 12113.) In sum, the Air District has entered into a contract as is allowed under the law (Health & Safety Code, § 40701, subd. (f)) and that contract allows the Air District to legally enforce the obligation to prepare and implement zero-emission infrastructure plans. This is not a “non-regulatory, toothless agreement.” (Reply, 9:7.)

 

Petitioner argues that a finding that the Air District not comply with the California Clean Air Act would be a literal interpretation that courts should avoid since it would “result in absurd consequences the Legislature did not intend.” (Los Angeles Unified School Dist. v. Superior Court (2023) 14 Cal.5th 758, 768.)

 

The Court is not “finding” that the Air District does not need to comply with the California Clean Air Act. All evidence suggests the Air District has complied with the California Clean Air Act, such as when it adopted the 2016 and 2022 AQMPs. Those documents demonstrated attainment of the NAAQS and the CAAQS as required by the Clean Air Act and the California Clean Air Act. Both attainment demonstrations were approved by the Clean Air Resources Board  and submitted to the Environmental Protection Agency.  (84 Fed. Reg. 28132, 28134 (June 17, 2019); Cal. Air Resources Bd., Res. No. 23–4 (Jan. 26, 2023).)

 

The Court concludes that the Cooperative Agreement does not itself violate any provision of the California Clean Air Act.

 

 

D.    Was there an unlawful abrogation of police powers?

 

Petitioner contends that the Air District surrendered their police powers because it has the exclusive duty to oversee and maintain air pollution control at the Ports, and has agreed “to not pursue any new rulemaking” relating to Port–related mobile sources during the five year term of the Agreement “unless the Cooperative Agreement is terminated before five years.” (Health & Safety Code § 40412, AR 65, Opening Brief, 22:21-23:27.)

 

Land use regulations involve the exercise of the state’s police power, and it is well settled that the government may not contract away its right to exercise the police power in the future. (Tiburon Open Space Committee v. County of Marin (2022) 78 Cal.App.5th 700, 730.) However, a contract is only void when a governmental entity completely surrenders its control of a proper agency function. (Morrison Homes Corp. v. City of Pleasanton (1976) 58 Cal.App.3d 724, 734.)

 

There are several factors a court considers to determine whether a governmental entity has “completely” surrendered control of a proper agency function. A contract might be invalid if it prohibits the government from enacting future regulations or leads to the government incurring monetary consequences as a result of a breach of the contract. (County Mobilehome Positive Action Committee, Inc. v. County of San Diego (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 727, 739-741.)

 

Courts have also identified factors when a governmental agency has not surrendered control of a proper agency function. For example, when a governmental agency has authority to enter into a contract, the contract is developed in accordance with the rules, the contract retains the agency’s discretionary authority, and the governmental agency’s decision to terminate its rule-making authority is not indefinite, the governmental agency has not abrogated its police power. (Santa Margarita Area Residents Together v. San Luis Obispo County (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th, 221, 230-233.)

 

Here, the Court concludes that the Air District has not completely surrendered their rulemaking authority. the Air District has authority to “contract with any federal, state, or local governmental agencies…” to reduce emissions. (Health & Safety Code, § 40701, subd. (f).) The Cooperative Agreement states that any party, including the Air District, can terminate the Agreement “for any reason” on forty-five days’ written notice. (AR 12112.) This indicates the Air District   retains the ability to exit the agreement and immediately pursue rulemaking. Contrary to Petitioner’s reply brief, the Court does not read AR 64 to suggest the Air District has promised not to invoke the exit clause. All AR 64 says is that the Air District does not have an interest to invoke the exit clause unless the Cooperative Agreement is unsuccessful.

 

The Agreement also expressly reserves each party’s future regulatory authority. (AR 12118 [“[n]othing in this Agreement shall be construed as a waiver of any Party's discretionary authority or deemed to restrict authority granted to any Party under law in any way with respect to future legislative, administrative, or other actions”].)

 

There is also no evidence that the Air District will be liable monetary damages associated with any sort of breach of contract claim. The Cooperative Agreement allows the Air District to exit the Agreement for any reason at all.

 

Based on the record, the Court finds that the Air District did not act arbitrarily or capriciously by signing onto the Cooperative Agreement.

 

CONCLUSION

 

            The Court DENIES the petition for writ of mandate.   

 

            Pursuant to Local Rule 3.231, subd. (n), the Air District shall prepare, serve, and ultimately file a proposed judgment.

[Tentative] Order Granting Application for Writ of Possession

 

 

INTRODUCTION

 

Plaintiff Mercedez-Benz Vehicle Trust and Mercedez-Benz Financial Services USA LLC (“Plaintiffs”) move for writ of possession against Defendant Neenu Khemlani and CCIX Holdings, Inc. (collectively, “Defendants”) over the following property: 2022 Mercedes-Benz S580V4 motor Vehicle, Serial No. W1K6G7GB2NA12939. Defendants have not filed an opposition.

 

The Court GRANTS the writ of possession and issues a turnover order.

             

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“Upon the filing of the complaint or at any time thereafter, the plaintiff may apply pursuant to this chapter for a writ of possession by filing a written application for the writ with the court in which the action is brought.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 512.010, subd. (a).)

           

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 512.010, subd. (b), the application must be submitted under oath and include:

 

(1) A showing of the basis of the plaintiff's claim and that the plaintiff is entitled to possession of the property claimed. If the basis of the plaintiff's claim is a written instrument, a copy of the instrument shall be attached.

 

(2) A showing that the property is wrongfully detained by the defendant, of the manner in which the defendant came into possession of the property, and, according to the best knowledge, information, and belief of the plaintiff, of the reason for the detention.

 

(3) A particular description of the property and a statement of its value.

 

(4) A statement, according to the best knowledge, information, and belief of the plaintiff, of the location of the property and, if the property, or some part of it, is within a private place which may have to be entered to take possession, a showing that there is probable cause to believe that such property is located there.

 

(5) A statement that the property has not been taken for a tax, assessment, or fine, pursuant to a statute; or seized under an execution against the property of the plaintiff; or, if so seized, that it is by statute exempt from such seizure.

 

Before the hearing on the Writ of Possession, the Defendant must be served with (1) a copy of the summons and complaint; (2) a Notice of Application and Hearing; and (3) a copy of the application and any affidavit in support thereof.  (Code Civ. Pro. § 512.030.)

 

“The writ will be issued if the court finds that the plaintiff's claim is probably valid and the other requirements for issuing the writ are established.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 512.040(b).)  “A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 511.090.) 

 

DISCUSSION

 

A.                Notice

 

Plaintiffs provided proper notice, per the proofs of service filed on July 14, 2026.

 

B.                   Probable Validity of Plaintiff’s Claim

 

Plaintiffs seek writs of possession based on its cause of action for (1) breach of contract; (2) breach of guaranty; (3) Claim & Delivery; and (4) conversion.

 

On or about November 29, 2022, Defendants took possession of a Motor Vehicle, according to the terms of a Motor Vehicle Lease Agreement (“Agreement”). (Rutherford Decl., ¶ 11.) CCIX agreed to pay for the use of the Vehicle in 39 consecutive base monthly lease payments in the sum of $2,115.50 plus taxes, commencing on November 29, 2022, and the remaining payments due on the 29th day of each month until the expiration of the Agreement on February 28, 2026. (Rutherford Decl., ¶ 12.)

 

Plaintiffs in this action received an interest in the car by assignment from the dealer. (Rutherford Decl., ¶ 14.) Plaintiffs have performed all of the conditions, covenants, and promises required under the terms of the Agreement. (Rutherford Decl., ¶ 16.)

 

On or about July 29, 2025, Defendants CCIX and Khemlani did not make the required monthly payment. (Rutherford Decl., ¶ 17.) Plaintiffs elected to accelerate the terms of the Agreement and declare all unpaid sums immediately due and payable. (Rutherford Decl., ¶ 17.) Defendants failed to return possession of the Vehicle, despite Plaintiffs requesting payment and the Vehicle itself. (Rutherford Decl., ¶¶ 17-19.)

 

There is due, owing, and unpaid from Defendants the sum of $89,749.90 plus the total monthly payment of $2,316.47 for each month after February 28, 2026. (Rutherford Decl., ¶ 18.)

 

The balance due suggests Plaintiffs will prevail on the claim.

 

C.                 Wrongful Detention

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 512.010, subd. (b)(2), the application must include “a showing that the property is wrongfully detained by the defendant, of the manner in which the defendant came into possession of the property, and, according to the best knowledge, information, and belief of the plaintiff, of the reason for the detention.”  Plaintiffs have made this showing.   

 

D.                Description and Value of Property

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 512.010, subd. (b)(3), the application must include a particular description of the property and a statement of its value.  Plaintiffs have provided a particular description of the property, by make and serial number.

 

 Plaintiffs therefore satisfies section 512.010, subd. (b)(3).

 

E.                 Statutory Statement

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 512.010, subd. (b)(5), Plaintiffs must provide a statement that the property has not been taken for a tax, assessment, or fine, pursuant to statute and has not been seized under an execution against the Plaintiff’s property. The application includes this statement. (Application, ¶ 8a.)

 

F.                  Location of the Property

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 512.010, subd. (b)(4), Plaintiffs must identify the probable location of the Vehicle and establish probable cause to that effect.

 

The Agreement states that after default, Defendants have consented and authorized Plaintiffs to obtain data from the vehicle including location of the vehicle. (Rutherford Decl., ¶ 21.) The Vehicle was equipped with an electronic tracking device, which revealed the location as 315 S. Las Palmas Ave., Los Angeles, CA 90020. (Ibid.)

 

That address is also Defendants’ billing address on file, and the United States Postal Service has confirmed that Defendants currently receive mail at that address. (Rutherford Decl., ¶ 23.)

 

Accordingly, the Court concludes that Plaintiffs have established probable cause as to the location of the Vehicle.

 

G.                Undertaking. 

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 515.010 requires an undertaking to be filed before the writ issues in the amount of “not less than twice the value of the defendant’s interest in the property.”  Section 515.010, subd. (a) states that the value of the defendant’s interest “is determined by the market value of the property less the amount due and owing on any conditional sales contract or security agreement and all liens and encumbrances on the property, and any other factors necessary to determine the defendant’s interest in the property.”

 

However, Section 515.010, subd. (b) says that “[i]f the Court finds that the Defendant has no interest in the property, the court shall waive the requirement of the Plaintiff’s undertaking. Instead, Section 515.010, subd. (b) requires Defendant to post an undertaking in an amount equal to the maximum retail value.

 

Plaintiffs have identified the value of the property as $63,340.00. (Rutherford Decl., ¶ 24.) There is no evidence that Defendants have any interest in the property, so the Court waives the requirement of Plaintiffs’ undertaking. Instead, Defendants must post an undertaking in an amount equal to the maximum retail value – so $63,340.00.

 

H.                Turnover Order

 

Section 512.070 states: “If a writ of possession is issued, the court may also issue an order directing the defendant to transfer possession of the property to the plaintiff. Such order shall contain a notice to the defendant that failure to turn over possession of such property to plaintiff may subject the defendant to being held in contempt of court.”  (emphasis added.) “Thus a ‘turnover’ order, issued pursuant to section 512.070, is not a separate remedy but rather an alternative means of enforcing a writ of possession.”  (Edwards v. Sup.Ct. (1991) 230 Cal.App.3d 173, 178.)

 

The Court also issues a turnover order.

 

I.                    Oral Evidence

 

“A party seeking permission to introduce oral evidence, except for oral evidence in rebuttal to oral evidence presented by the other party, must file, no later than three court days before the hearing, a written statement stating the nature and extent of the evidence proposed to be introduced and a reasonable time estimate for the hearing.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1306, subd. (b).) 

 

Plaintiffs have not complied with this rule and have not requested oral evidence.

 

CONCLUSION 

 

The Court GRANTS the writ of possession and issues a turnover order.
