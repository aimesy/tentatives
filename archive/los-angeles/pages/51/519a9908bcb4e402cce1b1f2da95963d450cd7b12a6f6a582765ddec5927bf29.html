Case Number: 24NNCV06167    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING PLAINTIFF YEELING KO’S APPLICATION FOR ORDER TO SHOW CAUSE RE CONTEMPT

I.          INTRODUCTION

            This case concerns a dispute between the two shareholders and officers of Calpack International, Inc. Plaintiff Yeeling Ko (“Plaintiff”), Calpack’s majority shareholder and CEO, filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging that Defendant Gordon Iu (“Defendant”), the company’s minority shareholder, Secretary, and CFO, wrongfully excluded her from control over the business, financial records, and corporate assets. The FAC asserts causes of action for breach of contract, breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, conversion, fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, and accounting.

On April 23, 2025, following an ex parte hearing, the Court issued a Temporary Protective Order ("TPO") prohibiting Defendant from transferring any funds from Calpack's bank accounts. On July 14, 2025, the Court granted Plaintiff's motion for a preliminary injunction, continuing that prohibition pending final judgment. Plaintiff now seeks an order holding Defendant in contempt, alleging Defendant violated the Court's TPO and Preliminary Injunction by making transfers from Calpack's bank account. Plaintiff filed the instant application on April 20, 2026. Defendant filed an opposition on April 29, 2026. Plaintiff thereafter filed supplemental affidavits on May 4, 2026, and June 25, 2026.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

            Code of Civil Procedure section 128, subdivision (a)(4), provides that every court has the power "[t]o compel obedience to its judgments, orders, and process." Code of Civil Procedure section 1209, subdivision (a)(5), provides that the "[d]isobedience of any lawful judgment, order, or process of the court" constitutes contempt of court. Where the alleged contempt is indirect—that is, not committed in the immediate view and presence of the court—the proceeding must be initiated by an affidavit or declaration setting forth the facts constituting the contempt. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1211, subd. (a).)

At the contempt hearing, the court considers the alleged contemnor's answer and the evidence presented and determines whether contempt has been established. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1218, subd. (a).) If the court finds the person guilty of contempt, it may impose a fine not exceeding $1,000, imprisonment not exceeding five days, or both, for each separate act of contempt. (Ibid.)

To establish indirect contempt, the moving party must prove: (1) the existence of a lawful court order; (2) the alleged contemnor's knowledge of the order; (3) the ability to comply with the order; and (4) willful disobedience of the order. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1209; People v. Gonzalez (1996) 12 Cal.4th 804, 816-823.) 

III.      ANALYSIS

            Plaintiff seeks an order holding Defendant in contempt for allegedly violating this Court's April 23, 2025, Temporary Protective Order and July 14, 2025, Preliminary Injunction by transferring funds from Calpack International Inc.'s Chase bank account on seven separate occasions. Plaintiff contends Defendant knowingly violated the Court's orders by making the following transfers: (1) $138.92 on April 23, 2025; (2) $6,222.51 on August 8, 2025; (3) $140.26 on December 26, 2025; (4) $100.00 on January 5, 2026; (5) $2,684.05 on January 14, 2026; (6) $203.88 on January 26, 2026; and (7) $100.00 on February 24, 2026. (Motion, pp. 2-3.) Defendant does not dispute that the transactions occurred, but contends they were either returns of mistaken deposits or automatic deductions for legitimate business expenses, and therefore did not constitute willful violations of the Court's orders. (Opposition, pp. 2-5.)

Existence of a lawful court order

This first element is satisfied. The Court issued a TPO prohibiting Defendant from transferring any money from Calpack's Chase bank account or any other Calpack bank account. Thereafter, on July 14, 2025, the Court entered a Preliminary Injunction containing the same prohibition, which remained in effect pending final judgment. Neither party disputes the existence of these orders.

Knowledge of the orders

The evidence likewise establishes Defendant's knowledge of the Court's orders. The Temporary Protective Order was prepared by Defendant's then-counsel and notice of entry was filed the same day it was issued. The Preliminary Injunction was likewise served on Defendant's counsel on July 14, 2025. Defendant does not contend he lacked notice of either order.

Ability to comply

The evidence also demonstrates Defendant had the ability to comply with the Court's orders. Plaintiff's evidence indicates Defendant exclusively controlled Calpack's Chase bank account and had sole authority over transfers from the account. Defendant does not argue he lacked the ability to comply with the Court's injunctions.

Willful disobedience 

In this case, the dispute concerns whether Defendant’s transfers constituted willful violations of the Court’s orders.

With respect to the April 23, 2025, transfer of $138.92 and the August 8, 2025, transfer of $6,222.51, Defendant asserts the transactions were returns of funds mistakenly deposited into Calpack's bank account by third parties, Gargo Food and BF Suma, respectively. (Lee Aff., ¶ 6.) In support, Defendant submitted documents reflecting the alleged mistaken deposits and return transactions. (Lee Aff., Exhs. C, D.) Plaintiff subsequently filed a supplemental affidavit concerning the August 2025 transfer after obtaining additional information from BF Suma. Plaintiff's counsel declares that BF Suma's Office Manager, Wendy Cheung, provided email communications indicating that Gordon Iu instructed BF Suma to remit payment for a July 2025 Calpack sale to an account designated as General Trust Investments; BF Suma initially transmitted payment to Calpack's Chase account, requested return of those funds after realizing the payment had been sent to the wrong account, and thereafter reissued payment to the General Trust Investments account. Plaintiff further submits that Wendy Cheung confirmed Plaintiff's understanding of those events and later provided screenshots reflecting the initial payment, return of funds, and reissued payment. (6/25/26 Supp. Aff., ¶¶ 4-9 & Exhs. A-B.)

As to the January 14, 2026, transfer of $2,684.05, Defendant contends the transaction was a State of California filing or processing fee rather than a manual transfer initiated for personal purposes. (Lee Aff., ¶¶ 2-4 & Exh. A.) Plaintiff thereafter submitted a supplemental affidavit attaching a California Department of Tax and Fee Administration Statement of Account and Payment Voucher. Plaintiff contends the document reflects only two methods of payment—online payment through the agency's website or payment by check—and therefore argues the payment required affirmative action rather than occurring by automatic withdrawal. (5/4/26 Supp. Aff; Exh. B.)

Regarding the remaining transfers—$140.26 on December 26, 2025, $100.00 on January 5, 2026, $203.88 on January 26, 2026, and $100.00 on February 24, 2026—Defendant explains that the transactions consisted of recurring GoDaddy website charges and State of California or Chase processing fees incurred in connection with Calpack's corporate obligations. Gordon Iu provided those explanations in an email to counsel dated April 15, 2026. Counsel declares that, although Mr. Iu instructed him to forward the email to Plaintiff's counsel, counsel inadvertently replied only to Mr. Iu and did not transmit the explanations until after Plaintiff filed the contempt application. (Lee Aff., ¶¶ 2-5; Exhs. A-B.)

The Court finds Plaintiff has established that the August 8, 2025, transfer of $6,222.51 constituted willful violations of the Court's orders. While Defendant characterizes transactions as returns of mistaken deposits, Plaintiff's supplemental evidence regarding the August 2025 transfer indicates the funds were returned so that payment could be reissued to an account designated by Defendant rather than Calpack's account.  Defendant submitted no supplemental declaration or other evidence rebutting Plaintiff's subsequently obtained evidence. Under these circumstances, the Court finds Defendant's explanations insufficient to overcome Plaintiff's showing that the April 23, 2025, and August 8, 2025, transfers were willful violations of the Court's orders.

As to the remaining alleged violations, the Court is not persuaded that Plaintiff has established willful disobedience. Defendant provided explanations that these transactions were recurring GoDaddy charges and State of California or Chase processing fees, and that the January 14, 2026, transaction was a State of California filing or processing fee. Although Plaintiff disputes those explanations, the evidence presented does not establish that these transactions were willful violations of the Court's orders.

Plaintiff has established that Defendant willfully violated the Court's orders. Therefore, the Court finds Defendant Gordon Iu in contempt.

IV.       CONCLUSION

            The Court GRANTS Plaintiff's application for contempt. The Court will determine the nature and extent of the sanction at the hearing.

            Plaintiff is to give notice.

Case Number: 24NNCV06446    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING PLAINTIFFS MOTION FOR RELIEF FROM DEEMED ADMISSIONS

I.          INTRODUCTION

Plaintiffs Gohar Bosnuyan, Eric Vivanco-Perez and David Bosnuyan, a minor, by Gohar Bosnuyan as his guardian ad litem (“Plaintiffs”), bring this action against Defendants Hyland Inn Near Pasadena Civic Center; RVN Inc. and Vimal Patel (“Defendants”), alleging causes of action for (1) battery, (2) negligence, (3) intentional infliction of emotional distress, (4) fraudulent concealment, (5) private nuisance, (6) public nuisance, and (7) breach of contract. Plaintiffs allege they were knowingly assigned to a hotel room infested with bedbugs, which caused physical and emotional injuries, property damage, and other harm.

On June 2, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion for relief from deemed admissions. On June 12, 2026, Defendants filed an opposition. No reply has been filed.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

[T]he court, on motion, may relieve that party from this waiver on its determination that party who fails to timely respond to discovery waives any objections, including those based on privilege. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.300, subd. (a) [RFPs], 2030.290 [interrogatories].) The Court may grant relief from waiver if (1) the responding party has subsequently served a response that is in substantial compliance; and (2) the failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.300, subd. (a) [RFPs], 2030.290 [interrogatories].) 

In determining what constitutes “mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect” under the Discovery Act, courts apply the same or similar standards as for relief from default under C.C.P. § 473(b). (City of Fresno v. Superior Court (1988) 205 Cal.App.3d 1459, 1467.) Generally, the standard of what constitutes “mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect,” is fact dependent.¿ (See City of Fresno, 205 Cal.App.3d at pgs. 1466-1467.) 

“The ‘excusable neglect’ referred to in the section is that neglect which might have been the act of a reasonably prudent person under the same circumstances. [Citation.]”  (Baratti v. Baratti (1952) 109 Cal.App.2d 917, 921.) In deciding whether counsel’s error is excusable, courts look to “(1) the nature of the mistake or neglect[] and (2) whether counsel was otherwise diligent in investigating and pursuing the claim.” (Tackett v. City of Huntington Beach (1994) 22 Cal.App.4th 60, 65, citing Bettencourt v. Los Rios Community College District (1986) 42 Cal.3d 270, 276.) In determining whether the attorney’s mistake or inadvertence was excusable, “the court inquires whether ‘a reasonably prudent person under the same or similar circumstances’ might have made the same error.” (Bettencourt, 42 Cal.3d at pg. 276.

III.      ANALYSIS

            Plaintiffs seek relief from the deemed admissions arising from Defendants' Requests for Admissions, Set One, and request that the Court permit the responses they served on January 27, 2026, to serve as the operative responses. Plaintiffs argue their failure to timely respond was the result of mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. They contend they have since served substantive responses and that Defendants will suffer no substantial prejudice because they have the responses and remain able to litigate the case on the merits. Alternatively, Plaintiffs seek discretionary relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473(b). (Motion, pp. 1-7.)

Defendants oppose the motion, arguing Plaintiffs have failed to establish that the deemed admissions resulted from mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Defendants contend Plaintiffs repeatedly failed to respond despite receiving numerous extensions, that the January 27, 2026, responses were unverified and therefore ineffective, and that Plaintiffs have not otherwise demonstrated entitlement to relief under Code of Civil Procedure sections 2033.300, 473(b), or 1008. (Opposition, pp. 3-5.)

The Court concludes that relief is not warranted under Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.300. Although Plaintiffs subsequently served responses to the Requests for Admissions, the record does not sufficiently establish that the failure to timely respond was the result of mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Counsel's declaration fails to explain the nature of the alleged mistake or why Plaintiffs did not timely serve responses despite receiving multiple extensions. Instead, the declaration merely parrots the statutory language, stating that the failure resulted from "mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect," without providing any factual basis for that assertion. 

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiffs have not established that their failure to timely respond resulted from mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Therefore, Plaintiffs have not made the requisite showing for relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.300.

Even assuming Plaintiffs had established excusable neglect, the Court notes that the January 27, 2026, responses Plaintiffs seek to have deemed operative were unverified. Under California law, unverified responses to requests for admissions are tantamount to no responses at all. (Appleton v. Superior Court (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 632, 636.) Therefore, the January 27, 2026, responses do not constitute effective responses under the Discovery Act.

IV.       ORDER

             The Court DENIES Plaintiffs motion for relief from deemed admissions.

Plaintiffs are to give notice.

Case Number: 24PDUD03208    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR ATTORNEYS’ FEES

I.          INTRODUCTION

            This matter arises from an unlawful detainer action concerning residential property located at 840 E. Green Street, Unit 122, Pasadena, California. Plaintiff Alana Johnson (“Plaintiff”) initiated the underlying action against Defendant Michael Saeedi (“Defendant”), which was voluntarily dismissed on December 19, 2024. Defendant thereafter moved for attorney's fees, and on April 25, 2025, the Court denied the motion. 

Defendant appealed, and on March 9, 2026, the Appellate Division affirmed the Court's order denying Defendant's motion for attorney's fees and awarded Plaintiff costs on appeal. Following issuance of the remittitur on May 18, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion seeking $5,402 in attorney's fees incurred in defending the appeal. Defendant filed an opposition on June 18, 2026, and Plaintiff filed a reply on July 1, 2026

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

A motion seeking attorney fees incurred on appeal under a contract must be served and filed within the time for serving and filing a memorandum of costs following issuance of the remittitur. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1702(c)(1); rule 8.891(c)(1).) In a limited civil appeal, the prevailing party is the respondent where the Appellate Division affirms the judgment without modification, and the clerk must include the award of costs in the remittitur. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.891(a)(2), (b)(1).)

III.      ANALYSIS

Plaintiff moves for an award of $5,402.00 in attorney fees incurred in successfully defending Defendant's appeal. Plaintiff contends the parties' lease contains a prevailing party attorney fee provision entitling the prevailing party to recover reasonable attorney fees in any action arising out of the agreement. Plaintiff further argues that because contractually authorized attorney fees are recoverable as costs, such fees may be sought in the trial court following issuance of the remittitur. Plaintiff also notes the Appellate Division affirmed the trial court's order, awarded Plaintiff costs on appeal, and the remittitur issued on May 18, 2026.

Plaintiff further contends the requested fees are reasonable under the lodestar method. Plaintiff submits declarations from Harold Greenberg and Jenifer Anisman stating that counsel expended 9.6 hours defending the appeal and anticipate an additional 2.0 hours preparing a reply and appearing at the hearing. Counsel declare that Mr. Greenberg's hourly rate was $495 in 2025 and $505 in 2026, while Ms. Anisman's hourly rate was $445 through January 31, 2026, and $465 thereafter.  Based on these hours and rates, Plaintiff seeks a total award of $5,402.00 in appellate attorney fees. (Greenberg Decl., ¶¶ 2-10.)

Defendant opposes the motion, arguing Plaintiff is not entitled to recover appellate attorney fees because the lease agreement bars recovery where a party refuses to participate in mediation after a request is made. (Opposition, pp. 5-6.)  Defendant contends Plaintiff rejected multiple pre-litigation requests for mediation and therefore forfeited any contractual right to attorney fees under paragraph 39A of the lease. Defendant further argues Plaintiff failed to establish that the requested fees were reasonably and necessarily incurred because Plaintiff did not file a respondent's brief, performed only limited work during the appeal, and submitted heavily redacted billing records that allegedly prevent meaningful review of the requested fees. (Opposition, pp.7-8.) Alternatively, Defendant requests that any fee award be substantially reduced. (Id. at 10.)

The Court finds Plaintiff is entitled to recover reasonable appellate attorney fees. The parties' lease contains a prevailing party attorney fee provision, the Appellate Division affirmed the order denying Defendant's motion for attorney fees, awarded Plaintiff costs on appeal, and the remittitur issued on May 18, 2026. Therefore, Plaintiff is entitled to seek contractual attorney fees incurred on appeal in this Court. 

Defendant's reliance on the lease's mediation provision is unavailing. Although Defendant argues Plaintiff forfeited any right to attorney fees by declining pre-litigation mediation, the lease expressly excludes unlawful detainer actions from the mediation requirement. Specifically, Paragraph 39B provides: "The following matters are excluded from mediation: (I) an unlawful detainer action." (See Reply, p. 2; Exh. 4, ¶ 39B.) Thus, the mediation provision does not preclude Plaintiff’s recovery to reasonable appellate attorney fees.

The Court further finds the amount requested to be reasonable. Plaintiff submits declarations and billing records establishing that counsel expended 9.6 hours defending the appeal, with an anticipated additional two hours preparing the instant motion and appearing at the hearing, at hourly rates ranging from $445 to $505. The Court finds both the hours expended and the hourly rates reasonable. The Court GRANTS Plaintiff's motion for appellate attorney fees in the amount of $5,402.00.

            IV.       CONCLUSION

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff's motion for attorneys’ fees, and awards $5,402.00. Defendant is ordered to pay these fees within 60 days of this order.

Plaintiff is to give notice.

Case Number: 25NNCV06644    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO SET ASIDE DEFAULTS

I.          INTRODUCTION

            This insurance coverage action arises from a motor vehicle collision that occurred on August 19, 2024. Plaintiff State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Company ("Plaintiff") filed this action for declaratory relief on September 22, 2025, seeking a judicial determination regarding insurance coverage arising from the accident. Defendants Arshak Pogosian and Zarui Khorozyan (“Defendants”) were named as defendants in the action. Defaults were entered against Defendants on April 30, 2026.

On June 9, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion to set aside the defaults pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473(b). On June 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On June 29, 2026, Defendants filed a reply.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) provides for either discretionary or mandatory relief from certain prior actions or proceedings in the trial court. (Luri v. Greenwald (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 1119, 1124.)

“‘Under the discretionary relief provision, on a showing of “mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect,” the court has discretion to allow relief from a “judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against” a party or his or her attorney. Under the mandatory relief provision, on the other hand, upon a showing by attorney declaration of “mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect,” the court shall vacate any “resulting default judgment or dismissal entered.”’ [Citation.] Applications seeking relief under the mandatory provision of section 473 must be ‘accompanied by an attorney’s sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.’ The mandatory provision further adds that ‘whenever relief is granted based on an attorney’s affidavit of fault [the court shall] direct the attorney to pay reasonable compensatory legal fees and costs to opposing counsel or parties.’” (Ibid.; Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)

Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 473, subd. (b).)

            III.      DISCUSSION

Defendants move for relief from the defaults entered against them pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473(b), contending the defaults resulted from the mistake, inadvertence, and excusable neglect of their counsel. Defendants rely on the declaration of attorney Stephan Airapetian, who accepts responsibility for the defaults and submits a proposed answer. (Airapetian Decl.; Exh. 6.)

Specifically, Airapetian declares that although his office became aware of Plaintiff's declaratory relief action, his firm failed to monitor the action and failed to ensure that a responsive pleading was timely filed. He explains that there was a miscommunication between his office and Defendants regarding who would contact GEICO and who would be responsible for responding to the complaint. Counsel further states that he believed Defendants were communicating with GEICO and that GEICO would provide a defense and retain counsel to respond to the action. Because of that mistaken belief, his office did not monitor the response deadline or file an answer, resulting in the entry of defaults. Counsel expressly accepts responsibility for allowing the defaults to be entered. Thereafter, GEICO advised counsel that it would not provide a defense, seek to set aside the defaults, or file a responsive pleading on Defendants' behalf. (Airapetian Decl., ¶¶ 7, 12-13.)

Plaintiff argues Defendants have failed to establish entitlement to relief because counsel's declaration consists only of vague and conclusory assertions of "miscommunication" and does not adequately explain the mistake or excusable neglect that caused the defaults. The Court disagrees.

The motion was filed within six months after entry of the defaults and is accompanied by counsel's sworn declaration accepting responsibility for the failure to timely respond to the complaint. Contrary to Plaintiff's contention, counsel does more than merely recite the statutory language. Counsel identifies the specific circumstances that led to the defaults—namely, his mistaken belief that Defendants would communicate with GEICO, that GEICO would undertake Defendants' defense, and his firm's resulting failure to monitor the litigation and ensure that a responsive pleading was filed. Counsel expressly acknowledges that these failures constituted his mistake and accepts responsibility for the defaults. (Airapetian Decl., ¶¶ 7, 13.)

Section 473(b)'s mandatory relief provision is intended to protect litigants from the consequences of their attorney's errors. Where, as here, a timely application is accompanied by an attorney's sworn affidavit admitting fault, the Court is required to vacate the default unless it finds the default was not in fact caused by the attorney's mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect. The record reflects that the defaults were the direct result of counsel's failure to monitor the action and timely file a responsive pleading while mistakenly believing GEICO would assume the defense. There is no evidence that Defendants themselves intentionally failed to respond or that counsel's admitted errors were unrelated to the entry of default.

Further, Plaintiff has not demonstrated any prejudice that would result from setting aside the defaults. No default judgment has been entered, Defendants have submitted a proposed answer, and permitting the matter to proceed on the merits is consistent with California's strong public policy favoring resolution of cases on their merits whenever reasonably possible.

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS Defendants motion to set aside defaults.

IV.       CONCLUSION AND ORDER

            The Court GRANTS Defendants’ motion to set aside defaults. Defendants are to file and serve their proposed answer within 20 days of this ruling.

            Defendants are to give notice.

Case Number: 26NNCV03788    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING MOTION FOR APPOINTMENT OF RECEIVER AND PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION IN AID OF RECEIVER

I.          INTRODUCTION

On May 13, 2026, Plaintiff Wilmington Trust, National Association, as Trustee for the benefit of the registered holders of GS Mortgage Securities Trust 2021-GSA3, Commercial Mortgage Pass-Through Certificates, Series 2021-GSA3 ("Plaintiff"), filed this action against Defendant 403 S. Raymond Avenue, LLC, a Delaware limited liability company ("Defendant"), arising from Defendant's alleged default under a commercial loan secured by real property located at 403-421 South Raymond Avenue, Pasadena, California.

On June 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion seeking appointment of a receiver and a preliminary injunction in aid of the receivership. In support of the motion, Plaintiff submitted the declarations of Jackson Kunasek and proposed receiver Chris Neilson. No opposition was filed.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Receivers are agents of the court and may be appointed only where authorized by statute. (Marsch v. Williams (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 238, 246.) The appointment of a receiver is a drastic equitable remedy that should be exercised with caution and only in exceptional cases where no other adequate remedy exists. (City & County of San Francisco v. Daley (1993) 16 Cal.App.4th 734, 744.) The party seeking appointment of a receiver bears the burden of establishing by a preponderance of the evidence that a receivership is necessary. (Alhambra-Shumway Mines, Inc. v. Alhambra Gold Mine Corp. (1953) 116 Cal.App.2d 869, 873.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 564, subdivision (b)(2), authorizes the appointment of a receiver in an action by a secured lender to foreclose a deed of trust or mortgage where it appears that the property is in danger of being lost, removed, or materially injured, or that the conditions of the deed of trust or mortgage have not been performed and the property is probably insufficient to discharge the secured debt.

III.      ANALYSIS

            Plaintiff moves for an order appointing a receiver for the subject Property and issuing a preliminary injunction in aid of the receivership.

Receiver

The evidence establishes that Defendant executed a $12,850,000 commercial loan in favor of the original lender, evidenced by a Loan Agreement and Promissory Note and secured by a Deed of Trust and Assignment of Leases and Rents encumbering the subject Property. (Kunasek Decl., ¶¶ 2-6.) Plaintiff further submits evidence demonstrating that it is the current holder of the Loan, beneficiary under the Deed of Trust, and assignee of the Loan Documents through a series of recorded assignments. (Kunasek Decl., ¶¶ 7-10; Exhs. F-N.)

Plaintiff also submits evidence establishing that Events of Default occurred under the Loan Documents. Specifically, Kunasek declares that Defendant failed to make the required monthly loan payments beginning March 6, 2026. (Kunasek Decl., ¶ 11.) The recorded Notice of Default likewise states that Defendant failed to make the installment of interest, tax and insurance escrows, and reserves due on March 6, 2026, and all subsequent installments. (Kunasek Decl., ¶ 16 & Exh. P.) Kunasek further declares that Defendant failed to maintain the required insurance on the Property. (Kunasek Decl., ¶ 12 & Exh. Q.) Plaintiff also submits declaration testimony that Defendant continues to collect rents into a clearing account rather than the contractually required cash management account. (Kunasek Decl., ¶ 11.)

Plaintiff further relies on Section 7.1(g) of the Deed of Trust, which provides that upon the occurrence of an Event of Default, Plaintiff is entitled to seek appointment of a receiver without notice and without regard to the adequacy of the security or Defendant's solvency. (Complaint., Exh. D.) Plaintiff argues that this contractual remedy should be enforced absent extraordinary circumstances.

Plaintiff also submits the declaration of proposed receiver Chris Neilson, who declares that he has extensive experience serving as a court-appointed receiver in commercial real estate matters, is willing to serve in this matter, and will obtain any bond required by the Court. (Neilson Decl., ¶¶ 1-7.)

            No opposition has been filed.

The Court agrees. Plaintiff has submitted evidence establishing Events of Default under the Loan Documents, and the Deed of Trust expressly authorizes Plaintiff to seek appointment of a receiver upon default. (Complaint., Exh. D.) Plaintiff has therefore established both statutory grounds under Code of Civil Procedure section 564 and a contractual basis for appointment of a receiver.

Thus, the Court GRANTS the motion for appointment of receiver. Chris Neilson of Trigild IVL is appointed receiver pursuant to the terms of the Court's order and shall post a receiver's bond in the amount of $165,000 before assuming his duties.

Injunction

Here, Plaintiff requests an injunction prohibiting Defendant from interfering with the receiver's operation and management of the Property.

Because the Court finds appointment of a receiver is warranted, the requested preliminary injunction is likewise appropriate to preserve the status quo and ensure the receiver is able to carry out the duties authorized by the Court. The injunction is limited to prohibiting Defendant from interfering with the receiver's possession, management, operation, and control of the Property pending resolution of this action.

Undertaking requirement

Code of Civil Procedure section 529, subdivision (a), provides that upon granting a preliminary injunction, "the court or judge must require an undertaking on the part of the applicant" to secure any damages sustained by the enjoined party if it is ultimately determined that the applicant was not entitled to the injunction. (Code Civ. Proc., § 529, subd. (a).) The statutory exceptions to the undertaking requirement set forth in subdivision (b) do not include receivership proceedings. (Code Civ. Proc., § 529, subd. (b).).

Here, Plaintiff requests that the Court waive the undertaking requirement, arguing that Defendant is unlikely to suffer interim harm because the requested injunction merely requires Defendant to cooperate with the receiver and refrain from interfering with the receiver's duties. (Motion, p. 12.) The Court declines to waive the undertaking requirement. Code of Civil Procedure section 529 mandates that an undertaking be posted unless a statutory exception applies, and no such exception is applicable here. Plaintiff shall therefore post an undertaking pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 529. Given that the requested injunction is limited in scope and ancillary to the appointment of a receiver, and that Defendant has neither opposed the motion, nor presented any evidence of potential damages that would result if the injunction were later determined to have been wrongfully issued, the Court finds that a undertaking of $20,000 is sufficient to secure any reasonably foreseeable damages, including attorney's fees and incidental costs.

IV.       ORDER

            The Court GRANTS Plaintiffs motion for appointment of receiver and preliminary injunction in aid of receiver.

The appointed receiver shall post a $165,000 receivers’ bond within 20 days of this order. Plaintiff shall post an undertaking of $20,00 within 20 days’ notice of this order.

Plaintiff is to give notice.
