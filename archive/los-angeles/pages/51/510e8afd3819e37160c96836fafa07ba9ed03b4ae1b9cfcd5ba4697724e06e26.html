[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: PLAINTIFF MICHAEL BRIAN SMITH’S MOTION TO SET ASIDE/VACATE JUDGMENT [CRS #8855] Dept. 731 8:30 a.m. July 21, 2026

Moving Party: Plaintiff, Michael Brian Smith

Opposing Party: Defendant, Michael Kors Inc.

Notice: OK

Plaintiff Michael B. Smith brought this suit against his former employer, Defendant Michael Kors Inc., alleging race discrimination in his employment and a subsequent unlawful layoff.

On March 26, 2026, the Court granted a motion for judgment on the pleadings filed by Defendant, which disposed of the then-operative March 19, 2024, Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”). Plaintiff filed a motion for reconsideration on March 30, 2026, which was opposed by Defendant, and was ultimately denied by the Court on May 20, 2026. The Court entered judgment in Defendant’s favor that same day.

Now before the Court is Plaintiff’s motion to set aside/vacate the May 20, 2026, judgment on the grounds that another action affecting the outcome of the proceedings before this Court is purportedly presently pending before the Compton, California branch of the Los Angeles Superior Court as 22CMCV00753, Smith v. Michael Kors Inc. (the “Santa Monica action”). (See Motion, p. 2, ¶ 4.)

The motion is DENIED, with prejudice.

Plaintiff’s motion is noticed as being premised on Code of Civil Procedure sections 473, subdivision (d), and 1008, subdivisions (a), (b), and (e). (Motion, pp. 1-2.) Both premises fail.

As argued in Defendant’s opposition, there is no applicable provision of Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d), here. (See Opposition pp. 11-12.) Plaintiff is asking that the Court vacate a final judgment based on the purported pendency of the Santa Monica action. This is not a valid ground for relief under section 473, subdivision (d), which is limited to clerical errors and void judgments and orders, including those voided by extrinsic fraud or mistake, neither of which Plaintiff’s paper show. (See Kramer v. Traditional Escrow, Inc. (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 13, 29 [discussing § 473, subd. (d)].)

Similarly, none of the grounds for party-requested reconsideration—new or different facts, circumstances, or law (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a))—appear here, as the requested relief is based on the purported pendency of the Santa Monica action rather than some fact, circumstance, or new law relating to this action.

Last, the Court takes judicial notice of Exhibit A attached to Defendant’s opposition, which shows a June 18, 2026, minute order for the Santa Monica action, in which the court in that case denied a similar motion by Plaintiff in this case to vacate or set aside the judgment in that case, which was entered on November 6, 2025. (Opposition, Request for Judicial Notice, Ex. A, pp. 6-7.)

Plaintiff’s motion is accordingly DENIED. No further motions seeking identical relief shall be entertained absent a proper statutory basis.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION OF AFFIRMATIVE DEFENSES

 

Dept. 731

September 22, 2026

8:30 a.m.

Matter No 20

 

 

Moving Party:               Plaintiff, McMurray Henricks, LLP

Opposing Party:           None

Notice:                         OK

 

  1. Factual Background

This action arises from an alleged campaign of extortion and libel following the conclusion of a prior civil rights representation.

    1. Pleadings

Plaintiffs McMurray Henriks, LLP and attorney Yana G. Henriks bring claims for Civil Extortion and Libel Per Se against Defendant Charmain Campbell, a certified court reporter and former client, seeking monetary damages and injunctive relief. The Complaint alleges that Campbell threatened to harm Plaintiffs’ business reputation, file knowingly false State Bar complaints, post defamatory online reviews, and otherwise interfere with Plaintiffs’ professional standing unless Plaintiffs paid additional settlement funds beyond those owed under the parties’ retainer agreement. Plaintiffs contend the posted Yelp review falsely accused them of mishandling Campbell’s case, misrepresenting retainer terms, withholding settlement proceeds, and racially discriminating against Campbell and her family.

b.     Motion Before the Court

Now before the Court is Plaintiff’s motion for summary adjudication of two of the affirmative defenses asserted in Defendant Campbell’s Answer to Plaintiffs’ Complaint: (1) Defendant’s Second Affirmative Defense (Truth/Substantial Truth); and (2) Defendant’s Third Affirmative Defense (Opinion / Protected Consumer Commentary).

 

  1. Motion for Summary Adjudication

a.     Legal Standard

A party may also seek summary adjudication of select causes of action, affirmative defenses, claims for damages, or issues of duty, which may be made by a standalone motion or as an alternative to a motion for summary judgment and proceeds in all procedural respects like a motion for summary judgment, but which must completely dispose of the challenged cause of action, affirmative defense, claim for damages, or issue of duty. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subds. (f)(1)-(2), (t).)

A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

The moving party bears the initial burden of production to make prima facie showing no triable material fact issues. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850 (Aguilar).) This burden on summary judgment or adjudication “is more properly one of persuasion rather than proof, since he must persuade the court that there is no material fact for a reasonable trier of fact to find, and not to prove any such fact to the satisfaction of the court itself as though it were sitting as the trier of fact.” (Id. at p. 850, fn. 11.) If the moving party meets this burden, the burden shifts to the opposing party to make a rebuttal prima facie showing that a triable issue of material fact exists. (Id. at p. 849.)

“[I]n ruling on motions for summary judgment courts are to ‘“liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment [or adjudication] and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.”’ [Citations].” (Cheal v. El Camino Hospital (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 736, 760.)

    1. Discussion

After review, and though unopposed, the Court determines that the moving papers present insufficient grounds for relief.

Specifically, under the Code of Civil Procedure, a motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

Here, the Second and Third Affirmative Defenses read:

SECOND AFFIRMATIVE DEFENSE

(Truth / Substantial Truth)

2. Defendant alleges that the statements referenced in the Complaint are true or substantially true, and therefore cannot constitute defamation or any other actionable claim.

THIRD AFFIRMATIVE DEFENSE

(Opinion / Protected Consumer Commentary)

3. Defendant alleges that the statements referenced in the Complaint constitute expressions of opinion, interpretation, and commentary based upon Defendant’s personal experience as a consumer of legal services. Such statements are subjective evaluations and impressions that are not provably false statements of fact. Statements reflecting dissatisfaction with professional services, impressions of conduct, and recommendations regarding whether others should use such services constitute protected opinion and consumer commentary and therefore cannot form the basis of a defamation claim.

(Answer, pp. 2-3.)

However, as reflected in the notice of motion, Plaintiffs seek summary adjudication of the applicability of the Second and Third Affirmative Defenses to two sets of statements supporting liability against Defendant Campbell in the Complaint, i.e., a fee misrepresentation statement and a withheld funds statement. (See Motion, 1:10-27; see also Complaint, ¶¶ 29-30 [above statements as well as statements regarding substandard legal representation and racial discrimination amounting to conduct supporting first cause of action for civil extortion], 36 [characterizing Yelp postings as libelous in support of second cause of action for libel per se.) Otherwise stated, Plaintiffs’ motion, if granted, would not dispose of the Second or Third Affirmative Defense in whole, just in part, as relates to the fee misrepresentation statement and withheld funds statement, but not as to the substandard representation or racial discrimination statement. Summary adjudication cannot be thus granted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

And critically, the moving party bears the initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of no triable issues of material fact. (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 850.) Here, the moving papers do not sufficiently clarify to this Court how disposition on summary adjudication as to the fee misrepresentation statement and the withheld funds statement would fully resolve all questions related to the Second and Third Affirmative Defenses in Plaintiffs’ favor, particularly, for example, where the alleged misrepresentations made by Defendant Campbell on Yelp vis-à-vis Plaintiffs involved representations that Plaintiffs had mishandled the underlying action, misrepresented retainer terms, wrongfully withheld settlement proceeds, and engaged in racial discrimination. (See, e.g., Complaint, ¶ 29.)

c.     Conclusion

Plaintiffs McMurray Henriks, LLP, and attorney Yana G. Henriks’s motion for summary adjudication is accordingly DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION TO COMPEL INITIAL DISCLOSURES PURSUANT TO CALIFORNIA CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE § 871.26 AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS

 

Dept. 731

September 22, 2026

8:30 a.m.

Matter No. 18

 

 

Moving Party:               Plaintiffs, Iskra Lindsey and Ileana Gaitan

Opposing Party:           Defendant, FCA US, LLC

Notice:                         OK

 

  1. Background Facts

Now before the Court is Plaintiffs Iskra Lindsey and Ileana Gaitan’s opposed motion to compel further initial disclosures from and request for monetary sanctions against Defendant FCA US, LLC, which is brought pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 in this lemon law action.

 

  1. Motion to Compel Further Initial Disclosures; Request for Monetary Sanctions

a.     Legal Standard

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26, which applies only to represented parties in actions filed on or after January 1, 2025, parties in civil actions seeking vehicle restitution or replacement under section 871.20 must exchange automatic initial disclosures and documents without awaiting a discovery request. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subds. (a), (f)-(i), (k)-(l).) These mutual disclosures are due within 60 days after the filing of the answer or responsive pleading, followed by a right to conduct limited two-hour initial depositions within 120 days, while all other discovery is stayed until mediation has concluded. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subds. (b)-(e).) Absent a showing of good cause, failure to comply with these requirements triggers mandatory monetary sanctions against the noncomplying party’s attorney—$1,500.00 for a plaintiff’s attorney and $2,500.00 for a defendant’s attorney—with repeated noncompliance resulting in dismissal of the case for plaintiffs or severe evidentiary trial sanctions for defendants. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subds. (j)-(j)(5).)

Notably, Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 contains no motion-to-compel step, no compel-order prerequisite, no meet-and-confer requirement in the statute, and no separate statement requirement. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (a)-(l); cf., e.g., Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(a) [separate statement, arising in relation to discovery requests or responses thereto as opposed to discovery obligations by operation of statute].) Section 871.26 instead contains a lone cross-reference to the Discovery Act at subdivision (e), which applies only to “standard discovery” after mediation concludes, not to enforcing the initial disclosures. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (e).)

This omission by the Legislature contrasts the express authority to move for an order compelling responses provided in Code of Civil Procedure sections 2016.090 (initial disclosures), 2025.250 and 2025.480 (depositions and requests for production), and 2031.300 to 2031.320 (standalone production requests), and appears to signify a lack of express standing to move to compel initial disclosures in the same manner as these latter identified statues. (See People v. Trevino (2001) 26 Cal.4th 237, 242 [“When the Legislature uses materially different language in statutory provisions addressing the same subject or related subjects, the normal inference is that the Legislature intended a difference in meaning”].)

The omission also appears to necessitate non-ex-parte requests for relief to courts for enforcement of rights arising from Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26, subdivision (j). (Cf., e.g., Le Francois v. Goel (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1094, 1108 [while party cannot request reconsideration based on erroneous court reasoning, a party can suggest to the court that it should reconsider its erroneous order on its own motion so long as the suggestion is not made ex parte].)

    1. Discussion

After review, the Court finds insufficient grounds for relief. Plaintiffs’ motion is arguably moot as to the relief requested and is otherwise unmeritorious based on supplemental disclosures on June 16, 2026, a position not disputed in reply.

While the moving papers represent that Plaintiffs met and conferred regarding deficient disclosures on June 3, 2026, with “FCA US LLC [failing to] cure[] the deficiencies in its mandatory disclosures” prior to filing this motion on June 22, 2026 (Motion, Rucker Decl., ¶¶ 4-7, Exs. 1-2), two points undermine Plaintiffs’ position.

First, Plaintiffs failed to attach the initial disclosures, for which reason the Court cannot assess the sufficiency thereof under section 871.26. (See Motion, Rucker Decl., ¶¶ 6-7, Exs. 1 [single FCA document], 2 [meet and confer letter].)

Second, FCA’s opposition undermines Plaintiffs’ papers by clarifying that on June 16, 2026—six days before Plaintiffs filed their motion—FCA supplied supplemental disclosures to Plaintiffs, which FCA contends satisfied its discovery obligations (see Opposition, Mehall Decl., ¶¶ 6-7, Ex. B), a position which is not disputed in any reply by Plaintiffs, where the record fails to reflect that Plaintiffs filed any reply prior to the hearing.

c.     Conclusion

Plaintiffs Iskra Lindsey and Ileana Gaitan motion to compel and request for monetary sanctions are accordingly DENIED.

Plaintiffs Iskra Lindsey and Ileana Gaitan are ordered to give notice.

  1. Background Facts

This cross-action arises from a landlord-tenant dispute involving the two commercial leases governing Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Nunzio Donato Ciaraulo’s tenancy at 7992 Sunset Blvd. and 7994 Sunset Blvd. in the Sunset-Laurel Plaza, Los Angeles, California shopping center.

    1. Pleadings

                                          i.    Plaintiff’s Second Amended Complaint

Plaintiff Nunzio Donato Ciaraulo (“Plaintiff”), an individual doing business as Vivoli Café, sues 7980 Sunset Associates (“Sunset”), Coral Realty, Inc. (“Coral Realty”), A. Douglas Rickard (“Rickard”), ADR Preferred Business Properties (“ADR”), and Does 1-50. Plaintiff asserts causes of action for Breach of Contract, Fraudulent Misrepresentation and Concealment, Negligent Misrepresentation, Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing, Private Nuisance, Negligence, Violation of the Unruh Civil Rights Act, and Breach of Fiduciary Duty. The claims address the parties’ landlord-tenant relationship for units 7992 and 7994 West Sunset Boulevard, commercial real estate property, including alleged concealment of Conditional Use Permit restrictions restricting use of the 7992 West Sunset space as a restaurant, improper CAM and valet-related charges, failure to maintain common areas and sewer systems, accessibility issues, and interference with Plaintiff’s restaurant operations.

                                         ii.    Defendants’ Cross-Complaint

In turn, Sunset, as landlord of the Premises, brings a Cross-Complaint against Plaintiff and Roes 1-10 pursuant to claims of Breach of the 7994 Lease and Breach of the 7992 Lease. The claims arise from Plaintiff’s alleged failure to pay rent, CAM charges, late fees, and other monetary obligations under both leases, ultimately resulting in two separate unlawful detainer actions, termination of both leases, and monetary judgments.

    1. Motion Before the Court

Now before the Court is Defendants 7980 Sunset Associates, Coral Realty, Inc., and A. Douglas Rickard’s opposed demurrer to the second through eighth causes of action alleged in Plaintiff’s June 30, 2026, Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”).

 

  1. Demurrer
    1. Meet and Confer

The Court determines that sufficient meet and confer efforts preceded the filing of Defendants’ demurrer. (Demurrer, Adelson Decl., ¶ 2; see Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subds. (a)(3)(A).)

b.     Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; see Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).)

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) “[E]ach evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)

In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-67.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) A demurrer, however, “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

A demurrer may only be used to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The face of the complaint includes exhibits attached to the complaint. (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94.) If facts appearing in the exhibits contradict those alleged, the facts in the exhibits take precedence. (Holland v. Morse Diesel Intern., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447, superseded by statute on other grounds as stated in White v. Cridlebaugh (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 506, 521.)

    1. Discussion

                                          i.    Demurrer, Second and Third Causes of Action, Fraudulent Misrepresentation and Concealment and Negligent Misrepresentation

After review, the Court SUSTAINS Defendants’ demurrer to the SAC’s second cause of action, WITHOUT leave to amend.

Claims for fraud by way of intentional and negligent misrepresentation require a showing of justifiable reliance on material misrepresentations of fact. (Service by Medallion, Inc. v. Clorox Co. (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 1807, 1816 [elements of intentional misrepresentation]; B.L.M. v. Sabo & Deitsch (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 823, 834 [same for negligent misrepresentation].) Here, the SAC fails to allege such justifiable reliance.

The SAC alleges misrepresentations by way of Defendants’ conduct in relation to the 7992 West Sunset space, which the City found was impermissibly being used as a restaurant, with Defendants misrepresenting the suitability of the 7992 West Sunset space as a restaurant annex, the percentage rate for CAM charges, the availability of parking and valet arrangements, and the availability of information regarding legality or suitability of the intended use for the 7992 West Sunset space. (See, e.g., SAC, ¶ 150.)

These allegations mirror allegations in the Complaint and the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”). (Cf., e.g., Complaint, ¶ 29 & FAC, ¶¶ 61-62.)

The Court has twice found those allegations insufficiently allege reliance in connection with the fraud claims. (See Mar. 10, 2026, Order, pp. 5-6; Jun. 11, 2026, Order, p. 6.)

Defendants’ now again demurrer to the fraud claims based on lack of sufficiency of pleading as to justifiable reliance. (Demurrer, pp. 6-10.)

Plaintiff argues in opposition that the SAC has been amended to properly allege that justifiable reliance. (Opposition, pp. 2, 9-10, 13-14.)

However, the Court agrees with Defendants, with the SAC approximating a sham pleading.

Here, as with the Court’s orders relating to demurrers to the original Complaint and First Amended Complaint, respectively dated March 10, 2026 (at pp. 5-6) and June 11, 2026 (at p. 6), the SAC fails to sufficiently allege reliance, either based on past conduct or otherwise, specifically based on the clause in the parties’ November 1, 2018, lease for the 7992 Sunset Blvd. space, specifying that “Tenant acknowledges and understands that neither Landlord nor any of Landlord’s agents or attorneys have made any representations or warranties, expressed or implied, as to the present or future suitability of the Premises for the conduct of the Tenant’s business, use and occupancy, the legality of Tenant’s proposed business or improvements under . . . applicable laws.” (See FAC, Exh. B at p. 6, § 6 [italics in original].)

This lease was not attached to the SAC, which results in a sham pleading insofar as the SAC is avoiding the incorporation factor of attaching the lease agreement to the pleadings, as Plaintiff did with the Complaint and FAC, having attached the November 1, 2018, lease agreement for the 7992 West Sunset space as Exhibit B. A sham amendment occurs when the plaintiff attempts to avoid a defect in the original complaint by filing an amended complaint that omits, corrects, or substitutes new facts for the facts creating the defect. (See Vallejo Dev. Co. v. Beck Dev. Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 929, 946 (Vallejo); Lee v. Hensley (1951) 103 Cal.App.2d 697, 709.) An amendment that omits, corrects, or substitutes new facts for facts stated in the original complaint in an attempt to avoid a material defect in the complaint does not supersede the original complaint if the plaintiff does not adequately explain the reason for the omission, correction, or substitution. (Vallejo, supra, 24 Cal.App.4th at p. 946.) Here, the Court determines that the FAC is not superseded by the filing of the SAC insofar as the SAC fails to attach a copy of the lease attached as Exhibit B to the Complaint and FAC, which the Court reads as an existing component of the pleadings given the failure of the face of the SAC and the opposition to demurrer to explain the omission thereof.

As with the Court’s March 10, 2026, Order (at pp. 5-6) and June 11, 2026, Order (at p. 6), the Court here determines that the release clause paraphrased above disclaims the justifiable reliance that Plaintiff attempts to use to remedy the shortcomings identified in the Court’s March and June Orders. (See, e.g., Opposition, 10:14-19 [arguing sufficiency of reliance in pleading based on allegations of newly pleaded communications, superior knowledge, partial disclosures, longstanding course of dealing, and licensed-agent participation create a factual question concerning whether Plaintiff’s reliance was reasonable].)

                                         ii.    Demurrer, Fourth Cause of Action, Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

The Court also SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the fourth cause of action for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, WITHOUT leave to amend.

The SAC alleges a breach of implied covenant claim based on conditions related to occupancy of 7992 West Sunset. (See, e.g., SAC, ¶ 170.)

However, as with the Court’s prior Orders, the Court again finds that this claim is duplicative of the claim for breach of contract, here pleaded as the first cause of action. (Compare SAC, ¶¶ 139-144, with ¶¶ 167-173.) The purpose of both claims is to make Plaintiff whole for alleged failure to live up to the parties’ agreements related to occupancy and use of the 7992 West Sunset space.

A general demurrer for sufficiency may be sustained against duplicative claims. (Palm Springs Villas II Homeowners Assn., Inc. v. Parth (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 268, 290 (“Palm Springs”) [“a cause of action for breach of governing documents [that] appear[ed] to be duplicative of [a] cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty” is “recognized … as a basis for sustaining a demurrer”]; Rodrigues v. Campbell Industries (1978) 87 Cal.App.3d 494, 501 [finding demurrer was properly sustained without leave to amend as to cause of action that contained allegations of other causes and “thus add[ed] nothing to the complaint by way of fact or theory of recovery”]; see also Award Metals, Inc. v. Superior Court (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 1128, 1135, opn. mod. Apr. 10, 1991 [demurrer should have been sustained as to duplicative causes of action].)

In Palm Springs, the court of appeal found that “the cause of action [therein pleaded] for breach of governing documents appear[ed] to be duplicative of the cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty,” supporting a finding that “the trial court properly sustained [a] demurrer” to the breach of governing documents claim, particularly where “[t]he [the pleading party] d[id] not address [the demurring party’s] argument or explain how its document claim differ[ed] from the fiduciary breach claim.” (Palm Springs, supra, 248 Cal.App.4th at p. 290.) The specific factual allegations at issue in Palm Springs were a “cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty [that] alleged that [the demurring party] had breached her duties to comply with the governing documents and to avoid causing harm to the [pleading party] by, among other things, refusing to submit bids or contracts to the Board, ‘unilaterally terminating’ PPM, and signing the contract with Desert Protection,” while, in turn, the “[t]he breach of governing documents cause of action identified CC&R and bylaw provisions and identified actions taken by [the demurring party] in breach of these provisions, including the termination of PPM and entering into the Desert Protection contract.” (Id. at p. 277.)

Here, as with the Orders relating to the Complaint and FAC (see Mar. 10, 2026, Order, pp. 6-7; Jun. 11, 2026, Order, p. 7), and with language as elaborate as Palm Springs—there sufficient to find duplicativeness in pleading, the SAC’s breach of contract and breach of implied covenant claims almost identically relate to Plaintiff’s unfulfilled expectations vis-à-vis obligations arising from lease of the 7992 West Sunset space. (Compare SAC, ¶¶ 139-144, with ¶¶ 167-173.)

Consistent with the Court’s past two Orders, and with Palm Springs, the Court thus determines that the SAC’s fourth cause of action is duplicative of the SAC’s first cause of action, rendering it subject to demurrer again.

                                        iii.    Demurrer, Fifth Cause of Action, Private Nuisance

The Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the fifth cause of action for private nuisance, WITHOUT leave to amend.

The fifth cause of action alleges private nuisance based on repeated sewage backups, foul odors, hazardous conditions, and the removal of required parking (SAC, ¶ 177), mirroring the allegations in the Complaint at ¶ 47 and the FAC at ¶ 88.

As reasoned by the Court in relation to the FAC (Jun. 11, 2026, Order, p. 8), Plaintiff has not alleged the breach of any duty independent of the subject leases, for which reason the fifth cause of action is barred by the economic loss rule.

“The economic loss rule provides that, [i]n general, there is no recovery in tort for negligently inflicted ‘purely economic losses,’ meaning financial harm unaccompanied by physical or property damage.” (Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 837.) “[U]nder the economic loss rule, tort recovery for a breach of contract duty is generally barred … unless two conditions are satisfied”: (1) “A plaintiff must first demonstrate the defendant’s injury-causing conduct violated a duty that is independent of the duties and rights assumed by the parties when they entered the contract”; and (2) the defendant’s conduct must have caused injury to persons or property that was not reasonably contemplated by the parties when the contract was formed.” (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 20-21.)

Here, as with the FAC, Plaintiff has not identified (1) a duty independent of the contract that was violated or (2) injury-causing conduct to persons or property that was not reasonably contemplated by the parties when the November 1, 2018, lease agreement was entered. (Cf. Jun. 11, 2026, Order, p. 8.) 

                                        iv.    Demurrer, Sixth Cause of Action, Negligence

The Court also SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the sixth cause of action for negligence, WITHOUT leave to amend, based on the economic loss rule.

While not addressed in the Court’s June 11, 2026, Order, as discussed in the preceding subsection, the economic loss rule bars recovery in tort for harm that could have reasonably been contemplated in contract. Here, the negligence claim, i.e., a tort claim, is premised on the same facts as the fifth cause of action, rendering the sixth cause of action as subject to the demurrer as the private nuisance claim. (Compare SAC, ¶ 177, with ¶¶ 183-184.)

The Court finds insufficient merit to the opposition’s reliance on Southern California Gas Leak Cases (2019) 7 Cal.5th 391, 400-401 for the position that an independent duty arose here. (Opposition, p. 17.) As noted in the reply at page 8, the Gas Leak Cases are distinguishable based on the scale of the industrial accident in that case compared to the sewer line and relatively local and contained allegations in the SAC.

                                         v.    Demurrer, Seventh Cause of Action, Violation of the Unruh Civil Rights Act

The Court likewise SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the seventh cause of action, WITHOUT leave to amend.

The seventh cause of action is premised on Defendants purported removal of access to parking spaces and accommodation related features to parking needed to comply with disability laws. (See, e.g., SAC, ¶ 192.) These allegations mirror the Complaint and FAC in large part. (Complaint, ¶ 68; FAC, ¶ 100.)

As with the Court’s prior Orders, the Court determines that the SAC’s Unruh violations claim is barred by the claim’s statute of limitations.

Claims under the Unruh Civil Rights Act are subject to one of two statutes of limitations. For claims derived from the common law, a two-year statute of limitations applies. (See Gatto v. County of Sonoma (2002) 98 Cal.App.4th 744, 756-759 (Gatto) [the statute of limitations for personal injury actions governs claims involving liability derived from common law]; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 335.1.) For claims based on a statutory liability that did not exist at common law, a three-year statute of limitations applies. (See Gatto, supra, at p. 759; Code Civ. Proc., § 338, subd. (a).)

Earlier in this litigation, the Complaint initially alleged that the elimination of parking-related services occurred in 2019, well before the filing of the Complaint in 2025, putting the claim beyond the statute of limitations, a deficiency recognized by the Court as against the Complaint (Mar. 10, 2026, Order, pp. 10-11), and which the FAC failed to remedy based on conclusory tolling allegations (Jun. 11, 2026, Order, pp. 9-10, citing FAC, ¶¶ 106-107).

Here, as with the Court’s June Order, the Court determines that the seventh cause of action is, in essence, a sham as to discovery of the trigger of the limitations period beyond 2019, with the SAC simply adding language seeking to invoke the continuing violations doctrine, which is conclusorily alleged. (See, e.g., SAC, ¶¶ 194-195; cf. FAC, ¶¶ 106-107.)

                                        vi.    Demurrer, Eighth Cause of Action, Breach of Fiduciary Duty/Duty Arising from Confidential Relationship

Last, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the eighth cause of action, WITHOUT leave to amend.

The SAC alleges a breach of fiduciary duty from Defendant Rickard to Plaintiff as based on Rickard being Plaintiff’s agent during the lease agreement transactions around November 2018. (See, e.g., SAC, ¶¶ 199-206.) These allegations mirror the Complaint and FAC. (Complaint, ¶ 75; FAC, ¶ 115.)

As with the Court’s Orders related to the Complaint and FAC, the Court again determines that the breach of fiduciary duty claim is defeated by the terms in the November 1, 2018, lease agreement for the 7992 West Sunset space, attached to the Complaint and FAC as Exhibit B, and providing that “[t]enant acknowledges that it is not being represented by any broker or agent.” (Complaint & FAC at Ex. B, § 55 at p. 31.)

The SAC’s allegations do nothing to remedy this shortcoming, instead omitting the November 1, 2018, lease agreement from the pleadings, and attempting to plead around this defect by conclusorily alleging superior and exclusive knowledge. (See, e.g., SAC, ¶¶ 203-204.)

d.     Conclusion

Defendants 7980 Sunset Associates, Coral Realty, Inc., and A. Douglas Rickard’s demurrer is accordingly SUSTAINED as to causes of action two through eight, WITHOUT leave to amend.

Defendants 7980 Sunset Associates, Coral Realty, Inc., and A. Douglas Rickard are ordered to give notice.

  1. Background Facts

Now before the Court is Defendant City of Los Angeles’s demurrer to all seven Fair Employment and Housing Act (“FEHA”) claims and the whistleblower retaliation Labor Code claim in Plaintiff Danielle Wells’ March 25, 2026, Complaint, which arise from allegations of race, gender, age, and disability discrimination, harassment, and retaliation and protected Labor Code retaliation in Plaintiff’s employment with the Los Angeles Police Department.

 

  1. Demurrer
    1. Meet and Confer

The Court determines that while the meet and confer efforts that preceded the City’s demurrer were insufficient (Demurrer, Franklin Decl., ¶¶ 2-3, Ex. A [meet and confer limited to physical mail, no in-person, telephonic, or videoconferencing communications]; cf. Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)), such a finding is not a basis to overrule the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(4) [“A determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer”].)

b.     Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; see Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).)

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) “[E]ach evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)

In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-67.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) A demurrer, however, “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

A demurrer may only be used to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The face of the complaint includes exhibits attached to the complaint. (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94.) If facts appearing in the exhibits contradict those alleged, the facts in the exhibits take precedence. (Holland v. Morse Diesel Intern., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447, superseded by statute on other grounds as stated in White v. Cridlebaugh (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 506, 521.)

    1. Discussion

                                          i.    Demurrer, First and Second Causes of Action, FEHA Discrimination, Race, Gender, and Age, and Disability

The Court OVERRULES the City’s demurrer as to the first and second causes of action for FEHA Discrimination based on race, gender, and age, and disability respectively.

The demurrer’s first argument against these claims is that the only actionable adverse employment action was demotion in October or November 2020 but that Plaintiff filed this action on March 25, 2026, well beyond the three-year limitations period that elapsed in November 2023 as to any Right to Sue Letter. (Demurrer, p. 4.) However, the Court determines that the discovery of the harm here is sufficiently alleged not to have occurred until April 3, 2025, which is when Plaintiff’s internal grievance complaint related to her demotion was resolved against her, as detailed in an email from Detective Brian Burdick. (Complaint, ¶¶ 46-50; see Doe v. Roman Catholic Archbishop of Los Angeles (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 953, 961 [A statute of limitations starts to run once the cause of action “accrues,” and although a cause of action typically accrues when it is complete with all of its elements, a cause of action will at times be deemed to accrue at a later date, such as when the plaintiff did not discover and had no occasion to discover the cause of action until that later date, when the defendant fraudulently concealed the existence of a possible claim until that later date, or when the defendant committed multiple wrongs that ended on that later date.].)

The City’s second argument against the discrimination claims contends that the Complaint insufficiently alleges a nexus between the protected characteristics and the adverse employment conduct, arguing that any such nexus is, at most, conclusorily alleged. (Demurrer, pp. 4-6.) The Court, however, agrees with the opposition that sufficient ultimate facts are alleged in the Complaint to allege a nexus between race, gender, age, and disability and Plaintiff’s demotion, where evidentiary facts can be elaborated on in discovery. (See, e.g., Complaint, ¶¶ 65-66, 77-80.)

The City’s demurrer thus fails as to the first and second causes of action.

                                         ii.    Demurrer, Third and Fourth Causes of Action, Failure to Provide Reasonable Accommodations or Engage in the Interactive Process

The Court however SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the Complaint’s third and fourth causes of action, with leave to amend.

These claims allege a failure to provide accommodations or engage in the interactive process relating to Plaintiff’s disabilities. (Complaint, ¶¶ 89, 96-99.)

However, the Complaint provides no temporal period for when these accommodations were not respected. (See, e.g., Complaint, ¶¶ 84-92, 93-103.)

As a result, the Court cannot determine when the limitations period began to run on discrete requests for reasonable accommodations by Plaintiff, as alleged in the Complaint generally, but critically not specifically as to any date. (See Demurrer, pp. 6-7 [making this argument].)

Consequently, the Complaint is insufficiently alleged as to the third and fourth causes of action.

                                        iii.    Demurrer, Fifth Cause of Action, FEHA Harassment

The Court also SUSTAINS the City’s demurrer to the Complaint’s FEHA harassment claim.

Here, the FEHA harassment claim is based on separate, discrete acts, here specifically harassment by Captains Mendoza and Shah, in language seeming to plead an Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress Claim. (SAC, ¶¶ 106-108.)

However, as with the third and fourth causes of action, the harassment claim is insufficiently alleged because it does not sufficiently allege a concrete start for the limitations period, where the actionable conduct could have occurred between 2019 and the filing of the Complaint, rendering the Complaint subject to demurrer.

                                        iv.    Demurrer, Sixth and Eighth Causes of Action, FEHA and Labor Code Retaliation

The Court however OVERRULES the demurrer as to the sixth and eighth causes of action.

The FEHA and Labor Code respectively make it unlawful to retaliate against an employee for reporting unlawful violations of the FEHA or violations of the Labor Code or other laws. (Yanowitz v. L’Oreal USA Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1042 [FEHA retaliation elements]; Lawson v. PPG Architectural Finishes, Inc. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 703, 712 [Labor Code retaliation elements].)

Here, the Court determines that the sixth and eighth causes of action, at the very least, sufficiently allege retaliation as of April 29, 2025, based on confirmation of Plaintiff’s demotion, as premised on her prior complaints of FEHA and Labor Code violations in her employment with the LAPD. (See, e.g., Complaint, ¶¶ 126, 142-143.)

                                         v.    Demurrer, Seventh Cause of Action, FEHA Failure to Prevent Harassment, Discrimination, and Retaliation

Last, the Court OVERRULES the demurrer as to the seventh cause of action, as this claim is derivative of liability vis-à-vis the first, second, and sixth causes of action, which survived demurrer, as discussed above.

d.     Conclusion

Defendant City of Los Angeles’s demurrer is SUSTAINED, in part, and OVERRULED, in part, as follows:

(1) OVERRULED as to causes of action one, two, six, seven, and eight; and

(2) SUSTAINED, with leave to amend, as to causes of action three, four, and five.

  1. Background Facts

Now before the Court is Defendant Inter-Con Security Systems, Inc.’s opposed motion to compel into arbitration the Fair Employment and Housing Act (“FEHA”), California Family Rights Act (“CFRA”), Family and Medical Leave Act (“FMLA”), Labor Code, Business and Professions Code, and common law claims alleged in Plaintiff’s April 10, 2026, Complaint, where the claims arise from allegations of age and disability discrimination, harassment, and retaliation and wage and hour violations experienced by Plaintiff in her employment with Defendant through her termination of employment.

 

  1. Motion to Compel Arbitration

a.     Legal Standard

The Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), while a federal statute, applies in California courts and requires state courts to enforce arbitration agreements as required by the federal common law developed under the FAA. (See Southland Corp. v. Keating (1984) 465 U.S. 1, 15-16 (Southland Corp.); Broughton v. Cigna Healthplans (1999) 21 Cal.4th 1066, 1074-78, superseded by statute on another ground as stated in Ferguson v. Corinthian Colleges, Inc. (9th Cir. 2013) 733 F.3d 928, 937.).) The FAA preempts and invalidates state law and state judicial decisions that disfavor arbitration or require arbitration provisions to pass higher scrutiny. (Southland Corp., supra, at p. 12; Perry v. Thomas (1987) 482 U.S. 483, 490.) If the parties designate the FAA applies, then California arbitration law is preempted. (See, e.g., Rodriguez v. American Techs., Inc. (2006) 136 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1121-1122.) However, courts have found that where the FAA is found not to apply, the California Arbitration Act (Code Civ. Proc. § 1280 et seq.) applies. (See Valencia v. Smyth (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 153, 178 (Valencia).)

 A court’s inquiry is limited to a determination of (1) whether a valid arbitration agreement exists and (2) whether the arbitration agreement covers the dispute. (9 U.S.C. § 4; Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostics Systems, Inc. (9th Cir. 2000) 207 F.3d 1126, 1130; Howsam v. Dean Witter Reynolds, Inc. (2002) 537 U.S. 79, 84; see Simula, Inc. v. Autoliv, Inc. (9th Cir. 1999) 175 F.3d 716, 720 [if the finding is affirmative on both counts the FAA requires the Court to enforce the arbitration agreement in accordance with its terms]; see Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961 [In determining the enforceability of an arbitration agreement, the court first considers “two ‘gateway issues’ of arbitrability: (1) whether there was an agreement to arbitrate between the parties, and (2) whether the agreement covered the dispute at issue”] Lacayo v. Cataline Restaurant Group Inc. (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 244, 257 (Lacayo) [Where moving party meets initial burden, “the party opposing arbitration must prove by a preponderance of the evidence any defense to the petition”].)

Even where the FAA governs the interpretation of arbitration clauses, California law governs whether an arbitration agreement has been formed in the first instance. (Baker v. Osborne Development Corp. (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 884, 893.) 

    1. Discussion

                                          i.    Defendant’s Burden

1.     Existence of Agreement

The Court first determines that Defendant carries its burden of showing an agreement to arbitrate.

“Parties are not required to arbitrate their disagreements unless they have agreed to do so. [….] In California, a ‘clear agreement’ to arbitrate may be either express or implied in fact. [Citation.]” (Davis v. Nordstrom, Inc. (9th Cir. 2014) 755 F.3d 1089, 1092-1093 [applying California law].) The court is only required to make a finding of the agreement’s existence, not an evidentiary determination of its validity. (Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 219.)

Here, Defendant provides a declaration from Heather Bean, Defendant’s Senior Vice President, Assistant General Counsel – Global Litigation & International (cir. Mar. 2025) (formerly Vice President, Assistant General Counsel- Global Litigation & International; Assistant General Counsel), which sufficiently authenticates Plaintiff’s September 30, 2026, electronic signature on onboarding documents including a Mutual Agreement to Arbitrate Claims. (Motion, Bean Decl., ¶¶ 14-16, Ex. A.)

The Court finds insufficient merit to Plaintiff’s two arguments against this conclusion.

First, Plaintiff’s electronic signature is sufficiently authenticated.

California case law has held that an electronic signature was properly authenticated when a declarant “detailed [the company’s] security precautions regarding transmission and use of an applicant’s unique username and password, as well as the steps an applicant would have to take to place his or her name on the signature line of the employment agreement …,” such that, “[b]ased on this procedure, [the declarant] concluded that the ‘name [of the employee] could have only been placed on the signature pages of the employment agreement … by someone using [the employee’s] unique user name and password,’” and that “ [g]iven this process for signing documents and protecting the privacy of the information with unique and private user names and passwords, the electronic signature was made by [the employee] on the employment agreement … at the date, time, and IP address listed on the documents.” (Espejo v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1062 (Espejo).)

Here, contrary to Plaintiff’s opposition arguments (Opposition, pp. 2-4), the Bean declaration reflects the requirements of Espejo. (Motion, Bean Decl., ¶¶ 4-20, Ex. A.)

Second, the Court finds insufficient merit to Plaintiff’s argument that there was no mutual assent in entering the agreement to arbitrate because Plaintiff’s recollection of the onboarding process fails to include recollection of signing an agreement to arbitrate.

The burden of persuasion is always on the moving party to prove the existence of an arbitration agreement with the opposing party by a preponderance of the evidence. (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 164-165 (Gamboa), citations omitted.) However, the burden of production may shift in a three-step process. (Id. at p. 165.)

The moving party first bears the burden of producing prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy, which can be met by the moving party by attaching a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the opposing party’s signature. (Gamboa, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th at p. 165, citations omitted.) Here, as discussed above, Defendant provides a copy of an electronic signature from Plaintiff on an agreement to arbitrate, meeting its initial burden.

Next, the burden shifts to the opposing party to produce evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement, which can be met by “testify[ing] under oath or declar[ing] under penalty of perjury that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement.” (Gamboa, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th at p. 165, citations omitted.) Here, Plaintiff disputes ever signing the agreement to arbitrate, meeting his burden. (Opposition, Ruchman Decl., ¶ 7.)

Last, the burden shifts back to the moving party, who must establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties. (Gamboa, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th at p. 165.) The burden of proving the agreement by a preponderance of the evidence remains with the moving party. (Id. at pp. 165-166, citations omitted.) Here, the Court accepts that the preponderance of the evidence, in the form of the Bean declaration and its attachments, shows an agreement to arbitrate between the parties.

Consequently, the Court determines Defendant has carried its burden of persuasion to show an agreement to arbitrate between the parties.

2.     Delegation Clause

The Court next determines that, though not raised by the parties, the remaining issues relating to arbitration—e.g., interstate commerce, scope, and defenses—must be delegated to the arbitrator.

Under the Federal Arbitration Act the gateway issue of arbitrability presumptively is reserved for the court, but the parties also may delegate that issue to the arbitrator, provided that the language of their arbitration agreement provides “clear and unmistakable” evidence that this was their intent. (First Options of Chicago, Inc. v. Kaplan (1995) 514 U.S. 938, 944; Caremark, LLC v. Chickasaw Nation (9th Cir. Aug. 9, 2022) 43 F.4th 1021, 1029-1030.) A delegation of issues of formation to the arbitrator does not prevent the court from making that determination. (Granite Rock Co. v. International Broth. of Teamsters (2010) 561 U.S. 287, 299; Caremark, supra, at p. 1030.)

Here, the agreement to arbitrate provides that “the parties agree that all other disputes between them, including without limitation, disputes concerning formation or enforceability of this Agreement, are delegated to the Arbitrator who shall have exclusive authority to resolve such disputes.” (Motion, Bean Decl., ¶ 16, Ex. A, § 12.) This language amounts to a clear and unmistakable delegation of authority to resolve formation and enforceability issues to the arbitrator. (Cf. Aanderud v. Superior Court (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 880, 891 [“‘There are two prerequisites for a delegation clause to be effective. First, the language of the clause must be clear and unmistakable. [Citation.] Second, the delegation must not be revocable under state contract defenses such as fraud, duress, or unconscionability.’ [Citations.]”].)

Consequently, the Court determines that a clear and unmistakable clause in the parties’ agreement to arbitrate delegates to the arbitrator the remaining questions raised by the parties’ papers regarding enforcement of the arbitration agreement.

3.     Interstate Commerce, Scope of Agreement

Based on the reasoning above, the Court declines to further discuss Defendant’s burden on a motion to compel arbitration.

                                         ii.    Plaintiff’s Burden

The Court likewise declines to further discuss Plaintiff’s burden on a motion to compel arbitration based on the above reasoning.

c.     Stay

“If any suit or proceeding be brought in any of the courts of the United States upon any issue referable to arbitration under an agreement in writing for such arbitration, the court in which such suit is pending, upon being satisfied that the issue involved in such suit or proceeding is referable to arbitration under such an agreement, shall on application of one of the parties stay the trial of the action until such arbitration has been had in accordance with the terms of the agreement, providing the applicant for the stay is not in default in proceeding with such arbitration.” (9 U.S.C., § 3; see Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.)

Here, the Court having found sufficient grounds for relief to grant Defendant’s motion, the Court also finds sufficient grounds for a stay.
