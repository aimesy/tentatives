     PREMIER BRANDS OF AMERICA, INC.’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

 

I.                Background

 

On October 11, 2024, Plaintiffs Antony and Janice Ward filed their complaint for personal injury alleging Mr. Ward developed malignant mesothelioma from direct exposure to asbestos-contaminated talcum powder products. On April 11, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Doe Amendment naming Defendant Premier Brands of America, Inc. (“Premier” or “Defendant”) as Doe 11. Ms. Ward was dismissed from this action on May 13, 2025.

            On February 9, 2026, Premier moved for summary judgment, arguing that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that he was exposed to an asbestos-contaminated product for which Premier was responsible.

            On July 17, 2026, Plaintiff opposed, arguing that Premier failed to meet its initial burden.

            On August 6, 2026, Premier replied, reiterating that Plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses support an inference that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support his claims against Premier.

            Premier failed to meet its initial burden to show that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that he was exposed to an asbestos-contaminated product for which Premier was responsible. Premier’s motion for summary judgment is denied.

 

II.             Discussion

 

A.    Legal Standard

A defendant seeking summary judgment must “conclusively negate[] a necessary element of the plaintiff’s case, or . . . demonstrate[] that under no hypothesis is there a material issue of fact that requires the process of trial.”  (Guz v. Bechtel Nat. Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 334.)  To show that a plaintiff cannot establish an element of a cause of action, a defendant must make the initial showing “that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854.)  “The defendant may, but need not, present evidence that conclusively negates an element of the plaintiff’s cause of action.  The defendant may also present evidence that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence – as through admissions by the plaintiff following extensive discovery to the effect that he has discovered nothing.”  (Id. at p. 855.) “ ‘If plaintiffs respond to comprehensive interrogatories seeking all known facts with boilerplate answers that restate their allegations, or simply provide laundry lists of people and/or documents, the burden of production will almost certainly be shifted to them once defendants move for summary judgment and properly present plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses.’ ”  (Weber v. John Crane, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1433, 1440.)

B.    Causation

In asbestos litigation, a plaintiff is required to show causation. A plaintiff must show “some threshold exposure to the defendant's defective asbestos-containing products” and must further establish in “reasonable medical probability” that a particular exposure or series of exposures was a “legal cause” of his injury, i.e., a substantial factor in bringing about the injury. (Rutherford v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (1997) 16 Cal.4th 953, 982.)  The proper analysis is to ask whether the plaintiff has proven exposure to a defendant's product, of whatever duration, so that exposure is a possible factor in causing the disease and then to evaluate whether the exposure was a substantial factor.” (Lineaweaver v. Plant Insulation Co. (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1416.

Where asbestos was not an intended ingredient in a talc product, plaintiffs bear the additional burden of showing that it is “more likely than not that the talc product was contaminated with asbestos during the time the plaintiff used it.” (LAOSD Asbestos Cases (2020) 44 Cal.App.5th 475, 489 [hereinafter “Gibbons”].) Evidence sufficient to support this finding includes “long term use of a talcum powder product alleged to contain asbestos by a mesothelioma sufferer who was not exposed to any other known source of asbestos above background asbestos levels that are ever-present in the environment, together with expert testimony reporting positive test results for the presence of asbestos in ore from the sources used to manufacture the product.” (Strobel v. Johnson & Johnson (2021) 70 Cal.App.5th 796, 815.) “In the absence of evidence explaining how asbestos in the source ore would have been eliminated in the process of mining and then milling talc, that is enough to support more than a mere possibility that the accused product… was a substantial factor in causing [plaintiff] to develop mesothelioma.” (Ibid.)

1.     Defendant’s Burden: Factually Devoid

Premier moves for summary judgment on the grounds that Plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses support an inference that he lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support his claims against it. Specifically, Premier argues that Plaintiff’s responses to standard interrogatories failed to mention Premier. (Motion at pp.4-5.) Additionally, Premier argues that Plaintiff “testified he has a general memory that he first used a powder product in 1987, has a general memory he last used a powder product in 2023, and has a general memory of having the same powder application routine; however, he could not provide specific dates of use for each of the various products, nor could he distinguish his product use by specific brand without speculating.” (Id. at pp.9-10.)

Premier directs the court’s attention to Plaintiff’s response to standard interrogatory number 18. The interrogatory reads as follows:

“For each product, material, compound, or equipment ( collectively referred to as "product") which YOU contend contains ASBESTOS allegedly manufactured, produced, prepared, distributed, or sold by any defendant named in this action or by its predecessors, subsidiaries, subdivisions or affiliates, and which YOU worked with or around or otherwise claim to have been exposed to at any time:

a.      Describe each product as specifically as possible, including its trade name, product type, ASBESTOS content, color, packaging, and manufacturer, together with a detailed description of when and how YOU became aware of this information;

b.     If not already identified in response to number 17(c) above, state the date(s) on which and places where YOU were exposed or YOUR best estimate thereof, together with the circumstances surrounding such exposure (i.e., whether YOU worked with it or were simply near an area where it was being used) to the product;

c.      Describe all instructions, recommendations or warnings of any kind that accompanied the product, together with the LOCATION(s) where this information appeared (e.g., printed on tag, tag covering, instruction sheet accompanying product, etc.);

d.     State the purpose for which YOU used the product;

e.      IDENTIFY all SAFETY PRECAUTIONS in place during YOUR use of the product;

f.      IDENTIFY (including name, address and telephone number) of YOUR supervisors and co-workers at the WORKSITE;

g.     IDENTIFY all PERSONS with knowledge of facts supporting YOUR response to this interrogatory and its subparts, not already identified in these responses; and

h.     IDENTIFY all DOCUMENTS which support YOUR response to this interrogatory and its subparts.”

(Spaulding Decl., Exh. D at pp.18-19.)

Plaintiff responded in relevant part as follows:

            “Plaintiffs have not had the opportunity to complete their investigation nor to conduct any discovery and thus have not fully formed all contentions as to each defendant nor marshaled all facts, witnesses, and documents supporting their allegations. Subject to and without waiving the foregoing objections and reference to Plaintiffs employment records per C.C.P. § 2030.230, Plaintiff further responds: (a)-(h): See Exhibit A Plaintiff ANTONY WARD's Work/Exposure History Sheet, attached, incorporated by this reference. Plaintiffs also anticipate that this response may be augmented by the testimony given by Plaintiffs at deposition and/or trial and evidence obtained from Defendants and experts though discovery. Plaintiffs' investigation and discovery are continuing. Plaintiffs reserve the right to amend and/or supplement this response.”

(Spaulding Decl., Exh. D at p.20.)

            Premier also directs the court’s attention to excerpts from Plaintiff’s deposition. Plaintiff testified that no other individual witnessed his personal use of body powder products. (UMF No.10; Spaulding Decl., Exh. G at 383:13-384:5.) Plaintiff also testified that he could not recall when he used specific body powder products or distinguish his product use by brand without speculation. (UMF No. 11; Spaulding decl., Exh. G at 384:6-400:2, 406:13-407:2.)

            In opposition, Plaintiff argues that standard interrogatories are not the defendant-specific “all facts” discovery required to shift the burden on summary judgment. (Opp. at p.4.) In reply, Premier argues that the standard interrogatories have the same effect as other written interrogatories. (Reply at p.2.)

            Factually devoid discovery responses may support an inference that a plaintiff lacks evidence to support his claims and shift the moving party’s burden on a motion for summary judgment. (Andrews v. Foster Wheeler LLC (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 96, 101, citing Scheiding v. Dinwiddie Const. Co. (1999) 69 Cal.App.4th 64, 81.) “If plaintiffs respond to comprehensive interrogatories seeking all known facts with boilerplate answers that restate their allegations, or simply provide laundry lists of people and/or documents, the burden of production will almost certainly be shifted to them once defendants move for summary judgment and properly present plaintiffs' factually devoid discovery responses.” (Andrews, supra, 138 Cal.App.4th at p.107, citing Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p.855.) Interrogatories are comprehensive when they are designed to elicit all the evidence a plaintiff has to support their contention that a defendant is liable for his injuries. (Weber v. John Crane, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1433, 1442.)

            Here, Plaintiff’s responses to standard interrogatories are insufficient to support an inference that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support his claims against Premier. As Plaintiff points out, the standard interrogatories are not specific to any particular defendant. Although standard interrogatory 18 requests that Plaintiff describe each product at issue and provide details such as the product’s asbestos content, color, and other details, the interrogatory does not request that Plaintiff state all facts supporting his contention that each defendant is liable for his injuries. The standard interrogatories are therefore not designed to elicit all the evidence Plaintiff has to support his claims against Premier.

Additionally, Plaintiff responded to the standard interrogatories in May 2025, seven months after filing the complaint and less than one month after naming Premier as a Doe 11 to this action. Plaintiff’s response stated that Plaintiff was in the process of conducting discovery and had not “fully formed all contentions as to each defendant nor marshaled all facts, witnesses, and documents supporting” his allegations. (Spaulding Decl., Exh. D at p.20.) It would be  unreasonable to infer from Plaintiff’s responses to standard interrogatories served so early in litigation that Plaintiff cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support his claims against Premier.

Premier fails to meet its initial burden to show that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support his claims against it. Accordingly, the motion for summary judgment is denied.

III.           Conclusion

Premier failed to meet its initial burden to show that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that he was exposed to an asbestos-contaminated product for which Premier was responsible. Premier’s motion for summary judgment is denied.

Plaintiff to give notice.

     ILLINOIS TOOL WORKS, INC.’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT OR, IN THE ALTERNATIVE, SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

 

I.                Background

 

On January 23, 2026, Plaintiffs Michael and Karyn Smith filed their complaint for personal injury alleging Mr. Smith developed mesothelioma after he was exposed to asbestos and asbestos-containing products. Mr. Smith alleges he was exposed to asbestos through his father’s work in the automotive industry. As to Illinois Tool Works, Inc. (“”ITW” or “Defendant”), Plaintiff alleges that he was exposed to ITW’s asbestos-containing Evercoat White Star (“Evercoat”) auto body filler products.

On June 15, 2026, ITW moved for summary judgment or summary adjudication on the grounds that Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their claims against it.

On July 27, 2026, Plaintiffs opposed, arguing that ITW failed to shift its moving burden. Alternatively, Plaintiffs argue that their evidence creates a triable issue of material fact over whether Mr. Smith was exposed to asbestos by ITW’s asbestos-containing products.

On August 3, 2026, ITW replied, arguing Plaintiffs’ evidence is insufficient to raise a triable issue of material fact over whether the Evercoat product Mr. Smith worked with contained asbestos.

ITW fails to meet its initial burden to show that Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their contention that Mr. Smith was exposed to an asbestos-containing product for which it is responsible. The motion for summary judgment is denied.

ITW’s motions for summary adjudication to the cause of action for intentional concealment and the demand for punitive damages are granted as unopposed.

 

II.             Discussion

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

A defendant seeking summary judgment must “conclusively negate[] a necessary element of the plaintiff’s case, or . . . demonstrate[] that under no hypothesis is there a material issue of fact that requires the process of trial.”  (Guz v. Bechtel Nat. Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 334.)  To show that a plaintiff cannot establish an element of a cause of action, a defendant must make the initial showing “that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854.)  “The defendant may, but need not, present evidence that conclusively negates an element of the plaintiff’s cause of action.  The defendant may also present evidence that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence – as through admissions by the plaintiff following extensive discovery to the effect that he has discovered nothing.”  (Id. at p. 855.) “ ‘If plaintiffs respond to comprehensive interrogatories seeking all known facts with boilerplate answers that restate their allegations, or simply provide laundry lists of people and/or documents, the burden of production will almost certainly be shifted to them once defendants move for summary judgment and properly present plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses.’ ”  (Weber v. John Crane, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1433, 1440.) 

B.    Causation

In asbestos litigation, a plaintiff is required to show causation. A plaintiff must show “some threshold exposure” to asbestos-containing products and must further establish to a “reasonable medical probability” that a particular exposure or series of exposures was a “legal cause” of his injury, i.e., a substantial factor in bringing about the injury. (Rutherford v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (1997) 16 Cal.4th 953, 982.) “[T]he proper analysis is to ask whether the plaintiff has proven exposure to a defendant's product, of whatever duration, so that exposure is a possible factor in causing the disease and then to evaluate whether the exposure was a substantial factor.” (Lineaweaver v. Plant Insulation Co. (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1416 (Lineweaver).)   

Where a plaintiff’s allegations pertain to asbestos as an intended ingredient of a product the plaintiff was exposed to occupationally, the plaintiff is required to present evidence “defendant's product was definitely at his work site and that it was sufficiently prevalent to warrant an inference that plaintiff was exposed to it…” (Turley v. Familian Corp. (2017) 18 Cal.App.5th 969, 980 [Citing Lineaweaver, supra, 31 Cal.App.4th at p. 1420.].) Where a defendant manufactures similar products, some of which contained asbestos and some of which did not, a plaintiff must present evidence from which the court can reasonably infer that it was more likely than not that the plaintiff was exposed to an asbestos-containing variety of the defendant’s¿product. (Collin v. CalPortland Co.¿(2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 582, 593.)

            i.     Defendant’s Burden: Factually Devoid

            ITW argues that Plaintiffs’ responses to written discovery and deposition testimony are devoid of facts supporting their claims against it. (Motion at pp.8-9 [“ITW propounded “all facts” written discovery on Plaintiffs to ascertain the evidence in support of Plaintiff’s allegations against ITW…Such ‘mere allegations’ are insufficient in the face of summary judgment. (CCP §

437c et seq.; Andrews, supra, 138 Cal.App.4th at 107.) No rational jury could ever use Plaintiffs’ conclusory responses to ITW’s written discovery to find by a preponderance of the evidence that ITW exposed Plaintiff to asbestos. Accordingly, causation cannot be established and summary judgment is appropriate.”].)

            In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that their discovery responses were not factually devoid. (Opp. at pp.7-8 [“Michael Dale Smith’s deposition testimony and verified interrogatory responses show that White Star auto body filler was routinely sanded in his presence from 1959 to 1961, 1964 to 1967, and 1970 to 1972. Plaintiff’s interrogatory responses identify Mr. Smith as the product identification witness, ITW’s Persons Most Knowledgeable and records custodians as witnesses with knowledge of the talc content in Evercoat White Star, and Plaintiff’s experts as witnesses with knowledge of the asbestos content in the talc in Evercoat White Star and the propensity of the products to release asbestos during activities such as sanding.”].)

            In reply, ITW reiterates that it properly shifted its burden. (Reply at pp.2-3.)

ITW fails to meet its initial burden to show that Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that Mr. Smith was exposed to asbestos by an asbestos-containing product for which it was responsible. Plaintiffs’ discovery responses were required to provide a factual basis to show products for which ITW was responsible were present at Mr. Smith’s worksite and that these products contained asbestos. (Turley, supra, 18 Cal.App.5th at p. 980, Collin, supra, 228 Cal.App.4th at p. 593.) Here, when ITW asked Plaintiffs to “STATE ALL FACTS that support [their] contention that [Mr. Smith was] exposed to ASBESTOS by ITW,” Plaintiffs responded as follows:

“Mr. Smith was exposed to asbestos from Evercoat White Star body filler while visiting his father’s job sites from the late 1950s through the early 1970s. Mr. Smith first encountered autobody mechanics working with Evercoat White Star autobody filer while visiting his father at Three-Way Chevrolet in Bakersfield, California. From approximately 1959 until 1961, Mr. Smith visited his father at Three-Way Chevrolet 3-4 days per week after school and most days during his summer vacations. Mr. Smith spent a lot of time visiting his father because his mother was frequently taking Mr. Smith’s younger sister to treatment and therapy for polio. While visiting his father in the body shop, Mr. Smith saw mechanics installing body filler and primer on “dozens and dozens” of occasions.

Mr. Smith next encountered autobody mechanics working with Evercoat White Star when he visited his father at Ventura Autobody in Ventura, California, from the summer of 1964 through the summer of 1967. He spent three two-week periods visiting his father at the body shop during the summer of 1964. He visited his father at Ventura Autobody after school approximately four days per week during the school year and 3-4 days per week during the summers of 1965 and 1966. During those visits, he was within feet of autobody mechanics applying and sanding body filler on ‘dozens and dozens’ of occasions. Mr. Smith also encountered autobody mechanics working with Evercoat White Star when visiting autobody shops with his father during the summers of 1969, 1970, 1971, and 1972. His father was working as an appraiser for Lee Stormont Insurance during those visits. During those visits, which usually lasted a couple of hours each, Mr. Smith observed workers applying and sanding body fillers and primers.

The Evercoat White Star body filler was in gallon-sized cans and had to be mixed with hardener, which came packaged in tubes, prior to installation. Mr. Smith testified that the autobody mechanics sanded the autobody fillers, including Evercoat White Star body filler, with an electric sander after it dried and then sanded by hand for detail work. Mr. Smith was exposed to dust from Evercoat White Star body filler on ‘dozens and dozens’ of occasions while visiting his father at Three-Way Chevrolet, Ventura Autobody, and various body shops with his father between 1969 and 1972. ITW automotive primers, sealers, and paints contained asbestos during the relevant years. ITW automotive repair products, including Evercoat White Star body filler, used at Plaintiff’s father’s jobsites between 1969 and 1972, contained industrial talc that was contaminated with asbestos supplied by Vermont Talc, Engelhard Minerals & Chemicals Corp., Barretts Minerals Inc., Minerals Technologies, Inc., Imerys Talc America, Inc., Pfizer, Inc., Luzenac America, Inc., Cyprus Industrial Minerals, Co., and Rio Tinto Minerals. (Vermont Talc Inc’s Responses to Plaintiff’s Interrogatories directed to Defendant served on March 13, 2026 in Douglas B. Young, et al. v. 3M Company, et al., Commonwealth of Massachusetts, Middlesex, Civil Action No. 25-0944, confirming it sold talc in 1976, 1977, 1979, 1981, 1982, 1983, 1984, 1985, 1988; and Evercoat Polyester Glazing Putty MSDS dated February 21, 2019 (10-30% talc)).

Scientific data, corporate documents, witnesses with knowledge and Plaintiffs’ experts confirm that the talc sourced from Montana mines for these products, was contaminated with asbestos. As explained by Dr. Rohl, et al: ‘The mineral talc is a hydrous magnesium sheet silicate that occurs in both platy and fibrous crystal forms. Talc tends to occur in rock masses coexisting with a number of other hydrous magnesium silicate minerals. Typically, talc deposits consist of fine grained, intergrown mixtures of minerals which may contain considerable amounts of asbestos.’ Rohl, Asbestos in Talc, Environmental Health Perspectives Journal, Volume 9, at pp. 129-132, 1974.) Thus, the processes used to mine talc ore (blasting, drilling) and mill talc ore (crushing and pulverizing) result in the presence of asbestos minerals, such as chrysotile and amphiboles, in talc sold for use in commercial industry and consumer products. See Rohl, Asbestos in Talc, at p. 129: ‘Since the mining of talc rock almost invariably includes the mining of asbestos as well, the asbestos contaminant may be carried over into the consumer product and thus introduce the risk of asbestos disease.’”

(Weiss Decl., Exhibit C at pp.2-10.) The responses also include extensive references to expert testimony, historical testing, and scientific articles showing talc mined in Montana is contaminated with asbestos. (Id.)

            ITW also directs the court’s attention to Mr. Smith’s deposition testimony wherein he testified that he had never heard of ITW, had no personal knowledge that he was exposed to asbestos by an ITW product, and had never spoken with an ITW representative. (Weiss Decl., Exh. D at 498:24-499:6, 499:19-500:6 [“Q:…I represent a company called Illinois Tool Works, Inc. Have you ever heard of that company before? A: No. Q:…Do you associate any products or services with the name Illinois Tool Works, Inc.? A: No…Q: Do you have any personal knowledge, sir, that you were exposed to asbestos as a result of any product or service attributable to Illinois Tool Works, Inc.? A: No…Q: Have you ever spoken with a representative of Illinois Tool Works, Inc. that you know of? A: No.”].) Mr. Smith had no knowledge that anyone at ITW acted with conscious disregard for his safety, that ITW acted with an intent to harm him, or that ITW acted with fraud or malice toward him. (Id. at 500:7-19.)

            Plaintiffs’ discovery responses are not factually devoid. Plaintiffs’ response to a comprehensive interrogatory requesting all facts supporting their contention that Mr. Smith was exposed to asbestos by an ITW product contained detailed facts describing how Mr. Smith was exposed to Evercoat from his visits to his father’s body shop from 1959-1961, 1964-1967, and 1969-1972. (Weiss Decl., Exhibit C at pp.2-10.) The response explains that Mr. Smith encountered mechanics at the shop sanding the dried Evercoat product on dozens of occasions. (Id.) The response also specified that the Evercoat product Mr. Smith encountered contained industrial talc supplied by “Vermont Talc, Engelhard Minerals & Chemicals Corp., Barretts Minerals Inc., Minerals Technologies, Inc., Imerys Talc America, Inc., Pfizer, Inc., Luzenac America, Inc., Cyprus Industrial Minerals, Co., and Rio Tinto Minerals.” (Id.) The response further specifies that these talc suppliers obtained their talc from mines in Montana and cites specific testing records, articles, and expert testimony showing that the talc mined in Montana is contaminated with asbestos. (Id.) The response contains specific facts, references specific documents, and names specific witnesses to support Plaintiffs’ contention that Mr. Smith was exposed to asbestos by ITW’s Evercoat body filler product. Even if Mr. Smith had no personal knowledge at his deposition of whether the Evercoat he encountered contained asbestos, the interrogatory response points to specific evidence showing the Evercoat body filler contained asbestos, that the product was present at his father’s worksite, and that Mr. Smith was exposed to dust produced when the product was sanded. Therefore, Plaintiffs’ discovery responses contain sufficient facts to support their claims against ITW.

Accordingly, ITW fails to meet its initial burden to show that Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their claims against ITW. The motion for summary judgment is denied.

C.    Motions for Summary Adjudication

ITW moves for summary adjudication of Plaintiffs’ cause of action for intentional concealment and Plaintiffs’ demand for punitive damages. Plaintiffs did not oppose ITW’s motions for summary adjudication. (Opp. at p.1.) Accordingly, the motions for summary adjudication are granted as unopposed.

 

III.           Conclusion

ITW fails to meet its initial burden to show that Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their contention that Mr. Smith was exposed to an asbestos-containing product for which it is responsible. The motion for summary judgment is denied.

ITW’s motions for summary adjudication to the cause of action for intentional concealment and the demand for punitive damages are granted as unopposed.

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice.

    PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR TRIAL PREFERENCE

     

 

 

I.                Background

 

On June 4, 2026, Plaintiff Carole E. Gordon filed her complaint for personal injury alleging she developed malignant mesothelioma (with sarcomatoid features) from non-occupational exposure to asbestos and asbestos-containing cosmetic products. On July 21, 2026, Ms. Gordon filed this amended motion for trial preference under Code of Civil Procedure section 36, subdivision (a). Ms. Gordon argues that she is 85-years old and her health is such that preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing her interests in the litigation. Ms. Gordon requests a trial be set 120 days from the date of the hearing on this matter.

 

On August 3, 2026, Defendant  Merle Norman Cosmetics, Inc. (“ Merle Norman”) filed an opposition.  Merle Norman opposed the motion on the grounds that Ms. Gordon has not presented sufficient evidence that her health is such that preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing Ms. Gordon’s interests in the litigation. Alternatively, Merle Norman argues that if the court were to grant preference, specific conditions should be imposed, such as for trial date timing and discovery considerations.

 

Ms. Gordon replied on August 7, 2026. She argued that preference under section 36, subdivision (a) is mandatory and  Merle Norman mistakenly contended that preference is discretionary. Ms. Gordon further argues that  Merle Norman failed to provide evidence that Ms. Gordon’s health is such that a preference is not necessary to prevent prejudice.

 

Ms. Gordon presented sufficient evidence to show that her health is such that preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing her interests in the litigation. Therefore, Ms. Gordon’s motion for trial preference is granted. Trial is set for Monday, December 7, 2026. The Final Status conference is set for Monday, November 16, 2026. A status conference for the proposed trial setting order is set for Wednesday, August 26, 2026.

 

II.               Discussion

 

A.    Legal Standards

 

A party who is over 70 years old may petition the court for a preference for trial date, which the court shall grant if it makes both of the following findings: (1) the party has a substantial interest in the action as a whole; and (2) the health of the party is such that a preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing the party’s interest in the litigation. (Code Civ. Proc., § 36, subd. (a).) An affidavit submitted in support of a motion for preference under this subdivision may be signed by the attorney for the party seeking preference based upon information and belief as to the medical diagnosis and prognosis of any party. (Id., § 36.5.)

 

“Upon the granting of such a motion for preference, the court shall set the matter for trial not more than 120 days from that date and there shall be no continuance beyond 120 days from the granting of the motion for preference except for physical disability of a party or a party’s attorney, or upon a showing of good cause stated in the record.”  (Id., § 36, subd. (f).)  “Any continuance shall be for no more than 15 days and no more than one continuance for physical disability may be granted to any party.” (Ibid.)

 

B.    Analysis

 

Ms. Gordon is eighty-five years old and was diagnosed with malignant mesothelioma of the pleura, sarcomatoid type, in February 2026. (Moline Decl., ¶¶ 4, 6, 12-13, Exh. C.) Ms. Gordon has a history of “left-breast ductal carcinoma in situ” that was treated with “bilateral mastectomies in 2013.” (Id., ¶ 4.) On January 26, 2026, a PET/CT scan demonstrated “FDG-avid left cardiophrenic lymph nodes (the largest measuring approximately 5 x 10 mm, with an SUV max of 5.4); a small-to-moderate left pleural effusion with an intensely FDG-avid soft-tissue pleural nodule measuring approximately 17 x 11 mm (SUV max 10.6); scattered left fissural nodularity; and focal moderate-to-intense FDG activity within the sacrum without a CT correlate (SUV max 4).” (Id., ¶ 5, Exh. B, Tab 1.) On February 14, 2026, Ms. Gordon’s pathologic examination (left pleural biopsy) was finalized, showing: “malignant mesothelioma with sarcomatoid features (at least 20%), and the left pleural mass biopsy demonstrated malignant mesothelioma with transitional features.” (Id., ¶ 6, Exhs. B, Tab 4, C [“mesotheliomas with transitional features are considered a subset of sarcomatoid mesotheliomas.1 Immunohistochemical staining of the tumor cells was positive for calretinin, WT-1, D2 40, CK5/6, and GATA3, and negative for claudin-4, BerEP4, and MOC31, with loss of BAP1 expression.”].)

Ms. Gordon was found to not be a surgical candidate on February 26, 2026. (Id., ¶ 7 [“Progression of her disease, therefore, cannot be slowed through surgical intervention.”].) On March 11, 2026, Ms. Gordon began non-curative “first-line systematic immunotherapy with nivolumab and ipilimumab.” (Id., ¶ 8, Exh. B, Tab 6.) Ms. Gordon received additional immunotherapy on April 1, April 22, and May 13, 2026, and continues to receive said therapy about every three weeks. (Id., ¶ 9, Exh. B, Tabs 7-8.) Ms. Gordon also has an indwelling tunneled pleural catheter to help manage her recurrent malignant left pleural effusion, which is drained weekly. (Ibid.)

On May 29, 2026, Ms. Gordon had a follow-up PET/CT scan. (Id., ¶ 10.) The study showed “a near-complete resolution of the previously intensely FDG-avid pleural nodule, with only a residual subtle 4 mm soft-tissue density below the threshold for FDG evaluation, and an interval resolution of the previously prominent FDG-avid left cardiophrenic lymph nodes.” (Ibid.) The scan “also showed persistent left fissural nodularity concerning for pleural carcinomatosis and metastatic disease; persistent intense focal FDG avidity in the sacrum without a definite CT correlate, for which osseous metastatic disease was not excluded; and a subcentimeter left axillary lymph node with a mild interval increase in FDG uptake.” (Ibid.) On June 2, 2026, Ms. Gordon’s oncologist determined that the imaging “showed a good response after four doses of immunotherapy.” (Id., ¶ 11, Exh. B, Tab 9.) However, because Ms. Gordon developed “immune-related adverse effects of treatment,” like pneumonitis and pruritic dermatitis, the ipilimumab was discontinued. (Ibid.)

As of June 2, 2026, Ms. Gordon’s Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status was 2 and her diagnoses has been characterized as “carrying a high risk of complication, morbidity, or mortality.” (Ibid.) Dr. Moline opines that “there is substantial medical doubt that Ms. Gordon will survive beyond six (6) months from the date of this declaration.” (Id., ¶ 14.)

 Merle Norman is the only Defendant to oppose Ms. Gordon’s motion. In opposition, it argues that Ms. Gordon has failed to meet the statute’s requirements:

 

“As shown above, the last two purported medical records of Ms. Gordon establish: (1) Plaintiff is ‘doing well overall,’  is ‘still able to perform ADLs’ and goes ‘out with friends and do other activities; (2) had a good response to chemotherapy; (3) that her PET/CT of May 29, 2026 shows ‘Near complete resolution of prior intensely FDG avid pleural nodule with a residual subtle 4 mm soft tissue density below the threshold for FDG evaluation’; and (4) Plaintiff’s physical exam was normal, including plaintiff’s respiratory system, Plaintiff was to resume infusions in mid-June and will have her next scan in August.

 

Importantly, what Plaintiff fails to present to the court is any actual medical evidence (or really any information whatsoever) regarding: 1) Whether Plaintiff’s symptoms and/or disease have been getting worse; 2) Plaintiff’s current diagnosis.”

(Opp., at p. 4 [emphasis in original].)

 

 Merle Norman further argues that the declarations of Ms. Gordon’s attorney and non-treating expert doctor “fail to establish that Plaintiff’s health is in immediate peril or that her present condition puts her at imminent risk of being unable to participate meaningfully at trial in this matter.” (Ibid.) Pointing to Ms. Gordon’s scheduled PET/CT scan in August,  Merle Norman argues the motion should be continued to allow for the results to be shared. (Id., at p. 5.)

 

The evidence before the court is sufficient to establish that Ms. Gordon is entitled to a trial preference under Code of Civil Procedure section 36, subdivision (a). There is no dispute that Ms. Gordon’s age is above the threshold required under the statute, or that she has a substantial interest in the litigation. The only issue in dispute is whether her health is such that her interest in the litigation is likely to be prejudiced in the absence of trial preference. 

 

The court finds that the evidence presented is sufficient to satisfy this burden. The declarations of Mr. Horowitz, Dr. Moline, and attached medical records are sufficient to show that Ms. Gordon’s current symptoms and the likely progression of her symptoms given the absence of viable treatment will impact her testimonial capacities to her prejudice. For instance, Dr. Moline states that due to the aggressive nature of Ms. Gordon’s mesothelioma and her increased age, her physical strength is diminished. (Moline Decl. ¶ 12 [“Although her most recent imaging reflects a favorable radiographic response of the dominant pleural nodule, responses in sarcomatoid mesothelioma are characteristically shallow and short-lived. Her residual and probable metastatic disease, limited tolerance of treatment, advanced age, and diminished performance status leave her with little physiologic reserve. Her symptoms are expected to progress as her disease advances, ultimately resulting in her death.”].) This, paired with the evidence that Ms. Gordon lacks viable treatment options and the extremely aggressive nature of malignant mesothelioma of the pleura, sarcomatoid type, is sufficient to persuade the court that her health is such that Ms. Gordon’s interest in the litigation will be prejudiced without trial preference.  

 

Moreover, Ms. Gordon was not required to present a declaration from a treating physician. (Code Civ. Proc., § 36.5 [“An affidavit submitted in support of a motion for preference under subdivision (a) of Section 36 may be signed by the attorney for the party seeking preference based upon information and belief as to the medical diagnosis and prognosis of any party. The affidavit is not admissible for any purpose other than a motion for preference under subdivision (a) of Section 36.”].) Dr. Moline’s and counsel’s declarations are sufficient. Accordingly, Ms. Gordon’s motion for trial preference is granted.

 

 Merle Norman’s opposition also requests that the court impose certain conditions as part of the trial setting order including, but not limited to, shortening the motion for summary judgment and/or adjudication notice period, ordering third-party witnesses’ immediate availability for depositions, and ordering Ms. Gordon to provide any outstanding relevant records. The court lacks the power to shorten notice for summary judgment. (McMahon v. Superior Court (2003) 106 Cal.app.4th 112, 116.) Merle Norman cites no authority for the proposition that the court can impose such conditions upon the parties as part of an order granting trial setting preference. The court expects the parties to meet and confer regarding the appropriate conditions for a proposed trial setting order. Ms. Gordon’s motion indicates that she is willing to agree to certain stipulations and to extend all discovery deadlines past the statutory cut-offs. (Motion at p. 8; Horowitz Decl., ¶¶ 9-10.) If there are disputes as to the appropriate conditions, the court will rule on these disputes at the status conference regarding the trial setting order.
