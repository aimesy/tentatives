Case Number: 19NWCV00885    Hearing Date: July 2, 2026    Dept: SED

LAKEVIEW LOAN SERVICING, LLC V. NATALIE HERNANDEZ, ET AL.

CASE NO.:  19NWCV00885

HEARING:  07/02/2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#6

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant Natalie Hernandez’s motion to vacate default judgment is DENIED.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant Natalie Hernandez (Hernandez) moves for an order vacating the default judgment entered on July 18, 2025, on the grounds that the judgment is void due to lack of proper service and lack of personal jurisdiction.  Hernandez also seeks a stay of enforcement pending hearing on this motion.

 

Background

 

In November 2019, Plaintiff Lakeview Loan Servicing (Lakeview) filed a foreclosure action against Hernandez, Jayreiner Mangahis Hernandez, Gilberto Herrera, Foundation Finance Company LLC, State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Company, and Does 1 through 50, inclusive (collectively, Defendants).

 

On February 21, 2020, at Lakeview’s request, default was entered to Hernandez. 

 

On October 9, 2023, Hernandez filed a “motion to set aside the judgment and any default, and to quash any writ of possession/execution.”

 

On January 23, 2024, Lakeview requested entry of default judgment against Hernandez.

 

On February 2, 2024, default judgment was entered against Defendants.

 

On April 26, 2024, the Court denied Hernandez’s motion to set aside the default or default judgment entered on February 21, 2020, but it granted the motion as to the default judgment entered on February 2, 2024. 

 

On August 14, 2024, Lakeview again requested entry of default judgment.

 

On October 25, 2024, Hernandez filed a second motion to set aside or vacate default.

 

On March 17, 2025, the Court denied Hernandez’s motion.

 

On April 8, 2025, Hernandez appealed the denial of her motion.

 

On July 18, 2025, the Court held a default prove up hearing and entered a default judgment of foreclosure and order of sale in favor of Lakeview against Hernandez. 

 

On April 13, 2026, Hernandez’s appeal was dismissed on the grounds that there was no judgment at the time Hernandez filed her notice of appeal from the denial order.

 

On April 14, 2026, Hernandez filed the instant motion to vacate void judgment.

 

Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure (CCP) section 473(d) provides that “[t]he court may . . . on motion of either party after notice to the other party, set aside any void judgment or order.”  (CCP, § 473(d).)  In circumstances where a court lacks jurisdiction, a judgment or order is void.  (People v. American Contractors Indemnity Co. (2004) 33 Cal.4th 653, 660.)  Where a court exceeds its jurisdiction, then “the order is merely voidable, and it cannot be collaterally attacked once it is final, i.e., after the time to appeal has run.”  (Torjesen v. Mansdorf (2016) 1 Cal.App.5th 111, 116.)

 

Timeliness

 

All of Hernandez’s moving papers, including declarations, were due on or before June 9, 2026.  (CCP § 1005(b) [“all moving and supporting papers shall be served and filed at least 16 court days before the hearing”].)  Hernandez filed a supplemental declaration on June 12, 2026.  Accordingly, the supplemental declaration is untimely and the Court declines to consider it in its tentative ruling.

 

Analysis

 

Hernandez seeks an order vacating the July 18, 2025 default judgment on the ground that it is void due to lack of proper service and lack of personal jurisdiction pursuant to CCP section 473(d).  Hernandez contends that she was never personally served with the summons and complaint in this action and did not receive notice of the lawsuit before entry of default.

 

The Court notes that, although Hernandez characterizes the instant motion as one to vacate the July 18, 2025 default judgment as void pursuant to section 473(d), the motion is predicated on the same jurisdictional challenge previously asserted in Hernandez's prior motions to set aside the default and default judgment.  In each of those motions, Hernandez argued that she was never properly served with the summons and complaint, that the Court therefore lacked personal jurisdiction, and that the default and resulting judgment were void.  Those arguments have already been considered and rejected by the Court. 

 

On April 26, 2024, the Court denied Hernandez's motion to set aside the February 21, 2020 default, concluding that Hernandez failed to establish any statutory or equitable basis for relief.  (See April 26, 2024 Minute Order.)  Although the Court vacated the February 2, 2024 default judgment because it had been entered while Hernandez's motion remained pending, the Court expressly denied relief from the underlying default, finding that Hernandez had offered no argument or evidence demonstrating that the default judgment was void on its face and had otherwise failed to establish entitlement to relief under CCP sections 473, 473.5, or the Court's equitable powers.  (Ibid.)  The Court further held that the February 21, 2020 default would remain in effect and expressly authorized Plaintiff to seek entry of a new default judgment.  (Ibid.)  Thereafter, Hernandez filed a second motion to set aside or vacate the default and default judgment, again arguing that she had not been properly served and that the Court lacked jurisdiction.  On March 17, 2025, the Court denied that motion in its entirety.  (See March 17, 2025 Minute Order.) 

 

Hernandez identifies no new admissible facts, changed circumstances, or intervening law that would warrant revisiting those prior determinations.  Although Hernandez submitted a supplemental declaration in support of the instant motion, the declaration was untimely filed after the deadline for moving papers, and the Court declines to consider it.  Accordingly, the instant motion presents no new evidentiary basis for concluding that the Court lacked personal jurisdiction or that the July 18, 2025 default judgment is void.

 

Although successive motions for relief under section 473 are not categorically barred, a party may not repeatedly relitigate the same grounds for relief without presenting new matter unavailable at the time of the prior motion.  (Rambush v. Rambush (1968) 267 Cal.App.2d 734, 741-742.)  Here, Hernandez identifies no new evidence, changed circumstances, or intervening authority that was unavailable when the Court denied her prior motions.  Rather, the instant motion simply renews the same contention that the Court lacked personal jurisdiction because she was not properly served.

 

Nor does the rule permitting a void judgment to be challenged "at any time" authorize perpetual successive motions raising the identical jurisdictional challenge.  As the court observed in Pittman v. Beck Park Apartments Ltd. (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1009, once a court has decided the jurisdictional issue on the merits, subsequent attempts to relitigate the same issue implicate principles of claim and issue preclusion because "somewhere along the line, litigation must cease."  (Id. at 1022, fn. 13.)

 

Hernandez has presented no new admissible evidence or legal basis warranting reconsideration of the Court's prior rulings.  Accordingly, she has failed to establish that the July 18, 2025 default judgment is void within the meaning of Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d).

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant Natalie Hernandez’s motion to vacate default judgment is DENIED.


Case Number: 22STCV30142    Hearing Date: July 2, 2026    Dept: SED

ELIZABETH SANCHEZ V. US VI DOWNEY, LLC, ET AL.

CASE NO.:  22STCV30142

HEARING:  07/02/2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#7

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Plaintiff Elizabeth Sanchez’s motion for reconsideration is DENIED.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Plaintiff Elizabeth Sanchez (Plaintiff) moves this Court to reconsider its February 17, 2026 Order.

 

Background

 

This is a premises liability action arising out of an alleged slip-and-fall incident that occurred on or about November 21, 2021, at a commercial shopping center located at 9050 Apollo Way in Downey, California.  Plaintiff alleges that she fell into a drainage area or sinkhole in the parking lot outside a T.J. Maxx store and sustained personal injuries as a result.

 

Plaintiff filed this action on September 15, 2022, against Defendants US VI Downey, LLC, City of Downey and Does 1 through 25, inclusive, asserting two causes of action: (1) Negligence and (2) Dangerous Condition of Public Property and Statutory Liability of Government Entities.

 

On November 6, 2025, Plaintiff filed a notice of settlement of the entire case which vacated all dates in this case, including the trial date.

 

On December 17, 2026, Defendant US VI Downey, LLC (Defendant) filed a motion to enforce settlement.

 

On February 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed an ex parte application seeking a continuance to retain new counsel evaluate the settlement, to oppose the instant motion, and to prepare for trial.  The Court denied Plaintiff’s ex parte application that same day.

 

On February 17, 2026, the Court found that the parties entered into a valid and binding settlement agreement, and enforcement under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 is warranted.  Thus, the Court granted Defendant’s motion to enforce the settlement.  The Court also ordered the case dismissed without prejudiced.

 

On April 17, 2026, Defendant filed a notice of entry of judgment.

 

On May 4, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion for reconsideration.

 

No opposition has been filed.

 

Legal Standard

 

“A party who originally made an application for an order which was refused in whole or part, or granted conditionally or on terms, may make a subsequent application for the same order upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, in which case it shall be shown by affidavit what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (b).)

 

Analysis

 

Plaintiff moves the Court for reconsideration of the February 17, 2026 Order.

 

On April 27, 2026, the Clerk served notice that Plaintiff had filed a Notice of Appeal from the Court's February 17, 2026 Order.

 

“…[T]he¿perfecting¿of an appeal stays proceedings in the trial court upon the judgment or order appealed from or upon the matters embraced therein or affected thereby, including enforcement of the judgment or order, but the trial court may proceed upon any other matter embraced in the action and not affected by the judgment or order.”  (Code of Civil Procedure¿§¿916, subd. (a).)  The automatic stay applies if¿the trial court proceeding¿“directly or indirectly seek[s]¿to enforce, vacate or modify appealed judgment or order[,]…[when]¿[a] trial court proceeding also affects the effectiveness of an appeal if the possible outcomes on appeal and the actual or possible results of the proceeding are irreconcilable…[or when] a¿proceeding affects the effectiveness of the appeal if the very purpose of the appeal is to avoid the need for that proceeding.”  (Varian Medical Systems, Inc. v. Delfino¿(2005) 35 Cal.4th 180, 189-90.)¿

 

Here, Plaintiff seeks reconsideration of the very order that is the subject of the pending appeal.  A ruling on the instant motion would necessarily modify, vacate, or otherwise affect the appealed order and would therefore affect the effectiveness of the appeal.  Accordingly, Code of Civil Procedure section 916 divests this Court of jurisdiction to rule on Plaintiff's motion for reconsideration while the appeal is pending.

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff Elizabeth Sanchez’s motion for reconsideration is DENIED.  

Case Number: 25NWCV03816    Hearing Date: July 2, 2026    Dept: SED

VANESSA A. ARAUJO, ET AL. V. GENERAL MOTORS LLC

CASE NO.:  25NWCV03816

HEARING:  07/02/2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#8

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant General Motors LLC’s demurrer is OVERRULED.

 

Defendant General Motors LLC’s motion to strike is GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Plaintiffs Vanessa A. Araujo and Daniel Picazo shall file and serve a First Amended Complaint within 30 days of notice of this ruling.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant General Motors LLC (Defendant) demurs to the Complaint filed by Plaintiffs Vanessa A. Araujo and Daniel Picazo (collectively, Plaintiffs).

 

Defendant also moves to strike portions of Plaintiffs’ Complaint.

 

Background

 

This is a lemon law action.  On October 27, 2025, Plaintiffs filed the operative Complaint against Defendant and Does 1 through 10, inclusive, asserting five causes of action: (1) Violation of Subdivision (d) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (2) Violation of Subdivision (b) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (3) Violation of Subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (4) Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability; and (5) Fraudulent Inducement—Concealment.

 

Legal Standard

 

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. ¿ (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.”  (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.)  As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Id.)  The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

 

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.”  (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245). 

 

Plaintiff is only required to allege ultimate facts, not evidentiary facts.  (See Committee on Children's Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212 [“the complaint should set forth the ultimate facts constituting the cause of action, not the evidence by which plaintiff proposes to prove those facts”); 1 Cal. Affirmative Def. § 10:2 (2d ed.) [allegations of agency, course and scope of employment, etc. qualify as ultimate facts].)  Plaintiff’s allegations must be accepted as true on demurrer.  (See Yvanova v. New Century Mortgage Corp. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 919, 924 [“For purposes of reviewing a demurrer, we accept the truth of material facts properly pleaded in the operative complaint”].)

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a) requires the moving party to meet and confer “in person, by video conference, or by telephone” in an attempt to resolve a demurrer’s deficiencies five days before the responsive pleading is due.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(2).)

 

The Court finds that Defendant has fulfilled the meet and confer requirement.  (Goh Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.) 

 

Demurrer

 

Defendant demurs as to the fifth cause of action for Fraudulent Concealment asserted in Plaintiffs’ Complaint on the grounds that (1) it fails to allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action under section 430.10(a) of the Code of Civil Procedure, and (2) it is barred by the Economic Loss Rule and Independent Tort Principle.  (Notice p. 2.) 

 

“[T]he elements of a cause of action for fraud and deceit based on concealment are: (1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (f) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.”  (Marketing West, Inc. v. Sanyo Fisher (USA) Corp. (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 603, 612-613.)  “Fraud must be pleaded with specificity… [t]o withstand a demurrer, the facts constituting every element of the fraud must be alleged with particularity, and the claim cannot be salvaged by references to the general policy favoring the liberal construction of pleadings.  (Goldrich v. Natural Y Surgical Specialties, Inc. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 772, 782.)  “This particularity requirement necessitates pleading facts which ‘show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.’”  (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 73.)

 

          Sufficiency of Allegations

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiffs’ allegations fails to allege with particularity the defect Defendant allegedly concealed and that Plaintiffs fail to identify by name who made the alleged omissions when they purchased the Subject Vehicle.  (Motion p. 9.) 

 

In Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 844, the Court found that Plaintiff’s allegation that the “CVT transmissions were defective in that they caused such problems as hesitation, shaking, jerking, and failure to function” to be sufficient.  (Id.)  Here, Plaintiffs allege that, “Plaintiffs are informed and believe, and based thereon allege, that the 3.0L engine and/or its related components installed in the Subject Vehicle suffer from one or more defects that can result in loss of power, stalling, engine running rough, engine misfire(s), failure or replacement of the engine (the "Engine Defect").  (Complaint ¶ 47.)  Plaintiff clearly pleads the Subject Vehicle contained an “Engine Defect.” 

 

Defendant also relies on the general rule for pleading fraud against a corporation, which requires a plaintiff to allege the identity of the speaker, their authority, to whom they spoke, what they said, and when it was said.  (See, e.g., Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157.)  However, this rule applies differently when the claim of fraud is based upon an assertion of fraudulent concealment.  (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Association, Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384 [“How does one show ‘how’ and ‘by what means’ something didn’t happen, or ‘when’ it never happened, or ‘where’ it never happened?”].)  Instead, such details, in the context of a fraudulent concealment claim, “are properly the subject of discovery, not demurrer.”  (Id. at 1384-85.) 

 

With the exception of Defendant's challenge to the existence of a duty to disclose, the Court finds the Complaint adequately pleads the remaining elements of fraudulent concealment.  Specifically, the Complaint alleges that Plaintiffs entered into a warranty contract with Defendant (Complaint ¶ 6), Defendant knew certain material facts (i.e., the 3.0L Engine Defect) prior to Plaintiffs purchasing the Subject Vehicle and intentionally withheld such facts from Plaintiffs (Id. at ¶¶ 51, 53, 57), Defendant had superior and exclusive knowledge of such facts (Id. at ¶¶ 56, 57-59), Plaintiffs relied on the non-disclosure (Id. at ¶¶ 54, 55, 61), and damages (Id. at ¶¶ 55, 61). 

 

The Court finds such allegations are adequate, and the demurrer may not be sustained on particularly grounds.¿

 

          Duty to Disclose

 

Defendant also argues that Plaintiffs failed to allege facts to establish duty to disclose.  (Motion pp. 9-12.)

 

When a duty to disclose is based on exclusive knowledge, such exclusive knowledge “presuppose a preexisting relationship between the parties, such as between seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement.”  (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (Rattagan) (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 40, internal quotations and citations omitted.)  “Such a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.”  (Ibid., citing Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 312.)

 

Firstly, the Court acknowledges Plaintiffs' reliance on authority suggesting that direct privity is not required for a duty to disclose.  (See OCM Principal Opportunities Fund, L.P. v. CIB World Markets Corp.¿(2007)¿157 Cal.App.4th 835, 859 [holding a vender “has a duty to disclose material facts not only to the immediate purchasers, but also to subsequent purchaser when the vendor has reason to expect that the item will be resold”].)¿¿

 

Regardless, the Court need not resolve the scope of those authorities because Plaintiffs allege a direct contractual relationship with Defendant arising from the express warranty.  (Complaint ¶ 6.)  Under Rattagan, a contractual agreement constitutes the type of preexisting relationship that may support a duty to disclose.  (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at 40.)  The relationship arising from the Warranty provided by Defendant specifically to Plaintiffs, and not merely to the¿general public, constitutes a sufficient transaction supporting a duty to disclose.  (See¿Scherer v. FCA US, LLC¿(S.D. Cal. 2021) 565 F.Supp.3d 1184, 1194 [discussing the¿Bigler-Engler¿case and holding that a duty to disclose was properly grounded in the parties’ contractual relationship reflected in the warranty agreement].)¿

 

Accordingly, the Court finds Plaintiffs have adequately alleged a preexisting transactional relationship sufficient to support Defendant's alleged duty to disclose at the pleading stage.

 

          Economic Loss Rule

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiffs’ fraudulent concealment cause of action is barred by the economic loss rule under Rattagan.  Specifically, the “risk that the engine in Plaintiffs’ vehicle might be defective is precisely one of the risks contemplated and allocated to [Defendant] when Plaintiff[s] accepted [Defendant’s] warranty promises.”  (Motion pp. 14-16.)  Thus, Plaintiffs are limited to contractual remedies only pursuant to Rattagan.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the economic loss does not apply to claims for fraudulent inducement, regardless of whether affirmative misrepresentation or concealment is involved.  (Opp., p. 6.)  Plaintiffs also argue that if were the Rattagan test applied, the economic loss rule still does not apply to the intentional tort alleged.  (Id. at pp. 8-10.)  Further, permitting their fraudulent concealment claim to proceed advances California public policy by holding manufacturers accountable for concealing known defects from consumers during the sale of vehicles.  (Id. at p. 10.) 

 

“A plaintiff may assert a tort claim for fraudulent concealment based on conduct occurring in the course of a contractual relationship, if the elements of the cause of action can be established independently of the parties' contractual rights and obligations and the tortious conduct exposes the plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond the reasonable contemplation of the parties when they entered into the agreement.”  (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at 38.)

 

The Court finds that Plaintiffs allege fraudulent inducement based on omissions that occurred prior to the sale of the Subject Vehicle.  (Complaint ¶¶ 51, 53, 57.)

 

To the extent alleged concealment occurred during the course of the contract, the Court finds that a party purchasing a vehicle does not bargain with the expectation that the automotive manufacturer is concealing known material defects from them.  The risks of such a concealment are not allocated within the warranty.  There are also public policy reasons, exclusive of the contract, for not wanting an automotive manufacturer to withhold information on such defects, including safety.  As noted by the Rattagan court, public policy has viewed fraudulent concealment on equal footing with affirmative misrepresentations.  (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at 26.)  These public policy reasons are exclusive of the contract, and therefore tort liability would exist in such instances of fraudulent concealment.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ fifth cause of action is therefore not barred by the economic loss rule.

 

Motion to Strike

 

Defendant moves to strike designated portions of the Complaint on the grounds that it contains improperly pled requests for civil penalties in violation of Code of Civil Procedure (CCP) section 871.24.  (MTS Notice p. 1.) 

 

A motion to strike lies either when (1) there is “irrelevant, false or improper matter inserted in any pleading”; or (2) to strike any pleading or part thereof “not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule or order of court.”  (CCP § 436.)¿

 

“At least 30 days prior to the commencement of an action seeking civil penalties under subdivision (c) of Section 1794 of the Civil Code, the consumer shall do all of the following: (1) Notify the manufacturer of the consumer's name, the accurate Vehicle Identification Number (VIN) of the motor vehicle, and a brief summary of the repair history and problems with the motor vehicle. (2) Demand that the manufacturer repurchase or replace the motor¿vehicle.”  (CCP § 871.24, subd. (a).)  The failure to¿comply with¿the pre-lawsuit notification requirement precludes recovery of civil penalties for a Song-Beverly claim.  (CCP § 871.24, subd. (h).)¿¿

 

Here, Plaintiffs have not alleged compliance with CCP section 871.24(a).  Thus, Defendant’s motion to strike allegations¿pertaining to¿civil penalties is GRANTED.

 

Because Plaintiffs can¿reasonably amend their Complaint to show compliance¿with CCP section 871.24(a), the Court GRANTS LEAVE TO AMEND.¿¿

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant General Motors LLC’s demurrer is OVERRULED.

 

Defendant General Motors LLC’s motion to strike is GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.  

 

Plaintiffs Vanessa A. Araujo and Daniel Picazo shall file and serve a First Amended Complaint within 30 days of notice of this ruling.
