(1)   PLAINTIFF PALOS VERDES BAY CLUB, INC.’S MOTION TO TAKE OFF CALENDAR, OR IN THE ALTERNATIVE TO STRIKE, DEFENDANT CSG/HUNT BROS.’ MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT OR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

(1)   Plaintiff Palos Verdes Bay Club, Inc.’s Motion to Take Off Calendar, or in the Alternative to Strike, Defendant CSG/Hunt Bros.’ Motion for Summary Judgment or Summary Adjudication is DENIED as MOOT pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure 437c, subdivision (a)(3).

(2)   Plaintiff Palos Verdes Bay Club, Inc.’s Request for Monetary Sanctions is DENIED pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 128.5, subdivision (f)(1)(A).

The Court considers the moving papers filed on July 24, 2026, and the notice of withdrawal filed on August 4, 2026.  To date, no opposition brief has been filed.

BACKGROUND

Factual Background

On January 25, 2021, plaintiff Palos Verdes Bay Club, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) filed the Complaint against Construction Service Group Inc. doing business as Hunt Bros Roofing, Bradley Hunt, National Roofing Consultants, Inc., and DOES 1 through 25.

On May 12, 2021, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint against Construction Service Group Inc. dba Hunt Bros Roofing, National Roofing Consultants, Inc., Antis Roofing and Waterproofing, LLC, and DOES 2 through 25.

On July 6, 2021, Plaintiff filed the Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) against Construction Service Group Inc. dba Hunt Bros Roofing, Bradley Hunt, National Roofing Consultants, Inc., Antis Roofing and Waterproofing, LLC, and DOES 2 through 25.  The SAC includes the following causes of action: (1) Negligence and (2) Breach of Implied Warranties.

On July 28, 2021, the parties filed a stipulation to allow James River Insurance Company to file an answer in intervention.  The Court signed the stipulation on the same day.

On September 2, 2021, National Roofing Consultants, Inc. filed a cross-complaint against cross-defendants Construction Service Group Inc. dba Hunt Bros Roofing, Antis Roofing and Waterproofing, LLC, and DOES 1 through 10.  The cross-complaint included the following causes of action: (1) Indemnification; (2) Apportionment of Fault; and (2) Declaratory Relief.

On November 1, 2021, the parties filed a stipulation to allow certain underwriters at Lloyd’s London to file an answer in intervention.  The Court signed the stipulation on the same day.

On August 12, 2022, National Roofing Consultants, Inc. dismissed its cross-complaint without prejudice.

On August 30, 2022, the Court dismissed Bradley Hunt from the SAC without prejudice at Plaintiff’s oral request.

On June 5, 2023, Plaintiff amended the SAC to name Broadstreet Energy Corp. as DOE 2.  On the same day, Plaintiff filed another amendment to the SAC, naming Regen America Inc. as DOE 3.

On August 30, 2023, the Court entered a default against Broadstreet Energy Corp.

On October 30, 2023, Plaintiff amended the SAC to name M. Roy Nelson, Inc. doing business as Horizon Management Company as DOE 4.

On January 16, 2024, Plaintiff dismissed M. Roy Nelson, Inc. dba Horizon Management Company without prejudice.

On May 30, 2024, Regen America Inc. filed the cross-complaint against cross-defendants Palos Verdes Bay Club, Inc., Antis Roofing & Waterproofing, LLC, and DOES 1 through 100.  The cross-complaint includes the following causes of action: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Open Book Account; (3) Account Stated; (4) Quantum Meruit; (5) Conversion; (6) Indemnification; (7) Contribution; (8) Apportionment of Fault; and (9) Declaratory Relief.

On July 8, 2025, February 18, 2026, and May 26, 2026, the parties submitted joint stipulations to waive and extend the five-year statute of limitations for bringing the case to trial.

On June 10, 2026, James Rover Insurance Company (“Defendant”), as Intervenor on behalf of Defendant Construction Service Group, Inc. d/b/a Hunt Bros Roofing, filed the Motion for Summary Judgment, Alternatively Summary Adjudication.  This motion was scheduled to be heard on September 2, 2026.

On June 18, 2026, Defendant filed the Motion to Specially Set Hearing for Summary Judgment/Adjudication, again scheduling the hearing for September 2, 2026.

On June 29, 2026, Defendant filed the Ex Parte Application for Order Shortening Time to Advance Hearing on Motion to Specially Set Motion for Summary Judgment Hearing.  On July 1, 2026, the Court denied this ex parte application.

On July 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Ex Parte Application for Order Specially Setting and Advancing the Hearing on Plaintiff’s Motion to Take Off Calendar Defendant CSG’s Motion for Summary Judgment.  On July 27, 2026, the Court placed this ex parte application off calendar due to defective notice.

Procedural Background

On July 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed this motion.  Following that, on July 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Ex Parte Application for Order Specially Setting and Advancing the Hearing on Plaintiff’s Motion to Take Off Calendar Defendant CSG’s Motion for Summary Judgment.  On July 30, 2026, the Court granted this ex parte application, scheduling the motion to be heard on August 7, 2026.

On August 4, 2026, Defendant filed Notice of Withdrawal of Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment, Alternative Summary Adjudication.  To date, no opposition brief has been filed.

LEGAL STANDARD

“The motion shall be heard no later than 30 days before the date of trial, unless the court for good cause orders otherwise.  The filing of the motion shall not extend the time within which a party must otherwise file a responsive pleading.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(3).)

DISCUSSION

Plaintiff seeks a court order to either remove from the calendar or, in the alternative, to strike Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment, or Alternatively for Summary Adjudication, filed on June 10, 2026, which is currently scheduled for a hearing on September 2, 2026.  (Notice of Motion, p. 2:7-10.)  This request is premised on the assertion that the scheduled hearing date violates Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (a)(3).  (Notice of Motion, p. 2:7-11.)  However, on August 4, 2026, Defendant formally withdrew its motion for summary judgment, or alternatively, motion for summary adjudication.  Consequently, Plaintiff’s motion is MOOT.

Request for Monetary Sanctions

Moreover, Plaintiff requests monetary sanctions in the amount of no less than $4,306.50 in accordance with Code of Civil Procedure section 128.5 and the Court’s inherent authority under Code of Civil Procedure section 128.  (Motion, p. 4:1-22.) 

“A trial court may order a party, the party’s attorney, or both, to pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by another party as a result of actions or tactics, made in bad faith, that are frivolous or solely intended to cause unnecessary delay.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.5, subd. (a).)  “A motion for sanctions under this section shall be made separately from other motions or requests and shall describe the specific alleged action or tactic, made in bad faith, that is frivolous or solely intended to cause unnecessary delay.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.5, subd. (f)(1)(A).)

Here, Plaintiff makes this request for sanctions concurrently with its request to remove from the calendar or strike Defendant’s motion for summary judgment or adjudication.  Although sanctions are warranted, the request does not comply with Code of Civil Procedure Section 128.5, subd. (f)(1)(A).  As such, the request is DENIED without prejudice.

Furthermore, the Court lacks authority to grant sanctions pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure Section 128 pursuant to Vidrio v. Hernandez (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 1443.  In Vidrio, the Court of Appeal has clarified that “trial courts have no inherent power to impose monetary sanctions” under Code of Civil Procedure section 128.  (Vidrio, @ 1455.)

As such, Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions is DENIED.

ORDERS

1)      Plaintiff Palos Verdes Bay Club, Inc.’s Motion to Take Off Calendar, or in the Alternative to Strike, Defendant CSG/Hunt Bros.’ Motion for Summary Judgment or Summary Adjudication is DENIED as MOOT.

2)      Plaintiff Palos Verdes Bay Club, Inc.’s Request for Monetary Sanctions is DENIED.

3)      Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of this Court’s ruling.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

[tentative] Order RE:

 

(1)   DEFENDANT SUBARU OF AMERICA, INC.’S MOTION TO STRIKE OR TAX PLAINTIFFS Laura Mayela Moreno and Michael Moreno’S MEMORANDUM OF COSTS

 

MOVING PARTY:                Defendant, Subaru of America, Inc.

RESPONDING PARTY:       Plaintiffs, Laura Mayela Moreno and Michael Moreno

 

(1)   Defendant Subaru of America, Inc.’s Motion to Strike or Tax Plaintiffs Laura Mayela Moreno and Michael Moreno’s Memorandum of Costs is GRANTED IN PART pursuant to California Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(b).

The Court considers the moving papers filed on April 2, 2026, the opposition brief filed on June 11, 2026, the reply brief filed on June 25, 2026, the declaration filed on July 2, 2026, and the opposition brief to the declaration filed on July 16, 2026.  To date, no reply brief to the declaration has been filed.

BACKGROUND

Factual Background

On February 29, 2024, plaintiffs Laura Mayela Moreno and Michael Moreno (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed their Complaint against defendants Subaru of America, Inc. (“Defendant”) and DOES 1 through 50.  The Complaint includes the following causes of action: (1) Violation of Subdivision (d) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (2) Violation of Subdivision (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (3) Violation of Subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (4) Breach of Express Written Warranty – Civil Code Sections 1791.2, subdivision (a), and 1794; and (5) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability – Civil Code sections 1791.1 and 1794.

On January 12, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Notice of Settlement of Entire Case.

On February 26, 2026, the parties executed the Settlement Agreement and Release.  As part of this agreement, Plaintiffs received a sum of $9,500.00 and were designated as the “prevailing parties” for the purposes of any motion for attorneys’ fees and costs.

On March 16, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their Memorandum of Costs for $1,764.15.  (Declaration of Susan Suzy Haroutunian (“Haroutunian Decl.”), ¶ 4, Exh. A.)

Procedural Background

On April 2, 2026, Defendant filed the motion.  On June 11, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an opposition brief.  On June 25, 2026, Defendant filed a reply brief.

On July 2, 2026, the Court called the motion to be heard; however, the hearing was continued at the oral request of Plaintiff’s counsel.  (July 2, 2026 Minute Order, p. 5.)  The Court “set[] a briefing schedule as follows: declaration (including invoices) to be filed and served by 7/9/26, opposition (no more than 5 pages in length) to be filed and served by 7/16/26, and reply (no more than 5 pages in length) to filed and served by 7/22/26.”  (July 2, 2026 Minute Order, p. 5.)

On July 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed a declaration and attached invoices.  On July 16, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition brief designated as an “objection to the declaration.”  To date, no reply brief to the declaration has been filed.

LEGAL STANDARDS

“Any notice of motion to strike or to tax costs must be served and filed 15 days after service of the costs memorandum.  If the cost memorandum was served by mail, the period is extended as provided in Code of Civil Procedure section 1013.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(b)(1).)  The failure to timely file a motion to tax or strike costs constitutes a waiver of the right to object to costs.  (Douglas v. Willis (1994) 27 Cal.App.4th 287, 289; see Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(b)(3) [“The party claiming costs and the party contesting costs may agree to extend the time for serving and filing the cost memorandum and a motion to strike or tax costs.  This agreement must be confirmed in writing, specify the extended date for service, and be filed with the clerk.  In the absence of an agreement, the court may extend the times for serving and filing the cost memorandum or the notice of motion to strike or tax costs for a period not to exceed 30 days.”].)

A prevailing party claiming costs must file and serve a memorandum of costs either (1) within 15 days after the date of service of a notice of entry of judgment or dismissal by the clerk under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5, (2) 15 days after the service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, or (3) within 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is first.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a).)  Any motion to strike or tax costs must be served and filed 15 days after service of the memorandum, plus an additional 5 days if served by mail or 2 days if served electronically.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(b)(1).)  “Unless objection is made to the entire cost memorandum, the motion to strike or tax costs must refer to each item objected to by the same number and appear in the same order as the corresponding cost item claimed on the memorandum of costs and must state why the item is objectionable.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(b)(2).)

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a), sets forth items allowable as costs.  Subdivision (b) lists the items that are not allowable as costs.  Generally, the court has discretion to allow costs that are not barred by subdivision (b) but are not listed under subdivision (a).  (Science Applications Internat. Corp. v. Superior Court (1995) 39 Cal.App.4th 1095, 1103.)  However, under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act, if a buyer-plaintiff prevails in their action, “the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.”  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d), emphasis added.)  “[I]n enacting Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) the Legislature intended the phrase costs and expenses to cover items not included in the detailed statutory definition of costs set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5.”  (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 42.)  Therefore, a buyer-plaintiff in a Song-Beverly action may recover typically “barred” costs and expenses, provided they were reasonably incurred in connection with commencing and prosecuting the action.

If an item of costs is expressly allowed by statute and if items appear on their face to be proper, the verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of their propriety, shifting the burden of proof to the objecting party to show that the items are not “reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation” or “reasonable in amount.”  (Benach v. County of Los Angeles (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 836, 855; Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131-132 [“trial court erred in requiring additional proof from” the party claiming costs, where the party attacking costs had the burden]; Santantonio v. Westinghouse Broadcasting Co. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 102, 116, 121 [after a prima facie showing based on verified cost memorandum, objecting party has the burden to prove costs should be disallowed]; Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 773.)

But if an item of cost does not appear proper on its face, the burden of showing that it is reasonable and necessary shifts to the party claiming the cost.  (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131 (Nelson); Jones v. Dumrichob (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1267; see Bach v. County of Butte (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 294, 308 [“Only after such costs are challenged by a motion to tax do the parties need to justify their claims by submitting documentation of the costs they have incurred.”].)

DISCUSSION

Defendant now contends that the Court should either strike the entire memorandum of costs or, in the alternative, tax the total costs by $392.59; this amount includes: (1) 150.00 in jury fees; (2) $111.97 in filing and motion fees; and (3) $116.39 in service of process fees.  (Obj., pp. 1:23-3:18.)  The Court notes that Defendant fails to provide a rationale for striking the entire memorandum of costs, particularly since Plaintiffs have now submitted relevant invoices and receipts.  (Motion, pp. 1:24-2:17.)  Consequently, the Court will concentrate its analysis on whether any of the requested costs should be taxed.  The Court finds that it is appropriate to tax $116.39 in costs.

Plaintiffs, who are the prevailing parties in this Song-Beverly case, are requesting reimbursement for the following costs: (1) $587.96 in filing and motion fees; (2) $150.00 in jury fees; (3) $147.29 in service of process fees; and (4) $879.00 for a deposition transcript.  (Haroutunian Decl., Exh. A.)

Filing fees, jury fees, and service of process fees are expressly authorized under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivisions (a)(1) and (a)(4).  Given that these expenses are explicitly permitted and appear proper, the verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of their propriety.  This places the burden of proof on Defendant, who must demonstrate that the costs were not “reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation” or “reasonable in amount.”  (Benach, supra, 149 Cal.App.4th at p. 855.)

Jury Fees

Defendant argues that Plaintiff should not be permitted to recover jury fees because these fees were not used for their “intended purpose,” specifically, juror food or lodging.  (Obj., pp. 1:24-2:1.) 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5 does not require jury fees to be utilized for juror food or lodging.  In fact, the statute makes a clear distinction between jury fees and the costs associated with “[j]uror food and lodging while they are kept together during trial and after the jury retires for deliberation.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(1)-(2).)  It is unclear why Defendant believes jury fees must be used for these purposes, as it does not provide any statutes or case law to support this claim.

Therefore, Defendant fails to demonstrate that the costs in question were not reasonably incurred in connection with commencing and prosecuting the action.  Consequently, the request to tax these costs is DENIED.

Filing and Motion Fees

Defendant contends that the Court should reduce the claimed costs by taxing $111.97 in filing and motion fees.  (Obj., p. 2:2-17.)  Defendant contends, “The Superior Court of California has a Statewide Civil Fee Schedule which indicates the cost of filing the Complaint is $435.00, the cost for civil case management statements is $0.00, notice of continuance of hearing of case management conference is $20.00, and the cost of filing notice of settlement is $0.00.”  (Obj, p. 2:3-7.)  Consequently, Defendant maintains that Plaintiffs should only be entitled to recover “$435.00 for filing the Summons and Complaint, $14.16 for the Notice of Continuance of the Final Status Conference, $14.16 for the Notice of Post Mediation Status Conference Hearing, and $12.57 for the Notice of Case Management Conference.”  (Obj., p. 2:14-17.) 

While Defendant accurately references the amounts stipulated in the Superior Court of California Statewide Civil Fee Schedule for initial filing fees in civil cases, the Court notes the existence of additional electronic filing fees that are not accounted for in this schedule.  These electronic filing fees address the discrepancies raised by the Defendant in their opposition to the declaration.

Defendant also asserts that the Court should tax the $14.23 charge for filing a notice of settlement, arguing that “[t]he filing of a Notice of Settlement occurs after resolution of the action and is not incurred in its commencement or prosecution, but rather relates to post-settlement matters including recovery of Plaintiffs’ attorneys’ fees and costs.”  (Obj., p. 2:25-28.)  The Court disagrees.  A notice of settlement is integral to the prosecution of the action, as it serves to inform the Court of the resolution of the case and effectively concludes the prosecution of Plaintiffs’ claims.

Therefore, Defendant fails to demonstrate that the costs in question were not reasonably incurred in connection with commencing and prosecuting the action.  Consequently, the request to tax these costs is DENIED.

Service Fees

Defendant contends that the Court should tax $116.39 for the service of a subpoena on a non-party dealership, citing two primary arguments: (1) the individual who was subpoenaed was never deposed, and (2) costs associated exclusively with investigatory purposes or convenience are not recoverable under section 1033.5, subdivision (c)(2), as the “testimony is of extremely limited probative value.”  (Obj., p. 3:8, 12-15.) 

The second argument is without merit.  The Court underscores that the pertinent issue is not Defendant’s assessment of the testimony’s value.  Rather, the focal point is whether the costs were reasonably incurred in the context of commencing and prosecuting the action.  Defendant’s perspective regarding the value of the testimony does not substantiate claims of the costs being unreasonable.

However, the Court concurs with the first argument.  Plaintiffs did not depose the non-party dealership.  Although Plaintiffs argue that “[s]ervice of subpoenas is a necessary component of litigation preparation, especially in Song-Beverly cases where dealership personnel often possess critical information regarding the plaintiffs’ repair presentations, warranty repairs, diagnostic findings, communications with the manufacturer, and service history[,]” their own conduct suggests otherwise.  (Opp., p. 7:3-6.)  Plaintiffs’ invoice indicates that the non-party deponent was served on September 23, 2025.  (Declaration of Joshua D. Fennell, ¶ 9, Exh. 3.)  The case did not reach a settlement until approximately January 12, 2026, when Plaintiffs filed the notice of settlement.  Consequently, Plaintiffs had several months to conduct the deposition but opted not to do so.  They cannot reasonably assert that the deposition and service of the subpoena were reasonably incurred in conjunction with the commencement and prosecution of the action when they chose not to follow through on the subpoena.

Consequently, the request to tax $116.39 from the service fees is GRANTED.

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS IN PART Defendant’s motion to strike or tax costs, taxing $116.39 from the service fees.

ORDERS

1)      Defendant Subaru of America, Inc.’s Motion to Strike or Tax Plaintiffs Laura Mayela Moreno and Michael Moreno’s Memorandum of Costs is GRANTED IN PART.  The Court taxes $116.39 from the service fees.

2)      Defendant is ordered to provide notice of this Court’s ruling.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

(1)   PLAINTIFF JULIE KESSER’S MOTION FOR ISSUE SANCTIONS, EVIDENTIARY SANCTIONS, AND ADVERSE JURY INSTRUCTION AGAINST DEFENDANT THE VONS COMPANIES, INC. FOR THE NEGLIGENT DESTRUCTION OF VIDEO SURVEILLANCE EVIDENCE

 

 

MOVING PARTY:                Plaintiff, Julie Kessler

RESPONDING PARTY:       Defendant, the Vons Companies, Inc.

 

(1)   Plaintiff Julie Kessler’s Motion for Issue Sanctions, Evidentiary Sanctions, and Adverse Jury Instruction Against Defendant the Vons Companies, Inc. for the Destruction of Video Surveillance Evidence is GRANTED IN PART pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 2023.010 and 2023.030 and Evidence Code section 413.

The Court considers the moving papers filed on March 12, 2026, the opposition brief filed on July 27, 2026, and the reply brief filed on July 31, 2026.

BACKGROUND

Factual Background

On September 25, 2024, plaintiff Julie Kessler (“Plaintiff”) filed the Complaint against defendants the Vons Companies, Inc. (“Defendant”) and DOES 1 through 50.  The Complaint includes the following causes of action: (1) General Negligence and (2) Premises Liability.

On September 5, 2024, Plaintiff served a Notice of Claim and Preservation of Evidence on an employee of Defendant.  (Declaration of Eddie B. Dennis (“Dennis Decl.”), ¶ 3, Exh. 1.)  This notice requested that Defendant “take all necessary steps to preserve any and all evidence, including but not limited to, all closed-circuit television footage, surveillance tapes, and/or video footage from VONS located at 410 Manhattan Beach Blvd, Manhattan Beach, CA 90266 has for one (1) day prior and one (1) day after, including the above referenced ‘Date of Incident.’ ”  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 1.)  Plaintiff attaches a receipt, signed by “T. Campbell,” which shows that the notice was served and received.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. 2.)

On November 15, 2024, Plaintiff propounded Request for Production of Documents, Set One, on Defendant, seeking all video surveillance related to the incident.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 10.)

On January 3, 2025, Defendant provided verified responses identifying the video surveillance and included a copy of the video.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 11, Exh. 8.)

On March 31, 2025, Plaintiff propounded Request for Production of Documents, Set One, on Defendant, requesting at least two hours of video surveillance.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 12.)

On May 1, 2025, Defendant responded with verified statements indicating, “[Defendant] is unable to comply because the particular item or category is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of [Defendant].”  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 13, Exh. 9.)

On October 24, 2025, Plaintiff sent Defendant a meet-and-confer letter regarding the destruction or spoliation of evidence.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 8, Exh. 6.)  In this correspondence, Plaintiff requested a conference to resolve the matter informally and avoid court intervention.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 8, Exh. 6.)  Additionally, Plaintiff notified Defendant that should an informal resolution prove unattainable, it will proceed to file a motion seeking sanctions and adverse jury instructions.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 8, Exh. 6.)

On October 31, 2025, Defendant responded to the meet-and-confer letter, asserting its disagreement with Plaintiff’s arguments and stating that it “remains open to further dialogue regarding reasonable, proportional discovery measures to avoid unnecessary motion practice.”  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. 7.)

Procedural Background

On March 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed this motion.

On May 18, 2026, the Court granted this motion, leading to the imposition of both evidentiary sanctions and adverse jury instructions.  (May 18, 2026 Minute Order, p. 7.)  However, on July 13, 2026, the Court vacated and set aside its May 18, 2026, Order; this decision was made in response to Defendant’s assertion that it had not received proper notice of the motion.  (July 13, 2026 Minute Order, pp. 4-8.)  Consequently, the motion was rescheduled for August 7, 2026, at 8:30 a.m., with Defendant’s opposition brief due on or before July 27, 2026, and Plaintiff’s reply brief due on or before August 3, 2026.  (July 13, 2026 Minute Order, pp. 7-8.)

On July 27, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition brief.  On July 31, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply brief.

LEGAL STANDARDS

The “court, after notice to any affected party, person, or attorney, and after an opportunity for hearing, may impose” monetary, issue, evidence, or terminating sanctions “against anyone engaging in conduct that is a misuse of the discovery process[.]”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subds. (a)-(d).)  Issue sanctions may designate facts that shall be taken as established in the action in accordance with the claim of the party adversely affected by a misuse of the discovery process.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (b).)  Evidentiary sanctions may prohibit any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from introducing designated matters in evidence.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (c).)

Failing to respond or submit to an authorized method of discovery and disobeying a court order to provide discovery are misuses of the discovery process.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subds. (d), (g).)  Though not expressly prohibited by the Discovery Act, courts have found destruction of evidence in response to, or in anticipation of, a discovery request to be a misuse of the discovery process.  (See Dodge, Warren & Peters Ins. Services, Inc. v. Riley¿(2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 1414, 1419; see Cedars-Sinai Medical Center v. Superior Court (1998) 18 Cal.4th 1, 11.)

“Spoliation of evidence means the destruction or significant alteration of evidence or the failure to preserve evidence for another’s use in pending or future litigation.  Such conduct is condemned because it ‘can destroy fairness and justice, for it increases the risk of an erroneous decision on the merits of the underlying cause of action.  Destroying evidence can also increase the costs of litigation as parties attempt to reconstruct the destroyed evidence or to develop other evidence, which may be less accessible, less persuasive, or both.’  While there is no tort cause of action for the intentional destruction of evidence after litigation has commenced, it is a misuse of the discovery process that is subject to a broad range of punishment, including monetary, issue, evidentiary, and terminating sanctions.”  (Williams v. Russ (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1223, citations omitted (Williams).)

A party asserting spoliation of evidence does not need to prove that the nonmoving party willfully destroyed evidence; rather, the intent element of intentional spoliation is satisfied when there is knowledge of “an existing or potential action for damages; and [] the destroyed or altered object might constitute evidence in that action.”  (Gomez v. Acquistapace (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 740, 747 [discussion still applicable even though intentional tort for spoliation is no longer valid under California law].)  Willfulness is not required; rather, intentional spoliation occurs where a party “destroys an object which might constitute evidence in a lawsuit: (1) with the purpose of harming the lawsuit, or (2) when harm to the lawsuit is substantially certain to follow.”  (Ibid.)  A party moving for discovery sanctions based on spoliation of evidence must make an initial prima facie showing that the responding party had destroyed evidence that had a “substantial probability of damaging the moving party’s ability to establish an essential element of his claim or defense.”  (Williams, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at p. 1227.)  Once the moving party meets that burden, the burden shifts to the responding party to prove the moving party did not suffer prejudice from the loss of the documents.  (Id. at pp. 1226-1227.)

“Discovery sanctions serve to remedy the harm caused to the party suffering the discovery misconduct.  [Citation.]  Because discovery sanctions are not designed to punish, ‘ “sanctions should be tailored to serve that remedial purpose, should not put the moving party in a better position than he would otherwise have been had he obtained the requested discovery, and should be proportionate to the offending party’s misconduct.” ’ [Citation.]”  (Kwan Software Engineering, Inc. v. Hennings (2020) 58 Cal.App.5th 57, 74 (Kwan).)  Discovery sanctions are matters squarely within the trial court’s discretion and will not be returned except in instances of extreme abuse.  (Liberty Mutual Fire Ins. Co. v. LcL Administrators, Inc. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1093, 1102.)

In addition to the imposition of sanctions, the Court may issue adverse jury instructions.  “In determining what inferences to draw from the evidence or facts in the case against a party, the trier of fact may consider, among other things, the party’s failure to explain or to deny by his testimony such evidence or facts in the case against him, or his willful suppression of evidence relating thereto, if such be the case.”  (Evid. Code, § 413.)

DISCUSSION

Plaintiff contends that the Court should impose issue sanctions, evidentiary sanctions, and/or provide an adverse jury instruction against Defendant, asserting that Defendant negligently destroyed video surveillance footage of Plaintiff’s incident after receiving Plaintiff’s Notice of Claim and Request to Preserve Video Surveillance Evidence.  (Motion, pp. 2:14-3:3.) 

Intentionally Permitting the Destruction of Evidence

On September 5, 2024, Plaintiff served a Notice of Claim and Preservation of Evidence on Tawnya Campbell, an employee of Defendant.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 1.)  This notice was served in relation to Plaintiff’s alleged slip and fall incident involving an unidentified liquid substance near the self-checkout area on August 14, 2024, at Vons, located at 410 Manhattan Beach Blvd., Manhattan Beach, CA 90266.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 2.)  The notice requested that Defendant “take all necessary steps to preserve any and all evidence, including but not limited to, all closed-circuit television footage, surveillance tapes, and/or video footage from VONS located at 410 Manhattan Beach Blvd, Manhattan Beach, CA 90266 has for one (1) day prior and one (1) day after, including the above referenced ‘Date of Incident.’ ”  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 1.)

Plaintiff attaches a certified mail receipt, signed by “T. Campbell,” which indicates that the notice was served and received.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. 2.)  On January 7, 2026, Plaintiff deposed Tawnya Campbell, the individual who signed the receipt.   (Dennis Decl., Exh. 5; Declaration of Kori N. Macksoud (“Macksoud Decl.”), ¶ 7, Exh. D.)  During the deposition, Campbell affirmed that she had accepted the receipt, that she possessed the necessary authorization to do so, and that she likely placed the receipt on a manager’s desk in accordance with her job responsibilities.  (Motion, pp. 9:22-10:2; Dennis Decl., Exh. 5, pp. 17:2-12, 18:5-16, 19:4-11, 21:8-18, 22:18-23:22, 25:8-27:11, 28:2-32:16.)  However, Campbell does not recall placing the letter on the desks of Store Director Sinead Mulvey or Assistant Manager Christina Davis.  (Dennis Decl., Exh. 5, p. 32:3-6.)

Mulvey stated that she neither received nor was aware of the preservation letter until after the overwrite occurred.  (Macksoud Decl., ¶¶ 4, 6, Exh. C, pp. 44:9-45:11.)  Furthermore, Defendant’s counsel claims that Davis was also unaware of the preservation letter until after the overwrite; however, the provided deposition transcript does not elaborate on whether Davis had prior knowledge of or received the preservation letter before the overwrite occurred.  (Macksoud Decl., ¶¶ 5-6, Exh. B.)

Defendant argues that “[t]he certified mail receipt here shows only that the letter reached the store’s front counter and was signed by an hourly employee; it does not establish that the notice reached, or was even routed to, an individual with authority to place a litigation hold on the surveillance system.  A corporation is charged with the knowledge of its agents only as to matters within the scope of the authority delegated to them, not with the knowledge of every employee who happens to accept certified mail.  (Trane Co. v. Gilbert (1968) 267 Cal.App.2d 720, 727.)”  (Opp., p. 9:5-11.)

The Court notes that the case of Trane Co. v. Gilbert (Trane) does not substantiate the proposed rule.  The Court of Appeal articulated that “it is a well established rule in California that the principal is chargeable with, and is bound by the knowledge of, or notice to, his agent, received while the agent is acting within the scope of his authority, and which is in reference to a matter over which his authority extends.”  (Trane, supra, 267 Cal.App.2d at p. 727.)  Nevertheless, even if Defendant were to present legal precedents indicating that a corporation is not held liable for the knowledge possessed by every employee who merely receives certified mail, the evidence still indicates that Defendant deliberately failed to preserve evidence in breach of both its duty to preserve evidence and its established procedures.

“Spoliation of evidence means the destruction or significant alteration of evidence or the failure to preserve evidence for another’s use in pending or future litigation.”  (Williams, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at p. 1223; see also Reeves v. MV Transportation, Inc. (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 666, 681.)  Parties have a duty to preserve such evidence despite no court order being issued ordering the defendant to preserve evidence.  (See Williams, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at p. 1223; Victor Valley Union High School Dist. v. Superior Court (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 1121, 1143-1149, 1152 [school had a duty to preserve evidence because litigation was reasonably foreseeable at the time the video was erased despite no lawsuit having yet been filed.].)

Here, the alleged incident occurred on August 14, 2024.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 2.)  Plaintiff filed the Complaint on September 25, 2024, and Defendant was personally served on October 3, 2024.  (PoS, ¶ 5a.)  Consequently, regardless of whether Defendant received the preservation letter, Defendant was on notice to preserve all relevant evidence beginning on October 3, 2024, at the latest.

On November 15, 2024, Plaintiff propounded Request for Production of Documents, Set One, on Defendant, seeking all video surveillance related to the incident.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 10.)  On January 3, 2025, Defendant provided verified responses identifying the video surveillance and included a copy of the video.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 11, Exh. 8.)  However, Plaintiff asserts that this video contained only approximately thirty minutes “of footage from the relevant interior cameras angles and … the remainder of the video that spanned hours before and after the incident [were] overwritten and lost forever.”  (Dennis Decl., Exh. 4, pp. 31:7-12, 51:1-4, 59:11-13.)  Defendant itself confirmed that the additional video had been overwritten.

On March 31, 2025, Plaintiff propounded Request for Production of Documents, Set One, on Defendant, requesting at least two hours of video surveillance.  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 12.)  On May 1, 2025, Defendant responded with verified statements indicating, “[Defendant] is unable to comply because the particular item or category is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of [Defendant].”  (Dennis Decl., ¶ 13, Exh. 9.)  Ergo, the only reasonable inference from this communication is that the remaining video footage has been overwritten, as the surveillance system at the store location automatically deletes unsaved video footage after a retention period of 90 days.  (Dennis Decl., Exh. 4, pp. 30:24-32:1.) 

Moreover, the Court notes that Defendant’s provision of approximately 30 minutes of footage is inconsistent with its standard practice, which typically involves the preservation of footage for a minimum of one hour prior to the incident and one hour after the incident.  (Dennis Decl., Exh. 4, pp. 28:11-22, 28:24-30:15, 32:5-33:4, Exh. 5, pp. 33:15-34:1, 38:5-12, 39:3-20.)

Based on this evidence, the Court finds that Defendant intentionally permitted the destruction of evidence requested by Plaintiff, in violation of standard procedures.  The incident is alleged to have occurred on August 14, 2024.  (Compl., p. 4.)  Notably, given the 90-day evidence retention period, Plaintiff had until November 12, 2024 to notify Defendant of their lawsuit, so that Defendant would be aware of its obligation to preserve relevant evidence, including video footage for at least one hour before and one hour after the incident, as per its standard practices.

Plaintiff personally served Defendant on October 3, 2024, effectively putting it on notice to preserve all relevant evidence.  Notwithstanding this notice, Defendant intentionally chose not to preserve the video surveillance for the required duration before and after the incident, in line with its standard procedures.

Furthermore, the Court concurs with Plaintiff’s observation that “[t]he dates of the downloads make [Defendant’s] conduct even more troubling.  At least one clip was downloaded on September 19, 2024 … and additional clips were downloaded on October 17, 2024.  [] Mulvey admitted that the complete footage still existed on both dates.  On September 19, [Defendant] had the ability to preserve as much footage as it wanted” but chose not to do so.  (Reply, p. 6:5-9.)

Therefore, the Court finds that the Defendant intentionally permitted the destruction of evidence despite their knowledge of impending ligation. This constitutes a breach of its duty to preserve such materials.

Prejudice

Moreover, the Court finds that Plaintiff has made a sufficient showing that Defendant destroyed evidence with a “substantial probability of damaging the moving party’s ability to establish an essential element of his claim or defense.”  (Williams, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at p. 1227.)

Plaintiff asserts that “Defendant denies all liability, contending that it lacked notice of any hazardous condition and that Plaintiff herself may have caused the spill[,]” as Defendant allegedly conducted cleaning and inspections in the area prior to the incident.  (Motion, pp. 7:8-9, 8:15.)  Conversely, Plaintiff argues that “she slipped on a clear foreign substance that existed for a sufficient amount of time for Defendant to have discovered it and cleaned it before her fall.”  (Motion, p. 8:16-18.)

Plaintiff further contends that Defendant’s video surveillance could offer crucial evidence regarding several key questions.  These include: “whether a spill existed before Plaintiff arrived, who caused the spill, how long it was present, whether employees conducted requisite inspections, whether Defendant had actual or constructive notice, and whether Defendant had an opportunity to prevent the fall.”  (Motion, pp. 7:27-8:2.)  The issue of constructive notice is pivotal in this premises liability case.  (Reply, p. 7:19.)  Defendant’s Person Most Knowledgeable also (“PMK”) confirmed that this video surveillance is a valuable tool for understanding the circumstances surrounding the incident, including the cause of the fall and if either party could have taken preventive action.  (Motion, pp. 8:26-9:6; Dennis Decl., Exh. 4, pp. 41:22-42:25.)  The PMK also acknowledged that failing to preserve this video evidence hampers both parties’ understanding of what actually occurred.  (Motion, p. 9:8-10; Dennis Decl., Exh. 4, p. 43:6-10.)  Thus, it is not speculative to assert that the unpreserved footage would have helped clarify the issues of constructive notice.

The Court finds that the destroyed video evidence would provide an impartial and objective depiction of the incident and its related circumstances.  However, due to Defendant’s intentional failure to preserve evidence that could be instrumental in resolving the case, Plaintiff lacks access to the comprehensive video evidence that might otherwise bolster their claims or counter Defendant’s defenses.  As Plaintiff asserts, “[t]he destroyed video would have eliminated speculation and its loss ensures that speculation now fills the void.”  (Motion, p. 14:13-14.)  The absence of the full video “creates a distorted and misleading evidentiary record[,]” as the thirty-minute video “deprive[s] the factfinder of the ability to understand what occurred before and after the incident.”  (Motion, p. 14:16-18.)

Defendant asserts that Plaintiff cannot demonstrate prejudice, because “[t]he preserved footage shows no visible hazard on the floor; Plaintiff herself testified she did not observe any substance before her fall; an employee crossed the identical area less than a minute before the incident and observed nothing; and Assistant Manager Davis confirmed the area is fully visible to staff, such that a hazard present for more than a few minutes would ordinarily have been seen and addressed.”  (Opp., pp. 9:26-10:3.) 

However, Defendant’s reasoning is inconsistent with its own theory of liability.  (Reply, p. 6:24.)  Defendant “does not genuinely dispute that Plaintiff slipped on a substance.  Its own witness testified that employees reported oil on the floor, that Plaintiff reported slipping on oil, and that the oil had already been cleaned by the time management arrived.  [Defendant’s] affirmative theory is that the oil came from a chicken container Plaintiff was carrying.”  (Reply, pp. 6:24-7:2; Dennis Decl., Exh. 3, pp. 68:9-70:20.)  Consequently, “the fact that a clear liquid or oil is not apparent in an overhead surveillance image does not resolve when it appeared, who created it, or whether [Defendant] should have discovered it.”  (Reply, p. 7:5-6.)  The Court concurs that the evidence could have “shown a customer dropping or leaking a substance, employees traversing the location, or an employee performing (or failing to perform) the required ‘sweep inspections.’ ”  (Reply, p. 7:8-9.)

Sanctions

Plaintiff requests that the Court impose issue sanctions to establish two key points: “(1) the hazardous condition existed for a sufficient period of time prior to Plaintiff’s fall such that Defendant had constructive notice; and (2) the hazardous condition was not caused by Plaintiff.”  (Motion, p. 15:22-24.)  Alternatively, Plaintiff seeks “evidentiary sanctions precluding Defendant from offering evidence or argument that it lacked notice of the hazardous condition or that Plaintiff created the condition.”  (Morion, pp. 15:25-16:1.)  Furthermore, Plaintiff requests that the Court instruct the jury that they “may infer that the destroyed surveillance footage would have been unfavorable to Defendant.”  (Motion, p. 16:4-5.)

Here, the imposition of evidentiary sanctions and an adverse jury instruction is warranted.    The requested issue sanctions are unwarranted. The Court finds that the requested evidentiary sanctions and the adverse jury instruction are more appropriate.  Said sanctions serve to restore balance concerning the destroyed evidence and position Plaintiff similarly to where they would have been had the requested discovery been preserved and obtained.  The imposition of issue sanctions would effectively adjudicate the merits of the case.

Defendant initially argues that Plaintiff’s failure to move to compel further responses or compliance with an inspection demand “forecloses the issue and evidentiary sanctions Plaintiff seeks as a matter of law, regardless of how the willfulness and prejudice questions addressed below are resolved” under New Albertsons, Inc. v. Superior Court (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1422–1423 (New Albertsons).  (Opp., p. 8:7-9.)  The Court disagrees.

In New Albertsons, the Court of Appeal stated that, “The general rule that we glean from these opinions is that if it is sufficiently egregious, misconduct committed in connection with the failure to produce evidence in discovery may justify the imposition of nonmonetary sanctions even absent a prior order compelling discovery, or its equivalent.  Furthermore, a prior order may not be necessary where it is reasonably clear that obtaining such an order would be futile.”  (New Albertsons, supra, 168 Cal.App.4th at p. 1426.)

Here, the Court finds that a court order would have been futile.  Defendant admits that the relevant surveillance footage was destroyed a significant time ago.  A motion to compel would not have advanced the resolution of this case.

Defendant also contends that “New Albertsons further instructs that, absent a violated discovery order, ‘the facts should be decided and any appropriate inference should be made by the trier of fact after a full hearing at trial,’ rather than through a nonmonetary sanction imposed on a pretrial motion.”  (Opp., pp. 8:27-9:2.)  Defendant interpretation of this statement is misplaced.

The Court of Appeal stated the following: “[W]e believe that the concern expressed in Cedars–Sinai, supra, 18 Cal.4th at pages 15–16, 74 Cal.Rptr.2d 248, 954 P.2d 511, about meritless spoliation claims where the evidence was destroyed innocently in the ordinary course of business is an appropriate concern in this context as well.  A party moving for discovery sanctions based on the intentional destruction of evidence could argue that the mere fact that the evidence no longer exists supports an inference of intentional spoliation.  Rather than decide the facts with respect to the intentional destruction of evidence and impose a nonmonetary sanction on a pretrial motion in circumstances not contemplated by the discovery statutes, we believe that in most cases of purported spoliation the facts should be decided and any appropriate inference should be made by the trier of fact after a full hearing at trial.”  (New Albertsons, supra, 168 Cal.App.4th at p. 1428.)

Here, this issue is correctly before the Court within the meaning of New Albertsons, supra.  As discussed above, the Court finds that Defendant intentionally destroyed the surveillance footage.  As such, Plaintiff’s motion is GRANTED IN PART.

 

 

 

 

 

 

ORDERS

1)      Plaintiff Julie Kessler’s Motion for Issue Sanctions, Evidentiary Sanctions, and Adverse Jury Instruction Against Defendant the Vons Companies, Inc. for the Destruction of Video Surveillance Evidence is GRANTED IN PART.

2)      The Court imposes the following evidentiary sanctions:

a.       Defendant is prohibited from presenting

                                                              i.      any evidence or

                                                            ii.      making arguments asserting that

                                                          iii.      it lacked notice of the hazardous condition,  

                                                          iv.      or that Plaintiff was responsible for creating said condition.

3)      The Request for an Adverse Jury Instruction is GRANTED.

a.       The jury is permitted to infer that the destroyed surveillance footage would have been unfavorable to Defendant.

4)      Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of this Court’s ruling.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

BACKGROUND

Factual Background

On February 6, 2026, plaintiff Lynn Couch (“Plaintiff”) filed the Complaint against defendants Save Medical & Aesthetic Group (“Save”), Jinah K. Choi, Sang S. Lee, and DOES 1 through 50.

On April 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Save, Jinah K. Choi, Sang S. Lee, and DOES 1 through 50.  The FAC includes the following causes of action: (1) Medical Negligence; (2) Fraud – Concealment; and (3) Medical Battery.

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable.  (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)  “To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.”  (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)  For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded.  (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.)  A demurrer “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.”  (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

A pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous or unintelligible.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).)  A demurrer for uncertainty may lie if the failure to label the parties and claims renders the complaint so confusing that the defendant cannot tell what he or she is supposed to respond to.  (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2.)  However, “[a] demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.”  (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)

MEET AND CONFER REQUIREMENT

Before filing a demurrer, the moving party is required to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or via video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion, to determine whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer or motion to strike.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).)  The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(2).)  Thereafter, the moving party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).)  A determination by the court that the meet and confer process was inadequate shall not be grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer.  (Code Civil Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(4).)

On May 9, 2026, Lee’s counsel sent a meet-and-confer letter to Plaintiff’s counsel, requesting a conference to discuss the alleged issues in the FAC.  (Kim Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.)  On May 13, 2026, Lee’s counsel met and conferred with Plaintiff’s counsel over the phone.  (Kim Decl., ¶ 5.)  Despite both parties articulating their positions during the conversation, they were unable to resolve the issues through informal discussion, necessitating the filing of this demurrer.  (Kim Decl., ¶¶ 6-8.)

Consequently, the Court finds that Lee sufficiently met and conferred with Plaintiff prior to filing this demurrer.

DISCUSSION

Second Cause of Action – Fraud – Concealment

Lee demurs to the second cause of action for fraud – concealment on several grounds: (1) Plaintiff “improperly relies on collective allegations against Choi and Lee without identifying [] Lee’s specific conduct[,]” preventing Lee from receiving “fair notice of the specific fraudulent conduct allegedly attributable to him[;]” (2) Plaintiff “fails to plead specific facts showing [] Lee’s knowledge[;] (3) Plaintiff “fails to plead specific facts showing [] Lee’s intent to deceive[;]” (4) Plaintiff “fails to plead reliance on any specific conduct by [] Lee[;]” and (5) the cause of action is uncertain as to Lee.  (Dem., pp. 5:3-4, 6:23-24, 7:6, 22, 8:12, 9:3.) 

“The elements of fraud, which give rise to the tort action for deceit, are (a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.”  (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638 (Lazar), quoting 5 Witkin, Summary of Cal. Law (9th ed. 1988) Torts, § 676, p. 778.)

The facts constituting the alleged fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked.  (Lazar, supra, 12 Cal.4th at p. 645.)  In alleging a cause of action for fraud, “[t]he specificity requirement means a plaintiff must allege facts showing how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were made, and, in the case of a corporate defendant, the plaintiff must allege the names of the persons who made the representations, their authority to speak on behalf of the corporation, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when the representation was made.”  (West v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 780, 793.)  However, “to prove reliance on an omission[, the plaintiff] need only prove that, had the omitted information been disclosed, [the plaintiff] would have been aware of it and behaved differently.”  (Mirkin v. Wasserman (1993) 5 Cal.4th 1082, 1093.)

Plaintiff alleges the following: Choi “is the chief financial officer, agent, and director of [Save].  [Lee] is the chief executive officer and director of [Save].  [Choi and Lee] are nurse practitioners providing medical services at [Save,] located in Torrance, California.”  (FAC, ¶ 3.)  “[O]n or about May 7, 2025, before and continuing thereafter, [P]laintiff presented to [Save] for cosmetic procedures, including body fat dissolving injections in her thighs.  Among other negligent acts and omissions, defendants, and each of them, negligently performed the injections into plaintiff’s thighs using improper equipment and technique, causing the injection needle to break off and became lodged in [P]laintiff’s left thigh”  (FAC, ¶ 9.)  Choi and Lee “intentionally failed to disclose to [P]laintiff the fact that the injection needle had broken off and was lodged in her left thigh during the injections performed that same day.”  (FAC, ¶ 17.)  “Plaintiff was positioned on her stomach during the procedure and could not observe the injections being performed.  Plaintiff did not see the needle that was used either during or after the procedure and had no way to know that it had broken.”  (FAC, ¶ 17.)  “On May 9, 2025, defendants [Choi and Lee] asked [P]laintiff to return to [Save] without disclosing this material fact.  Upon [P]laintiff’s arrival on May 9, 2025, [Choi and Lee] asked [P]laintiff to sign a consent form and to backdate it to May 7, 2025.  Defendants then performed what they represented to be additional fat-dissolving injections in Plaintiff’s left thigh.  Only after completing this procedure did defendants inform [P]laintiff that the needle had broken off in her left thigh during the May 7, 2025 injections.”  (FAC, ¶ 18.)

Plaintiff further claims: “Had [P]laintiff known this material fact, she would have behaved differently.  Specifically, [P]laintiff would have sought immediate medical evaluation and treatment on May 7, 2025, to have the needle promptly located and removed by competent, qualified medical providers, before any complications such as migration, infection, or other injuries developed.  Plaintiff would not have simply gone home and continued her normal activities for 2 days without seeking medical attention.  Further, [P]laintiff would not have returned to [Save] on May 9, 2025, nor consented to any additional injections or procedures performed by [Choi and Lee.]”  (FAC, ¶ 20.)

Moreover, Plaintiff alleges that “[t]he injection needle migrated within [P]laintiff’s body and became significantly more difficult to locate and remove.  As it migrated, the needle became very painful, uncomfortable and disturbing to [P]laintiff, causing her severe pain, suffering, emotional distress, anxiety, stress, despair, mental anguish, inconvenience, and other injuries to her body, nervous system, and overall health.  As a further direct and proximate result of defendants’ intentional fraudulent conduct, [P]laintiff was required to undergo multiple surgical procedures to locate and remove the migrated needle, suffering infection, prolonged recovery, multiple incisions and their corresponding scars, and other related sequalae.”  (FAC, ¶ 21.)  Plaintiff’s alleged injuries include “shock and injury to [P]laintiff’s nervous system and person, all of which has caused and continues to cause [P]laintiff great mental, emotional, physical and nervous pain, suffering, anguish, fear, anxiety, and distress.”  (FAC, ¶ 22.)

Lee contends that Plaintiff’s claim is deficient because she fails to plead specific facts that distinguish the actions of Choi from those of Lee.  (Dem., p. 5:6-7.)  He further grounds the remainder of his arguments on this premise.  (Dem., pp. 6:23-9:12.) 

Nonetheless, the Court finds these arguments to be unpersuasive.  There is no obligation for Plaintiff to distinguish Choi’s allegedly wrongful actions from Lee’s own, particularly given that Plaintiff alleges both Lee and Choi engaged in such conduct.  Moreover, the Plaintiff’s decision not to differentiate does not render the allegations conclusory.  Furthermore, Lee has not cited any applicable case law or statutes that impose such a requirement on Plaintiff.  It is apparent that Plaintiff attributes the actions to both parties, thereby providing Lee with adequate notice regarding the conduct attributed to him and ensuring that the claim remains sufficiently clear.  As such, Lee’s demurrer with respect to the second cause of action for fraud – concealment is OVERRULED.

Third Cause of Action – Medical Battery

Lee demurs to the third cause of action for medical battery on several grounds: (1) Plaintiff “fails to allege what procedure [] Lee personally performed[;]” (2) Plaintiff “fails to distinguish medical battery from medical negligence as to [] Lee[;]” (3) Plaintiff “fails to plead facts showing [] Lee personally exceeded the scope of consent[;]” and (4) the claim is uncertain.  (Dem., pp. 10:23, 11:11-12, 26-27, 12:14.)  The Court disagrees.

“A battery is a violation of an individual’s interest in freedom from intentional unlawful, harmful or offensive unconsented contacts with his or her person.  [Citation.]  The element of lack of consent to the particular contact is an essential element of battery.  [Citation.]”  (Rains v. Superior Court (1984) 150 Cal.App.3d 933, 938, internal citations omitted.)

“Where a doctor obtains consent of the patient to perform one type of treatment and subsequently performs a substantially different treatment for which consent was not obtained, there is a clear case of battery.”  (Cobbs v. Grant (1972) 8 Cal.3d 229, 239.)  “The battery theory should be reserved for those circumstances when a doctor performs an operation to which the patient has not consented.  When the patient gives permission to perform one type of treatment and the doctor performs another, the requisite element of deliberate intent to deviate from the consent given is present.  However, when the patient consents to certain treatment and the doctor performs that treatment but an undisclosed inherent complication with a low probability occurs, no intentional deviation from the consent given appears; rather, the doctor in obtaining consent may have failed to meet his due care duty to disclose pertinent information.  In that situation the action should be pleaded in negligence.”  (Id. at pp. 240-241.)

Plaintiff alleges: “On May 9, 2025, [P]laintiff was told by defendants [Choi and Lee] that they were going to perform additional fat-dissolving injections in her thighs.  Unaware of the broken needle lodged in her left thigh since May 7, 2025, [P]laintiff consented to the additional injections.  On information and belief, the purported additional injections were a pretext for defendants [Choi and Lee] to go re-enter [P]laintiff’s left thigh and search for the broken needle without having to first inform [P]laintiff of its presence, and to subsequently document the incident in the medical records as though it occurred on May 9, 2025, rather than 2 days earlier on May 7, 2025.  Accordingly, defendants [Choi and Lee] performed a substantially different medical procedure than that which plaintiff consented to – namely, an exploratory attempt to locate a retained foreign object rather than the administration of additional fat-dissolving injections – by deceiving [P]laintiff regarding the true reason for and nature of the medical procedure performed on her left thigh on May 9, 2025.”  (FAC, ¶ 26.)

Lee asserts that there are no specific allegations detailing his personal conduct, including the procedures he personally performed or whether he administered any injections.  (Dem., p. 11:1-3.)  The Court disagrees.  As indicated, Plaintiff alleges that although she consented to additional injections, Lee and Choi conducted an entirely different procedure, an exploratory surgery or procedure aimed at removing the broken needle, instead of receiving the fat-dissolving injections.  (FAC, ¶ 26.)  Plaintiff specifically alleges Lee’s personal involvement in this alternative procedure.  The Court deems this sufficient at the demurrer stage.

Furthermore, Lee argues that this situation constitutes a negligence claim rather than a claim of medical battery, asserting that the claim does not demonstrate “an unauthorized procedure or substantially different procedure attributable to Defendant Lee personally.”  (Dem., p. 11:19-20.) 

However, as previously discussed, Plaintiff maintains that she did not receive the injections but rather underwent an exploratory procedure performed by Lee and Choi.  (FAC, ¶ 26.)  Based on these allegations, the exploratory procedure did not fall within the scope of the procedure to which Plaintiff consented; it constituted a distinct procedure to which she did not provide consent.

Additionally, Lee asserts that “the FAC does not allege facts showing how [] Lee personally exceeded the scope of that consent.”  (Dem., p. 12:1-2.)  The Court disagrees with this assertion.  Plaintiff alleges that she consented to additional injections while Lee and Choi carried out an entirely different procedure.  (FAC, ¶ 26.)  As Plaintiff did not receive the injections and Lee did not perform an undisclosed inherent complication but rather an exploratory procedure, the Court finds that Plaintiff has adequately alleged that Lee exceeded the boundaries of her consent.  (FAC, ¶ 26.)  It is unnecessary for Plaintiff to specifically identify the name of the type of procedure, the precise manner in which Lee touched her, or to demonstrate that Lee obtained her consent personally.

In the reply brief, Lee similarly argues that Plaintiff must include much more specific allegations to survive this demurrer.  (Reply, pp. 4:10-5:3.)  However, these allegations are not required elements within the context of a medical battery claim, and Lee does not provide any relevant case law to support his position.

Lastly, Lee claims that the assertion is uncertain for the same reasons he believes the second cause of action is uncertain, namely that Plaintiff fails to articulate specific facts that distinguish the actions of Choi from those of Lee.  (Dem., p. 12:14-28.) 

However, as discussed above with respect to the second cause of action, the Court disagrees.  Plaintiff clearly attributes the actions to both parties, thereby providing Lee with adequate notice regarding the conduct attributed to him and ensuring that the claim remains sufficiently clear. As such, Lee’s demurrer with respect to the third cause of action for medical battery is OVERRULED.
