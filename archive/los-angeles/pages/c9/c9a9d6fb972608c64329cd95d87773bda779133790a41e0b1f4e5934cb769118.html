Case Number: 22GDCV01128    Hearing Date: August 26, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING DEFENDANT GLORIA TREVI’S MOTIONS FOR DISCLOSURE OF PLAINTIFFS and GRANTING MOTIONS TO SEAL

I.         INTRODUCTION

These related actions arise from allegations of childhood sexual abuse, sexual battery, assault, and exploitation. Plaintiffs Jane Doe K.C. 1, Jane Doe A.H. 2, Jane Doe 3, and Jane Doe 4 (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) allege that Defendants Gloria De Los Angeles Trevino Ruiz aka Gloria Trevi (“Trevi”), Sergio Gustavo Andrade Sanchez aka Sergio Andrade (“Andrade”), and Maria Raquenel Portillo Jimenez aka Mary Boquitas and Mary Raquenel (“Portillo”) sexually abused and exploited Plaintiffs while they were minors.

On July 20, 2026, Defendant Trevi filed the instant motions for an order disclosing Plaintiffs’ identities. Plaintiffs filed oppositions on August 3, 2026, and Trevi filed replies on August 7, 2026. The parties also filed related motions to seal portions of the briefing and supporting documents containing Plaintiffs’ identifying information

Request for judicial notice

            Defendant Trevi requests judicial notice of Exhibits 1–14 and Exhibits 1–17, consisting of various publicly available materials, including news articles, books, social-media posts, YouTube videos, and other online materials concerning Plaintiffs and the underlying events. Trevi requests judicial notice of the existence of these materials pursuant to Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (h), as evidence of information that has been made publicly available, rather than for the truth of any disputed factual assertions contained therein.

The Court GRANTS Trevi’s requests for judicial notice as to the existence and public availability of the identified materials but does not take judicial notice of the truth of any disputed factual assertions contained therein.

Evidentiary objections

            All evidentiary objections are OVERRULED.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Unless confidentiality is required by law, court records are presumed to be open to the public pursuant to a potent “open court” policy undergirded by the First Amendment and favoring the public nature of court proceedings.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(c); see NBC Subsidiary (KNBC-TV), Inc. v. Superior Court (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1178, 1199-10.)  Consequently, pleadings, motions, discovery documents, and other papers may not be filed under seal merely by stipulation of the parties; filing under seal requires a court order.1  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551(a); see H.B. Fuller Co. v. Doe¿(2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 879, 888.) 

In order to issue a sealing order, the Court must make expressly find that: (1) there exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) the overriding interest supports sealing the record; (3) a substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) the proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and (5) no less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d)(1)-(4), (e); see McGuan v. Endovascular Techs., Inc.¿(2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 974, 988.)  The constitutional policy favoring for disclosure must be balanced against other factors, e.g., privacy rights.  (See People v. Jackson (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1009, 1026-27.)  The Court acts within its discretion in making factual determinations on a motion to seal.  (Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Superior Court (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 1273, 1285.)¿

III.      ANALYSIS

Defendant Trevi moves for an order requiring Plaintiffs to disclose their true identities and proceed in this action under their legal names pursuant to California Rules of Court, rules 2.550 and 2.551.

Trevi argues that Plaintiffs can no longer justify proceeding anonymously. She contends that Plaintiffs’ identities and their connection to the underlying allegations have been publicly known for decades through media reports, books, and interviews. Trevi also argues that, since this action was filed, certain Plaintiffs have publicly discussed the allegations and the litigation on social media and YouTube and promoted books concerning the same underlying events, all while continuing to proceed anonymously in this action. According to Trevi, Plaintiffs’ own public conduct undermines their claimed need for privacy and shows that the information they seek to protect is already publicly known. (Motion, pp. 17-18.)

Trevi further argues that Plaintiffs cannot demonstrate a concrete threat of harm or particular vulnerability sufficient to warrant anonymity under Roe v. Smith (2025) 116 Cal.App.5th 227. According to Trevi, Plaintiffs’ asserted fears of ridicule, threats, attacks, and emotional harm are generalized and speculative, and Plaintiffs have not identified a specific concrete threat arising from disclosure of their identities. Trevi also argues that Plaintiffs are now adults and therefore are not particularly vulnerable merely because the alleged underlying events occurred when they were minors. (Motion, p. 19.)

Finally, Trevi argues that Plaintiffs’ continued anonymity prejudices her by allowing Plaintiffs to publicly identify and accuse Trevi while shielding their own identities. Trevi contends this creates an unfair asymmetry, harms her ability to respond publicly to the allegations, and imposes additional burdens on the litigation by requiring filings containing Plaintiffs’ identities to be submitted in both redacted and sealed form. (Motion, p. 20.)

Plaintiffs oppose the motion, arguing that Roe v. Smith does not change the standard previously applied by the Court and is distinguishable. Plaintiffs point out that Roe involved defamation claims and was decided without declarations or other evidence supporting anonymity, whereas Plaintiffs contend there is a substantial evidentiary record here concerning threats, harassment, and potential harm. Plaintiffs also emphasize that Roe itself recognized that allegations concerning sexual conduct are highly sensitive and private, particularly where the underlying events occurred when the parties were minors. (Opposition, pp. 3-4.)

Plaintiffs further argue that Trevi has not shown any changed circumstances warranting reversal of the Court’s prior order. They maintain that none of the Plaintiffs has publicly identified themselves as a litigant in this action and that Trevi’s evidence concerning prior media coverage and books either predates the Court’s order or merely connects Plaintiffs to the underlying events, not to this lawsuit. Plaintiffs similarly contend that the YouTube videos relied upon by Trevi do not contain statements identifying any Plaintiff as a party to this action. (Opposition, pp. 5-10.)

Finally, Plaintiffs argue that the safety concerns supporting anonymity remain and have become better documented since the Court’s prior ruling. They contend that the relevant risk is not that Defendants will learn their identities, which Defendants already know, but that Plaintiffs will be publicly identified to Trevi’s followers and others on social media and subjected to further harassment or threats. Plaintiffs argue that Trevi suffers little prejudice from continued anonymity because she knows who Plaintiffs are, whereas disclosure would permanently identify Plaintiffs to the public as parties to this action. (Opposition, pp. 2, 4-5.)

The Court is not persuaded that Trevi has demonstrated circumstances warranting reconsideration of Plaintiffs’ continued use of pseudonyms.

The Court has considered the materials submitted with Trevi’s requests for judicial notice. Although the materials reflect that certain Plaintiffs have publicly discussed the underlying events and contain references to matters associated with this litigation, the Court does not find that this evidence, standing alone, resolves whether Plaintiffs should continue to be permitted to proceed under pseudonyms. The materials are relevant to the extent to which Plaintiffs’ identities and their connection to the underlying allegations and these actions are already publicly available, and the Court considers that circumstance in weighing Plaintiffs’ asserted interests.

However, Plaintiffs’ asserted interests are not limited to keeping the underlying allegations or their identities entirely out of the public sphere. Plaintiffs contend that publicly identifying them by their legal names as litigants in these actions presents a distinct risk of further harassment, threats, and retaliation. The Court therefore considers the judicially noticed materials together with the evidentiary record as a whole in balancing Plaintiffs’ asserted privacy and safety interests against the public’s right of access.

Further, the Court agrees with Plaintiffs that Roe v. Smith is factually distinguishable. In Roe, the plaintiffs sought to proceed anonymously based on the allegations in their pleadings and did not submit declarations or other evidence supporting their claimed need for anonymity. The Court of Appeal emphasized that the record contained no evidence establishing the plaintiffs' asserted fears of harm and concluded that the allegations in the pleadings, standing alone, were insufficient. (Roe, supra, 116 Cal.App.5th at pp. 237, 243-244.) 

Here, by contrast, the Court has an evidentiary record supporting Plaintiffs' asserted concerns. Plaintiffs have submitted sworn declarations describing threats, harassment, and fears for their own safety and the safety of their families, together with supporting exhibits documenting the alleged harassment. Plaintiffs contend that these concerns have continued, and in some respects increased, since the Court entered its prior pseudonym order. Thus, unlike Roe, the asserted risk of harm is not based solely on allegations in the pleadings or generalized concerns about reputational or economic injury. (Opposition, pp. 3–5.)

Moreover, the nature of the asserted harm differs. In Roe, the trial court relied in part on the plaintiffs’ concern that future employers might discover the lawsuit through an Internet search. Here, Plaintiffs assert a risk of harassment, threats, and violence from third parties and social-media followers if their identities as litigants are publicly disclosed. Plaintiffs have also submitted declarations and supporting evidence describing harassment and threats they have already experienced, which they contend demonstrates the risk of further harm if their identities as Plaintiffs in this action are publicly disclosed. (Opposition, pp. 4, 11.)

The Court recognizes that the judicially noticed materials are relevant to the strength of Plaintiffs’ claimed privacy interests. Although the extent to which certain Plaintiffs have publicly discussed the underlying events and matters associated with this litigation weighs to some degree in favor of disclosure, that circumstance must be considered together with the other interests and evidence before the Court. In particular, the judicially noticed materials do not, standing alone, resolve Plaintiffs’ separate concerns regarding the risk of harassment, threats, and retaliation if they are publicly identified by their legal names as Plaintiffs in these actions.

The Court therefore finds that Plaintiffs’ asserted privacy and safety interests remain sufficient to support their continued use of pseudonyms. The evidence reflects more than a generalized concern of embarrassment or reputational harm and supports Plaintiffs’ contention that publicly identifying them as litigants in this action presents a risk of further harassment, threats, and retaliation. Moreover, Trevi already knows Plaintiffs’ identities and is able to defend against their claims. On balance, the Court finds that Plaintiffs’ privacy and safety interests continue to outweigh the public interest in disclosure. Therefore, Trevi has not demonstrated a sufficient basis to disturb the Court’s prior orders permitting Plaintiffs to proceed under pseudonyms. The motions are DENIED.

 Motions to seal

These related actions arise from allegations of childhood sexual abuse, sexual battery, assault, and exploitation. Plaintiffs Jane Doe K.C. 1, Jane Doe A.H. 2, Jane Doe 3, and Jane Doe 4 (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) allege that Defendants Gloria De Los Angeles Trevino Ruiz aka Gloria Trevi (“Trevi”), Sergio Gustavo Andrade Sanchez aka Sergio Andrade (“Andrade”), and Maria Raquenel Portillo Jimenez aka Mary Boquitas and Mary Raquenel (“Portillo”) sexually abused and exploited Plaintiffs while they were minors.

On July 20, 2026, Defendant Trevi filed the instant motions for an order disclosing Plaintiffs’ identities. Plaintiffs filed oppositions on August 3, 2026, and Trevi filed replies on August 7, 2026. The parties also filed related motions to seal portions of the briefing and supporting documents containing Plaintiffs’ identifying information.

            For the reasons discussed above, the Court finds that Plaintiffs’ privacy and safety interests constitute an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the identifying information. That overriding interest supports sealing because disclosure of the information would defeat the Court’s determination that Plaintiffs may continue to proceed under pseudonyms. There is a substantial probability that Plaintiffs’ interests would be prejudiced absent sealing because disclosure would reveal, or permit the public to determine, their identities as litigants in these actions. The requested sealing is narrowly tailored because it is limited to Plaintiffs’ true identities and information from which those identities may be determined, rather than the entirety of the parties’ filings. No less restrictive means would adequately protect Plaintiffs’ anonymity.

            Thus, the Court GRANTS the parties’ motions to seal in both related actions. The portions of the parties’ filings containing Plaintiffs’ true names or other information which their identities as Plaintiffs may be determined shall remain under seal.

IV.       ORDER

             The Court DENIES Trevi’s motion to disclose Plaintiffs identities. The Court GRANTS motions to seal.

Case Number: 23AHCV02367    Hearing Date: August 26, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANT PSF 588 ATLAS’ MOTION TO STAY

I.         INTRODUCTION

This consolidated construction action involves a troubled construction project located at 588 Atlas Avenue in Monterey Park, California. The property is owned by Defendant PSF 588 Atlas LLC (“PSF”). 

On October 12, 2023, Plaintiff Electrolurgy Manufacturing, Inc. filed this action. 

Numerous additional complaints by other parties were eventually consolidated into this action. In four of those cases (23AHCV02394, 23AHCV02515, 23AHCV02512, and 23AHCV02479), GCX, LLC (“GCX”) filed its operative First Amended Cross-Complaints (“FAXC”) against PSF, Sonnenblick Development, LLC, Sonnenblick Monterey Park, LLC, Robert Sonnenblick, and Roes 12 through 50, inclusive, asserting causes of action for: (1) breach of contract, (2) breach of contract, (3) open book account, (4) account stated, (5) quantum meruit, (6) foreclosure of mechanic’s lien, and (7) foreclosure of mechanic’s lien.

On August 3, 2026, PSF filed the instant motion to stay or enjoin GCX’s affirmative claims against PSF in arbitration pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 526. On August 13, 2026, GCX filed an opposition, and on August 19, 2026, PSF filed a reply.

Request for judicial notice

PSF requests judicial notice of Exhibits 1 through 8. Exhibits 1 through 6 are records of this Court, and Exhibits 7 and 8 are recorded instruments.

The Court GRANTS PSF’s request for judicial notice. The Court takes judicial notice of the existence and contents of the documents, but not the truth of any disputed factual assertions contained therein

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Arbitration is a matter of contract, and a party cannot be required to arbitrate a controversy it did not agree to submit to arbitration. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1281, 1281.2.) Whether an agreement to arbitrate exists is generally a question for the court. (Benaroya v. Willis (2018) 23 Cal.App.5th 462, 469.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 526 authorizes injunctive relief where, among other circumstances, a party is threatened with injury that cannot adequately be compensated by damages or where an injunction is necessary to restrain an act that would violate the rights of another. In determining whether to issue preliminary injunctive relief, the Court considers the likelihood that the moving party will prevail on the merits and the relative balance of harms. (IT Corp. v. County of Imperial (1983) 35 Cal.3d 63, 69–70.)

III.      ANALYSIS

PSF moves for an order staying or enjoining GCX from prosecuting its affirmative claims against PSF in the newly commenced arbitration, GCX, LLC v. PSF 588 Atlas, LLC, AAA Case No. 01-26-0000-40266. PSF does not seek to stay its own pending claims against GCX for defective, incomplete, and nonconforming work, or GCX’s indemnity claims against the subcontractors in the existing arbitration. (Motion, pp. 1-2.)

PSF argues that the Court, rather than the arbitrator, must determine whether PSF agreed to arbitrate GCX’s affirmative payment claims. PSF contends it is a nonsignatory to the Construction Agreement and that incorporation of the AAA Construction Industry Arbitration Rules cannot delegate the threshold issue of contract formation to the arbitrator unless PSF first agreed to the arbitration agreement and its delegation provision. (Motion, pp. 14-15.)

PSF further argues that its September 2024 arbitration demand constituted limited consent to arbitrate only its assigned defect, warranty, and incomplete-work claims, and did not extend to GCX’s later affirmative payment claims. (Id.)

PSF also argues that the assignment transferred contractual rights but did not impose Sonnenblick’s obligations on PSF. PSF contends the Assignment of Construction Documents expressly disclaims any assumption of obligations, and that section 13.2.2 conditions Sonnenblick’s right to assign but does not impose the Owner’s obligations on PSF. (Motion, pp. 15-18.)

Finally, PSF argues that GCX waived any right to arbitrate its affirmative payment claims by litigating against PSF through adverse rulings before commencing the new arbitration. PSF alternatively argues that GCX’s payment claim is barred by claim preclusion and claim splitting. PSF further contends that absent a stay, it will be forced to arbitrate claims to which it never consented, while GCX will suffer no prejudice from the limited stay. (Motion, pp. 19-23.)

GCX opposes the motion, arguing that the arbitrator, rather than the Court, must determine whether GCX’s affirmative claims are arbitrable. GCX contends the Construction Agreement incorporates the AAA Construction Industry Arbitration Rules, which authorize the arbitrator to determine objections concerning the existence, scope, or validity of the arbitration agreement. GCX further argues that PSF consented to those rules by commencing and participating in arbitration under the Construction Agreement. (Opposition, pp. 8-10.)

GCX alternatively argues that PSF consented to arbitration of GCX’s claims by invoking the Construction Agreement’s arbitration provision to pursue its own claims against GCX. GCX disputes PSF’s reliance on section 15.4.4.2, arguing that the provision concerns joinder of additional parties and does not limit the scope of claims subject to arbitration. (Opposition, pp. 11-13.)

GCX further argues that PSF assumed Sonnenblick’s obligations under the Construction Agreement. GCX contends that the Loan Agreement, Assignment Agreement, and Construction Agreement, read together, establish that PSF’s exercise of its rights following Sonnenblick’s default required PSF to assume the Owner’s obligations, including the obligation to pay GCX and arbitrate payment disputes. GCX also disputes PSF’s interpretation of the Assignment Agreement’s disclaimer of assumption. (Opposition, pp. 13-17.)

Finally, GCX argues that it did not waive arbitration because its mechanic’s lien foreclosure claims preceded PSF’s arbitration and were distinct from its present payment claim, which GCX contends did not ripen until the Court determined its mechanic’s liens were extinguished. GCX also argues that claim preclusion does not apply because neither the summary judgment on its foreclosure claims nor the denial of leave to add a quantum meruit claim adjudicated the merits of its present breach-of-contract claim. GCX further contends PSF will suffer no irreparable harm because PSF may raise its arbitrability objection before the arbitrator. (Opposition, pp. 17-20.)

The Court must decide whether PSF agreed to arbitrate GCX’s affirmative payment claims

The Court agrees with PSF that it must first determine whether PSF agreed to arbitrate GCX’s affirmative payment claims. GCX argues that the Construction Agreement incorporates the AAA Construction Industry Arbitration Rules, which authorize the arbitrator to determine objections concerning the existence, scope, or validity of the arbitration agreement. (Opposition, pp. 8-10.) Here, however, PSF is a nonsignatory to the Construction Agreement and disputes whether it agreed to arbitrate GCX’s affirmative payment claims in the first instance. Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2, the Court may order arbitration only if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists. California authority likewise provides that whether an arbitration agreement binds a nonsignatory is a threshold question for the court. (Benaroya v. Willis (2018) 23 Cal.App.5th 462, 469.) Moreover, incorporation of arbitral rules does not displace the Court’s role where the threshold dispute is whether the party agreed to arbitrate in the first place. (Banc of California, National Assn. v. Superior Court (2021) 69 Cal.App.5th 357, 367.)

Thus, incorporation of the AAA Rules does not, by itself, establish that PSF agreed to arbitrate or delegate arbitrability as to GCX’s affirmative payment claims. The Court must first determine whether PSF’s assent to arbitration extends to those claims.

PSF’s arbitration demand did not constitute consent to GCX’s affirmative payment claims

GCX argues that PSF consented to arbitration by bringing its own claims against GCX under the Construction Agreement. According to GCX, PSF cannot invoke the arbitration provision to pursue its own claims while limiting its consent to those claims. (Opposition, pp. 11-12.)

The Court is not persuaded. GCX bears the burden of establishing an agreement to arbitrate the controversy at issue. PSF’s September 2024 arbitration demand shows that PSF consented to arbitrate its assigned claims against GCX for defective, incomplete, and nonconforming work. It does not, however, establish that PSF agreed to arbitrate all claims arising under the Construction Agreement, including GCX’s affirmative payment claims.

GCX argues that section 15.4.4.2 concerns joinder and does not limit the scope of the arbitration provision. (Opposition, pp. 12-13.) While the provision addresses joinder, it also states that an additional entity’s written consent to arbitration does not constitute consent to arbitrate claims not described in the written consent. The provision is therefore consistent with PSF’s position that its consent was limited to the claims it submitted and does not establish the broader consent urged by GCX.

Thus, PSF’s commencement of arbitration, standing alone, does not establish that it agreed to arbitrate GCX’s affirmative payment claims.

PSF did not assume Sonnenblick’s obligations under the Construction Agreement

GCX also argues that PSF assumed Sonnenblick’s obligations under the Construction Agreement through the Loan Agreement and Assignment of Construction Documents. GCX relies principally on section 13.2.2 of the Construction Agreement, which permits the Owner to assign the contract to a construction lender if the lender assumes the Owner’s rights and obligations. (Opposition, pp. 13-17.)

The Court is not persuaded. Section 13.2.2 addresses when the Owner may assign the Construction Agreement without the Contractor’s consent. It does not, by itself, constitute an assumption by a nonsignatory lender of the Owner’s obligations. PSF was not a party to the Construction Agreement, and GCX must therefore establish some separate basis for finding that PSF assumed Sonnenblick’s obligations.

The Assignment of Construction Documents weighs against such a finding. The agreement gave the lender certain rights, including the right, but not the obligation, to cure defaults and enforce the Construction Documents for its own benefit. However, the same agreement provides that the lender “shall not be obligated to perform or discharge” obligations under the Construction Documents and that “[n]either this assignment nor any action by Assignee shall constitute an assumption by Assignee of any obligations under any Construction Documents.” GCX argues that these provisions must be read together and that PSF’s exercise of its rights following Sonnenblick’s default required it to perform the Owner’s obligations. (Opposition, pp. 15-16.) However, that interpretation is difficult to reconcile with the agreement’s express language that neither the assignment nor any action by the assignee constitutes an assumption.

Nor do the later documents establish such an assumption. The Assignment and Assumption of Contracts Agreement expressly identifies the “Management Agreement” as the agreement whose obligations PSF assumed, not the Construction Agreement. Thus, the assignment documents do not establish that PSF assumed Sonnerblick’s payment obligations to GCX or, on that basis, agreed to arbitrate GCX’s affirmative payment claims.

Stay is permitted

Having determined that PSF did not agree to arbitrate GCX’s affirmative payment claims, the balance of harms favors the requested relief. Absent a stay, PSF would be required to arbitrate claims it did not agree to arbitrate. Thus, the Court finds that staying or enjoining GCX’s affirmative payment claims against PSF is warranted.

Because the Court finds that PSF did not agree to arbitrate GCX’s affirmative payment claims, the Court does not reach PSF’s alternative arguments concerning waiver, claim preclusion, or claim splitting.

The Court GRANTS PSF’s motion to stay arbitration.

IV.       ORDER

            The Court GRANTS PSF’s motion to stay arbitration. GCX is stayed from prosecuting its affirmative claims against PSF in AAA Case No. 01-26-0000-40266 based on alleged payment or other obligations under the Construction Contract. PSF’s assigned defect, warranty, and incomplete-work claims in AAA Case No. 01-24-0007-6210, and GCX’s claims against the subcontractors, may proceed.

            PSF is to give notice.
