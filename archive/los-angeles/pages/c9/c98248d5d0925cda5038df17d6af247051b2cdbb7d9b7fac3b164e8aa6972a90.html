Case Number: 25STCV26972    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 414

25STCV26972 Aaron Celious v. Ana Gabriela Lopez, et al.

Thursday, September 17, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING MOTION FOR RECONSIDERATION OF JUNE 2, 2026 ORDER GRANTING  JUDGMENT ON THE PLEADINGS AS TO THE THIRD AND FOURTH CAUSES OF ACTION; ALTERNATIVE REQUEST FOR SUA SPONTE RECONSIDERATION (0933)

 

I.       BACKGROUND

The complaint alleges Defendants, Plaintiff’s neighbors, deprived Plaintiff from using public roads and sideways, engaged in overly loud nuisance activities, improperly operated a mobile car wash, defamed Plaintiff and generally harassed Plaintiff. Plaintiff’s complaint asserts causes of action for public nuisance, private nuisance, defamation, false light, intentional infliction of emotional distress, civil harassment and negligence.

On June 2, 2026, the Court considered Defendant, Ana Gabriela Lopez’s (“Lopez”), motion to dismiss claims alleged against her. Construing the motion as one for judgment on the pleadings, the Court granted the motion and dismissed the third and fourth causes of action for defamation and false light, respectively, on grounds the claims were barred by the statute of limitations.

Plaintiff, Aaron Celious, moves for reconsideration of the Court’s ruling. Plaintiff contends the Court did not address three provisions of California law that independently bear on whether the complaint was timely filed: Code of Civil Procedure section 356 (statutory tolling while commencement is barred by statutory prohibition), section 12a (extension of a deadline that falls on a weekend or holiday), and section 1010.6, subdivision (e)(4)(E) (statutory tolling for electronically filed complaints rejected on technical grounds).

Plaintiff also alleges that the two factual premises on which the Court relied are refuted by the record with respect to Plaintiff’s 26-day delay in filing the complaint once granted leave by the presiding judge. Plaintiff argues he did not have any means of becoming aware of the ruling which was served by mail during a time that Plaintiff was traveling abroad.

Plaintiff argues that these issues were not raised earlier because Plaintiff opposed the arguments actually made. The order introduced a materially difference circumstances by rejecting tolling because “Plaintiff was not pursuing an alternative remedy or forum.” (Mot.  6:22-25.)

Plaintiff served the motion on Defendants including Ana Gabriela Lopez, who made the motion to dismiss. No opposition has been filed.

II.     LEGAL STANDARDS

      A motion for reconsideration may be brought by any party affected by the court’s order within 10 days after service of written notice of entry of the order if new or different facts, circumstances, or law warrant reconsideration and modification, amendment, or revocation of the prior order. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008 subd. (a).) The clerk was directed to give notice of the Court’s ruling, however, the Court’s file does not reflect that notice was given. Regardless, Plaintiff’s motion is timely made, six days after the Court’s June 2, 2026, ruling.

      The court also has discretion to reconsider an order on its own motion, pursuant to its inherent authority. Section 1008 “limit[s] the parties' ability to file repetitive motions but do not limit the court's ability, on its own motion, to reconsider its prior interim orders so it may correct its own errors." (Le Francois v. Goel (2005) 29 Cal.Rptr.3d 249.)

      Regardless of whether the motion is statutorily made or is based on the Court’s inherent authority, Plaintiff is required to show new or different facts, circumstances, or law that would warrant reconsideration. Plaintiff is required to offer some fact or circumstance not previously considered and a valid reason for not offering it earlier. (Gilberd v. AC Transit (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 1494, 1500.)

 

III.  DISCUSSION

      On June 2, 2026, the Court granted Lopez’s motion to dismiss on statute of limitations grounds. As noted in the Court’s ruling, Lopez’s motion was granted because the applicable statute of limitations barred the claims, and Plaintiff did not demonstrate that equitable tolling applied. (M.O. 6/2/26.) Plaintiff was required to show three elements: (1) timely notice of the claim to Defendant during the statutory period, (2) lack of prejudice to a defendant in gathering and preserving evidence; and (3) Plaintiff’s reasonableness and good faith. (Saint Francis Memorial Hosp. v. State Dept. of Pub. Health (2020) 9 Cal.5th 710, 724.) Addison v. State of California (1978) 21 Cal.3d 313, 319 on which Plaintiff relied in opposing the motion to dismiss concurs that the same three elements are required to establish equitable tolling.

      Plaintiff’s motion challenges facts on which the Court relied. (Mot.  3:14-18.) This is not a new, fact, circumstance, or law that could support a motion for reconsideration. Plaintiff argues that Court’s factual findings or interpretation of those facts were unsupported by the record. In Gilberd where the plaintiff argued that the Court misinterpreted the law, the Gilberd Court determined that the argument was “utterly specious” as a disagreement with the Court’s ruling is not a proper basis for the motion. (Gilberd at 1500 ["Since in almost all instances, the losing party will believe that the trial court's ‘different’ interpretation of the law or facts was erroneous, to interpret the statute as the respondent urges would be contrary to the clear legislative intent to restrict motions to reconsider to circumstances where a party offers the court some fact or authority that was not previously considered by it.”].)

      Plaintiff then cites three statutory provisions that he argues would independently bear on whether the claims were barred on limitations grounds. Plaintiff faults the Court for not addressing these statutes. (Opp.  3:5-10.)  It was incumbent on Plaintiff, however, to provide a basis to avoid the limitations bar. It is not the Court’s role to advocate for Plaintiff and determine every possible defense that would avoid the limitations bar. Additionally, the legal authority cited by Plaintiff are not new, or different facts, circumstance, or law to support reconsideration, nor has Plaintiff shown why he did not make these arguments earlier.

 

IV. CONCLUSION

      Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion for reconsideration is DENIED.


Case Number: 26STCP00104    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 414

26STCP00104 Peter Schroeder v. Telzio, Inc.

Thursday, September 17, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER ON PETITION TO COMPEL INSPECTION OF CORPORATE RECORDS

 

I.       BACKGROUND

      Plaintiff filed this petition to compel inspection of corporate records on July 8, 2026, pursuant to Corporations Code § 1601, which entitles shareholders to inspect and copy

accounting books, records, and minutes of proceedings of shareholders and the board of

directors for purposes reasonably related to their interests as shareholders. (Corp. Code, § 1601 (a)(1).) Petitioner is a founder, former officer, and current shareholder of Respondent, Telzio, Inc. (“Telzio”) (Petn. ¶ 7.)

      Petitioner alleges that he made a written statutory demand to inspect and copy corporate records by email and certified mail directed to Respondent and its Chief Executive Officer. (Id. at ¶ 9.) While Petitioner was granted access to certain documents, the documents were partial and incomplete. (Id. ¶ 12.) Respondent’s co-founder and officer of Telzio asserts that Telzio has complied with its statutory obligations to permit inspection. (Diana Chu decl., ¶2 filed 8/24/26.)

      Respondent, Telzio, filed its response to the petition asserting affirmative defenses. (Rsp. 8/24/26.)

      Respondent filed a Notice of Stay of this action pursuant to a stipulation filed in the Family Court bearing Case No. 24STFL06012. (Alliance decl., 2/11/26.) Mr. Alliance is counsel of record for Ms. Chu in that proceeding. The stipulation, signed by Hon. Joshua D. Wayser, provides that “[t]here will be a stay on the related action filed by Mr. Schroeder against Telzio under a separate case number 26STCP00104. Should that case move forward, then the documents Mr. Schroeder received through discovery concerning Telzio's records can be used by Mr. Schroeder within that lawsuit as 'evidence' in his case.” (Id. at ¶ 10.) Despite this stipulation and order, Mr. Schroeder requests a resolution on the merits of his civil petition. Notably, the stipulation provides that Ms. Chu “will provide the applicable corporate documents” requested by Mr. Schroeder. (Id., pg. 2, ¶ 4.)

      Mr. Alliance contends that because of this Court-ordered stay, this Court does not have the authority to proceed with this matter.  (Alliance decl. filed 8/24/26.)

      Mr. Schroeder requests that this court determine the procedural interaction between this case and the family law case as directed by the Hon. David I. Wasserman that “[a]ny requests regarding the scheduling deadlines in the civil matter and their interaction with the stipulation entered in the family law matter should be raised with Dept. 414.” (Reply 8/28/26, Petr’s RJN, Ex. A.)

      Respondent’s brief argues that this dispute is a “byproduct” of the divorce proceedings, and that the Family Court has “continued to oversee the parties’ document dispute.” (Brief filed 9/10/26, 2:14-15.)  Telzio requests that this Court honor the stay and continue the September 17 hearing without reaching the merits as the Family Court supervises the stipulation and order. (Id. 2:21-22.)  

 

II.   ANALYSIS

      After review of all of the briefs and declarations submitted by both parties, it appears that the documents requested by petition in this action overlap with the parties’ discovery disputes in the Family Court matter bearing Case No., 24STFL0612 pending in Department 829 of the Stanley Mosk Courthouse, Hon. David I. Wasserman presiding. While this Court questions another court’s authority to stay matters not before it, it is clear from the Family Court order that this matter affects the Family Court’s supervision and control over discovery of matters at issue in the dissolution proceeding.

      Accordingly, the Court orders Respondent, who requests that this Court honor the stay, to file a Notice of Related Cases to relate this matter to the Family Court proceeding.  (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.300.) As one of the cases is a family law case, “the presiding judge or a judge designated by the presiding judge must determine whether the cases should be ordered related and, if so, to which judge or department they should be assigned.” ( Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.300(h)(1)(C).) Respondent is directed to file the Notice of Related Cases in Department 534, (formerly Department 1.) Further proceedings in this action are stayed pending a determination by the Presiding Judge on the Notice of Related Case filing.

 


Case Number: 26STCV09282    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 414

26STCV09282 Stephen Foster v. Los Angeles Unified School District

Thursday, June 18, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER OVERRULING DEMURRER TO PLAINTIFF’S FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT (9827)

 

I.         BACKGROUND

      The first amended complaint filed June 10, 2026, alleges that Defendant employed Plaintiff for 30 years during which time Plaintiff served as an Assistant Principal for 20 years. Defendant allegedly discriminated against Plaintiff based on his disability stemming from a work-related injury. Defendant allegedly failed to provide reasonable accommodation and instead placed Plaintiff on unpaid leave.

      Plaintiff alleges he was constructively terminated as Defendant did not honor Plaintiff’s request to return to work. The first amended complaint asserts claims for disability discrimination, failure to reasonable accommodate, and failure to engage in the interactive process in good faith all in violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act (“FEHA”).

      Defendant demurs to all causes of action.           .

II.        LEGAL STANDARDS

      A demurrer tests the sufficiency of a complaint as a matter of law and raises only questions of law. (Schmidt v. Foundation Health (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1702, 1706.) In testing the complaint’s sufficiency, the court must assume the truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations as well as facts that can be reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded facts. The court may also consider matters properly subject to judicial notice. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

      The court may not consider contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law. (Moore v. Conliffe (1994) 7 Cal.4th 634, 638.) Plaintiff is required to allege facts sufficient to establish every element of each cause of action. (Rakestraw v. California Physicians Service (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 39, 43.) Where the complaint fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, courts should sustain the demurrer. Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(e); (Zelig v. County of Los Angeles (2002) 27 Cal.4th 1112, 1126.)

      Sufficient facts are the essential facts of the case “with reasonable precision and with particularity that is sufficiently specific to acquaint the defendant with the nature, source, and extent of his cause of action.” (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 643-644.)  Whether the Plaintiff will be able to prove the pleaded facts is irrelevant. (Stevens v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 605, 609–610.)

      A demurrer may also be sustained if a complaint is “uncertain.” Uncertainty exists where a complaint’s factual allegations are so confusing, they do not sufficiently apprise a defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2; Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(f).)

      A pleading is required to assert general allegations of ultimate fact. Evidentiary facts are not required. (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal. 4th 26, 47; Lim v. The.TV Corp. Internat. (2002) 99 Cal. App. 4th 684, 690.) However, unlike federal courts, California state courts are not a notice pleading jurisdiction, and notice alone is not a sufficient basis for any pleading. California is a fact pleading jurisdiction. Merely putting an opposing party on notice is not sufficient. (Bach v. County of Butte (1983) 147 Cal.App.3d 554, 561; see Diodes, Inc. v. Franzen (1968) 260 Cal.App.2d 244, 250.)

III.       DISCUSSION

A.     The allegations are not “legal conclusions” unsupported by any facts as Defendant contends.

      Defendant argues that the allegations “parrot” the statute or jury instruction without providing any factual predicate. (Dem 3:3-4.)  The difference between a conclusion of law and an ultimate fact is “not at all clear.” (Perkins at 7.) The language must be read in the context of the complaint as a whole. (Perkins v. Superior Court (1981) 117 Cal.App.3d 1, 6 ["What is important is that the complaint as a whole contain sufficient facts to apprise the defendant of the basis upon which the plaintiff is seeking relief.”].) Thus, each cause of action alleged must be read in the context of the specific “common facts” from which each cause of action is based. (FAC ¶¶ 12-17.) Demurrer on this ground is OVERRULED.

 

B.     First cause of action for disability discrimination

      To state a claim for disability discrimination in violation of FEHA, the plaintiff must show that he or she “(1) suffered from a disability, or was regarded as suffering from a disability; (2) could perform the essential duties of the job with or without reasonable accommodations, and (3) was subjected to an adverse employment action because of the disability or perceived disability." (Gov. Code, § 12940 subd. (a).)

      Defendant contends Plaintiff fails to allege facts supporting the allegation that Plaintiff’s disability was a substantial motivating reason for the adverse employment action. Discriminatory motive, however, need not be established by direct evidence and may be inferred from circumstantial facts. (Sandell v. Taylor-Listug, Inc. (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 297, 310.) Moreover, at the pleading stage, a plaintiff is required to plead ultimate facts, not the evidence by which those facts will ultimately be proved. (Perkins, supra.) The relevant question, therefore, is whether the facts alleged, taken as true and considered together, are sufficient to support a reasonable inference that Plaintiff’s disability was a substantial motivating reason for Defendant’s challenged conduct.  

      The timeline provides the necessary inference of discrimination. Plaintiff alleges he suffered injury on December 12, 2022. He began medical leave on January 13, 2023. (FAC ¶ 14.) Plaintiff requested reasonable accommodation, instead Defendant placed Plaintiff on medical leave beginning January 22, 2023 until his constructive termination on January 31, 2025 during which time Plaintiff participated in multiple meetings with Defendant’s “Return to Work” team to be reinstated to a position consistent with his physical restrictions. However, these requests were never honored by Defendant. As a result, the pleading alleges Plaintiff was forced to use his accrued paid sick leave time beginning in January of 2023, until his benefits ran out on January 31, 2025. By that time, Plaintiff had been attempting to be reinstated to work for over two years without any cooperation from Defendant, although Plaintiff alleges he was able to work with reasonable accommodation. (FAC ¶ 15-17.)

      The alleged refusal to accommodate Plaintiff or to reinstate Plaintiff for over a two-year period implies that the disability was the substantial motivating factor.

      Defendant also argues that Plaintiff must allege a statement, communication, or conduct by any decision-maker linking Plaintiff’s disability to the alleged constructive discharge. Defendant does not cite any authority for this level of specificity. However, Plaintiff alleges he participated in multiple meetings with Defendant’s Return to Work team, who did not honor Plaintiff’s requests. (FAC ¶ 15.) Plaintiff alleges he reasonably concluded that further efforts would be futile, and he resigned from employment. (FAC ¶ 16.)

 

C.     Second cause of action for failure to accommodate

      FEHA imposes an affirmative obligation on the employer to reasonably accommodate a known disability of an employee only where the accommodations would enable the employee to perform the essential functions of his job. (Gov. Code, § 12940 subd. (m); Nadaf-Rahrov v. Neiman Marcus Group, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 952, 975 ["We conclude an employer is liable under section 12940(m) for failing to accommodate an employee only if the work environment could have been modified or adjusted in a manner that would have enabled the employee to perform the essential functions of the job."].)

      The elements of this claim are similar to a disability discrimination claim, however, Plaintiff does not have to show an adverse employment action, as the failure to adhere to statutory obligations is itself a violation. (Jensen v. Wells Fargo Bank (2000) 85 Cal.App.4th 245, 256

 Plaintiff also does not need to show he is a “qualified" employee. (Bagatti v. Department of Rehabilitation (2002) 97 Cal.App.4th 344, 361 fn. 4.)

      Defendant argues Plaintiff does not allege what specific accommodation was requested, the specific position that was available, when the request was made, to whom it was directed and the Defendant’s response thereto. There is no authority cited requiring this level of specificity. Plaintiff is not required to allege the specific evidence necessary to prove the claim. General rules of pleading do not require that the plaintiff plead evidentiary facts supporting the allegation of ultimate fact. (Careau & Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1371, 1390 [“It is both improper and insufficient for a plaintiff to simply plead the evidence by which he hopes to prove such ultimate facts."].)

 

D.     Third cause of action for failure to engage in good faith in the interactive process.

 

      FEHA imposes a duty on an employer to engage in a timely, good-faith interactive process to identify a reasonable accommodation for an employee with a known disability. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (n).) Although failure to engage in the interactive process and failure to provide a reasonable accommodation are separate causes of action, the two duties are closely related. As Gelfo v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 34, 54, explains, “[e]ach necessarily implicates the other.”

      Plaintiff alleges that he participated in the interactive process on numerous occasions between 2023 and 2025. (FAC ¶ 62.) Defendant argues these allegations are insufficient because Plaintiff does not describe what occurred at each meeting or identify the specific matters discussed. Defendant, however, cites no authority requiring that degree of evidentiary detail at the pleading stage. Plaintiff need not plead the substance of every interactive-process meeting. The issue is whether the FAC alleges sufficient ultimate facts to support the claim that Defendant failed to participate in the process in a timely and good-faith manner.

      Regardless, the first amended complaint read as a whole alleges that disability discrimination was the motivating factor. This inference supports the requirement that Defendant acted unreasonably even if it participated in meetings with Plaintiff over a two-year period.         

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing, demurrer to the complaint is OVERRULED. Defendant is ordered to answer within 10 days.
