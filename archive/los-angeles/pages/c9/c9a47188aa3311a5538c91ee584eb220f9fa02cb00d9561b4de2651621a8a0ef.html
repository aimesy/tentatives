[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES

 

Petitioner Timothy Ryan, M.D (“Petitioner” or “Ryan”) moves for attorney fees and costs under Government Code section 7923.115, subdivision (a), and the January 16, 2026 “Second Amended Stipulation and Order Re: Timing for Petitioner’s Attorney’s Fees and Costs.”

The motion is granted in reduced amount.

I.                Background

Petitioner filed his verified Petition for Declaratory Relief and Writ of Mandate on March 16, 2023. The Petition asserts a single cause of action under the California Public Records Act (“CPRA”) (Gov. Code § 7920.000 et seq.).

Petitioner alleges that from 2013 to 2018, he was employed at Harbor-UCLA Medical Center (“Harbor-UCLA”) as a vascular surgeon. Petitioner alleges that Respondent County of Los Angeles (“Respondent” or “County”) failed to provide timely and lawful determinations on multiple CPRA requests and unlawfully withheld public records relating to three subjects: (1) records concerning allegations of misconduct and the suspension of a former department chair at Harbor-UCLA Medical Center; (2) records concerning County investigations related to Petitioner’s prior whistleblower complaints, including matters for which a jury previously found the County had retaliated against him; and (3) records concerning alleged diversion or theft of controlled substances at Harbor-UCLA that were discussed by the hospital’s Medical Executive Committee in 2016.

Petitioner alleges that he made several CPRA requests seeking documents on these topics and that the County repeatedly invoked extensions, refused to confirm the existence of certain records, and ultimately denied the requests based on various claimed exemptions. He further alleges that the County did not provide timely determinations as required by statute, did not assist him in identifying or obtaining responsive records, and improperly withheld documents in their entirety. As a result, the Petition seeks a writ compelling disclosure, declaratory relief, and recovery of attorney fees and costs.

On March 28, 2024, the Court held a hearing on the petition and ordered disclosure of certain categories of documents. On October 24, 2024, the Court ordered the County to produce additional documents, after which the Court of Appeal issued a writ regarding privileged records. On February 4, 2025, the Court issued an order consistent with the Court of Appeal’s writ, finding no waiver of the attorney-client privilege for certain categories of documents sought by Petitioner.

II.             Evidentiary Matters

Respondent objects to portions of the Declaration of Michael F. Ram (“Ram”). Each objection is one or more of lack of personal knowledge, lack of foundation, inadmissible speculation and conclusion, and relevance. Respondent does not object to Ram’s expert qualifications. Ram explains that he reviewed the filings in the case and the hours Petitioner’s counsel billed. (Ram Decl., ¶¶ 7-12.) Ram’s expert opinion on the reasonableness of Petitioner’s counsel’s billing is therefore relevant, founded expert opinion not requiring personal knowledge. The objections are overruled.

Respondent likewise objects to portions of the Declaration of Thomas R. Burke (“Burke”) for lack of personal knowledge, lack of foundation, inadmissible speculation and conclusion, and relevance. Respondent does not object to Burke’s expert qualifications. Burke explains that he reviewed the filings in the case and the hours Petitioner’s counsel billed. (Burke Decl., ¶¶ 13-16.) Burke’s expert opinion on the reasonableness of Petitioner’s counsel’s billing is therefore relevant, founded expert opinion not requiring personal knowledge. The objections are overruled.

Petitioner objects to portions of the Declaration of Mira Hashmall (“Hashmall”). The objections to testimony on unrelated prior litigation are sustained for irrelevance. This includes paragraphs 2 through 11 (objection nos. 1 through 3), footnote no. 1 (objection no. 4), paragraph 64 (objection no. 7), and Exhibits 1 and 6 (objection nos. 9 and 10). The objection to part of paragraph 63 (objection no. 6) is also sustained for irrelevance. The remaining objections are overruled.

The Court will assign the proper weight to admitted evidence.

III.           Petitioner’s Motion for Attorney Fees and Costs

On March 18, 2026, Petitioner filed the instant Motion for Attorney’s Fees and Costs under the CPRA, seeking $388,350.50 in attorney fees, $75,000 in “fees on fees,” and $13,832.82 in costs. Petitioner claims to have obtained multiple court rulings ordering disclosure of records and securing the release of thousands of pages of previously withheld public records since this lawsuit was filed.

It is undisputed that Petitioner is entitled to fees, though the parties dispute the appropriate amounts. The County contends that Petitioner achieved only partial success, that many of the hours billed were excessive, duplicative, block-billed, or devoted to issues on which Petitioner did not prevail, and that substantial portions of the billing are too vague or redacted to evaluate. The County also argues that Petitioner’s requested rates are inflated, and that certain claimed costs were unnecessary.

An award of costs and fees is mandatory under the CPRA if the plaintiff prevails. (Gov. Code § 7923.115; Riskin v. Downtown Los Angeles Property Owners Assn. (2022) 76 Cal.App.5th 438, 445; see also Filarsky v. Superior Court (2002) 28 Cal.4th 419, 427.) A plaintiff “prevails” in a CPRA action “when he or she files an action which results in defendant releasing a copy of a previously withheld document.” (Riskin, 76 Cal.App.5th at 445.) “An action results in previously withheld documents ‘if the lawsuit motivated the defendants to produce the documents.’” (Ibid. [quoting Galbiso v. Orosi Public Utility Dist. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1063, 1085].) 

Reasonable attorneys’ fees are determined pursuant to the “lodestar” or “touchstone” method. Under this approach, a base amount is calculated from a compilation of time reasonably spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 48; Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 639; Meister v. Regents of Univ. of Calif. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 437, 446-47.) The lodestar method applies to statutory attorney fee awards unless the underlying statute provides for another method of calculation. (Meister, 67 Cal.App.4th at 448-49.)

The lodestar method vests the Court with discretion to decide which of the hours expended by the attorneys were “reasonably spent” on the litigation. (Hammond v. Argan (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 115, 133; Meister, 67 Cal.App.4th at 449.) The predicate of any attorney fee award is the necessity and usefulness of the conduct for which compensation is sought. (Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 819, 846.) The base amount may then be adjusted in light of various factors including, but not limited to, the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, the skill displayed in presenting them and/or the success achieved.  (See e.g., Chavez y. Netflix, Inc. (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 43, 61; Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal. 4th 1122, 1139; Harman v. City and County of San Francisco (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 407, 425-426.)

IV.           Analysis

1.               Reasonable Attorney Fees

            The court employs the “lodestar” analysis when looking to determine the “reasonableness” of an attorney fee award.  The lodestar figure is calculated by multiplying the number of hours reasonably spent by the reasonable market billing rate. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 48.)

a.                Hourly Rates

Ryan seeks the attorney fees at the following rates:

(a)  $945 hourly for Karl Olson (“Olson”), senior partner;

(b)  $945 hourly for Therese Cannata (“Cannata”), senior partner;

(c)   $600 hourly for Zachary Colbeth (“Colbeth”), partner;

(d)  $600 hourly for Aaron Field (“Field”), partner;

(e)  $350 hourly for Irene Lee (“Lee”), senior associate;

(f)    $300 hourly for Haley Meyer (“Meyer”), associate; and

(g)  $175 hourly for paralegal time.

(Olson Decl., ¶ 21.) Ryan supports the reasonableness of these rates with expert declarations. (Ram Decl., ¶ 6; Burke Decl., ¶ 4.)

The County, by reference to the Wolters Kluwer 2023 Real Rate Report, argues that these rates are outside the reasonable range for attorneys in the Los Angeles and San Francisco markets from firms of less than 51 attorneys. (Opp. at 16; Hashmall Decl., Ex. 10.) Per the 2023 Real Rate Report, partners at firms of 50 or fewer attorneys charged, at median, $349 hourly for litigation, while associates charged $209. (Hashmall Decl., Ex. 10 at p. 60.) For general liability practice in San Francisco specifically, the median partner charged $280 hourly in 2023. (Hashmall Decl., Ex. 10 at p. 128.)

In reply, Ryan argues the 2023 Real Rate Report does not show rates for attorneys specializing in public access, media, or First Amendment issues.[1]

“The courts repeatedly have stated that the trial court is in the best position to value the services rendered by the attorneys in his or her courtroom [citation], and this includes the determination of the hourly rate that will be used in the lodestar calculus. [Citation.]” (569 E. Cnty. Boulevard LLC v. Backcountry Against the Dump, Inc. (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 426, 436-437.) This determination considers the complexity of the case, the quality of services provided, and the attorneys’ experience. An award at the rates requested comes with the expectation that the attorneys work in an efficient manner that reflects the elevated premium paid for their services. The Court considers this factor in addressing the reasonableness of the hours expended.

The Court finds most of the hourly rates charged here reasonable, except for the senior partner $945 hourly rate. The senior partner rate is therefore reduced to $700 per hour.

b.               Hours Expended

Ryan’s counsel provided a verified fee bill, which is prima facie evidence that Ryan’s listed fees and costs were necessarily incurred. (Colbeth Decl., Ex. FF; Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682.)

The County bears the burden of challenging the specific portions of the fee bill it contends are improper or unreasonable. (Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc. v. California Ins. Guarantee Assn. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 564.) “In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence.” (Ibid.) “General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.” (Ibid.)

The County submits billing charts highlighting the billing entries it believes should be rejected as unreasonable. (Hashmall Decl., Exs. 7-9.) The Hashmall Declaration provides only a cursory explanation of why the Court should find the fees unreasonable, and fails in the majority of cases to tie the explanations to specific entries. (See Hashmall Decl., ¶¶ 71-74.) The Hashmall Declaration includes supporting legal argument, yet even so, only discusses the fees on pages 15-17 of the 17-page declaration. (Hashmall Decl., ¶¶ 66-74.) Of these pages, only about one page discusses the reasonableness of the hours billed. (Hashmall Decl., ¶¶ 69-71, 73-74.) While the opposition provides a more thorough description of the County’s objections, it fails to more particularly tie its objections to the identified billing entries. (Opp. at 11-15.)

The County has the burden to challenge specific items in the fee bill. Therefore, the Court will review only those entries the County objects to and explains in enough detail for the Court to understand the basis of the objection.

First, the County objects to block-billed entries. The County identifies entries for: (a) 2.8 hours by Field for “[p]repare for and attend additional calls w/ client re: [. . .]; further revise response to County based on calls, and finalize and send to County;” (b) 7.8 hours by Field for “[p]repare for and attend meet and confer call w/ opposing counsel and Mr. Colbeth re: disputed issues and correspond re: same. 3.1. Research, analyze, and draft brief listing matters in dispute in light of call, revise and file same. 4.7;” (c) 3.7 hours by Colbeth for “[d]raft and conduct research re status brief; telecon and email correspondence with Dr. Ryan re [. . .];” and (d) 11.5 hours by Colbeth for “[p]repare for PRA merits briefing, including preparing outlines and reviewing documents re same; travel to Los Angeles for PRA merits hearing; telecon and email correspondence with Dr. Ryan re [. . .].” (Opp. at 15.)

The Court agrees that these identified entries are block-billed. The Court therefore reduces Field’s hours by 10.6, and Colbeth’s by 15.2.

Second, the County objects to redacted time entries. The County identifies entries for (a) 6.8 hours by Colbeth for “[d]raft, edit, revise, conduct research, and finalize preliminary response to County’s appellate writ petition, including incorporating all edits; email correspondence with Dr. Ryan re [. . .].” and (b) 0.5 hours by Colbeth for “[r]eview email correspondence from Dr. Ryan re [. . .], and review notes from Dr. Ryan re [. . .].” (Opp. at 13-14.)

The Court does not find these entries are so unclear as to prevent the Court from determining whether the work was reasonable. These entries show only redactions to the content of communications between Ryan and counsel. These entries are presumptively reasonable, and the County has not met its burden to show otherwise.

Third, the County objects to entries it contends reflect excessive overlap between attorneys. The County identifies two instances, including a privilege log letter for which Colbeth and Field collectively billed 12.9 hours in May and June 2023 and June and July 2024. (Opp. at 12-13.)

The Court is not persuaded this time was unreasonable. The issue of the privilege log remained pending for over a year, and the Court on multiple occasions ordered the parties to meet and confer over the log’s production. Therefore, even if the Court accepts, arguendo, that the entries show work on the same letter, it is not clear that billing entries for a meet and confer letter relating to the privilege log, entered a year apart, establish duplicative effort.

Fourth, the County objects to excessive daily billing by Field. The County identifies entries for (a) 8.6 hours for “[r]esearch and continue drafting opening brief and associated documents re: writ petition;” (b) 10.9 hours on January 28, 2024 for “[r]eview and pull documents from relevant cases, research and draft opening brief and associated documents re: writ petition;” (c) 7.1 hours on January 28, 2024 for “[r]esearch and draft opening brief and supporting declarations, obtain input and comments from Dr. Ryan re [. . .] and implement, and review and pull additional case files and other records supportive of access in case;” and (d) 10.2 hours for “[r]esearch and draft supplemental brief re: scope of search and proposed in camera review and supporting Ryan declaration, work w/ Dr. Ryan to obtain approval of and to file and serve same.” (Opp. at 11.)

The Court agrees these entries suggest a failure to exercise billing judgment. Moreover, Ryan does not address this argument. The Court therefore reduces Field’s hours by 36.8.

Finally, the County objects to billed travel time, and Ryan does not address this argument. Field billed 34.6 hours for travel time not otherwise reduced above. Colbeth billed 5.9 hours for travel time not otherwise reduced above.

In total, the Court reduces Field’s hours by 82 hours from the claimed 367.5 hours (yielding 285.5 hours). The Court reduces Colbeth’s hours by 21.1 hours from the claimed 181.1 hours (yielding 160 hours).

c.                Total Lodestar Fees

Based on the foregoing, the total reasonable fees are:

(a)  19.4 hours at $700 hourly for Olson, totaling $13,580;

(b)  8.5 hours at $700 hourly for Cannata, totaling $5,950;

(c)   160 hours at $600 hourly for Colbeth, totaling $96,000;

(d)  285.5 hours at $600 hourly for Field, totaling $171,300;

(e)  25.9 hours at $350 hourly for Lee, totaling $9,065;

(f)    54.7 hours at $300 hourly for Meyer, totaling $16,410; and

(g)  42 hours at $175 hourly for paralegal time, totaling $7,350.

Together, the total lodestar value is $319,655.

d.               Multiplier

The County argues the total fees should be reduced for Ryan’s limited success.

As the cases cited by both Ryan and the County show (Mot. at 11-12; Opp. at 9-10; Reply at 2-4), a court may consider, but is not required to consider, the degree of the petitioner’s success as a factor in determining an award of reasonable attorney fees. “¿‘[W]hile the degree of the plaintiff’s success in obtaining the objectives of the litigation is a factor that the trial court may consider in determining an award of reasonable attorney fees under a fee statute [citations] including the CPRA fee statute [citation], . . . there is no requirement that the trial court make an award of attorney fees in an amount that is commensurate with or in proportion to the degree of success in the CPRA litigation.’¿” (Pacific Merchant Shipping Assn. v. Board of Pilot Commissioners (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 1043, 1060 [“Pacific Merchant”], quoting Bernardi v. County of Monterey (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1379, 1389 [“Bernardi”].) Whether to consider the degree of petitioner’s success is a matter of the trial court’s discretion. (See Pacific Merchant at 1060-61; Bernardi at 1395-98.)

Here, the County’s uncontroverted evidence shows it produced 414 records while withholding over 3,000. (Hashmall Decl., ¶¶ 32, 67.) Stated differently, Ryan was successful in compelling the County to produce approximately one in every eight records.

The County primarily relies on the assertion that Ryan consistently litigated clearly losing positions, including by repeatedly attempting to expand the scope of the requests after the Court required the parties to narrow the issues in a raise-it-or-waive-it briefing. (Opp. at 9-11.)

However, the record shows that at each substantive hearing, Ryan prevailed on significant issues that he raised. At the March 28, 2024 hearing on the petition, the Court ordered the County to produce documents, produce a privilege log, and inform the Court about the existence of other records which the Court might review in camera. At the October 24, 2024 hearing, the Court clarified its ruling in favor of Ryan. At the July 24, 2025 hearing on privilege and scope objections, the Court found that the County failed to meet its burden for attorney-client privilege on multiple issues.

While Ryan did not prevail on several significant issues, the record does not support the County’s characterization of Ryan as widely pursuing meritless positions and seeking to needlessly expand and protract litigation. Accordingly, the Court declines to reduce Ryan’s fees on this basis.

In reply, Ryan seeks an upward multiplier to compensate counsel for taking the case on a contingent risk. While contingency is a factor the court weighs when considering whether to award a multiplier (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122 at 1132; Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49), it is one of several factors and will not alone generally support an upward multiplier.

Based on its careful review of the record, the Court finds that a lodestar multiplier is not appropriate. Consideration of risk is already included within the lodestar amount. Nothing in the record indicates that the case presented novel issues or that the quality of representation far exceeded the quality of representation that would have been provided by attorneys of comparable skill and experience billing at the same rates.

The Court declines to award a lodestar multiplier.

2.               Costs

Regarding the County’s travel-related costs reduction request, the Court agrees with the County that such travel expenses do not appear to have been reasonably incurred. Ryan’s reply does not address County’s arguments in this regard, which the Court construes as a tacit admission that County’s arguments are correct. (See D.I. Chadbourne, Inc. v. Superior Court (1964) 60 Cal.2d 723, 728, fn. 4 [where nonmoving party fails to oppose a ground for a motion, "it is assumed that [nonmoving party] concedes" that ground].) The Court will therefore also reduce Ryan’s costs request to $10,781.95.

3.               Fees on Fees

Ryan seeks $75,000 for fees on the instant motion. $75,000 represents about 80 hours at the senior partner rate of $945 hourly, or 250 hours at the junior associate rate of $300. This is patently unreasonable for a fee motion where the matter of entitlement is not even at issue. The fees for the instant motion are reduced to $10,000.

V.              Conclusion

The motion is GRANTED in the amount of $329,655 in attorney fees, including fees on fees, and $10,781.95 in costs.

[TENTATIVE] RULING ON PETITION FOR WRIT OF MANDATE

 

Petitioner Louisiana Glen LP (“Petitioner”) petitions for a writ of traditional mandate against Respondent City of Los Angeles (“Respondent”) directing it to set aside its determination that the residential rental units on its property located at 10405 W Louisiana Ave., Los Angeles CA 90025 (“Property”) is subject to the Rent Stabilization Ordinance (“RSO”), Los Angeles Municipal Code (“LAMC”) sections 151.00 to 151.35.

The petition is denied.

I.                Factual Background

When Petitioner purchased the Property in about January 2019, it was improved with a ten-unit residential apartment building. (Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 5.)

On March 13, 2019, Petitioner filed a Notice of Intent to Withdraw the existing units from the residential market pursuant to Government Code sections 7060-7060.7 (“Ellis Act”) and LAMC sections 151.22-28. (Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 6.) Several tenants invoked mandatory one-year extended tenancy rights under Government Code section 7060.4(b) and LAMC section 151.23(B). (Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 7.) All tenants physically vacated before December 24, 2019. (Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 21; AR 570-76.) Respondent’s Housing Department (“LAHD”) confirmed the Property was fully vacated on May 13, 2020. (Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 16.)

Petitioner demolished the previous improvements and constructed a new, sixteen-unit residential apartment building on the Property. (Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 17.) On December 24, 2024, Respondent’s Department of Building and Safety (“LADBS”) issued a Certificate of Occupancy for the new structure. (Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 17.) No new tenants moved into the Property until April 1, 2025. (Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 17.)

On April 7, 2025, LAHD’s RSO Determinations Unit issued a letter, Determination No. RD056759, concluding the new units were subject to the RSO. (Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 19.) On April 20, 2025, Petitioner challenged the determination by an email to the RSO Determinations Unit. (Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 20.) Petitioner later sent a formal letter challenging the determination. (Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 20.) On June 5, 2025, Petitioner also sent a Conditional Exemption Application. (AR 568-69.)

II.             Procedural History

On July 7, 2025, Petitioner filed its verified petition. A proof of service filed July 17, 2025 shows Petitioner served the petition on Respondent by personal service on July 15, 2025. On August 14, 2025, Respondent filed its Answer.

On April 30, 2026, Petitioner filed its opening brief. On June 12, 2026, Respondent filed its opposition. On June 29, 2026, Petitioner filed its reply.

III.           Standard of Review

Petitioner seeks relief pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 1085. A party may seek to set aside an agency decision by petitioning for either a writ of administrative mandamus (CCP § 1094.5) or traditional mandamus (CCP § 1085).

A petition for traditional mandamus is appropriate in all actions “to compel the performance of an act which the law specially enjoins as a duty resulting from an office, trust, or station . . . .” (CCP §1085; see Rodriguez v. Solis (1991) 1 Cal.App.4th 495, 501-02.) Generally, mandamus will lie when (1) there is no plain, speedy, and adequate alternative remedy (2) the respondent has a duty to perform, and (3) the petitioner has a clear and beneficial right to performance.” (Pomona Police Officers’ Assn. v. City of Pomona (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 578, 583-84 [internal citations omitted].)

“Generally, mandamus is available to compel a public agency’s performance or to correct an agency’s abuse of discretion when the action being compelled or corrected is ministerial…” (AIDS Healthcare Foundation v. Los Angeles County Dept. of Public Health (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 693, 700.) Whether a statute imposes a ministerial duty for which mandamus is available, or a mere obligation to perform a discretionary function, is a question of statutory interpretation. (Id. at p. 701.)

In a petition for writ of mandate brought under CCP section 1085, “the petitioner bears the burden of pleading and proving the facts on which the claim for relief is based.” (California Correctional Peace Officers Assn. v. State Personnel Bd. (1995) 10 Cal.4th 1133, 1153.)

IV.           Evidentiary Matters

1.               Request for Judicial Notice

Respondent seeks judicial notice of the following exhibits: (1) the RSO (LAMC § 151.00-151.35) (RJN Ex. 1); (2) LAMC sections 22.600 to 22.601 (RJN Ex. 2); (3) LAMC sections 91.109.1 to 91.109.8 (RJN Ex. 3); (4) LAHD Council Transmittal: Report Back on Enforcement of Ellis Provisions of the Rent Stabilization Ordinance and Recommendation to Amend LAMC 152.22 – 151.28 on Withdrawal and Demolition of RSO Units (“Ellis Withdrawals”) and Replacement Rental Units, April 15, 2016, Council File 14-0268-S5 (RJN Ex. 4); and (5) Los Angeles Ordinance No. 184,873 (2017), Council File 14-0268-S5 (RJN Ex. 5).

The requests are granted. (Evid Code § 452(b), (c).)

2.               Evidentiary Objections

Respondent objects to portions of the Cohanzad Declaration. Respondent objects to paragraphs 19 and 20, in part, for offering legal conclusions. Paragraphs 19 and 20 include legal conclusions intermixed with factual testimony. The objections are overruled; while this evidence is admitted, the Court will disregard the legal conclusions.

The remaining objections are overruled. The evidence is admitted, and the Court will assign it the proper weight.

Petitioner objects to portions of the Garcia Declaration. The objection to paragraph 34 is sustained for inadmissible oral testimony to prove the contents of a writing. (Evid. Code § 1523.) The remaining objections are overruled. The evidence is admitted, and the Court will assign it the proper weight.

V.              Analysis

Petitioner seeks to compel Respondent to set aside its decision that the Property is subject to the RSO. Petitioner further seeks to compel Respondent to make a new determination that the Property is not subject to the RSO, or alternatively, to provide Petitioner an opportunity to submit evidence before making a new determination. Petitioner argues Respondent (1) relied on the wrong legal standard; (2) violated Petitioner’s due process rights; and (3) abused its discretion.

1.               Respondent Correctly Determined the RSO Applies to the Property

Petitioner argues Respondent applied the incorrect legal standard. Specifically, Petitioner argues the Ellis Act and RSO state that new units are subject to rent control if they are offered for lease or rent within five years of the date previous accommodations were withdrawn from the rental market, but Respondent instead calculated the time between the latest right to tenancy and issuance of a certificate of occupancy. (Pet. Op. Br. at 5.)

Government Code section 7060.2, subdivision (d), provides that public entities may apply rent control to newly constructed units if they are constructed on the same property as demolished units, and if the new units are offered for rent or lease within five years of the date the demolished units were withdrawn from rent or lease. (Gov. Code § 7060.2(d).) LAMC section 151.28, subdivision (A) applies the RSO to new units in accordance with Government Code section 7060.2, subdivision (d). (LAMC § 151.28(A).)

To obtain writ relief under CCP section 1085, the petitioner has the burden to show that the public entity has a mandatory and ministerial duty. (Siskiyou Hospital, Inc. v. County of Siskiyou (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 14, 36-37 [“Siskiyou”]; Brown v. Department of Motor Vehicles (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 206, 213.) The petitioner must further show that the public entity violated that ministerial duty. (Siskiyou at 37, quoting Carrancho v. California Air Resources Board (2003) 111 Cal.App.4th 1255, 1267.) A petitioner may also obtain writ relief under CCP section 1085 for abuse of discretion. (CV Amalgamated LLC v. City of Chula Vista (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 265, 278-79.) However, Petitioner has not alleged any discretionary duty by Respondent.

Respondent argues it correctly calculated the relevant dates.

a.                The Withdrawal Date Was March 13, 2020

LAMC section 151.23, subdivision (B) provides that a unit’s withdrawal date is “at least 120 days from the date of the delivery . . . of the Notice of Intent to Withdraw.” (LAMC § 151.23(B).) It further provides that, if certain qualified tenants give written notice, the withdrawal date is extended “to one year after the date of delivery of that Notice”. (Id.)

Here, the parties agree that Petitioner filed its Notice of Intent to Withdraw on March 13, 2019 (AR 13; Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 6.) The parties further agree such qualified tenants requested the extended one-year tenancy. (AR 526; Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 7.)

Petitioner argues that the withdrawal date should be December 24, 2019, when tenants physically vacated the units. (Pet. Op. Br. at 12; Reply at 7-8.) However, Petitioner cites no authority, statutory or otherwise, providing that the withdrawal date under either Government Code section 7060.4 or LAMC section 151.23 is determined by the date of actual vacancy. The statutory scheme defines “withdrawal” by reference to the date of delivery of the Notice of Intent to Withdraw plus the applicable statutory period, not by the date tenants happen to vacate. (LAMC § 151.23(B); Gov. Code § 7060.4(b).) Because neither provision makes actual vacatur relevant to calculating the withdrawal date, Petitioner’s factual evidence regarding vacancy does not alter the statutory withdrawal date.

Petitioner further suggests that Respondent must be relying on the 2020 amendment to Government Code section 7060.4, specifically subdivision (b)(6), which establishes the “latest termination date among all tenants” rule. (Reply at 9-10.) But Respondent does not cite section 7060.4(b)(6), and the withdrawal date determined here does not depend on that subdivision. Instead, Respondent’s position relies on LAMC section 151.23(B), which independently sets the withdrawal date at one year after delivery of the Notice where qualified tenants elect an extension. Because the withdrawal date in this matter is dictated by the municipal code provision that mirrors the pre-2020 Ellis Act framework, Petitioner’s retroactivity argument is not implicated.

Thus, based on the statutory formula, the withdrawal date is one year after Petitioner delivered the Notice of Intent to Withdraw on March 13, 2019, i.e., March 13, 2020. The parties do not dispute either the delivery date or the qualified tenants’ exercise of their one-year extension rights. (AR 13, 526; Cohanzad Decl. ¶¶ 6-7.) Because the governing provisions require calculation based on these dates rather than actual vacancy, the withdrawal date is March 13, 2020.

b.               Petitioner Has Not Met its Burden

Petitioner’s briefing invokes concepts associated with discretionary or adjudicatory decision-making (e.g., such as “abuse of discretion,” failure to bridge analytic gaps, or failure to make formal findings), but Petitioner does not identify any statute requiring Respondent to exercise discretion or to issue findings when making an RSO coverage determination. The governing provisions simply direct Respondent to apply Government Code section 7060.2(d) and LAMC section 151.28(A).

Although Petitioner does not expressly articulate a mandatory ministerial duty, the Court understands Petitioner’s theory to be that Respondent was required to apply these provisions correctly by determining the statutory withdrawal date and the statutory offer date. The Court therefore construes the petition as attempting to identify a ministerial duty grounded in proper application of the statutory framework. Even assuming Petitioner has framed such a duty, Petitioner must still prove that Respondent violated it.

Pursuant to CCP section 1085, a petitioner must first identify a mandatory, ministerial duty owed by the agency and then prove the agency violated that duty. (Siskiyou Hosp., Inc. v. County of Siskiyou (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 14, 36-37.) Petitioner attempts to shift this threshold burden by arguing that Respondent was required to establish statutory predicate facts before applying the RSO and by characterizing re-control as an “exception” that Respondent must affirmatively prove. These arguments do not alter Petitioner’s obligation under section 1085, nor do they substitute for evidence demonstrating that the statutory conditions for re-control were not met.

Here, the statutory framework governing Ellis Act re-control is mechanical. Re-control applies only if new units are “offered for rent or lease” within five years of the withdrawal date. (Gov. Code § 7060.2(d); LAMC § 151.28(A).) To show a violation of any ministerial duty under this framework, Petitioner needed to supply evidence that Respondent’s determination contradicted the statutory facts. Petitioner did not do so.

Respondent acknowledges that it used the certificate of occupancy date as a proxy for the offer date. Petitioner challenges that proxy but submits no evidence of the actual offer date. For example, Petitioner provides no declaration stating the units were first offered for rent after March 13, 2025, and no documentation showing when they were marketed. Because Petitioner’s theory depends on demonstrating that the units were not offered for rent within the five-year period, the absence of evidence on the actual offer date is fatal even under the most generous construction of Petitioner’s claimed ministerial duty.

The only undisputed dates in the record are that (1) the units could not have been offered earlier than December 24, 2024, when the certificates of occupancy issued (Cohanzad Decl., ¶ 17); and (2) at least one tenant moved in on April 1, 2025 (ibid.). These dates establish the outer bounds of the “offer for rent” window but do not establish that Petitioner refrained from offering the units for rent until after March 13, 2025. Without evidence of the actual offer date, Petitioner cannot show that Respondent violated any mandatory duty under the Ellis Act or the RSO. Because Petitioner bears the burden under CCP section 1085 to prove such a violation, and because the record permits the inference that Petitioner may have offered the units for rent within the five year period, Petitioner has not met its burden.

2.               Respondent Did Not Violate Petitioner’s Due Process

Petitioner argues Respondent violated its procedural due process because it received no notice or opportunity to be heard until after Respondent made its determination.

In opposition, Respondent argues that the California and United States Constitutions provide due process protections only for deprivation constitutionally-protected property interests. Respondent contends Petitioner has no constitutionally-protected interest in not being subject to the RSO.

“A denial of due process giving rise to a claim for relief arises only if application of the defective measure deprives one of a constitutionally protected right.” Hillsboro Properties v. City of Rohnert Park (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 379, 390 [“Hillsboro”], citing Los Angeles v. Heller (1986) 475 U.S. 796, 799, Carey v. Piphus (1978) 435 U.S. 247, 253-254, Utah Animal Rights Coalition v. Salt Lake City Corp. (10th Cir. 2004) 371 F.3d 1248, 1260-1261, Diesel v. Town of Lewisboro (2d Cir.2000) 232 F.3d 92, 103-104, Reyes v. City of Lynchburg (4th Cir. 2002) 300 F.3d 449, 455-457, Richardson v. City of South Euclid (6th Cir. 1990) 904 F.2d 1050, 1053-1055, cert. denied 498 U.S. 1032.) The court only examines procedural due process if it first finds a deprivation of a protected property interest. (American Mfrs. Mut. Ins. Co. v. Sullivan (1999) 520 U.S. 40, 42, citing Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319, 332.)

The constitutionally protected right involved in rent control cases is the right to receive a fair rate of return on one’s property. (Hillsboro at 391.) Therefore, “¿‘[f]air return is the constitutional measuring stick by which every rent control board decision is evaluated.’ (Carson Harbor Village, Ltd. v. City of Carson Mobilehome Park Rental Review Bd. (1999) 70 Cal.App.4th 281, 288, 82 Cal.Rptr.2d 569.)” (Ibid.)

In reply, Petitioner does not dispute that a constitutionally protected property interest must be at issue for due process protections to apply. (See Reply at 9-10.) Instead, Petitioner argues that its protected interest is “the statutory right of a newly constructed building to remain free from local rent control unless [Respondent] first established the recontrol conditions prescribed by section 151.28(A).” (Reply at 9.) Petitioner offers no legal authority to support the proposition that its interest in the Property not being subject to the RSO is such a constitutionally protected interest. (Ibid.)

Petitioner offers significant evidence of the impact of the RSO on its property value and expected return. (Cohanzad Decl., ¶¶ 22-28.) However, even after Respondent presents the fair return standard (Opp. at 13-14), Petitioner makes no attempt to demonstrate that the application of the RSO prevents it from receiving a fair return on the Property. (See Reply at 9-10.)

Based on the foregoing Petitioner has not met its burden to show deprivation of any constitutionally protected property interest. Accordingly, the Petitioner’s due process claim fails.

3.               Petitioner Has Not Shown Abuse of Discretion

Petitioner argues that Respondent’s determination was “arbitrary as a matter of law” for failure to make required findings and lack of evidentiary basis. Petitioner contends Government Code section 7060.2, subdivision (d), Evidence Code section 500, and Topanga Assn. for a Scenic Community v. County of Los Angeles, (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506 (“Topanga”), require Respondent to make particular factual findings to determine a property is subject to the RSO. (Pet. Op. Br. at 9-10.) Petitioner therefore concludes that, because the determination letter did not set out findings or the underlying basis for those findings, the determination was an abuse of discretion.

First, as discussed in section 1, supra, Petitioner neither alleges nor identifies any discretionary duty by Respondent.

Second, Topanga interpreted the requirements of CCP section 1094.5. (Topanga, supra, 11 Cal.3d. at 514.) CCP section 1094.5 applies to “inquiri[es] into the validity of any final administrative order or decision made as the result of a proceeding in which by law a hearing is required to be given, evidence is required to be taken, and discretion in the determination of facts is vested in the inferior tribunal, corporation, board, or officer.” (CCP § 1094.5.) As discussed above, Petitioner had no constitutional due process right to a hearing. Petitioner cites no other legal basis requiring a hearing. (See generally Pet. Op. Br.)

Relatedly, Government Code section 7060.2, subdivision (d), does not require Respondent to conduct a hearing or make specific, explicit factual findings. (Gov. Code § 7060.2(d).) While it does specify circumstances in which a public entity may provide that newly constructed units may be subjected to rent control (namely, the new units are offered for rent or lease within five years of withdrawal of previous accommodations subject to rent control), nothing in the provision discusses any procedural requirements. (Id.)

As for Evidence Code section 500, that statute merely provides the general standard that a party bears the burden of proof to establish the facts supporting their own claims and defenses. It has no bearing on whether Respondent abused its discretion.

Finally, LAMC section 151.28, subdivision (A), the basis for Respondent’s determination, is not discretionary, and so the abuse of discretion standard does not apply. (LAMC § 151.28(A).)

Accordingly, Petitioner has not met its burden to show Respondent abused its discretion.

VI.           Conclusion

Petitioner has not met its burden to show a violation of a ministerial duty, to show any discretionary duty, or to show constitutional entitlement to due process. The petition for traditional mandamus is DENIED.
