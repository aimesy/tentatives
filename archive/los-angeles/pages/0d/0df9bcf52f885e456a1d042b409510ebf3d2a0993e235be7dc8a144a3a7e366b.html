DEFENDANT AND CROSS-COMPLAINANT VIVIAN SUAREZ, AS THE REPRESENTATIVE OF THE ESTATE OF HENRY SUAREZ MOVES FOR MANDATORY DISMISSAL FOR FAILURE TO BRING CASE TO TRIAL WITHIN FIVE YEARS (C.C.P. §583.310-583.360)

AND RELATED CROSS-ACTIONS

           

Background

On December 11, 2019, Plaintiffs Robert Yu (“Yu”) and Minghui Huang (jointly, “Plaintiffs”) filed this action against a number of defendants, including Henry Suarez and Raymond Munro (“Munro”).

Plaintiffs filed the operative first amended complaint (“FAC”) on June 17, 2020, alleging causes of action for (1) breach of written contract for sale of real property, (2) violation of Civil Code sections 1102 et seq., (3) violation of Civil Code sections 2079 et seq., (4) negligence, (5) fraudulent concealment, (6) fraudulent misrepresentation, (7) negligent misrepresentation, (8) violation of Civil Code sections 896 et seq., (9) breach of third-party beneficiary contract, and (10) breach of fiduciary duties.

On September 18, 2020, Henry Suarez filed a cross-complaint against Munro and Vista Enterprise, Inc. (“Vista”), alleging causes of action for (1) negligence, (2) comparative indemnity, (3) contribution, and (4) declaratory relief.

On November 6, 2023, Munro filed a cross-complaint against a number of cross-defendants, alleging causes of action for (1) implied and equitable indemnity, (2) contribution, and (3) declaratory relief.

On June 17, 2024, a notice of death was filed for Henry Suarez, and on December 26, 2024, Plaintiffs named Doe 1 as Vivian Suarez, Personal Representative of the Estate of Henry J. Suarez (“Suarez”).

Suarez now moves to dismiss the action. Defendant/Cross-Complainant Munro and Cross-Defendant Vista filed a notice of joinder in the instant motion. Plaintiffs oppose. Suarez replied.

The Court notes that Plaintiffs filed an objection to Munro and Vista’s notice of joinder, stating that “Defendant/Cross-Complainant Raymond Munro and Cross-Defendant Vista Enterprises, Inc. filed their Joinder untimely.” (Plaintiffs’ Obj. to Joinder, ¶ 1.) Code of Civil Procedure section 1005, subdivision (b), requires that “all moving and supporting papers shall be served and filed at least 16 court days before the hearing.” Plaintiffs state that “[h]ere, all moving papers, including the subject Joinder had to be filed and served no later than April 24, 2026, but instead, the Joinder was filed and served, via electronic service on May 12, 2026, prejudicing Plaintiffs’ rights in connection with preparing its Opposition.” (Plaintiffs’ Obj. to Joinder, ¶ 1.) The Court agrees that the joinder was untimely. It was filed after Plaintiffs submitted their opposition, and it presents substantive arguments. Given this, the Court will not consider the joinder because the significant untimeliness prejudices Plaintiffs.

Previously, the Court held a hearing for the instant motion but continued the hearing stating that “[b]ecause the parties did not provide courtesy copies and because Plaintiff has recently learned that this Department had a temporary Judge during August 2023 through and part of January 2024, the Court is allowing Plaintiff to file and serve an amended opposition on or before June 3, 2026 with a courtesy copy delivered to Department 508 and Defendant may serve an amended reply on or before June 18, 2026.” (Minute Order 5/20/26.)

Plaintiffs filed an amended opposition on June 3, 2026 and Suarez filed an amended reply on June 18, 2026.

Amended Requests for Judicial Notice

Plaintiffs request judicial notice of Exhibits 1-20.

Suarez requests judicial notice of Exhibits 1-13.

The Court grants all the requests.

Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 583.310 provides, “[a]n action shall be brought to trial within five years after the action is commenced against the defendant.” Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 583.360, “(a) An action shall be dismissed by the court on its own motion or on motion of the defendant, after notice to the parties, if the action is not brought to trial within the time prescribed in this article. (b) The requirements of this article are mandatory and are not subject to extension, excuse, or exception except as expressly provided by statute.”

“In computing the time within which an action must be brought to trial pursuant to this article, there shall be excluded the time during which any of the following conditions existed: (a) The jurisdiction of the court to try the action was suspended. (b) Prosecution or trial of the action was stayed or enjoined. (c) Bringing the action to trial, for any other reason, was impossible, impracticable, or futile.” ((Id., § 583.340.) “[T]he Legislature has specifically stated that for purposes of that section impossibility, impracticability or futility must be due to causes beyond a party’s control.” (Westinghouse Electric Corp. v. Superior Court (1983) 143 Cal.App.3d 95, 105 (Westinghouse).) Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 583.330, “[t]he parties may extend the time within which an action must be brought to trial pursuant to this article by…written stipulation” and “[b]y oral agreement made in open court, if entered in the minutes of the court or a transcript is made.”

In addition, pursuant to Emergency Rule 10(a), “[n]otwithstanding any other law, including Code of Civil Procedure section 583.310, for all civil actions filed on or before April 6, 2020, the time in which to bring the action to trial is extended by six months for a total time of five years and six months.” (Cal. Rules of Court, Appen. 1, Emergency Rule 10(a).)

Discussion

As set forth above, the instant action was filed on December 11, 2019. Suarez explains that “[c]ertain events did toll the five-year deadline, such as the Covid-19 Emergency Order and Mr. Suarez’ death. Yet, even in accounting for these tolling periods, the statutory deadline for Plaintiffs to bring this matter to trial was April 8, 2026. That deadline has passed, and trial is not scheduled to begin for another six months.” (Mot., 3:7-11.)

Suarez asserts that “Plaintiffs commenced this action by filing their Complaint on December 11, 2019. (RJN, No. 1.) Thus, the original deadline to bring the matter to trial was December 11, 2024. (Code Civ. Proc., §583.310.) This deadline was extended on only two occasions.” (Mot., 5:7-9.) Suarez explains that under California’s Emergency Rule 10, the deadline was extended by six months, making the new deadline to bring the matter to trial June 11, 2025. Suarez next discusses Henry Suarez’s passing, and how between the date of his death on June 1, 2024, and the date “the Court signed the Order and substituted Mrs. Suarez as a defendant in her representative capacity of the Estate of Henry Suarez” on March 28, 2025, a total of 301 days passed. (Mot., 6:8-10.) Thus, Suarez contends that ”[a]s a result of these two events, the deadline to bring the matter to trial was extended for a total of 483 days. The original trial deadline was December 11, 2024. Adding 483 days makes April 8, 2026 the true deadline by which Plaintiffs must have brought the case to trial. (RJN, No. 3; Powell Decl., at ¶ 8.) There are no other circumstances which legally extended the statutory deadline. As of the filing of this Motion, the statutory deadline for trial has already passed. Trial is not calendared to begin until October 14, 2026, another 6 months away.” (Mot., 6:15-21.) Suarez explains that they never agreed to extend the five-year deadline and asserts that “[b]ecause Plaintiffs failed to bring this action to trial within the proscribed statutory time, this Court has no discretion and must dismiss the Complaint.” (Mot., 8:17-18.)

Plaintiffs explain a history of continuances, including that “[t]rial in this matter was originally scheduled for April 6, 2022, pursuant to the Case Management Conference held on February 3, 2021. The parties stipulated to continue trial on March 18, 2022, and again on September 13, 2022, resulting in the trial being continued to June 28, 2023.” (Opp., 3:8-10.) Plaintiffs’ counsel also explains that “[i]n 2023, Henry Suarez obtained trial continuances via ex parte applications filed on April 24, 2023 (granted April 25, 2023) and November 2, 2023 (granted November 14, 2023, alongside motions to compel that were later denied in their entirety on February 8, 2024). The trial date was continued approximately 10 months in response to this ex parte application, and the January 2024 trial date was moved to November 2024.” (Guzman Decl., ¶¶ 6-7.) Then, “[o]n or around August 18, 2023, Defendant/Cross-Defendant Raymond Munro (‘Munro’) filed a Notice of Motion and Motion for Leave to File Cross-Complaint.” (Guzman Decl., ¶ 8.) However, Code of Civil Procedure section 583.360 “does not contemplate that time consumed by the delay caused by ordinary incidents of proceedings like disposition of demurrer, amendment of pleadings and the normal time of waiting for a place on the court's calendar or securing a jury trial is to be excluded from a computation of the five-year period.” (O’Donnell, Pacific Emp. Ins. Co., Interveners v. City & County of San Francisco (1956) 147 Cal.App.2d 63, 65-66.) Accordingly, ex parte continuances are not sufficient to extend the deadline.

However, Plaintiffs also explain that “Movant concedes the emergency rule extended the trial deadline in this action to June 11, 2025.” (Supp. Opp., 7:8-9.) Next, “[t]he Court was on a temporary leave of absence from late August 2023 to early January 2024. (RFJN Ex. 20 at 6:6-9.) The temporary judge assigned to this department held no jury trials and only limited bench trials during that time.” (Supp. Opp., 3:23-25.) Thus, “[a]s a result, on August 10, 2023, the Court continued the trial date in this action on its own motion from September 27, 2023 to January 10, 2024 (105 days). Accordingly, the Court’s unavailability tolled the trial deadline to September 24, 2025.” (Supp. Opp., 7:22-24.)[1]

Counsel explains that delays also came from the death of Henry Suarez (see Guzman Decl., ¶¶ 10-17), which is presently accounted for. However, prior to Henry Suarez’s death, “[o]n January 19, 2024, Suarez filed Doe amendments to his cross-complaint, naming Herron, Robles R.C., and Bardez as Roes 1-4.” (Supp. Opp., 4:9-10.) And, “[d]espite naming Herron, Robles, R.C. and Bardez as cross-defendants, Suarez and Munro failed to timely serve them within 60 days of filing their cross-complaints, as required by Cal. Rule of Court 3.110(b).” (Supp. Opp., 4:10-12.)

Plaintiffs explain that the Court vacated the November 13, 2024 trial date, and “[o]n January 9, 2025, the Court once again continued the trial setting conference, noting that ‘no proof of service or responses from the cross-defendants Vista, Bardez, R.C. Drilling, Robles, and Herron have been filed on the Suarez cross-complaint and Bardez and Herron on the Munro cross-complaint ….’” (Supp. Opp., 4:24-26.) Plaintiff further explains that “[o]n April 11, 2025, Munro and Vista filed a Doe amendment, naming CHA Properties, Inc. (‘CHA’) as Doe 1.” (Supp. Opp., 5:20-21.) Further, “[o]n September 16, 2025, the Court ordered Munro to file and serve [a] request for default for the served Cross-Defendants and to file and serve a request for dismissal CHA by September 19, 2025.” (Supp. Opp., 6:3-4.)

At the June 18, 2025 case management conference, Plaintiffs’ opposition explains that “all parties’ counsel (including Movant’s counsel) appeared. The Court proposed a trial date of October 14, 2026. The parties’ counsel had no objections to the trial date, and all counsel indicated the trial date was acceptable. (Guzman Decl. ¶ 20, RFJN Ex. 19.) Vivian Suarez filed a case management statement prior to the hearing, indicating that discovery had not yet been completed and she intended to conduct further written discovery and depositions on dates to be determined. (6/6/25 Case Management Statement at p. 4, ¶ 16.) Suarez requested and obtained entry of defaults against Robles, R.C., and Bardez on June 23, 2025.” (Supp. Opp., 5:24-6:2.)

Plaintiffs contend that Westinghouse, supra, 143 Cal.App.3d at p. 106 is a parallel situation to here, thus necessitating denial of the motion to dismiss, “due to Suarez’s and Munro’s lengthy delays in joining the cross-defendants in this action, which resulted in multiple trial continuances.” (Opp., 7:27-28.) In Westinghouse, “the delay between the filing of the lawsuit in May 1977 and the final joining of all parties by the filing of cross-defendant Sprague’s answer to Procon/Signal’s cross-complaint in March 1982 was a delay of four years and ten months. Westinghouse itself stipulated as late as January 19, 1982, that Procon/Signal might file a cross-complaint against Westinghouse, Basler and Sprague. We do not consider such a delay to have been the responsibility of Travelers or an ‘ordinary incident’ of proceedings.” (Ibid.) The Court’s finding focused on factors outside of the Plaintiffs’ control in bringing the lawsuit to trial, finding that “impossibility, impracticability or futility must be due to causes beyond a party’s control.” (Id. at p. 105.) The Court of Appeal also explained that it “attach[ed] considerable significance to the trial court’s finding that there was no prejudice to any of the other parties involved in this litigation which resulted from the delay in bringing the matter to trial, notwithstanding the fact that prejudice is presumed. [Citation.] Because that presumption is not declared by law to be conclusive, it may be rebutted. [Citation.] The facts of the instant case sustain the trial court’s finding that the presumption of prejudice was rebutted. Travelers assured the regular progression of the litigation through the usual steps to the best of its ability. It cannot be responsible for the fact that the parties were engaged in ongoing discovery at the time the five years expired. Extending the time before trial to permit the other parties to file additional cross-complaints and to complete their discovery cannot be deemed to be prejudicial.” (Id. at p. 110.)

Plaintiffs contend that “[n]early all of the delay in bringing this matter to trial was caused by Defendants Saurez and Munoz. No delay is attributable to any ‘unreasonable conduct’ of Plaintiffs. The delay here is even longer than in Westinghouse: it took Saurez five years and six months between the filing of this lawsuit on December 11, 2019, and the ‘final joining’ of the cross-defendants to Saurez’s cross-complaint on June 23, 2025, when all named cross-defendants had either filed a responsive pleading, had defaults entered against them, or were dismissed. As in Westinghouse, Suarez’s substantial delay in joining the cross-defendants is not an ‘ordinary incident’ of proceedings and is not attributable to Plaintiffs. It is solely attributable to Suarez’s own lack of diligence in bringing this case to trial.” (Supp. Opp., 8:25-9:4.) Plaintiffs further assert that “[a]t a minimum, the statutory deadline should be tolled for 521 days from January 19, 2024, when Suarez filed his Doe amendments naming the additional cross-defendants, to June 23, 2025, when the “final joining” of the cross-defendants was completed. This would further extend the trial deadline to February 27, 2027.” (Supp. Opp., 9:2-8.)

In response, Suarez asserts that Westinghouse is inapposite, because “in that case, the Cross-Defendants appeared in the case and they, in turn, filed their own claims against other parties, which caused substantial delay in bringing the case to trial,” but “[o]ur case is much different because none of the named Doe Defendants ever appeared in this case. There was never any delay in pleadings, discovery or scheduling caused by the presence of any other party.” (Supp. Reply, 7:20-8:3.) Suarez further asserts that “Plaintiffs’ Opposition is devoid of any statement or analysis of why the addition of Doe Defendants hampered their ability to prepare for and bring this case to trial in a timely manner, other than to argue the case was not yet ‘at issue.’ … Filing an amended complaint or adding additional parties does not extend the statutory deadline to bring the matter to trial.” (Supp. Reply, 6:22-27.)

The Court agrees that filing an amended complaint or adding additional parties does not in and of itself extend the deadline to bring a matter to trial; however, the record here shows that the trial could not practicably be set in this matter until all the named cross-defendants either appeared, were dismissed or were defaulted. Cross-Complainants, not Plaintiffs, were the cause of the resulting trial delay as was the situation in the Westinghouse case. Severing Plaintiffs’ causes of action from those of the various cross-complainants in this construction dispute was not a practicable solution because it would have resulted in unnecessary duplication and judicial inefficiency. Consequently, pursuant to the Westinghouse case and the case cited by Plaintiff at the hearing of Pomona Federal Plaza v Investment Concepts, Inc.(1988) 203 Cal. App. 3d 217, 219, the Court finds that the time for bringing the case to trial was further extended.[2]

Plaintiffs assert that “[a]lternatively, even if the Court declines to exercise its discretion to toll the deadline due to Defendants’ delay in joining the cross-defendants, there is no dispute that the trial deadline was tolled following Henry Suarez’s death.” (Supp. Opp., 9:10-12.) “Here, defendant Henry Suarez died on June 1, 2024. As a direct result, the trial Court vacated the then-scheduled trial date, pending substitution of a per4sonal representative. Vivian Suarez was substituted as a defendant on April 2, 2025, which was 305 days after Henry Suarez’s passing. When combined with the tolling due to COVID-19 and the Court’s unavailability, the trial deadline was extended to July 26, 2026. Even with this more limited tolling, the Motion is premature and must be denied.” (Supp. Opp., 9:17-21.)

There is no question that the death of Henrey Suarez and the COVID-19 pandemic extended the five-year deadline. However, the parties contest whether Judge Beaudet’s temporary absence pauses the five-year deadline. Suarez contends that “this case is a Non-Jury Trial. Plaintiffs admit and concede in their Opposition that the Judge assigned to Department 50 in Judge Beaudet’s absence was conducting bench trials. Opp. at p. 2 and 7. This case has been set as a non-jury trial since 2021 when the Court issued its Minute Order designating the case for a non-jury trial, and filed its Case Management Conference Order which specifically states that none of the parties requested a Jury Trial. RJN, No. 9 and 10. All the parties know this. Again in 2022, the Court noted this case was a non-jury trial. RJN, No. 11. In August 2023, when Judge Beaudet continued trial, she noted she was continuing a non-jury trial. RJN., No. 12. … There is no reason why this case could not have been heard by the temporary judge if it had been set for trial during that time period or reassigned to another department if the 5-year deadline was approaching.” (Supp. Reply, 5:3-17.) However, the Court notes that on both June 2, 2023 and August 18, 2023, Munro demanded a jury trial and posted jury fees. The Court’s docket also shows that the trial is scheduled as a jury trial. Because the Court was not holding jury trials during Judge Beaudet’s absence from August 2023 to January 2024, it would have been impossible to bring the case to trial. Accordingly, the Court finds it reasonable to toll the five-year deadline during this time.

The Court notes that Plaintiffs also contend that the parties orally stipulated to a trial continuance at the June 18, 2025 Case Management Conference. Code of Civil Procedure section 583.330 states that “[t]he parties may extend the time within which an action must be brought to trial pursuant to this article… [b]y oral agreement made in open court, if entered in the minutes of the court or a transcript is made.” In the Court’s Minute Order from June 18, 2025, it states that “Jury Trial is scheduled for 10/14/26 at 01:30 PM in Department 50 at Stanley Mosk Courthouse.” (P-RJN, Ex. 19, at p. 2.) Plaintiffs contend that “at the June 18, 2025 case management conference, this Court proposed the trial date of October 14, 2026. The parties’ counsel (including Movant’s counsel) had no objections and all counsel indicated that date was acceptable;” thus, demonstrating the parties stipulated to a trial continuance. (Supp. Opp., 10:23-11:1.)

Plaintiffs find Nunn v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 346, 356 instructive because, there, “[t]he judge proposed a trial date beyond the statutory deadline to accommodate the Chase Defendants, who requested additional time to conduct discovery and bring a summary judgment motion. The parties expressed their agreement to extend the trial deadline to this date when counsel for both sides ‘indicated’ on the record that they had ‘no objection’ to a January 13, 2020 trial date.” However, in response, Suarez explains that the case differs from the present situation because “[i]n that case, Defendant’s counsel rejected an earlier proposed trial date and thereafter expressly stated he had no objection to a later trial date which was beyond the five-year deadline. Id. The court held that taken together, defense counsel’s conduct constituted an agreement to extend the deadline to bring the case to trial. (Id.) That circumstance is dramatically different than here, when counsel did not expressly accept any scheduling dates or waive the statutory deadline.” (Supp. Reply, 9:21-25; See Nunn v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A., supra, 64 Cal.App.5th at p. 356 [“The court suggested [trial for] November 2019, but [Defendants’ counsel] indicated November would be too early because defendants intended to file a summary judgment motion and needed to take the plaintiffs’ depositions before filing that motion. [¶] The court then proposed a trial for January 13, 2020. The lawyers both indicated to the court that they had no objection to the trial being set on January 13, 2020.”].) Suarez explains that “[m]erely acquiescing to a trial date is insufficient to avoid operation of the mandatory dismissal statute” (Supp. Reply, 9:26-27), because under Central Mutual Ins. v. Executive Motor Sales (1983) 143 Cal.App.3d 791, 796, the Court found that “[t]he presence of defendant’s counsel at the trial setting conference does not constitute a waiver of the requirements of section 583, subdivision (b). Mere attendance cannot be converted into an implied agreement to waive the statute.” The Court agrees that Plaintiffs did not present evidence demonstrating that Suarez waived the five-year dismissal statute by attending the June 18, 2025 case management conference. Further, from the Minute Order, there is no explanation or demonstration that the parties stipulated or agreed to the later date.

However, the Court does find that the five-year deadline has been tolled due to the death of Henrey Suarez and the time spent to appoint the representative of his estate (305 days), the COVID-19 extension (6 months), the Court’s unavailability for jury trials (119 days), and the delay due to the failure of Suarez to obtain an appearance by the cross-defendants on the Suarez cross-complaint or a dismissal or default until June 23, 2025 (82 days, i.e., from April 2, 2025 to June 23, 2025). The motion is premature because the five-year deadline is tolled until October 30, 2026. Accordingly, the Court denies the motion to dismiss under the five-year statute. The Court will discuss with the parties the advancement of the trial date to October 7, 2026 to be sure there is sufficient time to start the trial before the expiration of the five-year deadline. The Court will also discuss with the parties the dismissal or default of the remaining cross-defendants on the Munro cross-complaint.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Suarez’s motion to dismiss is DENIED.

Suarez is ordered to give notice of this Order.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE:

 

PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO APPROVE AND ENTER CONSENT JUDGMENT BETWEEN PLAINTIFF CONSUMER ADVOCACY GROUP, INC. AND DEFENDANT INTERDESIGN, INC.

           

Background

On April 3, 2023, Consumer Advocacy Group, Inc. (“CAG”) filed a Proposition 65 (“Prop 65”) action against Defendants InterDesign, Inc. (“InterDesign”), Bed Bath & Beyond Inc., and Bed Bath & Beyond of California LLC (collectively, “Defendants”), alleging one cause of action for violations of Prop 65. CAG alleges that Defendants are manufacturers, distributors, promoters, or retailers of Fridge Freezer Labels which contain Diethyl Hexyl Phthalate and Bis (2-ehtylhexyl) phthalate (“DEHP”). (Compl., ¶¶ 18, 26-28.) CAG alleges that “Defendants knowingly and intentionally exposed California consumers and users of Fridge Freezer Labels … without first providing any type of clear and reasonable warning of such to the exposed persons before the time of exposure.” (Compl., ¶ 30.)

CAG now moves for the Court to approve and enter consent judgment between CAG and InterDesign. InterDesign does not oppose.

 

Legal Standard

Proposition 65 prohibits “knowingly and intentionally expos[ing] any individual to a chemical known to the state to cause cancer” without “first giving clear and reasonable warning” to that individual. (Consumer Def. Grp. v. Rental¿Hous. Indus. Members¿(2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 1185, 1213; Health & Saf. Code, § 25249.6.)¿Prop 65 authorizes persons to bring private enforcement actions in the public interest against an alleged violator. (Health &¿Saf. Code, § 25249.7, subd. (d).) To comply, the individual must provide notice of the alleged violation to (1) the Attorney General, (2) the district attorney, city attorney, or prosecutor in the jurisdiction of the alleged violation, and (3) the alleged violator. (Ibid.) If neither the Attorney General nor the appropriate prosecutor commences an action against the violation, then the litigant may commence the action no less than 60 days after providing notice. (Ibid.)¿

If¿a private enforcement action settles, the plaintiff must submit the settlement to the court for approval upon noticed motion. (Health &¿Saf. Code,¿§ 25249.7, subd. (f)(4).)¿The¿court may approve the settlement only if it makes the following findings: (i) the warning that is required by the settlement complies with Prop 65; (ii) the award of attorney fees is reasonable under California law; and (iii) the penalty amount is reasonable. (Id.¿§ 25249.7, subd. (f)(4)(A)-(C).)

Discussion

            CAG requests that the Court approve the stipulated consent judgment between CAG and InterDesign. As a preliminary matter, CAG adequately complied with the 60-day notice of intent to sue, as required by Health and Safety Code section 25249.7, for both lawsuits. (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶¶ 7-10; Exs. B-C.)

A.    The Warning Labels Comply with Prop 65

A warning must be clear and reasonable to comply with Prop 65. (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 27, § 25607.2.)

The proposed consent judgment states that after the Court approves the instant settlement, InterDesign will “not sell in California, offer for sale in California, or ship for sale in California any Covered Products unless the level of DEHP does not exceed 0.1% (1,000 parts per million) by weight (‘reformulation level’).” (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. A, Consent Judgment, § 3.1.) As for products that exceed the reformulation level of DEHP that are manufactured for distribution or sale into California prior to the consent agreement’s effective date, InterDesign will “provide a Proposition 65 compliant warning for the Covered Products as permitted by Proposition 65 and its implementing regulations or as set forth below. Any warning provided pursuant to this section shall be affixed to the packaging of, or directly on, the Covered Products, and be prominently placed with such conspicuousness as compared with other words, statements, designs, or devices as to render it likely to be read and understood by an ordinary individual under customary conditions before purchase or use.” (Consent Judgment, § 3.2.) Additionally, should InterDesign sell or distribute any products in its “existing inventory through the internet[,] the warning will be posted in the manner provided for with respect to internet sales, as provided for in 27 CCR sections 25601 and 25602.” (Ibid.) The agreement also provides explicit language and a warning sign for what InterDesign must place on its products. (Consent Judgment, § 3.3.)

Thus, according to the terms outlined in the agreement, the Court finds that the warning labels are clear and reasonable, such that they adequately comply with Prop 65’s requirements.

B.     The Award of Attorney Fees is Reasonable

CAG and InterDesign’s consent agreement stipulates that InterDesign will pay $420,000.00 for CAG’s attorney fees. (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 25; Consent Judgment, § 4.1.3.) This amount represents a portion of the attorney fees and costs CAG incurred. (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 25; Ex. F.) CAG’s counsel attests that approximately $452,331.00 in Lodestar fees were incurred from the inception of the lawsuit. (Ibid.)

In support, CAG’s counsel provides a detailed declaration discussing the work it conducted to bring the lawsuit to fruition. (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 13.) Prior to the suit, CAG engaged in “extensive and time-consuming” pre-notice investigations and research phases with investigators, attorneys, and scientific consultants. (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 14.) Next, during the complaint stage, CAG continued to research and investigate, in addition to drafting agreements. (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 15.) For the discovery and litigation phase, CAG’s counsel details the actions they took, including, inter alia, filing and responding to motions, preparing discovery requests and responses, and preparing for trial. (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 16.) Finally, at the settlement phase, CAG’s counsel had to prepare the motion and declaration, communicate with the client and opposing parties, and more. (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 17.)

To calculate fees, the lodestar method is used. “[T]he court tabulates the attorney fee touchstone, or lodestar, by multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended by the reasonable hourly rate prevailing in the community for similar work,” plus any reasonable fees a party incurred in filing the motion. (Marshall v. Webster (2020) 54 Cal.App.5th 275, 285.) This is “an objective, nonarbitrary, and easy to administer calculation of attorney fees” because it fixes the fees “at the fair market value for the legal services provided.” (Reynolds v. Ford Motor Co. (2020) 47 Cal.App.5th 1105, 1117.) This “approach anchors the trial court’s analysis to an objective determination of the value of the attorney’s services, ensuring that the amount awarded is not arbitrary.” (Ibid.)

CAG’s counsel discusses the differences in fees for the various attorneys who worked on the case, based on each person’s education, experience, and involvement in the lawsuit. (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶¶ 19-22; 32.) CAG seeks hourly attorney fees at the rate of $990, $900, and $700, as well as support staff fees that range from $250 to $95. (Ibid.) CAG’s counsel points to similarly approved rates in Prop 65 litigation to bolster the contention that the rates are reasonable. (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 23.) Further, CAG’s counsel attaches the Adjusted Laffey Matrix to provide a fee schedule that some courts utilize to determine appropriate legal fees. (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 24; Ex. E.)

CAG’s counsel states that “[a]lthough the timesheets show that counsel spent over 830.9 hours on this case, this does not reflect all counsel’s hours. Moreover, much of my own time, which was spent supervising and directing my staff, was not recorded.” (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 28.) CAG’s counsel also attests, “[t]he lodestar amount does not include litigation costs such as investigation fees, expert consultant costs, laboratory testing fees, filing fees, service fees, or motion fees.” (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 30.)

The complexity, length of time, and results of this case leads the Court to believe that the stipulated amount is reasonable, as it also reflects a decrease from the lodestar. Accordingly, the Court finds the attorney fees reasonable.

C.     The Penalty Amount is Reasonable

To determine the reasonableness of a settlement amount, the court must consider:

 

(A) The nature and extent of the violation.

(B) The number of, and severity of, the violations.

(C) The economic effect of the penalty on the violator.

(D) Whether the violator took good faith measures to comply with this chapter and the time these measures were taken.

(E) The willfulness of the violator’s misconduct.

(F) The deterrent effect that the imposition of the penalty would have on both the violator and the regulated community as a whole.

(G) Any other factor that justice may require.

(Health & Saf. Code, § 25249.7, subd. (b)(2).) Additionally, a civil penalty cannot exceed $2,500 per day for each violation, in addition to any other legally established penalties. (Health & Saf. Code, § 25249.7, subd. (b)(1).)

The parties stipulated to a total payment amount of $520,000.00. (Consent Judgment, § 4.1.) InterDesign’s civil penalty totals $57,160.00. (Consent Judgment, § 4.1.1.) 75% of the total penalty, $42,870.00, will go to the State of California’s Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment. (Consent Judgment, § 4.1.1(a).) The other 25%, $14,290.00, will go to CAG. (Ibid.) InterDesign will also make a separate payment of $42,840.00 to CAG, “pursuant to Health & Safety Code § 25249.7(b) and California Code of Regulations, Title 11 § 3203(d),” for fees for investigation, purchasing, storage, and testing for Prop 65 listed chemicals in various products, for expert fees for evaluating exposures, and for administrative costs. (Consent Judgment, § 4.1.2.) Lastly, InterDesign will pay attorney fees to CAG in the amount of $420,000.00. (Consent Judgment, § 4.1.3.)

This case “required extensive and ongoing consultation with experts regarding the exposure analysis from exposure to Labels.” (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 10.) “Prior to settlement, CAG has been working on this matter for over four years on a contingency basis, including advancing a substantial amount of costs for investigating, testing and experts related to each claim. CAG maintains that DEHP exposures are severe violations due to the highly toxic nature of this chemical. Given the complexity of the exposure analysis and what is required for the enforcement of the law, a significant amount of work is necessary before each 60-day notice is issued.” (Ibid.) CAG’s counsel mentions that “[o]ne factor in determining civil penalties is the nature and extent of the violations. Documents provided by InterDesign in response to discovery stated that InterDesign sold approximately 3,000 units of the Covered Product in California in the relevant period.” (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 12.) Further, CAG’s counsel explains that InterDesign’s “willingness to reformulate the Covered Products to reduce the DEHP levels exhibits less willfulness for the alleged violations. Defendant’s prompt willingness to comply mitigates penalties.” (Yeroushalmi Decl., ¶ 11.) CAG asserts that InterDesign’s “total payment to settle this case, $520,000.00, plus their costs and attorney's fees, should be a sufficient deterrent against future violations. Further, the Consent Judgment, if approved, would become public record and could encourage others in the industry to comply with the law through word of mouth. It is reasonable that others in the industry will be put on notice of the consequences and costs associated with this Consent Judgment and would induce them to take action to comply with Proposition 65's safeguards.” (Mot., 14:10-15.)

The Court finds that in light of the number of violations, the length of time, and InterDesign’s willingness to comply, the proposed penalty is reasonable.

 

 

 

 

 

Conclusion 

            Based on the foregoing, CAG’s motion to approve and enter the consent judgment is GRANTED.

DEFENDANTS DOWNTOWN WOMEN’S CENTER AND DOWNTOWN WOMEN’S CENTER HOUSING LLC’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

           

Background

On November 17, 2023, Plaintiff Jane Doe filed a complaint against Defendants Downtown Women’s Center, Downtown Women’s Center Housing, LLC, Good Guard Security, Inc., City of Los Angeles, Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority, Housing Authority of the City of Los Angeles, County of Los Angeles, State of California, California Department of Social Services, and John Doe alleging five causes of action. On August 29, 2025, Plaintiff Shannon Lee Seymour (“Plaintiff”), formerly Jane Doe, filed the operative first amended complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants Downtown Women’s Center (“DWC”), Downtown Women’s Center Housing, LLC, Good Guard Security, Inc. (“Good Guard”), City of Los Angeles, Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority, State of California, California Department of Social Services, and John Doe, alleging five causes of action for (1) assault; (2) battery; (3) intentional infliction of emotional distress; (4) negligence; and (5) negligent hiring, supervision and retention.

Defendant DWC filed a cross-complaint on March 19, 2025 against Roes 1 through 50, alleging five causes of action for (1) total or comparative equitable indemnification, (2) contribution, (3) declaratory relief, (4) express indemnity, and (5) comparative fault.

Defendant City of Los Angeles filed a cross-complaint on April 28, 2025 against Roes 1 through 10, alleging three causes of action for (1) indemnification, (2) apportionment of fault, and (3) declaratory relief.

Defendant Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority filed a cross-complaint on October 6, 2025 against Roes 1 through 50, alleging three causes of action for (1) equitable indemnification, (2) apportionment of fault, and (3) declaratory relief.

On April 8, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s counsel’s motion to be relieved as counsel. Plaintiff does not appear to have obtained new counsel.

Defendants DWC and Downtown Women’s Center Housing, LLC (jointly, “Downtown”) now move for summary judgment on the two causes of action Plaintiff alleges against them. Plaintiff did not file an opposition.

Legal Standard

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” ((Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.) A triable issue of material fact exists if the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof. (Ibid.)

“When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467; Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).)

A motion for summary judgment or adjudication’s function is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. ((Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co., supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 843.) In analyzing such motions, courts must apply a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent’s claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.” ((Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) Summary judgment or adjudication is proper when “all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c); see also Villa v. McFerren (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 733, 741.)

The moving party bears the initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact. ((Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co., supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 850.) As to each claim as framed by the complaint, the defendant moving for summary judgment or adjudication must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting affirmative evidence to disprove at least one essential element of the plaintiff’s cause of action, show that an element cannot be established, or to establish an affirmative defense. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2); Sanchez v. Swinerton & Walberg Co. (1996) 47 Cal.App.4th 1461, 1465.)

Once the moving defendant meets its burden for a particular cause of action, the burden shifts to the nonmoving plaintiff. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) The plaintiff may defeat the motion by producing evidence showing that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action. (Ibid.)

On a motion for summary judgment or adjudication, the moving party’s supporting documents are strictly construed, while the nonmoving party’s documents are liberally construed. (D’Amico v. Board of Medical Examiners (1974) 11 Cal.3d 1, 24.) Any “doubts as to the propriety of granting the motion should be resolved in favor of the party opposing the motion.” (Ibid.)

 

Discussion

Downtown asserts that no triable issue of material fact exists and that they are entitled to summary judgment because they “have no affiliation with the subject premises where Plaintiff alleges that she was assaulted, nor with Good Guard.” (Mot., 1:12-13.)

A.    Allegations of the FAC

Plaintiff’s FAC alleges that she “was a patient and/or resident who was sexually assaulted by Defendant JOHN DOE on or about April 16, 2023.” (FAC, ¶ 1.) Plaintiff alleges that Supervising Defendants Downtown, the City of Los Angeles, Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority, the State of California, and California Department of Social Services “act[ed] as agencies, organizations, and/or entities responsible for the oversight, funding, referral, provision, coordination, and/or administration of homeless services, housing placements, and related programs for individuals such as Plaintiff.” (FAC, ¶ 9.) People Assisting the Homeless (“PATH”) “work[ed] in coordination with Supervising Defendants to provide shelter and housing placement services to homeless individuals.” (FAC, ¶ 10.) Plaintiff alleges that Good Guard employed John Doe “for the provision of security services at the facility,” and “[b]y virtue of their contracts, funding, oversight, and coordination responsibilities, the Supervising Defendants shared in and/or delegated duties relating to the hiring, training, supervision, retention, monitoring, and control of Defendant JOHN DOE while he was providing security services on their behalf.” (FAC, ¶ 11.)

Plaintiff alleges that PATH referred Plaintiff for housing placement, placing her “at the subject facility intended to provide safe housing and services for people such as Plaintiff experiencing homelessness.” (FAC, ¶ 12.) Plaintiff then alleges that “[o]n or about April 16, 2023, at approximately 4:30 a.m., Plaintiff was returning to her dormitory from the female restroom when Defendant JOHN DOE approached her near the dorm area. … Without consent, Defendant JOHN DOE grabbed Plaintiff’s wrists and forcefully pushed her right hand down onto his genitals while pressing his body against hers.” (FAC, ¶¶ 14-15.) “Plaintiff immediately reported the incident to a PATH counselor who acknowledged the incident and informed Plaintiff that they had contacted GOOD GUARD and advised Plaintiff that Defendant JOHN DOE would no longer be working at the facility.” (FAC, ¶ 16.)

B.     Negligence

Downtown contends that Plaintiff cannot establish that Downtown owed Plaintiff a duty, nor that Downtown caused Plaintiff’s injuries. “The elements of a negligence cause of action are duty, breach, causation and damages.” (County of Santa Clara v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 292, 318.)

a.      Duty

“To prevail in a negligence action, a plaintiff must show that the defendant owed a legal duty, the defendant breached that duty and the breach proximately caused injury to the plaintiff.” (J.L. v. Children’s Institute, Inc. (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 388, 396.) “A tort involves a violation of a legal duty, imposed by statute, contract or otherwise, owed by the defendant to the person injured. Without such a duty, any injury is an injury without wrong. [Citation.] A person is ordinarily not liable for the actions of another and is under no duty to protect another from harm, in the absence of a special relationship of custody or control.” (Roman Catholic Bishop v. Superior Court (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 1556, 1564.) “‘Duty, being a question of law, is particularly amenable to resolution by summary judgment.’” (Romero v. Superior Court (2001) 89 Cal.App.4th 1068, 1078.)

“‘A defendant cannot be held liable for the defective or dangerous condition of property which it [does] not own, possess, or control. Where the absence of ownership, possession, or control has been unequivocally established, summary judgment is proper.’” (Donnell v. California Western School of Law (1988) 200 Cal.App.3d 715, 720.)

The police report from Plaintiff’s alleged assault indicates that it occurred at 3210 Riverside Drive in Los Angeles, California. (Cary Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. C., RFP No. 7.) Downtown provides the declaration of Amy Turk, the CEO of DWC, which states that DWC does not own, operate, maintain, or control the property located at 3210 Riverside Drive in Los Angeles, nor is DWC affiliated with the location in any way. (Turk Decl., ¶ 5.) DWC is a housing and homeless response program with two housing sites that it owns and maintains, separate from the location where Plaintiff’s causes of action arise. (Turk Decl., ¶ 3.) At the time of the incident, DWC also operated a temporary shelter out of a hotel in downtown Los Angeles. (Ibid.)

DWC operates the Access Center, located at 442 S. San Pedro Street, Los Angeles, California 90013, a daytime drop-in center that is not open overnight. (Turk Decl., ¶ 6.) DWC’s records show that Plaintiff received services at the Access Center. (Ibid.) Unhoused women whom the DWC serves sometimes use DWC’s address as their mailing address when they do not have a permanent address, so this explains why the police report for Plaintiff’s incident provides the Access Center’s address. (Ibid; see Cary Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. C., RFP No. 7.)

The Los Angeles Homeless Authority maintains records for unhoused women that DWC can access. (Turk Decl., ¶¶ 2, 6.) The last recorded contact Plaintiff had with the Access Center case manager was on January 6, 2023. (Turk Decl., ¶ 6.).

Plaintiff was also enrolled in the DHS-Housing for Health Program, for which DWC is a contracted service provider. (Turk Decl., ¶ 7.) DHS is required to enter program notes into a database that DWC can access. (Ibid.) A note dated March 31, 2023, demonstrates that Plaintiff asked the DWC case manager why they could not provide interim housing referrals, and the case manager “explained that Housing for Health program does not provide interim housing referrals and that [case manager] can direct [plaintiff] to 211 or LAHSA outreach worker Jonathan Oskins.” (Ibid.) Accordingly, DWC’s records demonstrate that DWC did not refer to or place Plaintiff at the 3210 Riverside Drive location, nor did Plaintiff ever reside at any of DWC’s locations. (Turk Decl., ¶ 6.)

As for Downtown Women’s Center Housing, LLC, it is an entity for the real estate and financing aspects of the DWC and is not involved in DWC’s daily operations. (Turk Decl., ¶ 4.) Moreover, Downtown is not affiliated with Good Guard, as Good Guard did not provide services to any properties DWC operated at the time of the incident. (Turk Decl., ¶ 9.)

The unrefuted evidence presented by Downtown demonstrates that Downtown did not operate, nor is it connected to the premises where the incident occurred. Downtown similarly lacks a relationship with the Good Guard, the company that employed John Doe. Accordingly, Downtown establishes that it did not owe Plaintiff a legal duty. The burden now shifts to Plaintiff, but she did not file an opposition with facts that could raise a triable issue of material fact. Because Plaintiff has not demonstrated that the element of duty is plausible, Downtown’s motion for summary judgment as to the fourth cause of action for negligence is proper.

C.     Negligent Hiring, Supervision, and Retention

Downtown also moves for summary judgment on Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action for negligent hiring, supervision and retention.

“An employer may be liable to a third person for the employer’s negligence in hiring[, supervising,] or retaining an employee who is incompetent or unfit.” (Federico v. Superior Court (Jenry G.) (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 1207, 1213.) “Liability is based upon the facts that the employer knew or should have known that hiring the employee created a particular risk or hazard and that particular harm materializes.” (Doe v. Capital Cities (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 1038, 1054.)

Downtown asserts that “Plaintiff’s discovery responses consist only of allegations that Defendants employed John Doe, a security guard, and that they were negligent in their hiring, training, and supervising of their employees, that Defendants knew or should have known that John Doe had sexual or inappropriate tendencies but failed to supervise him. (Pltf’s resp to SRs no. 5, 6, 52, Exh. E). Plaintiff has no evidence to support these allegations. Again, these uninformative interrogatories consist only of allegations, and therefore Defendants meet their burden of showing that Plaintiff cannot establish an essential element of her cause of action.” (Mot., 8:2-8.) Plaintiff’s discovery responses state, inter alia, “Defendant JOHN DOE, a security guard and/or employee of Defendants DOWNTOWN WOMEN’S CENTER, a California corporation; DOWNTOWN WOMEN’S CENTER HOUSING, LLC … intentionally acted with the intent to cause harmful contact, approached Plaintiff and sexually assaulted Plaintiff JANE DOE with such force that Plaintiff suffered injuries and damages… Defendants DOWNTOWN WOMEN’S CENTER, a California corporation; DOWNTOWN WOMEN’S CENTER HOUSING, LLC … owed a duty of care to Plaintiff (and to all other residents and/or dependents to the SUBJECT PREMISES) to hire employees that could conform their conduct to the law and that would not inflict harm upon their security guard and/or employee.” (Casry Decl., ¶ 6; Ex. E, SROG No. 6, pp. 6-7.) These are merely legal arguments and cannot serve as evidence that Downtown is liable for supervising, retaining, or hiring John Doe. (See Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 573, 580-581 [“Defendant contends that the factually devoid answers to the Judicial Council form interrogatories were sufficient to require plaintiffs to prove their [motion for summary judgment]… We agree. Plaintiffs’ Judicial Council form interrogatory answers which contained no facts supporting the existence of misrepresentations or a fraudulent conspiracy shifted the burden of proof in connection with the summary judgment.”].)

Based on Downtown’s evidence that they lack any affiliation with John Doe or Good Guard, Downtown cannot be liable for the negligent hiring, supervision, or retention of John Doe. Plaintiff does not establish a triable issue of material fact. Without any evidence raising a question regarding Downtown’s affiliation with John Doe or Good Guard, summary judgment is also proper on Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action for negligent hiring, supervision, and retention.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Downtown’s motion for summary judgment is GRANTED in its entirety. Downtown is ordered to file a proposed judgment within ten days of this Order.

Downtown is ordered to give notice of this Order.
