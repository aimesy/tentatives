Tentative Ruling

Jonathan Baker, et al. v. Maree, Inc., et al., Case No. 18SMCV00115

Erica Gleaton’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

Hearing Date: July 17, 2026

Background

This is a dispute over a home improvement contract between Plaintiffs Jonathan Banker and Jenny Ljungberg Baker (collectively, “the Bakers”) and Defendants Maree, Inc., Maree Interiors, Inc.,  (collectively, “Maree”) and Erica Gleaton (“Gleaton”).

On October 26, 2018, the Bakers filed the initial complaint.

On January 30, 2019, the Bakers filed the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”).

On August 9, 2019, the Court overruled Defendant’s demurrer to the FAC.

On June 12, 2026, Gleaton filed this Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings. On July 6, 2026, the Bakers filed opposition. On July 8, 2026, Gleaton filed a reply.

Arguments

Gleaton argues that the FAC fails to state a claim against Gleaton because it does not allege that she is Maree’s alter ego and does not specifically describe what misrepresentations Gleaton allegedly made to the Bakers. (MJOP, p. 7.) Gleaton argues that Plaintiffs’ second cause of action is not brought against her, and that she is not liable because she is not an alter ego of Maree. (Id. at p. 8-9.) Gleaton argues that the FAC fails to state a claim for negligence because it does not allege facts showing that she owed the Bakers any duty as an individual. (Id. at p. 9.) Finally, Gleaton argues that leave to amend would be futile because no facts suggest that Gleaton acted outside of her corporate role at Maree. (Id. at p. 10.)

In opposition, the Bakers argue that Gleaton’s individual tort liability does not depend on alter ego allegations, but rather on individual liability for her own tortious conduct. (Opposition, p. 9.) The Bakers argue that they plead fraud against Gleaton with specificity. (Id. at p. 10-12.) The Bakers argue that they are not required to allege that Gleaton was a party to the oral contract with Maree in order to maintain separate tort claims against her. (Id. at p. 12-13.)  The Bakers argue that the FAC states a claim for negligence against Gleaton because it alleges Gleason’s personal participation in the alleged tortious conduct. (Id. at p. 14.) The Bakers ask for leave to amend if the Court grants Gleaton’s motion. (Id. at p. 15.)

In reply, Gleaton argues that the Bakers do not address her central argument, that the FAC does not state a claim against her as an individual. Gleaton concedes that a corporate officer may be held personally liable for torts in which she personally participates but argues that the complaint does not allege facts showing that Gleaton personally participated in the alleged fraud and negligence. Gleaton argues that Plaintiffs have not shown a reasonable basis for amendment. (Reply, p. 7.)

Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

The standard for ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings is essentially the same as that applicable to a general demurrer. The motion may be granted if it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law based on the pleadings and matters that may be judicially noticed (Bezirdjian v. O'Reilly (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 316, 321-322, citing Schabarum v. California Legislature (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1216.)  Matters which are subject to mandatory judicial notice may be treated as part of the complaint and may be considered without notice to the parties. Matters which are subject to permissive judicial notice must be specified in the notice of motion, the supporting points and authorities, or as the court otherwise permits. (Id.)  The motion may not be supported by extrinsic evidence. (Barker v. Hull (1987) 191 Cal.App.3d 221, 236.)   

 

When the moving party is a defendant, he must demonstrate either of the following exist: (1) the court has no jurisdiction on the subject of the cause of action alleged in the complaint; or (2) the complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant. (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (c)(a)(B)(i)-(ii).)   

 

Additionally, a motion for judgment on the pleadings must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration demonstrating an attempt to meet and confer in person or by telephone, at least five days before the date a motion for judgment on the pleadings is filed. (Code Civ. Proc., § 439.) A statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings must be filed thirty days before trial. (Ibid.) Common law motions for judgment on the pleadings are not bound by the deadline or the meet and confer requirement and may be made at any time. (Sofias v. Bank of America (1985) 172 Cal.3d 583, 585-586 (timing); Code Civ. Proc. § 439(a) (meet and confer requirement for statutory motions).) There is no fundamental difference between a common law and statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings. (Cordova v. 21st Century Ins. Co. (2005) 125 Cal.4th 89, 109.)

 

Analysis

Procedure

a.      Meet and Confer

Gleaton’s counsel met and conferred with the Bakers’ counsel regarding this motion on June 4, 2026. (Rodriguez Decl., ¶ 6.) The meet and confer requirement is satisfied.

b.      Prior Demurrer Ruling

A motion for judgment on the pleadings may be brought where the pleadings have previously been upheld on a general demurrer. (Ion Equipment Corp. v. Nelson (1980) 110 Cal.App.3d 868, 877 [“if the demurrer was overruled by a different judge, the trial judge is equally free to reexamine the sufficiency of the pleading.”].)

On August 9, 2019, Judge Ford overruled the Defendants’ demurrer, brought on these grounds and others, finding that the FAC stated a fraud and negligence claim against Plaintiff. Nonetheless, that demurrer was significantly broader than this one, and the Court will address Gleaton’s arguments.

Substantive Arguments

a.      Corporate Officer Liability for fraud

 

“The well-known elements of a cause of action for fraud or concealment are: (1) a misrepresentation, which includes a concealment or nondisclosure; (2) knowledge of the falsity of the misrepresentation, i.e., scienter; (3) intent to induce reliance on the misrepresentation; (4) justifiable reliance; and (5) resulting damages.” (Cadlo v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (2004) 125 Cal.App.4th 513, 519-520.)

Plaintiffs alleging fraud must describe, with particularity, the specific statements defendants made that constituted fraud, or facts defendants omitted that constitute alleged fraud. Pleadings must allege facts as to “‘how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.’” (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 73. Accord Wald v. Truspeed Motorcars, LLC (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 378, 393; Morgan v. AT & T Wireless Services, Inc. (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1235, 1262. Cf. also Goldrich v. Natural Y Surgical Specialties, Inc. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th772, 783 [“conclusory allegations offer no facts at all and it is impossible to determine what was said or by whom or in what manner.”].)

“To maintain a tort claim against a director in his or her personal capacity, a plaintiff must first show that the director specifically authorized, directed or participated in the allegedly tortious conduct [citation]; or that although they specifically knew or reasonably should have known that some hazardous condition or activity under their control could injure plaintiff, they negligently failed to take or order appropriate action to avoid the harm [citations]. The plaintiff must also allege and prove that an ordinary prudent person, knowing what the director knew at that time, would not have acted similarly under the circumstances.” (PMC, Inc. v. Kadisha (2000) 78 Cal.App.4th 1368, 1380, quoting Frances T. v. Village Green Owners Assn. (1986) 42 Cal.3d 490, 508-509, italics added.)

 

The Court agrees with Judge Ford’s finding that the FAC states a claim against Gleaton for fraud. The FAC alleges that Gleaton made specific misrepresentations at specific times, including that Gleaton would timely deliver cabinetry, closets, doors, appliances, beds, and other furnishings, install those items, and supervise that installation. (FAC, ¶ 18.) The FAC alleges that Gleaton took the Bakers’ money and did not intend to provide the appliances agreed upon. (Id. at ¶ 16.) To establish individual liability, the Bakers allege that they interacted substantially with Gleaton on behalf of Maree, and that she made misrepresentations on behalf of Maree, thereby alleging that she participated substantially in the fraudulent conduct. The Bakers also allege that Gleaton requested additional payments and never delivered the promised materials, and that she was Maree’s only employee and principal. (FAC, ¶¶ 7, 20.) These allegations show that Gleaton was aware of the potential harm to the Bakers. Finally, the FAC alleges that Gleaton took no action to mitigate the harm. (FAC, ¶ 22.) The FAC does not specifically allege that Gleaton did not act as an ordinary prudent person, but that conclusion is easily drawn from the Bakers’ factual allegations. For example, the Bakers allege that on January 23, 2018, after paying over $150,000 for flooring, they instructed Gleaton to order the requested flooring. (FAC, ¶ 12.) The Bakers allege that in April or May 2018, they called Bleaton’s vendor about the flooring, and found out that Gleaton had not ordered the flooring. (Ibid.) Gleaton then told the Bakers that the flooring was discontinued, when it actually just required a special order. (Ibid.) The Bakers allege that they have not received most of the flooring. (Ibid.) A reasonable person would not have delayed four months after receiving funds to make an order and then refused to make that order.

The FAC states a claim for fraud against Gleaton as an individual.

b.      Breach of Contract

Neither party contends that the Bakers bring a breach of contract claim against Gleaton in her individual capacity. To the extent that it is necessary, this ruling will confirm that the Bakers’ breach of contract claim is only against Maree.

c.       Negligence

A corporate officer’s individual liability for their company’s tortious conduct stems from the officer’s own conduct if they personally participated in or directed the tortious conduct. Then they may be liable for the corporation’s tortious acts. (PMC, Inc. v. Kadisha, supra, 78 Cal.App.4th at 1379.) “In the context of a negligence claim, the Supreme Court has held that, like any other person, “directors individually owe a duty of care, independent of the corporate entity's own duty, to refrain from acting in a manner that creates an unreasonable risk of personal injury to third parties.” (Id. at p. 1381, citing Frances T. v. Village Green Owners Assn., supra 42 Cal.3d at 505; see also Michaelis v. Benavides (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 681, 686 [finding that the principal of a construction company could be individually liable where he personally participated in and directed the defective construction].)

The Court again agrees with Judge Ford’s demurrer ruling. The Bakers’ negligence claim incorporates the specific facts set out in paragraphs 1-23, which describe Gleaton as Maree’s principle and allege that she directed the allegedly-negligent conduct that forms the basis for the Bakers’ negligence claim. For example, the Bakers allege that Defendants negligently failed to deliver and install items that the Bakers paid for. (FAC, ¶ 44.) The Bakers also allege that Gleaton represented that kitchen appliances would be delivered and installed in a timely fashion if the Bakers paid approximately $15,000. (FAC, ¶ 16.) The Bakers allege that they made the payment, but Defendants did not deliver the products. (Ibid.) The allegations in paragraph 16 allege that Gleaton directly participated in an incident that forms the basis for the Bakers’ negligence claims.  

Conclusion

The Bakers’ Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is DENIED, except to the extent that the Bakers sought breach of contract liability against Gleaton individually.


Case Number: 24SMCV03549    Hearing Date: July 17, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Halee Dion v. General Motors, LLC, Case No. 24SMCV03549

General Motors, LLC’s Motion to Tax Costs

Hearing Date: July 17, 2026

Background

This is a lemon law case. Plaintiff Halee Dion brought suit against Defendant General Motors, LLC alleging GM’s failure to repair or re-purchase Dion’s 2023 Cadillac CT5’s defects, including transmission and engine issues. (Complaint, ¶ 12.)

On July 24, 2024, Dion filed the complaint. On August 28, 2024, GM answered.

In October 2025, both parties filed pre-trial motions in limine.

On October 30, 2025, Dion filed a Notice of Settlement of Entire Case.

On February 17, 2026, Dion filed a Memorandum of Costs. On February 27, 2026, GM filed a motion to tax costs. On July 6, 2026, Dion filed opposition. On July 10, 2026, GM replied.

On March 10, 2026, Dion filed a motion for attorneys’ fees. The Motion for Attorneys’ fees is set for hearing on August 17, 2026.

Arguments

GM moves to tax Plaintiff’s service fees for trial subpoenas served on Cadillac dealership employees as not reasonably necessary because Plaintiff’s vehicle had never been serviced at that Cadillac dealership. (Motion to Tax Costs, p. 5.) GM also moves to tax Plaintiff’s administrative filing fees for notices of settlement, ruling, and stipulation and order to continue trial as expenses that did not advance Plaintiff’s case. (Motion to Tax Costs, p. 5.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that it is entitled to recover expenses in addition to costs under Civ. Code 1794(d). (Opposition, p. 2.) Plaintiff also argues that GM ignores the applicable standard, wherein the verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of their propriety. (Ibid.) Plaintiff argues that GM’s assertion that it filed a motion for summary judgment in this case is false and that Plaintiff produced many repair warranties in this case. (Id. at p. 3.) Plaintiff argues that dealerships are percipient third-party witness who must be deposed in cases such as this. (Ibid.) Finally, Plaintiff argues that its filing fees are recoverable costs under Code Civ. Proc. § 1033.5. (Id. at p. 4.)

In reply, GM argues that Plaintiff provided no concrete evidence that the contested costs were required, as the attached receipts do not show that the expense was reasonable or necessary. (Reply, p. 4.) GM argues that Plaintiff’s service fees were duplicative because all the subpoenas were served at the same location and at the same time. (Id. at p. 5.) It also argues that the administrative fees improperly include third-party service fees and processing fees. (Ibid.)

Motion to Tax Costs

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5(a) sets forth the items allowable as costs while Section 1033.5(b) details the items that are not allowable as costs.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1033.5, subd. (a), (b).)  “If the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary. On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.  Whether a cost item was reasonably necessary to the litigation presents a question of fact for the trial court and its decision is reviewed for abuse of discretion.”  (Acosta v. SI Corp. (2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 1370, 1380.)  Costs must be both “reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation,” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5(c)(1)), and reasonable in amount.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5(c)(2).) 

Generally, the burden on a motion to tax costs depends upon whether the costs appear proper. “If the items appear to be proper charges the verified memorandum is prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses and services therein listed were necessarily incurred by the defendant, and the burden of showing that an item is not properly chargeable or is unreasonable is upon the objecting party.”  (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131.)  “The court's first determination, therefore, is whether the statute expressly allows the particular item, and whether it appears proper on its face.  If so, the burden is on the objecting party to show them to be unnecessary or unreasonable.”  (Ibid.) 

Analysis

Both challenged costs are allowable under Code Civ. Proc. § 1033.4(a)(1) and 1033.5(a)(4). The burden therefore shifts to GM to show that the costs are unreasonable.

GM argues that the costs are unreasonable because Plaintiff’s car was never serviced at Cadillac of Beverly Hills and because all the subpoenas were served together, and should be billed as one cost. Plaintiff produces repair records showing that the vehicle at issue was serviced at Cadillac of Beverly Hills, where it served the subponeas on Cadillac employees. (See Wood Decl., Exhibit A.) Cadillac of Beverly Hills employees were percipient witnesses to Plaintiffs’ car issues, so the subpoenas would have been necessary had the case not settled. (Wood Decl., Exhibit B.) Additionally, where a process server charges a flat fee per subpoena served, as here, those costs are reasonable even when service is at one address because were unavoidable. The Court finds that Plaintiff’s service costs are reasonable.

GM does not make any argument as to why the three filing fees it identifies were unreasonable. The filing fees were necessarily incurred in filing necessary documents. It is not clear on what basis GM has identified the stipulation to continue trial, notice of settlement, and notice of ruling as unnecessary filings. GM argues that it should not have to pay processing fees associated with filing. The Court finds that GM has not shown that the processing fees were unreasonable.

Conclusion

Plaintiff’s Motion to Tax Costs is DENIED.

Barbara Burrell, et al. v. Essex Management, et al., Case No. 25SMCV03450

Plaintiff’s Motion for Sanctions

Hearing Date: July 17, 2026

Background

Plaintiffs Barbara Burrell and Sky’s Gourmet Marketplace sue Defendants Essex Management Corporation, Essex Marina City Club, Marina City Club Condominium Association, Property Management Professionals, the County of Los Angeles, and Does 1-350 for fourteen causes of action arising out of their lease to the premises at 4344 Promenade Way, Unit 202 and Suite 001, Marina Del Rey, California. Plaintiffs initially also made claims against the Essex Defendants’ attorneys, Kimball, Tirey, & St. John (“KTS”).

On August 29, 2025, Defendant KTS filed an Anti-SLAPP motion. On November 10, 2025, the Court granted KTS’s Anti-SLAPP on November 10, 2025.

Essex Management, Essex Marina City Club, and Essex MCC (“The Essex Defendants”) filed a demurrer on August 26, 2025. Marina City Club Condominium Association and Property Management Professionals filed a notice of Joinder to the Essex Demurrer on January 12, 2026. Essex replied on January 29, 2026. Plaintiffs filed opposition on January 23, 2026.  

The County of Los Angeles filed a demurrer on August 29, 2025. Plaintiffs filed opposition on January 23, 2026. The County replied on January 29, 2026.  

Plaintiffs filed a Motion for Leave to Amend Complaint on January 9, 2026. Essex filed opposition on January 16, 2026. KTS filed opposition on January 22, 2026. Plaintiffs replied on January 28.  

On February 2, 2026, the Court continued the hearing on the above motions from February 5, 2026 to February 11, 2026.  

On February 11, the Court heard argument on all three motions and took them under submission. On March 13 the Court ruled on the demurrer, sustaining Plaintiffs’ demurrer to the Los Angeles Rent Stabilization Ordinance without leave to amend.

On March 17, Plaintiffs filed this Motion for Reconsideration because the Court’s March 13, 2026, ruling relied on a superseded version of the Los Angeles County Rent Stabilization Ordinance. On May 13, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiffs’ Motion for Reconsideration.

On April 15, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Motion for Sanctions against the Essex Defendants, based on their failure to stipulate to correct the demurrer error. On July 2, 2026, the Essex Defendants filed opposition. On July 10, 2026, Plaintiffs replied.

Arguments

Plaintiffs move for sanctions against the Essex Defendants for presenting a superseded version of the Los Angeles County Rent Stabilization Ordinance in their demurrer to Plaintiff’s complaint, filed on August 26, 2025. (Motion for Sanctions, p. 2.) Plaintiffs argue that the Essex Defendants’ argument on that demurrer was not supported by existing law, and that a reasonable pre-filing inquiry would have shown that the ordinance was outdated. (Id. at pp. 4-5.) Plaintiffs argue that even if the Essex Defendants’ error was unintentional, they had an obligation to correct the error that they violated when they refused to stipulate to amend the Court’s demurrer ruling. (Id. at p. 6.) Plaintiffs request attorneys’ fees as a sanction.

In Opposition, the Essex Defendants argue that they did not file the demurrer for an improper purpose, so Plaintiffs cannot prevail on their motion for sanctions. (Opposition, p. 3.) The Essex Defendants argue that Plaintiffs mis-represent the argument their former counsel made on the demurrer and falsely accuse the Essex Defendants’ counsel of impropriety for failing to stipulate to an amendment, when the proposed amendment was unreasonable. (Id. at p. 4.)

In reply, Plaintiffs argue that there is no requirement that the offending document be brought with an improper purpose, and argue that the test is objective unreasonableness. (Reply, p. 3.) Plaintiffs argue that presenting the demurrer with superseded law was objectively unreasonable. (Id. at p. 4.) Plaintiffs argue that the Essex Defendants fail to provide evidence supporting their assertion that the error was mistake. (Id. at pp. 6-7.)

Governing Law

CCP section 128.7(b) provides that by signing and filing a pleading, an attorney “is certifying that to the best of the person’s knowledge, information, and belief, formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances, all of the following conditions are met: (1) [i]t is not being presented primarily for an improper purpose, such as to harass or to cause unnecessary delay or needless increase in the cost of litigation[;] (2) [t]he claims, defenses, and other legal contentions therein are warranted by existing law or by a nonfrivolous argument for the extension, modification, or reversal of existing law or the establishment of new law[;] (3) [t]he allegations and other factual contentions have evidentiary support or, if specifically so identified, are likely to have evidentiary support after a reasonable opportunity for further investigation or discovery[;] (4) [t]he denials of factual contentions are warranted on the evidence or, if specifically so identified, are reasonably based on a lack of information or belief.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7(b).) CCP section 128.7 authorizes the court to impose appropriate sanctions upon attorneys or parties that have violated subsection (b). (Id., § 128.7(c).) 

Code Civ. Proc. 128.7 provides a 21-day safe harbor provision whereby the offending party can avoid sanctions by withdrawing the improper pleading. (See Galleria Plus, Inc. v. Hanmi Bank (2009) 179 Cal.App.4th 535, 538.)  In pertinent part section 128.7, subdivision (c)(1) reads as follows: "A motion for sanctions under this section shall be made separately from other motions or requests and shall describe the specific conduct alleged to violate subdivision (b). Notice of motion shall be served as provided in Section 1010, but shall not be filed with or presented to the court unless, within 21 days after service of the motion, or any other period as the court may prescribe, the challenged paper, claim, defense, contention, allegation, or denial is not withdrawn or appropriately corrected. . . ." Section 1010 provides that a notice of a motion "must state when . . . the motion will be made." 

A formal notice of motion is required to begin the 21-day period. (Barnes v. Department of Corrections (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 126, 135-136.)  An informal notice that substantially complied with the requirements of 128.7 is insufficient. (Id.) In most cases counsel should be expected to give informal notice to the other party, whether in person or by a telephone call or letter before formal notice is given. Therefore, such informal notice could not serve as a substitute for a formal noticed motion under the rule. (Barnes v. Department of Corrections (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 126, 135.) When the document failed to specify when the motion would be made as required by CCP 1010, it is fatally defective. (Galleria Plus v. Hanmi Bank, 179 Cal.App.4th 535, 538.) When a party filed its motion for sanctions the same day it was served, it did not comply with section 128.7, subdivision (c)(1) and should be denied. (Id.) 

Discussion

Procedural Issues

Plaintiff attests that he noticed this motion on March 18, 2026, by serving a copy of the motion on all defendants by email. (Hakimfar Decl., ¶ 9, Exhibit 3.) Plaintiffs’ declaration does not include a copy of the motion he attached to his email. Since Defendants do not raise any defects in the safe harbor period in opposition to Plaintiffs’ motion, the Court will presume that Plaintiffs served an identical motion alongside the safe harbor letter.

Motion for Sanctions

Plaintiffs present no evidence that the Essex Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiffs’ claim under the Los Angeles County Rent Stabilization Ordinance was unreasonable. Instead, Plaintiffs seem to argue that the burden is on Defendants to show that the action was reasonable. (Reply, pp. 2, 6-7.) Not so. It is the moving party’s burden to show that the challenged conduct was unreasonable. (Peake v. Underwood (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 428, 441 [“A court has broad discretion to impose sanctions if the moving party satisfies the elements of the sanctions statute”]; Kumar v. Ramsey (2021) 71 Cal.App.5th 1110, 1120 [“to obtain sanctions, the moving party must show the party’s conduct in asserting the claim was objectively unreasonable”].) None of Plaintiffs’ evidence shows that the Essex Defendants’ actions were objectively unreasonable. Plaintiffs’ evidence primarily consists of Plaintiffs’ counsel’s emails to the Essex Defendants containing Plaintiffs’ demands for a stipulation correcting the error, and, importantly, “to confirmation [sic] that the County will remain as a defendant.” (Hakimfar Decl., Exhibit 2, p. 4.) The Court dismissed the County for reasons completely unrelated to the superseded LA RSTPO statute. (See corrected March 13, 2026 Minute Order.) Plaintiffs evidence not only provides no basis for a finding that the Essex Defendants’ conduct was objectively unreasonable, it also shows a reasonable basis for the Essex Defendants’ counsel to refuse to stipulate to correct the error in the demurrer ruling.

Considering the totality of the circumstances, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have not met their burden. Plaintiffs’ Motion for Sanctions is DENIED.
