ORDER OVERRULING DEMURRER TO SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT AND DENYING MOTION TO STRIKE

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Kristen Sutherland (“Kristen”) and Keith Sutherland (“Keith”) entered into a warranty contract with Defendant General Motors, LLC (“GM”) regarding a 2022 GMC Yukon XL (“subject vehicle”). The subject vehicle allegedly has defects and nonconformities. GM allegedly was aware of the subject vehicle’s defects and failed to disclose the existence of defects at all times to Plaintiffs. GM also failed to replace or reimburse Plaintiffs for the vehicle. Plaintiffs sue GM for five causes of action, including fraudulent concealment and violation of the Consumers Legal Remedies Act.

On March 10, 2026, GM filed the instant demurrer with motion to the strike to the SAC’s Fourth and Fifth Causes of Action for fraud on the grounds they are barred by the statute of limitations and fail to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against GM. Plaintiffs filed an opposition. GM filed a reply.

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.101 states: “The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) The person who filed the pleading does not have the legal capacity to sue; (c) There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) There is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) The pleading is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, “uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible; and (g) In an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct.”

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.  (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.”  (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.)  As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations.  (Ibid.)  The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.”  (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

           

III.      DISCUSSION

A.        Statute of Limitations

GM first demurrers to both the fourth and fifth cause of action for fraudulent concealment and violation of the Consumer Legal Remedies Act (“CLRA”) on the basis they are barred by the three-year statute of limitations. Both causes of action are based upon an intent to defraud by intentionally withholding relevant information regarding the subject vehicle’s defective engine, making both causes of action for relief on the grounds of fraud. (SAC ¶ 39.)

Pursuant to section 338, subdivision (d), any action for relief on the ground of fraud must be brought within three years of accrual of the cause of action. However, the discovery rule “postpones accrual of a cause of action until the plaintiff discovers, or has reason to discover, the cause of action [for fraud].” (Fox v. Ethicon Endo-Surgery, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 797, 807.)  Plaintiffs “are required to conduct a reasonable investigation after becoming aware of an injury, and are charged with knowledge of the information that would have been revealed by such an investigation.”  (Id. at p. 808.)  “The limitations period begins once the plaintiff has notice or information of circumstances to put a reasonable person on inquiry.” (Alexander v. Exxon Mobil (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1236, 1251, citation omitted).

If a plaintiff’s complaint shows, on its face, that the plaintiff’s claim would be barred by the statute of limitations without the benefit of the discovery rule, the complaint “must specifically plead facts to show (1) the time and manner of discovery, and (2) the inability to have made earlier discovery despite reasonable diligence.’” (Fox, supra 35 Cal.4th at p. 809, citation omitted.)

 Although the date of purchase was of the subject vehicle was December 30, 2021, Plaintiffs allege the first time Plaintiffs were, or should have been, made aware of the defective engine was July 7, 2025. (SAC ¶¶ 5, 43.) On July 7, 2025, Plaintiffs delivered the subject vehicle to GM’s authorized repair facility to perform “recall N252494001, which addresses a condition wherein ‘the connecting rod and/or crankshaft engine components in these vehicles may have manufacturing defects that can lead to engine damage and engine failure. If the engine fails during vehicle operation, the vehicle will lose propulsion, increasing the risk of a crash.’” (SAC ¶ 42.) Prior to July 7, 2025, Plaintiffs had not presented the vehicle for engine repair. (SAC ¶ 41.) At the appointment, GM’s authorized technician stated the vehicle was operating as intended and was safe. (Ibid.) Plaintiffs continued to the experience the same issues caused by the engine after said appointment. (SAC ¶ 44.) Plaintiffs have sufficiently pled the time and manner of discovery (or when discovery should have occurred) by outlining the first attempt at engine repair, and the persisting problem.

The SAC also alleges sufficient facts pleading Plaintiffs could not have been made aware of the fraud concerning the defective engine earlier. GM and GM’s agents allegedly failed to disclose the defective engine or otherwise provide information necessary for Plaintiffs to discover the defect. (SAC ¶¶ 37-40.) Plaintiffs did not bring the vehicle in for service concerning engine defects until July 7, 2025, pursuant to the safety recall issued by GM on April 24, 2025. (SAC ¶¶ 41-42, 65.) Plaintiffs have adequately pled inability to have reasonably discovered the defect until, at the earliest, April 24, 2025, when the recall was issued. Therefore, Plaintiffs have adequately pled that this action accrued, at the earliest, on April 24, 2025. Plaintiffs’ initial complaint, filed on September 5, 2025, falls within the statute of limitations for a fraud cause of action.

The Court overrules the demurrer as to both the fourth and fifth causes of action as to the issue of the statute of limitations.

B.        Required Specificity / Duty – Fraudulent Concealment

GM next demurrers to the fourth causes of action for fraudulent concealment on the basis it is not pled with required specificity and that there is no duty to disclose. The Court ruled on these issues when addressing GM’s demurrer to the first amended complaint (“FAC”), finding the FAC alleged sufficient facts to meet the required particularity and to establish a duty to disclose. (See February 10, 2026, minute order.) The SAC has not been amended in such a manner that raised a new potential argument regarding these issues; therefore, GM has no basis to demurrer on these grounds. (See § 430.41, subd. (b): “A party demurring to a pleading that has been amended after a demurrer to an earlier version of the pleading was sustained shall not demur to any portion of the amended complaint, cross-complaint, or answer on grounds that could have been raised by demurrer to the earlier version of the complaint, cross-complaint, or answer.”)

The Court overrules the demurrer as to these issues, as the Court has already addressed these issues and GM has no legal authority to present new argument. The demurrer as to the fourth amended complaint is OVERRULED.

C.        Notice – Violation of the CLRA

GM demurrers to the fifth cause of action for violation of CLRA on the basis it does not comply with the Civil Code section 1782 notice requirements. Demurrer on this issue has also already been considered and overruled by the Court. (See February 10, 2026, minute order.) The SAC has not been amended in such a manner that raised a new potential argument regarding notice for CLRA; therefore, GM has no basis to demurrer on these grounds. The Court overrules the demurrer on this basis.

D.        Required Specificity – Violation of the CLRA

Finally, GM demurrers to the fifth cause of action for failure to plead with requisite specificity, specifically on the basis Plaintiffs failed to identify what GM misrepresented. The SAC alleges GM violated Civil Code sections 1770, subdivision (a)(5), (a)(7), (a)(9) and (a)(14), by representing goods or services have characteristics they do not have, are of a particular standard, quality, or grade, or that were not as advertised. (SAC ¶¶ 153-166.). GM argues the SAC does not allege any acts supporting such a violation, as when, where, and how the representations were made.

The Court notes that Plaintiff is bringing a fraudulent concealment claim, not a fraud claim premised on an affirmative misrepresentation. The particularity requirement—facts showing “how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered”—is directed toward affirmative misrepresentations. (Alfaro v. Cmty. Hous. Improvement Sys. & Plan. Assn., Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384.) This requirement is more difficult to apply to pure nondisclosure: “How does one show ‘how’ and ‘by what means’ something didn’t happen, or ‘when’ it never happened, or ‘where’ it never happened?” (Ibid.) Instead, courts utilize an exception to the heightened pleading standard for fraud when “the defendant must necessarily possess full information concerning the facts of the controversy” or “when the facts lie more in the knowledge of the opposite party.” (Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 158.)

Here, the SAC adequately pleads GM had full or superior knowledge of the defective engine. The SAC alleges, among other facts, GM issued Technical Service Bulletins (“TSBs”) for several issues with the subject defective engine, pursuant to widespread customer issues and internal testing. (SAC ¶¶ 52-64.) The eventual recall of the subject engine was issued after GM conducted and closed three prior investigations into the condition between February 2022 and July 2024. (SAC ¶ 65.) The SAC also alleges GM was made aware of engine defects via consumer complaints as early as 2007. (SAC ¶ 73.) The relevant information was exclusively within the purview of GM, as it was largely obtained via GM’s private research, testing, investigation and information. (SAC ¶ 47.)

The Court finds the SAC has adequately pled that GM both withheld information concerning the defective engine and that GM had exclusive and/or superior knowledge of said information. Therefore, the Court overrules the demurrer as to this issue for the fifth cause of action.

The Court overrules the demurrer in full.

E.         Motion to Strike

GM requests the Court strike the request for punitive damages from the SAC. GM’s request is, in part, based upon the Court sustaining the demurrer. As the Court overruled the demurrer in full, the Court does not address this argument.

GM also argues the request for punitive damages should be stricken as the existence of fraud, the basis for the punitive damages, is not proven by clear and convincing evidence. Pursuant to Civil Code section 3294, subdivision a, punitive damages may only be imposed if proven by clear and convincing evidence. However, this is not a pleading standard; this standard controls imposition of punitive damages at trial. Therefore, this is irrelevant for purposes of a motion to strike.

Whether a party may plead a request for punitive damages is based upon the allegations of a complaint. Facts must be alleged in the pleading to support a claim for oppression, fraud, or malice. (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 161.) Here, Plaintiffs adequately pled facts alleging claims for fraud against GM, as addressed previously. Plaintiffs did not merely assert a cause of action for fraud but provided sufficient factual allegations to support said claim. Therefore, the Court denies the motion to strike.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

Defendant GM US LLC’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ Second Amended Complaint is OVERRULED.

Defendant GM US LLC’s motion to strike is DENIED.

Plaintiffs are ordered to give notice.

LUIS ANTONIO ORTIZ, 

Plaintiff, 

vs. 

ELEVANCE HEALTH, INC., et al. 

Defendants 

Case No.: 

26STCV07921 

Hearing Date: 

July 16, 2026 

 

 

ORDER GRANTING MOTION TO CONTINUE TRIAL 

 

I. BACKGROUND 

Plaintiff Luis Antonio Ortiz (“Ortiz”) was allegedly denied reasonable accommodations by employers Defendants Elevance Health, Inc. (“EH”), Anthem Blue Cross Life, and Health Insurance Company (“Anthem”), and Blue Cross of California (“BC”), during Ortiz’s triple bypass surgery recovery period, eventually terminating Ortiz’s employment for job abandonment. Plaintiff sues Defendants for twelve causes of action including disability discrimination and failure to determine reasonable accommodations. 

On June 12, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion to continue trial at least 150 days from October 19, 2026, and to continue all related dates to trail the new trial date. Plaintiff filed an opposition, and Defendants filed a reply.  

 

II. LEGAL STANDARD 

Under California Rules of Court rule 3.1332, subdivision (c), the Court may grant a continuance only on an affirmative showing of good cause requiring the continuance. Circumstances that may indicate good cause include “a party's excused inability to obtain essential testimony, documents, or other material evidence despite diligent efforts,” or the unavailability of a party, counsel, or expert due to death, illness or other excusable circumstance. The Court should consider all facts and circumstances relevant to the determination, such as proximity of the trial date, prior continuances, prejudice suffered, whether all parties have stipulated to a continuance, and whether the interests of justice are served. (Cal. Rule of Court rule 3.1332, subd. (d).) 

 

III. DISCUSSION 

Defendants request the Court continue trial at least 150 days from the current trial date to complete discovery and allow Defendants EH, The Elevance Health Companies, Inc. (“EHC”) and Susan Millet-Reilly (“Millet-Reilly”) to file motions for summary judgment. The Court may grant a continuance only pursuant to a showing of good cause, such as a party's excused inability to obtain essential testimony, documents, or other material evidence despite diligent efforts. (Cal. Rules of Court rule 3.1332, subd. (c)(6).) The Court must consider all factors, including proximity of the trial date, previous continuances, length of continuance requested, availability of alternative means to address the issue, prejudice to parties and witnesses, the Court’s calendar, and whether the interests of justice are best served by continuance. (Cal. Rules of Court rule 3.1332, subd. (d).) 

As Defendants allege EH and EHC were unable to file and serve motions for summary judgment before the required service date, due to Ortiz’s refusal to comply with discovery requirements, the Court reviews the motion within this context.1  

All motions for summary judgment must be heard at least 30 days prior to the trial date, unless a court orders otherwise. (Code of Civil Procedure § 437c, subd. (a)(3)2.) The moving papers must be served on all other parties to the action at least 81 days before the time appointed for the hearing. (§ 437c, subd. (a)(2).) Therefore, all motions for summary judgment must be served at least 111 days prior to the trial date, or by June 30, 2026.  

Following a denied attempt to compel arbitration, most Defendants filed an answer to the subject complaint on September 20, 2024. The remaining Defendant, Millet-Reilly, filed an answer on March 5, 2025. Pursuant to section 2025.210, subdivision (a), Defendants could have served notice of Ortiz’s deposition at any point after appearing in this action. Similarly, pursuant to section 2032.220, subdivision (b), Requests for mental examination (“IME”) are not guaranteed; parties must either stipulate or seek a court order, granted only upon a showing good cause. (§ 2032.320.) IME requests may be made any time after a defendant appears. Therefore, Defendants could have sought the relevant discovery as early as September 20, 2024. 

A. Ortiz’s IME.  

Defendants first requested Ortiz’s IME on August 19, 2025, 11 months after first appearing in this action. Defendants sent a draft stipulation on September 3, 2025, of an IME agreement, to which Ortiz’s counsel replied on September 17, 2025. Parties met and conferred regarding limitations for the following two months but were unable to agree. Finally, on November 18, 2025, EH filed a motion to compel Ortiz’s IME. The motion was initially heard on February 11, 2026. The Court continued the hearing on the motion as the meet and confer requirement was not met: 

“However, the Court finds the meet and confer requirement not met. Section 2016.040 provides that a meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion. As previously stated, an exchange of emails or letters does not meet the requirement.” (February 11, 2026, Minute Order.) 

Following the meet and confer process, parties resolved all but five issues. The Court ruled on those issues on April 16, 2026, and granted the motion. The IME was conducted on May 11, 2026. As Ortiz’s IME was completed approximately 45 days prior to the motion for summary judgment service date, the Court does not find it is relevant to assessing the need for a continuance.  

B.  Ortiz’s Deposition 

On August 15, 2025, Defendants first requested Ortiz’s availability for September deposition dates. Defendants later followed up, on August 27, 2025, stating “finding September dates at this point is going to be tough.” (Opp., Carr Decl. ¶ 15, Ex. 12.) In the first half of September, Ortiz provided multiple October dates; (Id. at ¶ 15, Ex. 13.) Parties finally agreed to an October 17, 2025, deposition date.  

The deposition commenced at 10:13 a.m. At 4:36 p.m., Ortiz requested the deposition be postponed due to pain. Parties briefly met and conferred on the record to discuss future dates but did not set a date at that time. Defendants’ counsel stated, “we will meet and confer on a new date, and we will do something as soon as we can.” (Id. at ¶ 19, Ex. 15.)  

Defendants filed a motion to compel further testimony on December 3, 2025. On the court’s ruling on the motion, the Court found Defendants again failed to properly meet and confer prior to filing this motion. (February 23, 2026, Minute Order.) However, the Court ruled on the merits and granted the motion as to all issues, except one, ordering Ortiz to appear for further deposition on numerous topics. (Ibid.)  

The second session of Ortiz’s deposition occurred on March 17, 2026. However, parties did not complete the deposition on that date. Defendants did not request additional deposition dates until May 29, 2026—over two months after completion of the March deposition. On June 2, 2026, Ortiz’s counsel offered five June dates—three of which were on weekdays. Defendants rejected all proposed dates; there is no indication Defendants have requested or provided alternative dates.  

C. Analysis 

The Court first notes it will not consider discovery sought by Ortiz as a reason to continue the trial date because Ortiz is opposing this motion in full. Therefore, the Court assumes Ortiz is willing to forfeit Ortiz’s right to pursue this discovery in order to avoid a trial continuance.  

The Court may only continue trial upon a showing of good cause, such as an inability to obtain essential discovery despite diligent efforts. Here, the Court does not find good cause, as Defendants did not diligently pursue discovery. Defendants waited 11 months, until August 2025, to pursue any sort of discovery—at the time, this was only 8 months prior to the then trial date of April 6, 2026. Defendants presented limited availability for Ortiz’s deposition and IME due to Defendants’ own impacted calendar. Although the Court granted Defendants’ motions to compel, for both motions, the Court noted Defendants failed to meet the meet and confer requirements, indicating a lack of diligence in pursuing discovery.  

Even after the Court granted an order requiring Ortiz to appear for future depositions, Defendants failed to secure Ortiz’s appearance. Parties understood, following Ortiz's March deposition, at least one other Ortiz deposition was necessary. Despite this, Defendants waited two months to request dates for Ortiz’s deposition—only one month prior to the motion for summary judgment service due date. Four days later, Ortiz’s counsel provided June 12, 17, and 24 as available dates. Defendants did not reply until June 12, stating June 12th was unreasonable as “ten days' notice did not provide sufficient time” to prepare for a deposition, and a lack of availability for the other dates. (Opp., Carr Decl. ¶ 27, Ex. 21.) Defendants also did not provide any specific availability, instead simply asking to Ortiz’s availability.  

Defendants’ failure to request Ortiz’s availability for two months, to agree to a deposition with 10 days-notice, to timely reply to the Ortiz’s provided availability, and to seek dates prior to the motion for summary judgment service date all are indicative of Defendants’ failure to diligently seek discovery in order to submit a motion for summary judgment. There is no indication discovery will not be completed prior to trial, as parties are allegedly attempting to set Ortiz’s deposition for July.  

Therefore, the Court does not find good cause to continue trial simply to allow Defendants the ability to file a timely motion for summary judgment. Defendants failed to diligently pursue discovery, even after obtaining a court order despite insufficient meet and confer attempts, in order to file said motion.  

D.  Hearing on Motion for Summary Judgment 

The Court will grant a short continuance to allow the timely served motions for summary judgment to be heard at least 30 days prior to the current trial date. One motion is set for September 30, 2026—less than 30 days prior to the current October 19, 2026, date—due to the Court’s own impacted calendar. Therefore, the Court grants a brief continuance to allow this motion to be heard at least 30 days prior to trial. 

 

IV. CONCLUSION 

Defendants’ motion to continue trial is therefore GRANTED, in part. Trial is continued to the court’s first available date at least 30 days after September 30, 2026.  The final status conference is continued to a date about 10 days before the new trial date. 

All dates, except for the motion for summary judgment due date, are continued to trail the new trial date.  

Defendants are ordered to give notice.
