I.               BACKGROUND

This case is a personal injury action arising from two separate incidents: a slip and fall at an apartment complex on July 31, 2022, and a subsequent motor vehicle accident in which Plaintiff was a Lyft passenger on April 7, 2023.

On June 26, 2023, Plaintiff Sophia Olga Maslakova filed her complaint against Defendants SPI Collins Court, LLC (“SPI”); Warner Villa Co. (“Warner”); FPI Management, Inc. (“FPI”); Christian Benson (“Benson”); Hertz Vehicles LLC (“Hertz”); Lyft, Inc. (“Lyft”); and Wilma Z. Goldman (“Goldman”), alleging: (1) general negligence against SPI, Warner, and FPI; (2) premises liability against SPI, Warner, and FPI; (3) motor vehicle negligence against Benson, Hertz, Lyft, and Goldman; and (4) general negligence against Benson, Hertz, Lyft, and Goldman.

On December 1, 2023, Plaintiff filed a form amendment, naming 5807 Topanga LLC (“Topanga”) as DOE #1 in her complaint.

On December 26, 2023, Cross-Complainant Benson filed his cross-complaint against Cross-Defendant Goldman, seeking/alleging: (1) total equitable indemnity; and (2) comparative indemnity and contribution.

On June 3, 2024, Cross-Complainant Hertz filed its cross-complaint against Cross-Defendants FPI, Goldman, and Topanga, seeking/alleging: (1) indemnification; (2) apportionment of fault; and (3) declaratory relief.

On February 5, 2025, Plaintiff dismissed Hertz as a defendant from the complaint.

On August 8, 2025, the Court granted Plaintiff’s attorneys’ motion to be relieved as counsel.  As of that date, Plaintiff has been proceeding as a self-represented litigant.[1]

On November 24, 2025, Benson filed his form amendments naming FPI as ROE #1 and Topanga as ROE #2 in his cross-complaint.

On January 12, 2026, the Court granted Benson leave to file a first amended cross-complaint, after which Benson filed his first amended cross-complaint (the “FACC”) against Goldman, FPI, and Topanga, seeking/alleging: (1) total equitable indemnity; and (2) comparative indemnity and contribution.

On January 13, 2026, the Court conducted a mandatory settlement conference off the record.  The case did not settle.

On January 30, 2026, Goldman filed her answer to Benson’s FACC.

On March 9, 2026, the Court denied FPI and Topanga’s motion to enforce a settlement previously entered with Plaintiff.

On April 23, 2026, the Court held a Final Status Conference and granted Lyft’s motion to bifurcate the trial in this matter.

On April 27, 2026, a jury trial commenced.

On May 6, 2026, the jury returned a verdict, and the Court entered judgment against FPI and Topanga in favor of Plaintiff in the total amount of $96,250.00.  The Court also entered judgment against Plaintiff in favor of Benson, Goldman, and Lyft.  The judgment notes that Defendants may seek their recovery of costs from Plaintiff.

II.            LEGAL STANDARD

A prevailing party to any action or proceeding is entitled to recover its costs for suit as a matter of right.¿ Code Civ. Proc., § 1032 (b).¿ By submitting a verified memorandum of costs, the prevailing party has provided prima facie evidence of the proper costs of suit.¿ Adams v. Ford Motor Co. (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 1475, 1486-1487 (Adams); Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5 (c)(2).

III.          DISCUSSION

Plaintiff moves to tax costs claimed in Goldman’s costs memorandum.  The motion is denied.

a.     Plaintiff’s arguments that Goldman’s 998 offer(s) was/were defective due to invalid service or insufficiently specific terms are without merit.

Here, Plaintiff disputes that Goldman is entitled to recover her costs because Goldman’s 998 offer was “transmitted solely by electronic mail despite Plaintiff’s prior communications regarding notice concerns.”  (Motion, p. 2:5-6.)  Plaintiff complains that Goldman’s electronic communication did not plainly identify itself as a 998 offer in the subject line.  Plaintiff says she does not recall receiving the 998 offer and was not aware of its existence until trial had already begun.

Plaintiff complains that the “998 offer did not attach formal release documentation or define important practical settlement mechanics, including but not limited to: 1. release scope, 2. payment timing, 3. payment protections, 4. additional released entities, 5. future documentation requirements, 6. and other material settlement obligations.”  (Motion, p. 3:12-18.)

Goldman explains that she served two (2) different 998 offers on Plaintiff, the first of which was served on Plaintiff’s then-counsel of record on April 25, 2024.  (Paz decl., ¶ 3.)  Plaintiff’s motion fails to address Goldman’s first 998 offer, which offered to settle with Plaintiff for a total of $7,500.00.  Because the jury returned a verdict in favor of Goldman and against Plaintiff, Plaintiff received a worse result at trial.  Goldman is thus allowed to recover her costs after service of the first 998 offer on Plaintiff’s then-counsel.

As to electronic service of Goldman’s second 998 offer, Goldman presents evidence that Plaintiff previously provided an email address for service when she became self-represented, thus consenting to electronic service.  While Plaintiff’s email consenting to electronic service states that she requests to be served “BOTH by email and mail” (Opposition, Exh. 5), Plaintiff cites no authority allowing for a party to demand two methods of service.  Goldman’s electronic service of the second 998 offer on Plaintiff was thus valid.

The same logic applies to Plaintiff’s complaint that she never received mailed service of Goldman’s costs memorandum.  Because Plaintiff consented to electronic service, Goldman’s electronic service of the costs memorandum was proper.  In short, service of both Goldman’s first and second 998 offers and service of Goldman’s costs memorandum were valid.

As to Plaintiff’s complaints regarding the substance of the 998 offer, a 998 offer for a dismissal with prejudice is valid without needing to include a draft settlement with specific terms.  See Hartline v. Kaiser Foundation Hospitals (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 458, 470 (“an offer that provides for the plaintiff's dismissal of the action with prejudice is a valid form of offer within section 998”).  Here, Goldman’s first 998 offer to Plaintiff required that Plaintiff dismiss her case against Goldman with prejudice in exchange for $7,500.00.  Goldman was not required to, as Plaintiff suggests, attach a formal release or define “settlement mechanics” for the 998 offer to be valid.

In sum, Plaintiff’s arguments that Goldman’s 998 offers were defective due to invalid service or insufficiently specific terms are without merit.

b.     Goldman sufficiently itemizes and substantiates its expenses, and those expenses were reasonably necessary to the conduct of litigation.

Goldman seeks the following $33,449.97 in litigation costs: $435.00 for filing and motion fees; $640.91 for jury fees; $2,189.10 for deposition costs; $10,894.49 for witness fees; $12,351.50 for court reporter fees; $849.84 for models, enlargements, and photocopies of exhibits; and $6,089.13 for “other” fees, identified as trial tech expenses.

Plaintiff objects to Goldman’s costs memorandum on grounds that: Goldman has not substantiated her expenses and/or the expenses are vague/unsupported, Goldman’s expert witness fees are excessive, Goldman’s costs were not reasonably necessary to the conduct of litigation, Goldman’s costs are duplicative and/or are not related to Goldman herself, and Goldman’s costs were incurred outside the period covered by the 998 offer.

As to Plaintiff’s objection that Goldman’s costs memorandum “failed to adequately itemize and substantiate claimed expenses” (Motion, p. 4:14), the Court disagrees.  Goldman’s costs memorandum sufficiently itemizes each of the expenses claimed.  Goldman’s opposition brief includes invoices and receipts to prove that said expenses were actually paid.  For purposes of ruling on Plaintiff’s motion to tax costs, this is sufficient for Goldman to establish what her expenses were.  See Adams, supra, 199 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1486-1487 (by submitting verified memorandum of costs, prevailing party has provided prima facie evidence of proper costs of suit).

As to reasonableness of Goldman’s claimed expert costs, recoverable expert costs under Code of Civil Procedure section 998 (c)(1) include costs “actually incurred and reasonably necessary in either, or both, preparation for trial or arbitration, or during trial or arbitration, of the case by the defendant.”  Here, Goldman claims $10,894.49 for services provided by her retained expert, Swathi Kode, Ph.D.  Goldman says Dr. Kode assisted her in evaluating Plaintiff’s claims, preparing for trial, and putting on a defense at trial.

Dr. Kode spent time reviewing records, inspecting vehicles, performing accident reconstruction and biomechanical research and analysis, and sharing her findings with Goldman’s counsel.  Dr. Kode also testified at trial.  Goldman’s counsel attests that $10,894.49 was actually incurred for Dr. Kode’s services after service of Goldman’s first 998 offer.  Goldman’s counsel attests that that amount has actually been paid out by Goldman.  (Paz decl., ¶ 5, Exh. 4.)  The Court agrees with Goldman that Dr. Kode’s fees are reasonable.  Because Dr. Kode’s expert fees were actually incurred, the Court will not strike them from Goldman’s costs memorandum.

As to court reporter costs, the court reporter at trial in this matter was a certified shorthand reporter hired by the parties and appointed as an official court reporter pro tempore.  (Paz decl., ¶ 9, Exh. 8.)  The fees for a certified shorthand reporter appointed as an official court reporter pro tempore are recoverable court reporter costs.  See Gov. Code, § 68086 (d)(2) (“fees and charges of the certified shorthand reporter shall be recoverable as taxable costs by the prevailing party”).  Goldman seeks recovery of the fees she actually paid the court reporter for her services at trial.  (Paz decl., ¶ 9, Exh. 9.)  Because court reporter fees are recoverable, they will not be taxed or stricken.

As to exhibit costs, costs for “[m]odels, the enlargements of exhibits and photocopies of exhibits, and the electronic presentation of exhibits, including costs of rental equipment and electronic formatting, may be allowed if they were reasonably helpful to aid the trier of fact.”  Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5 (a)(13).  Here, Goldman claims $849.84 that she paid for her share for the costs of photocopying exhibits and putting together the exhibit binders used at trial.  (Paz decl., ¶ 8, Exh. 7.)  Such exhibits were reasonably necessary for Goldman to put on a complete defense to Plaintiff’s claims at trial.  Given that the jury returned a defense verdict for Goldman, Goldman is allowed to recover her fees to produce said exhibits.

Plaintiff disputes Goldman’s claim for $6,089.13 in “other” fees that were paid to Magna Trialgraphix/Cogent Edge to assist with the preparation and presentation of evidence at trial.  (Paz decl., ¶ 10, Exh. 10.)  Costs not expressly allowed under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5 (a) may still be awarded in the Court’s discretion if they were actually incurred, reasonable in amount, and reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation.  See Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5 (c)(4).  Such trial court discretion extends to costs for “a technician to monitor [] equipment and quickly resolve any glitches” because such a service “has become commonplace, if not expected by jurors.”  Green v. County of Riverside (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 1363, 1374.

The Court agrees with Goldman that a trial technology technician was reasonably necessary to the conduct of litigation in this case given the complexity of the trial due to the multiple incidents involved.  The fee charged by the trial technology technician service provider appears to have been reasonable.  As such, the Court exercises its discretion to allow Goldman to recover fees paid to Magna Trialgraphix/Cogent Edge for trial technology services in this case. However, the Court further exercises its discretion as this category of costs and reduces the recoverable costs to about one-half of the requested cost.

Finally, Plaintiff complains that Goldman fails to explain how costs shared among the several defendants “were calculated, who paid the original invoices, whether the allocations were reasonable, or whether the same expenses are being claimed by other defendants.”  (Motion, p. 9:20-22.)  The Court disagrees with Plaintiff that Goldman failed in this regard.  Goldman’s attorney’s declaration specifies where certain costs were shared among the several defendants in this case (see Paz decl., ¶¶ 8-10), and Goldman’s costs memorandum identifies what the total amount of that portion paid by Goldman was.  This is sufficient.  See Adams, supra, 199 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1486-1487.  Goldman does not need to specify who paid the original invoice to establish that Goldman is entitled to her share of the costs paid.

IV.          CONCLUSION

Plaintiff’s motion to tax costs from Defendant Wilma Z. Goldman’s costs memorandum is DENIED in part, with the Court exercising its discretion to tax $3.045 in requested costs.

 

Defendant Wilma Z. Goldman is ORDERED to give notice.

I.               BACKGROUND

This case arises from a motor vehicle accident that occurred on August 30, 2024.

On May 7, 2025, Plaintiff Razi Shapira filed his complaint against Defendants A to Z Used Auto Parts Corp. (“ATZ”), Steven Matthew Hanzel (“Hanzel”), and Sherry Mahshid Hosten (“Hosten”).

On July 11, 2025, Plaintiff filed proofs of service against all three (3) Defendants.  As to ATZ, Plaintiff’s proof of service documents substituted service via Hanzel, ATZ’s agent for service of process, by leaving papers with “John Doe,” identified as a “person in charge,” at ATZ’s business address located at 42137 4th Street East in Lancaster at 9:38 A.M. on May 22, 2025.  As to Hanzel and Hosten as individuals, Plaintiff’s proofs of service document substituted service by leaving papers with “John Doe,” identified as a “person in charge,” at the same address at 9:40 A.M. on May 27, 2025.

On July 16, 2025, the Clerk entered all three (3) Defendants’ defaults.

On May 1, 2026, Judge Valerie Salkin held a prove-up hearing where Plaintiff presented evidence (including live testimony) to support his claimed damages.  The Court thereupon entered default judgment in favor of Plaintiff and against Defendants in the total amount of $4,478,167.50.  (See Minute Order dated May 1, 2026.)

On May 26, 2026, Defendants filed their motion to set aside and vacate the defaults and default judgment entered in this case.

On June 2, 2026, Judge Anthony Mohr granted Defendants’ ex parte application to advance the hearing date on their motion.

On June 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed his opposition brief.

On July 2, 2026, Defendants filed their reply brief.

II.            LEGAL STANDARD

“When service of a summons has not resulted in actual notice to a party in time to defend the action and a default or default judgment has been entered against him or her in the action, he or she may serve and file a notice of motion to set aside the default or default judgment and for leave to defend the action.  The notice of motion shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of: (i) two years after entry of a default judgment against him or her; or (ii) 180 days after service on him or her of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.”  Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5 (a).

“A notice of motion to set aside a default or default judgment and for leave to defend the action shall designate as the time for making the motion a date prescribed by subdivision (b) of Section 1005, and it shall be accompanied by an affidavit showing under oath that the party’s lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by his or her avoidance of service or inexcusable neglect.  The party shall serve and file with the notice a copy of the answer, motion, or other pleading proposed to be filed in the action.”  Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5 (b). 

“The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.”  Code Civ. Proc., § 473 (b).

III.          DISCUSSION

Defendants move to set aside and vacate the defaults and default judgment entered in this case.  The motion is granted.

Defendants present declaration testimony stating that although Plaintiff’s proofs of service document substituted service of the summons and complaint by leaving papers with “John Doe,” Defendants never received actual notice of this action.  Defendants emphasize that Plaintiff should have been able to provide Defendants with actual notice of this action given that Defendants submitted an insurance claim regarding the subject incident.  On April 24, 2025, Defendants’ insurer informed Defendants that they were denying liability based on their determination that Plaintiff was entirely at fault.  (See Hanzel decl., ¶ 3.)

Plaintiff’s counsel (Attorney John Pollitt) had previously contacted Defendants’ insurance company on September 3, 2024, to submit a claim for damage in connection with the accident.  On December 12, 2024, Defendants’ insurance company informed Mr. Pollitt that as of January 1, 2025, the claim would be transitioned to Gallagher Bassett, a third-party administrator for Defendants’ insurance company.  Mr. Pollitt was subsequently provided with evidence to support Defendants’ version of liability.  On April 16, 2025, Gallagher Bassett emailed Mr. Pollitt, informing him that it was denying Plaintiff’s claim.  (Amy Huston decl., ¶¶ 3-7.)

Despite the foregoing pattern of communication between Defendants’ insurer and Mr. Pollitt regarding the subject car accident, Mr. Pollitt did not further communicate with Gallagher Bassett regarding litigation until May 8, 2026, when Mr. Pollitt communicated to Gallagher Bassett that Plaintiff had obtained a default judgment in the amount of $4,478,167.50.  (Amy Huston decl., ¶ 8.)

Given that Plaintiff’s counsel was in contact with Defendants’ insurance company as early as September 3, 2024, months before Plaintiff filed this case on May 7, 2025, the Court finds it highly suspect that Plaintiff sufficiently exercised due diligence when attempting to serve Defendants before affecting substituted service on “John Doe” on May 22, 2025, and on May 27, 2025.  This combined with Defendants’ declarant’s testimony that Defendants did not receive actual notice of this action in time to respond to the complaint before the Clerk’s entry of their defaults is sufficient to warrant an order vacating the defaults and default judgment.  See Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5.

Plaintiff argues the Court should find otherwise because Hanzel concedes that he received notice of Plaintiff’s request for entry of default by paper mail on July 18, 2025.  (See Hanzel decl., ¶ 4.)  Plaintiff also cites other documents that Plaintiff says he mailed to Defendants at ATZ’s business address during the pendency of this case.  (See Pollitt decl., ¶¶ 38, 47, 51.)

Hanzel explains that after he received the notice of request for entry of default, he personally traveled to the Van Nuys Courthouse where he was advised by a clerk to consult his attorney or insurance carrier.  Hanzel thereafter forwarded the notice to his insurance carrier, which advised him that they were already in communication with Plaintiff’s counsel and the matter was being handled.  (See Hanzel decl., ¶¶ 4-5.)

Hanzel’s conduct in this case cannot be fairly characterized as avoidance of notice or service.  When Hanzel received notice of this action, he promptly tried to address the situation by consulting the Court and by consulting his insurance carrier.  That the insurance carrier advised Hanzel that the matter was being handled reasonably caused Hanzel to fail to pursue the matter further.  In short, Hanzel’s receipt of a mailed notice is insufficient to show Defendants failed to adequately respond to this action, particularly where Defendants’ insurance carrier previously contacted Plaintiff’s counsel regarding the same claims at issue in this case and Plaintiff’s counsel failed to follow up with the insurer directly.

The motion is granted.  Defendants should file their proposed answer to the complaint as a separate document in the eCourt system within five (5) days.

IV.          CONCLUSION

Defendants’ motion for relief from defaults and default judgment is GRANTED.  See Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5.

 

Defendants are ORDERED to give notice.
