I.            INTRODUCTION

On April 7, 2026, defendant City of Pasadena (“Defendant”) filed a motion for protective order with a request for $4,562.50 in sanctions against Corey W. Glave, counsel for plaintiff Jessica Austin (“Plaintiff”). Defendant argues that a protective order is needed to bar Plaintiff’s counsel from making speaking objections and coaching deposition witnesses.

On April 8, 2026, Defendant filed a motion to compel Plaintiff’s appearance and deposition testimony. Defendant also requests sanctions in the amount of $6,410. Defendant argues an order is necessary because Plaintiff’s counsel unilaterally suspended the deposition without good cause.

Plaintiff filed an opposition brief to the motion for a protective order on April 27, 2026.

Defendant filed a reply brief in support of its motion for a protective order on April 28, 2026.

On May 5, 2026, the Court scheduled the two motions to be heard on the same day.

Plaintiff filed an opposition brief to Defendant’s motion to compel on July 14, 2026.

Defendant filed a reply brief in support of its motion to compel on July 29, 2026.

II.          DISCUSSION

On February 10, 2026, the Court ordered Plaintiff to appear for a second session of her deposition to answer 71 questions that she previously refused to answer after asserting objections based on Pitchess v. Superior Court (1972) 11 Cal.3d 531 (“Pitchess”). The Court also ordered Plaintiff to appear and answer “any other questions about other relevant, non-privileged matter” not previously asked. Plaintiff appeared for her deposition as ordered on March 6, 2026, but Plaintiff’s counsel suspended the deposition after less than four hours on the record.

Defendant requests the Court order Plaintiff to appear for her continued deposition and issue a protective order prohibiting Plaintiff’s counsel from interposing speaking objections. Defendant identifies seven instances of Mr. Glave’s speaking objections and claims that he coached Plaintiff to provide certain testimony.

First, Defendant argues that Mr. Glave improperly injected factual matters into his speaking objections so that Plaintiff could use them in formulating her responses. The Court disagrees. Although Mr. Glave referred to certain facts that Plaintiff previously testified about, there’s no evidence that it affected her testimony. She responded that she would have to refresh her recollection with documents (p. 143:7-8) and that she did not recall (p. 143:13, 148:11.)

Second, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s counsel improperly instructed Plaintiff to incorporate or adopt her previous testimony by expressly limiting the scope of her allowed response or directing his client to consider whether to respond with “all of the above.” The Court agrees that Plaintiff’s counsel improperly coached his client when he repeatedly interjected that Plaintiff “gets credited with the testimony given this morning”, which would then prompt Plaintiff to respond to questions by stating, “Outside of what I’ve stated earlier, not that I can recall.” (See e.g., 144:25-145:11.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Mr. Glave’s objections were appropriate because the questions required Plaintiff to repeat prior testimony and apply facts to law in order to answer questions about legal contentions in violation of Rifkind v. Superior Court (1994) 22 Cal.App.4th 1255, 1262.) Plaintiff further argues that no additional deposition should be compelled because the deposition was suspended with defense counsel’s agreement and Defendant did not adequately meet and confer before filing these motions.

The record does not reflect Plaintiff’s narrative. Mr. Glave’s objections were lengthy and went beyond simply stating the ground for the objection. And even if the questions were objectionable based on Rifkind, depositions “shall proceed” subject to objections unless the objecting party suspends the deposition to pursue a motion for protective order. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.4560, subd. (b).) Plaintiff did not seek a protective order and even if one were requested, Plaintiff fails to make any evidentiary showing that one is necessary. Defense counsel never restricted Plaintiff from relying on former testimony and would not know if Plaintiff would in fact rely on former testimony unless Defendant asked the questions Plaintiff’s counsel found objectionable.

Moreover, contrary to Plaintiff’s insistence, defense counsel did not agree to suspend the deposition but instead stated that he would be “happy” to have the Court address the parties’ dispute. Additionally, defense counsel made sufficient attempts to resolve the issue by sending a meet and confer letter by email on March 20, 2026. Plaintiff’s counsel responded to this letter on March 30, 2026, arguing that Defendant “refused to credit [Plaintiff] for her prior factual testimony” and only agreed to produce Plaintiff for another deposition session to answer questions about her damages without justifying this unilaterally-imposed limitation, making these two motions necessary.

 

III.        CONCLUSION

In light of the foregoing, Defendant’s motion to compel another deposition of Plaintiff is GRANTED and Defendant’s motion for a protective order is GRANTED.

Defendant’s request for sanctions is GRANTED in part and imposed against Plaintiff’s counsel in the reduced amount of $3,535 consisting of $1,285 for the cost of the deposition held on March 6, 2026 and $2,250 for 6 hours at defense counsel’s hourly rate of $375.

Plaintiff is ordered to appear for her deposition within 20 days of the date of this Order to answer Question Nos. 41 through 43 and 45-71 as ordered on February 10, 2026, as well as questions about any other nonprivileged matters that have not been previously asked.

On November 20, 2025, defendant Ralphs Grocery Company dba Ralphs (“Defendant”) served plaintiff Mark Aguirre (“Plaintiff”) with Special Interrogatories, Set One. After several extensions were granted, Plaintiff’s responses were due February 12, 2026. Plaintiff served responses on February 15, 2026, which Defendant contends were insufficient. Defendant filed this motion on March 20, 2026, to compel Plaintiff to serve further responses. Defendant also requests sanctions be imposed against Plaintiff and the Law Offices of Theida Salazar, jointly and severally, in the amount of $1,132.

On a motion to compel further responses, the objecting party has the burden of justifying its objections and showing why a further response is not needed. (Coy v. Superior Court (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210; Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541.) Plaintiff did not oppose this motion and failed to meet his burden to show why further responses should not be ordered. Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED and Plaintiff is ORDERED to serve further responses to Special Interrogatories (Set One) Nos. 2, 8, 13, 19, and 22 within 20 days of the date of this Order.

Sanctions are imposed against Plaintiff and counsel of record, jointly and severally, in the reduced amount of $782, consisting of 2 hours at an hourly rate of $350 and $82 in filing costs, payable within 20 days of the date of this Order

Moving party to give notice.

On September 30, 2025, plaintiff Gang Yang (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against defendants Anne Chui, Rogers Chui, A&R Trust, and A1 Meat, Inc. (collectively, “Defendants”). Now, Plaintiff moves for twelve orders compelling Defendants’ responses to written discovery and deeming admitted the truth of the matters specified in requests for admission. The underlying written discovery was served on April 16, 2026, but no responses have been received.

Compel Responses

Where a party fails to serve timely responses to discovery requests, the court may make an order compelling responses. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, 2031.300.) A party that fails to serve timely responses waives any objections to the request, including ones based on privilege or the protection of attorney work product. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, subd. (a), 2031.300, subd. (a).)

Defendants did not oppose Plaintiff’s motions and it is undisputed responses were not served. Plaintiff’s motions are GRANTED as follows:

-      Anne Chui is ordered to serve verified responses to Form Interrogatories (Set One), Special Interrogatories (Set One), and Request for Production of Documents (Set One), without objections, within 20 days of the date of this order.

-      Rogers Chui is ordered to serve verified responses to Form Interrogatories (Set One), Special Interrogatories (Set One), and Request for Production of Documents (Set One), without objections, within 20 days of the date of this order.

-      A&R Trust is ordered to serve verified responses to Request for Production of Documents (Set One) and Form Interrogatories (Set One), without objections, within 20 days of the date of this order.

-      A1 Meat, Inc. is ordered to serve verified responses to Requests for Production of Documents, Set One and Form Interrogatories (Set One), without objections, within 20 days of the date of this order.

Deem Admitted

Where a party fails to timely respond to a request for admission, the propounding party may move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the requests be deemed admitted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (b).) The party who failed to respond waives any objections to the demand, unless the court grants them relief from the waiver, upon a showing that the party (1) has subsequently served a substantially compliant response, and (2) that the party’s failure to respond was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subds. (a)(1)-(2).)

The court shall grant a motion to deem admitted requests for admissions, “unless it finds that the party to whom the requests for admission have been directed has served, before the hearing on the motion, a proposed response to the requests for admission that is in substantial compliance with Section 2033.220.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).)

Anne Chui and Rogers Chui did not oppose Plaintiff’s motions and it does not appear that substantially-compliant responses have been served before the hearing. Accordingly, the motions to deem admitted are GRANTED:

Monetary Sanctions

The Code of Civil Procedure provides that the court shall impose a monetary sanction against the party who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel, unless the party acted with substantial justification or the sanction would otherwise be unjust. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (c), 2031.300, subd. (c).) Where a party fails to provide a timely response to requests for admission, “[i]t is mandatory that the court impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) on the party or attorney, or both, whose failure to serve a timely response to requests for admission necessitated this motion.” (Code of Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).)

Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions is GRANTED. Sanctions are imposed as follows:

(1)  On Anne Chui and counsel of record, jointly and severally, in the reduced amount of $1,948.80, consisting of $1,660 in attorneys’ fees (4 hours at Plaintiff’s counsel’s hourly rate of $415) and $288.80 in filing fees, payable within 20 days of the date of this Order.

(2)  Rogers Chui and counsel of record, jointly and severally, in the reduced amount of $1,948.80, consisting of $1,660 in attorneys’ fees (4 hours at Plaintiff’s counsel’s hourly rate of $415) and $288.80 in filing fees, payable within 20 days of the date of this Order.

(3)  A&R Trust and counsel of record, jointly and severally, in the reduced amount of $974.4, consisting of $830 in attorneys’ fees (2 hours at Plaintiff’s counsel’s hourly rate of $415) and $144.40 in filing fees, payable within 20 days of the date of this Order.

(4)  A1 Meat, Inc. and counsel of record, jointly and severally, in the reduced amount of $974.4, consisting of $830 in attorneys’ fees (2 hours at Plaintiff’s counsel’s hourly rate of $415) and $144.40 in filing fees, payable within 20 days of the date of this Order.

Plaintiff Gang Yang
(“Plaintiff”) seeks writs of attachment against Defendants Anne Chui, Rogers
Chui, A&R Trust and A1 Meat Inc. (“A1 Meat”) (collectively, “Defendants”)
in the amount of $500,000. On August 3, 2026, Defendants filed notices
of opposition and claims of exemption, as well as a memorandum of point and
authorities and joint declaration of Anne Chui and Rogers Chui. No reply brief
has been filed.



Plaintiff alleges claims
for breach of contract arising from a written settlement agreement entered into
on February 22, 2024 (“2024 Agreement”). The 2024 Agreement required Defendants
to provide “application materials and supplementary materials for the
application of removing conditions” on Plaintiff’s conditional green card
following Plaintiff’s $500,000 investment in A1 Meat as part of a visa
application. Plaintiff alleges he cannot remove the conditions on his green
card due to Defendants’ actions and, as a result, he has suffered damages “in
the sum of $500,000 plus consequential damages according to proof.” (Compl., ¶
33.)



 “Upon the filing of the complaint or at any
time thereafter, the plaintiff may apply pursuant to this article for a right
to attach order and a writ of attachment by filing an application for the order
and writ with the court in which the action is brought.” (CCP § 484.010.) The
application shall be executed under oath and must include:



-     
a statement showing that the attachment is sought to secure the
recovery on a claim upon which an attachment may be issued;



-     
a statement of the amount to be secured by the attachment;



-     
a statement that the attachment is not sought for a purpose other
than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based;



-     
a statement that the applicant has no information or belief that
the claim is discharged or that the prosecution of the action is stayed in a
proceeding under the Bankruptcy Act (11 U.S.C. section 101 et seq.); and



-     
a description of the property to be attached under the writ of
attachment and a statement that the plaintiff is informed and believes that
such property is subject to attachment.



(CCP § 484.020.)



Plaintiff argues that his
breach of contract claim entitles him to a writ of attachment as his damages of
$500,000 are fixed and readily ascertainable from Defendants’ refusal to
cooperate with his EB-5 visa application.



Defendants oppose
Plaintiff’s applications on the grounds that the claimed damages of $500,000 is
incorrect and that damages are uncertain due to the parties’ agreement in 2024,
which allocated responsibility for payroll taxes, labor insurance premiums, and
management fees to Plaintiff, depending on the requirements of Plaintiff’s visa
application. However, the 2024 Agreement accounts for different scenarios in
which Plaintiff would be responsible for certain expenses depending on when his
visa application was approved; since Plaintiff alleges that his green card has
not been approved, those scenarios are irrelevant and damages are not
uncertain.



Next, Defendants argue that
Plaintiff cannot prove a breach of contract. Defendants claim they complied
with their obligations under the 2024 Agreement because Plaintiff has not been
in communication with them and that it is unknown why the conditions on his
green card were not removed. Defendants point to another party to the 2024
Agreement, Junhui Li, who, like Plaintiff, invested in A1 Meat and was
successfully able to remove the conditions on his green card. Defendants emphasize
that Li and Plaintiff used the same immigration attorney. (Opp., p. 2.) Defendants
assert that Plaintiff likely abandoned his petition for a visa because of
issues proving that his funds were legitimate and that their conduct is not to
blame for the Plaintiff’s unsuccessful visa application. (Opp., pp. 2-3; Chui
Joint Decl., ¶ 16.)



Plaintiff did not file a
reply brief and address Defendants’ arguments regarding his ability to prove
that any purported breach of contract caused his damages. Accordingly, the
applications for writ of attachment are DENIED.



Plaintiff to give notice.
