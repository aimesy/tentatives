            [TENTATIVE] ORDER: The Court DENIES Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Compliance. The Court also DENIES Plaintiff’s request for statutory sanctions. Finally, the Court schedules an OSC Re: Sanctions against Plaintiff and their attorney of record for September 14, 2026. 

Background

This is a lemon law case. Plaintiff Phillip Kale alleges that on June 5, 2025, he purchased a new Dodge Charger manufactured by Moving Defendant FCA US, LLC. Plaintiff alleges that this vehicle experienced numerous defects with its electric battery and wiring. Plaintiff alleges that even after multiple repair attempts, Defendants were unable to fix these issues.

On September 4, 2025, Plaintiff brought suit against Defendants, alleging six causes of action: 1) Violation of Civil Code § 1793.2(d); 2) Violation of Civil Code § 1793.2(b); 3) Violation of Civil Code § 1793.2(a)(3); 4) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability (Civ. Code, § 1791.1; § 1794; § 1795.5); 5) Negligent Repair; and (6) Fraudulent Concealment.

Now, Plaintiff moves to compel Defendant FCA US LLC to comply with mandatory discovery disclosures under CCP § 871.26.

            Discussion

i)                   Motion to Compel Compliance

Plaintiff moves to compel Defendant FCA US LLC to comply with mandatory discovery disclosures under CCP § 871.26(h)(7-13).

In 2024, the California Legislature passed AB 1755, which created multiple new procedural and timing rules for lemon law cases. As part of this bill, the legislature enacted CCP § 871.26, which outlines mandatory discovery requirements for all Song-Beverly cases. This includes the rule that, “[w]ithin 60 days after the filing of the answer or other responsive pleading, all parties shall, without awaiting a discovery request, provide to all other parties an initial disclosure and documents pursuance to [CCP § 871.26] subdivisions (f), (g), and (h).” (CCP § 871.26(b).)

Defendant FAC filed its demurrer on November 19, 2025. (Answer, p. 1.) Thus, according to Plaintiff, Defendant FCA was obligated to provide all initial disclosures required under CCP § 871.26 by January 18, 2026. Plaintiff states that Defendant has failed to provide the required documents and that the discovery disclosures are untimely. (Rucker Decl. ¶ 4.)

In Opposition, Defendant FAC argues that January 18, 2026, was not the deadline. As Defendant points out, Defendant filed its demurrer on November 19, 2025, putting the 60 statutory deadline on January 18, 2026 – a Sunday. (Opp. p. 1:12-16.) Additionally, January 19, 2026, was MLK Jr. Day, a court holiday. (Ibid.) Thus, Defendant argues that their actual deadline was January 20, 2026. (Ibid.) Defendant also states that on January 20, 2026, they served the initial disclosures on Plaintiff. (Feeley Decl. ¶ 3.)

The Court finds Defendant’s argument correct. Under CCP § 12, “[t]he time in which any act provided by law is to be done is computed by excluding the first day, and including the last, unless the last day is a holiday, and then it is also excluded.” (CCP § 12.) Thus, Defendant is correct that the true deadline was January 20, 2026 – the same day they served their initial disclosures. Notably, Plaintiff did not file a Reply perhaps acknowledging that his argument has no merit.  The Court finds Defendant FCA’s initial discovery disclosure was timely under CCP § 871.26.

Additionally, the parties executed a stipulated protective order on August 4, 2026. (Supp. Feeley Decl., ¶ 3.) On August 7, 2026, Defendant served Plaintiff with an additional batch of confidential discovery. (Id. ¶ 5.) It appears that all the discovery at issue in this motion have been resolved.

For these reasons, the Court DENIES Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Compliance.

ii)                 Request for Sanctions

Plaintiff also requests $2,500 in statutory sanctions under CCP § 871.26. Given that the Court has found that Defendant FCA’s disclosures were timely under CCP § 871.26, Plaintiff’s request for sanctions is denied.

iii)               OSC Re: Sanctions

Finally, the Court schedules an OSC Re: Sanctions against Plaintiff and their attorney of record for September 14, 2026, for abuse of the discovery process.

Under CCP § 2023.030, “the court, after notice to any affected party, person, or attorney, and after opportunity for hearing, may impose the following sanctions against anyone engaging in conduct that is a misuse of the discovery process: (a) The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct. The court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney who advised that assertion, or on both.”

Plaintiff’s actions in filing this motion constitute an abuse of the discovery process. Plaintiff asked this Court to compel discovery from Defendant FCA that had already been properly and timely. (See generally, Mot. for Compliance.) However, Plaintiff’s motion has multiple glaring omissions.

First, nowhere in Plaintiff’s motion do they actually calculate Defendant’s deadline for initial disclosures. Plaintiff only points out the initial filing date of the demurrer, and then uses vague and generic language like, “FCA US LLC was required to serve mandatory initial disclosures containing specified documents and information within the timeframes prescribed by statute” and, “[d]espite the passage of substantial time since the commencement of this action and the service of FCA US LLC's Demurrer to the Complaint on November 19, 2025, FCA US LLC has failed to provide complete and compliant disclosures.” (Rucker Decl. ¶ 4 [emphasis added].) The Court suspects that Plaintiff’s firm deploys this vague boilerplate language so that these motions can be mass-filed in every lemon law case it handles with little to no work or particularization. The downside of that strategy is that, had Plaintiff actually done the work to calculate Defendant FCA’s correct deadline, they would have realized that Defendant’s initial disclosures were in fact, timely.

Second, even if Plaintiff takes the position that they were aware of these timely initial disclosures, but that Defendant’s document production was inadequate, that is something that should be explicitly raised and argued in the motion itself. Plaintiff’s motion makes no mention of any disclosure by Defendant on January 20, 2026. (See generally, Mot. for Compliance.) The declaration of Breana Rucker also makes no mention of this fact. (See generally, Rucker Decl.) Until it read Defendant’s Opposition, the Court was unaware that Defendant had produced any discovery. Leaving out such crucial information from a discovery motion is misleading, and constitutes a misuse of the discovery process.

A further illustration of Plaintiff’s indifferent approach to his case is his paltry meet and confer efforts. Plaintiff sent a single meet and confer email to Defendant FCA on June 2, 2026. (Feely Decl. ¶ 5.) This email was typical discovery boilerplate and does not indicate that Plaintiff made a genuine and good faith effort to meet and confer to resolve any discovery issue without having to burden the court with another unnecessary lemon law discovery motion. (Id. Ex. C.) Moreover, Defendant actually explained to Plaintiff that the initial disclosures were timely via an email on June 16, 2026 – just one day after this motion was filed. (Feeley Decl. ¶ 7.) Despite having this information directly pointed out to Plaintiff, Plaintiff allowed this meritless motion to proceed.

For these reasons, the Court schedules an OSC Re: Sanctions against Plaintiff and hisr attorney of record for September 14, 2026.

Conclusion

The Court DENIES Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Compliance. The Court also DENIES Plaintiff’s request for statutory sanctions. Finally, the Court schedules an OSC Re: Sanctions against Plaintiff and his attorney of record for September 14, 2026.

            IT IS SO ORDERED.

 Plaintiff TO GIVE NOTICE.

            [TENTATIVE] ORDER: The Court GRANTS Intervenor Benchmark Insurance’s Motion for Leave to File Complaint in Intervention. 

Background

This is a motor vehicle personal injury case. Plaintiff Sophia Jarrin alleges that on June 4, 2025, she was driving northbound on Topanga Canyon Boulevard when Defendant, driving southbound, made a sudden left-hand turn, negligently causing an accident.

On November 25, 2025, Plaintiff brought suit against Defendant, alleging two causes of action: general negligence, and negligence per se.

Now, non-party Benchmark Insurance seeks leave to intervene in this case.

            Discussion

Non-party Benchmark Insurance seeks leave to intervene in this case. (Mot. for Leave, p. 2:4-6.) Benchmark Insurance asserts that it has paid out workers compensation to Plaintiff for the injury she sustained from this accident. (Id. pp. 1:26-2:3.) Benchmark now seeks to pursue subrogation reimbursement from Defendant, pursuant to LAB § 3852.

i)                   Legal Basis

Benchmark’s motion is based on CCP § 387, which states, “[t]he court shall, upon timely application, permit a nonparty to intervene in the action or proceeding if either of the following conditions is satisfied:

(A) A provision of law confers an unconditional right to intervene.

(B) The person seeking intervention claims an interest relating to the property or transaction that is the subject of the action and that person is so situated that the disposition of the action may impair or impede that person's ability to protect that interest, unless that person's interest is adequately represented by one or more of the existing parties.

[or]

(2) The court may, upon timely application, permit a nonparty to intervene in the action or proceeding if the person has an interest in the matter in litigation, or in the success of either of the parties, or an interest against both.”

(CCP § 387(d)(1-2).)

ii)                 Potential Suit

First, the Court notes that Benchmark does have a potential claim under LAB § 3852. This statute states that, “[t]he claim of an employee . . . for compensation does not affect their claim or right of action for all damages proximately resulting from the injury or death against any person other than the employer. Except as provided in subdivision (b), an employer who pays, or becomes obligated to pay compensation, or who pays, or becomes obligated to pay salary in lieu of compensation, or who pays or becomes obligated to pay an amount to the Department of Industrial Relations pursuant to Section 4706.5, may likewise make a claim or bring an action against the third person. In the latter event the employer may recover in the same suit, in addition to the total amount of compensation, damages for which they were liable including all salary, wage, pension, or other emolument paid to the employee or to their dependents.” (LAB § 3852(a) [emphasis added].) Based on this statute, Benchmark, as the insurer of Plaintiff’s employer, has a potential claim against Defendant.

iii)               Permissive Intervention Analysis

When analyzing a request for permission intervention, the Court must analyze three factors: “[t]he Intervenor must have a direct interest in the lawsuit, the Intervenor must not enlarge the issues raised by the original parties and the Intervenor must not tread on the rights of the original parties to conduct their own lawsuit." (Kuperstein v. Superior Court (1988) 204 Cal.App.3d 598, 600.)

            Here, all three of these factors weigh in favor of allowing intervention. First, Benchmark has a direct interest in this litigation, as both parties’ claims are based on the assertion that Defendant’s negligence caused the underlying car accident. (Compl. ¶¶ 23, 34; see also, Matthews Decl. ¶¶ 8-10.)  Thus, a victory or loss for Plaintiff is a victory or loss for Benchmark.

            For similar reasons, Benchmark’s intervention, will not enlarge the issues raised in this suit. Both of Plaintiff’s claims are based on the argument that Defendant negligently caused this traffic collision. (Compl. ¶¶ 23, 34.) Meanwhile, Benchmark’s proposed Complaint-in-Intervention seeks reimbursement of the workers compensation benefits paid out to Plaintiff as a result of Defendant negligently causing this same traffic collision. (Matthews Decl. ¶¶ 8-10.) Thus, the primary factual dispute for both claims is identical.

Finally, Benchmark states that its intervention will not interfere with the parties’ rights to conduct the instant lawsuit. Specifically, , Benchmark states that they will not seek a delay of trial. (Mot. for Leave, p. 3:16-18.) The Court also notes that the trial in this case is scheduled for October 25, 2027, thus giving all parties sufficient time to try this case as set.  

Thus, all three of the permissive intervention factors weigh in favor of granting intervention. Additionally, this motion is unopposed.

For these reasons, the Court GRANTS Intervenor Benchmark Insurance’s Motion for Leave to File Complaint in Intervention.

Conclusion

The Court GRANTS Intervenor Benchmark Insurance’s Motion for Leave to File Complaint in Intervention which shall be filed within 20 days. 

            IT IS SO ORDERED, Benchmark Insurance TO GIVE NOTICE.

             ORDER: The Court GRANTS Taylor Schwartz’s Motion to be Relieved as Counsel. 

Background

            This is a premises liability case. Plaintiff Racquel Wolbert alleges that on June 8, 2024, she slipped and fell on an unknown substance on the floor at a grocery store operated by Defendant Ralphs Grocery Company. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants failed to maintain the premises  in a safe manner.

            On June 4, 2026, Plaintiff brought suit against Defendants alleging a single claim of premises liability.

            Now, Plaintiff’s attorney Taylor Schwartz moves to be relieved as counsel.

            Discussion

Counsel Taylor Schwartz moves to be relieved as Plaintiff’s counsel.

The requirements for a Motion to be Relieved as Counsel are outlined in CRC 3.1362. Moving counsel must: (1) submit form MC-051 - notice of the motion to the client, (2) submit form MC-052 - a declaration explaining why they could not acquire client’s consent to withdraw, (3) submit form MC-053 – a copy of the proposed order, and (4) serve these documents and notice of the motion to both the client and all other parties in the case. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362(a)-(e).) Additionally, relief cannot be granted if it is reasonably foreseeable that the client would suffer prejudice as a result of the withdrawal. (Mossanen v. Monfared (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1402, 1409.)

First, counsel has submitted Form MC-051 and has served Plaintiff with notice of this motion. (Motion to be Relieved, MC-051.) Second, counsel has submitted Form MC-052, stating that: “Despite counsel's repeated efforts to advise Plaintiff regarding the facts, applicable law, and realistic expectations concerning this matter, Plaintiff has persisted in taking positions that hav e resulted in a complete breakdown of trust and confidence essential to the attorney-client relationship. As a result, counsel is no longer able to effectively represent Plaintiff or fulfill his professional obligations in this matter. Therefore, it is respectfully submitted that good cause exists for these moving attorneys to be relieved as counsel of record.” (Declaration in Support, ¶ 2.) Third, counsel submitted a copy of the proposed order via Form MC-053. (Proposed Order, MC-053.) Fourth, counsel served these documents on all parties in this case. (MC-051, Proof of Service, 7/16/26.) Thus, counsel has met all the procedural requirements under CRC 3.1362.

Finally, the Court must determine whether it is reasonably foreseeable that the client will suffer prejudice if the Court grants this motion. There are no motions scheduled in this case. The most imminent hearing is a case management conference scheduled for October 2, 2026. Thus, it is not reasonably foreseeable that Plaintiff could be prejudiced by counsel withdrawing.

For these reasons, the Court GRANTS Taylor Schwartz’s Motion to be Relieved as Counsel.

Conclusion

The Court GRANTS Taylor Schwartz’s Motion to be Relieved as Counsel. 

            IT IS SO ORDERED.

Taylor Schwartz TO GIVE NOTICE to all parties and to the client.
