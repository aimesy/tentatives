DEPARTMENT F43 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 23CHCV03048    Hearing Date: June 24, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 06-24-26

Case # 23CHCV03048, 23STLC00535, Nakia Miraka Dillard, et al. v. Cruez, et al.

Trial Date: 10-26-26

 

MOTION TO BIFURCATE AND RECLASSIFY

 

MOVING PARTIES:  Plaintiffs Nakia Mirka Dillard and Aubrey Richardson, a minor by and through her guardian ad litem, Nakia Mirka Dillard

RESPONDING PARTIES:  Defendants Christiebelle Fors Cruz and Raul Cruz

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order bifurcating trial of liability from trial of damages and reclassifying the action from unlimited civil jurisdiction to limited civil jurisdiction.

 

RULING:  Motion to bifurcate is granted.  Motion to reclassify is denied without prejudice.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

Wawanesa v. Cruz (Case No. 23STLCV00535) - On January 25, 2023, Wawanesa General Insurance Company filed a complaint in subrogation against defendant Christiebelle Fors Cruz, seeking damages for injuries suffered in a vehicle collision by its insureds.  The insureds are plaintiffs Nakia Mirka Dillard and Aubrey Richardson, a minor by and through her guardian ad litem, Nakia Mirka Dillard (Plaintiffs).  Wawanesa asserts a single motor vehicle claim.  The case was filed as a limited civil matter seeking $24,361.95 in damages.

 

Dillard v. Cruz (Case No. 23CHCV03048) - On October 10, 2023, Plaintiffs filed a separate complaint, asserting motor vehicle and negligence causes of action against defendants Christiebelle Fors Cruz and Raul Cruz.  Plaintiffs allege that on November 13, 2021, Fors Cruz collided with their vehicle when she travelled at an unsafe speed and made an unsafe lane change.  Fors Cruz was driving a vehicle owned by defendant Raul Cruz.  Plaintiffs filed their complaint as an unlimited civil matter.

 

The cases were related on July 30, 2024.  On December 6, 2024, the court (Hon. Gary Micon) granted a joint stipulation to consolidate the cases, with Case No. 23CHCV03048 serving as the lead case.

 

On May 26, 2026, Plaintiffs moved to bifurcate trial into separate liability and damages phases and to reclassify the case to a limited civil jurisdiction matter.  Defendants Fors Cruz and Cruz filed a notice of non-opposition on June 10, 2026.

 

 

//

//

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Bifurcation

 

“The court, in furtherance of convenience or to avoid prejudice, or when separate trials will be conducive to expedition and economy, may order a separate trial of any cause of action . . . or of any separate issue or of any number of causes of action or issues[.]”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1048, subd. (b) [based on Fed. R. Civ. P. 42(b)].)  “The court may, when the convenience of witnesses, the ends of justice, or the economy and efficiency of handling the litigation would be promoted thereby, on motion of a party, after notice and hearing, make an order, no later than the close of pretrial conference in cases in which such pretrial conference is to be held, or, in other cases, no later than 30 days before the trial date, that the trial of any issue or any part thereof shall precede the trial of any other issue or any part thereof in the case, except for special defenses which may be tried first pursuant to Sections 597 and 597.5.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 598.)

 

“Section 598 of the Code of Civil Procedure gives the trial court power to order ‘that the trial of the issue of liability shall precede the trial of any other issue in the case . . . ‘Its objective is avoidance of the waste of time and money caused by the unnecessary trial of damage questions in cases where the liability issue is resolved against the plaintiff.’”  (Cohn v. Bugas (1974) 42 Cal.App.3d 381, 385, citation omitted.)  However, the court cannot order the separate trial of an issue of liability when because of the nature of the case it is necessary to prove the plaintiff’s damages in order to establish that liability.  (Cook v. Superior Court (1971) 19 Cal.App.3d 832, 834 [court could not bifurcate an issue of liability from damages in legal malpractice claim where it was necessary for plaintiff to prove his former attorney was negligent in the prosecution of a claim and but for that negligence, plaintiff would have obtained a more favorable result].)

 

Bifurcation is appropriate in cases where the “liability issue is resolved against the plaintiff and bifurcation will avoid the waste of time and money caused by the unnecessary trial of damage questions.”  (Trickey v. Superior Court In and For Sacramento County¿(1967) 252 Cal.App.2d 650, 653.)  The primary purpose of bifurcation is to “avoid wasting court time in cases where the plaintiff loses on the liability issue, to promote settlements where the plaintiff wins on the liability issue, and to afford a more logical presentation of the evidence, thus simplifying the issues for the jury.”  (Foreman & Clark Corp. v. Fallon¿(1971) 3 Cal.3d 875, 888, fn. 8;  Code Civ. Proc., § 1048, subd. (b).)  Whether a single action should be bifurcated is a matter within the discretion of the trial court, whose ruling will not be disturbed absent a manifest abuse of discretion.  (Downey Savings & Loan Assn. v. Ohio Casualty Ins. Co.¿(1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 1072, 1086.)  Where there are both issues of law and fact, the issue of law must be first disposed of.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 592.)

 

The parties must raise bifurcation issues at the case management conference or in a noticed motion.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.727(10).)  If bifurcation is sought under Code of Civil Procedure section 598, bifurcation must be ordered at least 30 days before trial and the motion must be filed at least 16 court days before the court must enter its order.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 598.)

 

Plaintiffs timely filed and served their motion over 30 days before trial.

 

Plaintiffs move to bifurcate trial in this matter into two phases:  Liability (Phase 1) and Damages (Phase 2).  According to Plaintiffs, bifurcation will serve the interests of judicial economy, procedural efficiency, and fundamental fairness.  Furthermore, bifurcation will prevent undue prejudice by separating contested liability issues from the emotional weight of damages evidence.

 

After reviewing the moving papers, the court finds Plaintiffs have properly demonstrated a basis for bifurcation.

 

If liability is tried first, judicial economy, efficiency, and fairness are served.  This is a personal injury matter arising from a vehicle collision between Plaintiffs, defendant Fors Cruz, and vehicle owner defendant Cruz.  If Defendants prevail on the issue of liability, then no damages phase will be necessary.  If Plaintiffs prevail, the likelihood of the parties settling will increase and the issue of damages may be avoided altogether.  (Declaration of Arya Azizi ¶ 5.)  Next, plaintiffs’ counsel anticipates the liability phase will last no more than two days and the damages phase will last no more than two days.  Plaintiffs will have a least one expert witness for liability and one for damages, with Defendants likely calling experts in the same categories.  Plaintiff Dillard and defendant Fors Cruz will testify regarding damages, and the damages phase will involve testimony of treating medical personnel and Plaintiffs’ family members.  Plaintiffs also claim the weight of the evidence (accident reconstruction, medical treatment, and testimony on pain and suffering) demonstrates the damages portion of trial will consume over half the total trial time.  (Azizi Decl. ¶ 4.)  Furthermore, trying liability first will reduce the risk of prejudice on the damages issue.  If the jury is required to make a determination on liability before hearing evidence or testimony on damages, the jury will be insulated from exposure to testimony and evidence concerning any harm and pain suffered by Plaintiffs.  This will allow the jury to objectively decide the issue of negligence without the emotional weight of damages testimony.

 

Defendants do not oppose the motion.

 

Therefore, the court grants Plaintiffs’ motion to bifurcate trial in this matter into two phases: Liability (Phase 1) and Damages (Phase 2).

 

 

            B.  Reclassify

 

“The following civil cases and proceedings are limited civil cases:  A case at law if the demand, exclusive of interest, or the value of the property in controversy amounts to thirty-five thousand dollars ($35,000) or less.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 86, subd. (a)(1).)

 

A plaintiff or defendant may move to reclassify an action within the time allowed for that party to respond to the initial pleading.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 403.040, subd. (a).)  If the case has been classified in the incorrect jurisdiction, the court must enter an order for reclassification, regardless of any fault of lack of fault. 

 

If the motion is made after the time for the defendant to respond to the pleading, the motion may only be granted if (1) the case is incorrectly classified;  and (2) the moving party shows good cause for not seeking reclassification earlier.  (Code Civ. Proc.,  § 403.040, subd. (b).)  Both conditions are mandatory.  (Leonard v. Superior Court (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 34, 43.)

 

Here, Plaintiffs have moved to reclassify this matter to a limited civil jurisdiction matter well after the time to file a responsive pleading expired.  Thus, Plaintiffs must also show “good cause for not seeking reclassification earlier.”

 

According to Plaintiffs, medical records, billing statements, wage loss documentation, and other evidence to date demonstrate that the total recoverable damages do not exceed $35,000.  (Aziz Decl. ¶ 6.)  Although this demonstrates the case is incorrectly classified, Plaintiffs fail to demonstrate good cause for the later filing.  Plaintiffs do not state when they determined the case was incorrectly classified or when they received evidence demonstrating they do not meet the jurisdictional threshold for an unlimited civil jurisdiction matter.

 

Therefore, the court denies Plaintiffs’ motion to reclassify without prejudice.

 

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

Motion to bifurcate is granted.  Motion to reclassify is denied without prejudice.

 

Plaintiffs to give notice.


Case Number: 24CHCV00697    Hearing Date: June 24, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Case # 24CHCV00697, Buenrostro v. Bolototspy, et al.

MOTION TO COMPEL PLAINTIFF’S INDEPENDENT MENTAL EXAMINATION

 

MOVING PARTIES:  Defendants Carlos Horacio Guarino, Michael Bolotsky, and The Appliance Doctor

RESPONDING PARTY:  Plaintiff Rosa Buenrostro

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order compelling a mental examination of plaintiff by neuropsychologist Dr. Earl Thorndyke, Ph.D. according to the “Description of the Neuropsychological Evaluation” attached as Exhibit K to the Declaration of Richard E. McGreevy.

 

RULING:  Motion to compel a mental examination is granted in part as described below in Section A.  Request to seal Exhibits A, B, and C to the McGreevy Declaration is granted.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

This personal injury action arises from a vehicle-pedestrian collision that occurred at the intersection of Hubbard Street and Eldridge Avenue on December 5, 2023.  (Compl., ¶¶ 12-13.)  Plaintiff Rosa Buenrostro (Plaintiff) alleges that defendant Michael Bolotsky made a left turn into her as she was crossing the street in a designated pedestrian cross walk path, causing Plaintiff to fly back 5-10 feet on the ground and to suffer significant bodily injuries.  According to Plaintiff, defendants Carlos Horacio Guarino and The Appliance Doctor owned the vehicle driven by defendant Bolotsky.  On March 5, 2024, Plaintiff filed a complaint asserting two causes of action:  (1) negligence - “negligence per se” against defendant Bolotsky;  and (2) negligent entrustment against defendants Guarino and The Appliance Doctor.  Each defendant filed and served an answer.

 

Between February 26, 2024 and October 3, 2025, Plaintiff received treatment for mental harm stemming from the alleged incident.  (Declaration of Richard E. McGreevy ¶¶ 2-4, Exh. A: Premier Forensic Psychology Evaluation and Report, Exh. B: Neurological Injury Specialists Follow Up Report, Exh. C:  NeuroPraxis report.)  At some unspecified time, defense counsel engaged Dr. Earl Thorndyke, Ph.D. to conduct a mental examination of Plaintiff that did not exceed three reports concerning Plaintiff’s mental harm: Premier Forensic Psychology, Neurological Injury Specialists, and NeuroPraxis.  (Id. ¶ 6.)

 

On January 16, 2026, defense counsel Brian Leach sent plaintiff’s counsel Brittany Farias a letter demanding Plaintiff stipulate to an independent medical examination by a neuropsychologist.  (Declaration of Brittany Farias ¶ 5, Exh. B.)  Ms. Farias responded on January 20, 2026, requesting the identity of the proposed examiner, the detailed description of the scope and purpose of the examination, and the specific list of diagnostic instruments, cognitive tests, and psychological assessments Defendants intend to administer.  (Farias Decl. ¶ 6, Exh. C.)

 

Two months later on March 25, 2026, Mr. Leach sent Ms. Farias, a letter requesting she stipulate to Plaintiff’s mental examination and provided the information Ms. Farias had previously requested.  (McGreevy Decl. ¶ 7, Exh. D.)  From April 1, 2026 through June 17, 2026, counsel have engaged in back and forth meet and confer discussions concerning the scope of the examination, the length of the examination, whether Plaintiff may have a representative or observer at the examination, and whether the proposed examiner should be required to send raw data from the exam to Ms. Farias.  The parties have not come to an agreement.

 

On May 6, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion to compel Plaintiff’s mental independent medical examination.  Plaintiff filed an opposition and Defendants filed a reply (supplemental brief) on June 17, 2026.

 

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

A motion to compel a mental independent medical examination must be accompanied by a “meet and confer” declaration showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution before the motion was filed.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2032.310, subd. (b), 2016.040.)  In Department F43, meet and confer means in person or via phone.  A letter or email does not suffice.  (Department F43 Courtroom Information, p. 2.)

 

The “meet and confer” requirement is met because counsel spoke telephonically on March 31, 2026.  (McGreevy Decl., Exh. J at p. 7.)

 

 

ANALYSIS

            B.  Conducting Mental Independent Medical Examinations

 

A motion for a mental independent medical examination (IME) must “specify the time, place, manner, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination, as well as the identity and the specialty, if any, of the person or persons who will perform the examination.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 2032.310, subd. (b).)

 

The court cannot order the examination of a party who has not asserted his or her physical or mental condition to support a defense or claim.  (Vinson v. Superior Court (1987) 43 Cal.3d 833, 389 (Vinson).)  However, once a “party who chooses to allege that he has mental and emotional difficulties,” the party puts his mental state in controversy.  (Id.;  Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.020, subd. (a).)  The moving party has the burden to show by declarations or other evidence that the examinee’s condition is “in controversy.”  (Reuter v. Superior Court (1979) 93 Cal.App.3d 332, 340.)  Pleadings and discovery tools such as interrogatories or depositions may also put a party’s mental or physical condition in controversy when the pleading alleges a party suffers psychiatric conditions as a result of sustained injuries.  (Id.)

 

The court may only grant such a motion for “good cause,” in order to protect the examinee from “excessive and unwarranted intrusions.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2032.310, subd. (a), 2032.320, subd. (a);  Sporich v. Superior Court (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 422, 428.)¿¿ A showing of good cause generally requires “that the party produce specific facts justifying discovery and that the inquiry be relevant to the subject matter of the action or reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.”  (Vinson, supra, 43 Cal.3d at p. 840.)  “Good cause is established by facts that ‘appear in the record as a demonstrable reality.’  Mere speculation, standing alone, will not suffice.”  (Sporich v. Superior Court, supra, 77 Cal.App.4th at p. 428, superseded on other grounds by Albertson v. Superior Court (2001) 25 Cal.4th 76.)

 

“An order granting a physical or mental examination shall specify the person or persons who may perform the examination, as well as the time, place, manner, diagnostic tests and procedures, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.320, subd. (d), emphasis added;  Carpenter v. Superior Court (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 249, 261 [“Requiring the court to identify the permissible diagnostic tests and procedures, by name, confirms that the court has weighed the risks of unwarranted intrusion upon the plaintiff against the defendant’s need for a meaningful opportunity to test the plaintiff’s claims of physical or mental injury.  Furthermore, the resulting specificity and clarity of the order will also aid the examiner in understanding and complying with the parameters imposed by the court.”].)

 

 

Defendants move for Plaintiff’s mental independent medical examination (IME) based on Plaintiff putting her mental health at issue in this matter. 

 

From February 26, 2024 through February 26, 2025, Plaintiff received treatment from Premier Forensic Psychology.  (McGreevy Decl. ¶ 2, Exh. A.)  According to Premier’s evaluation and report Plaintiff has suffered from “substantial mental harm and psychological trauma” as a result of the vehicle-pedestrian accident in this matter.  Between July 10 and October 3 of 2025, Plaintiff was treated with Neurological Injury Specialists for memory impairment, depression, anxiety, and stress intolerance.  (Id. ¶ 3, Exh. B.)  On December 10, 2025, Plaintiff was evaluated at NeuroPraxis and diagnosed with memory and cognitive deficits including, but not limited to, problems with comprehension, decision making, learning, cognitive flexibility, planning, organization, time management, problem solving, math, reading, and writing. Her proposed plan of care includes cognitive therapy and psychotherapy.  (Id. ¶ 4, Exh. C.)

 

Defendants do not state when or how they received these reports or why they waited until March 25, 2026 to respond to Ms. Farias’s January 20, 2026 email asking for clarity.  Regardless, Plaintiff’s mental health injuries are now at issue in this matter.[1]

            1.  Defendants have demonstrated “good cause” for the mental exam.

 

Defendants claim good cause exists for a mental IME because Plaintiff has put her mental health at issue.  According to defendants, the IME will provide relevant background information about the circumstances concerning the onset of Plaintiff’s mental health issues concerning her mental state before, during, and after the alleged incident and the manifestation of Plaintiff’s symptoms over time to present.  (Motion at p. 5:8-23.)  Considering this is a personal injury matter and assuming Plaintiff provided the three reports to Defendants, Plaintiff has put her psychological issues stemming from the incident at issue.  Because Plaintiff seeks damages for injuries she has suffered and continues to suffer, Defendants properly demonstrate good cause for a mental IME as limited to the issues in the three reports.

 

 

2.  The scope of the examination is overly broad.

 

“[W]hile the filing of a lawsuit may implicitly bring about a partial waiver of one’s constitutional right of associational privacy, the scope of such ‘waiver’ must be narrowly rather than expansively construed.”  (Britt v. Superior Court (1978) 20 Cal.3d 844, 859.)

 

The “Description of the Neuropsychological Evaluation” states in relevant part:

 

The scope of the MSE is to evaluate Ms. Buenrostro’s mental and emotional conditions that she has put into controversy in this matter by her complaint and the analysis and evaluations of her treating medial [sic] providers as set forth in their reports produced in this litigation. The MSE will not involve questions about how the incident, which is the subject of this lawsuit [sic] occurred. The MSE will include relevant background information about the circumstances of onset of the alleged mental state before, at the time of and after the alleged events and how those symptoms have manifested over time to the present.

 

The MSE will consist of a clinical interview covering a variety of relevant topics, including background information; the reported circumstances of onset of the alleged mental state injury or injuries; and how those symptoms have unfolded over time to the present, including any other assessments, treatment, or functional impairment.  A detailed evaluation of this type is important in differentiating between mental state injuries/complaints that may be pre-existing and not related to the alleged actions of the defendants;  injuries/complaints that were pre-existing but may have been exacerbated by the alleged actions of defendants;  injuries/complaints that arose concurrently but unrelated to alleged actions of defendants;  complaints that arose as a result of defendants’ alleged conduct;  complaints that are actually due to substance use or medications; complaints that are due to an underlying general medical disorder;  or complaints that arose subsequent to the alleged actions of defendants and were unrelated.

 

Pertinent areas of inquiry with regard to plaintiff’s background include developmental history, education, employment, social and marital history, legal history (such as disability claims, if any), general medical history, mental health history, substance use, and family history. Additionally, the clinical interview will elicit from plaintiff a detailed account of the onset and course of his/her mental state problems/impairments related to the alleged conduct of the defendants, culminating in a review of current mental state and physical complaints.

 

(McGreevy Decl., Exh. K at p. 1, paras. 2-4.)

 

As phrased, this language is overbroad because it does not state the examination is limited to the issues in the three reports which serve as the basis of Defendants’ motion.  This is also reflected in the most recent stipulation Defendants served on April 23, 2026, which is silent concerning the names of the reports or the specific symptoms and diagnoses in the cited reports.  (McGreevy Decl., Exh. H at pp. 2-11.)  As the “Description of the Neuropsychological Evaluation” is currently phrased, Dr. Thorndyke may ask questions beyond the scope of the reports.

 

In order to limit the overbreadth of the Description, the court limits the scope of the examination to inquiries concerning the symptoms and diagnoses in the Premier Forensic Psychology report (dated February 26, 2025), Neurological Injury Specialists report (dated October 3, 2025), and NeuroPraxis report (dated December 10, 2025), attached as Exhibits A, B, and C to the McGreevy Declaration, executed on May 6, 2026.  More specifically, the court modifies the scope of the examination as follows:

 

·         The examination is limited to matters reasonably related to Plaintiff’s claimed mental injuries from the alleged incident that occurred on December 5, 2023:  TBI, post concussive syndrome, PTSD, Major Depressive Disorder, memory impairment, cognitive deficits, changes in mood and sleep/wake cycle changes, anxiety, mood swings,  stress intolerance;

·         The inquiry may include developmental, educational, employment, social, marital, legal, disability, family, medication, and substance-use.  The inquiry may include physical health as it relates to the claimed mental injuries (e.g., decreased or increased appetite as a symptom of Major Depressive Disorder);

·         Prohibited from seeking unrelated information which was already obtained in previous depositions.

 

(Opposition at p. 12:17-24.)

 

 

3.  Because the exam is being conducted as an oral inquiry, the raw data issue is moot.

 

An ongoing issue throughout counsel’s “meet and confer” efforts was whether the raw data from the exam could be sent directly to plaintiff’s counsel.  Defendants claim this is no longer an issue because the examination will only consist of an oral interview, rather than standardized testing, and will not involve a physical examination of any kind.  (Motion at pp. 5:24-6:1.)

 

Thus, the “raw data” issue is moot.

 

 

4.  Plaintiff does not demonstrate this is an “exceptional case” requiring counsel’s presence at the mental examination.

 

Defendants contend the court should deny Plaintiff’s request to have a personal representative present at the mental IME based on Edwards v. Superior Court (1976) 16 Cal.3d 905.  Plaintiff’s opposition does not address this issue or state why her counsel’s or another representative’s presence would be necessary at the mental IME.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2032.510 expressly provides that the attorney for the examinee, or the attorney’s representative, must be permitted to attend and observe any physical IME conducted for discovery purposes.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.510, subd. (a).)  No equivalent mandatory provision exists for mental examinations.  Instead, Section 2032.530, subdivision (b) states:  “Nothing in this title shall be construed to alter, amend, or affect existing case law with respect to the presence of the attorney for the examinee or other persons during the examination by agreement or court order.”  Courts have interpreted this distinction to indicate the Legislature’s intent that counsel not be present at a mental examination except in “exceptional cases.”  (People v. Burns (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 794, 805 [citing Golfland Entertainment Centers, Inc. v. Superior Court (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 739, 746-749].)  The California Supreme Court previously held that a plaintiff in a personal injury action may not insist upon the presence of their attorney at a psychiatric examination conducted by the defense.  (Edwards v. Superior Court (1976) 16 Cal.3d 905, 909-912.)  Because the purpose of a mental examination is to ascertain the examinee’s mental processes and the personal interview requires rapport between the examiner and examinee, the attorney’s presence and participation would hinder the purpose of the exam.  (Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc. v. Superior Court (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 1391, 1396.)

 

Here, Plaintiff’s opposition fails to present facts demonstrating how this is an “exceptional case,” requiring counsel’s or another representative’s presence at the mental exam.

 

Therefore, the court denies any request for plaintiff’s counsel or plaintiff’s counsel’s representative to attend and observe the mental exam.

 

5.  The parties do not dispute Dr. Thorndyke’s qualifications for performing the mental examination.

 

A mental exam “shall be performed only by a licensed physician, or by a licensed clinical psychologist who holds a doctoral degree in psychology and has had at least five years of postgraduate experience in the diagnosis of emotional and mental disorders.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.020, subd. (c)(1).)

 

Dr. Earl Thorndyke, Ph.D. is a clinical psychologist, licensed in California, a fellowship-trained neuropsychologist, and serves as a clinical neuropsychology supervisor at Executive Mental Health.  (Declaration of Earl C. Thorndyke, Ph.D. ¶ 1.)  The moving papers do not present any further information about Dr. Thorndyke’s background and whether he has at least five years of postgraduate experience in diagnosing emotional and mental disorders.  Defendants’ March 25, 2026, Letter demanding the mental IME states Dr. Thorndyke’s curriculum vitae is attached, but Defendants did not include a copy of the document with the moving papers.  (McGreevy Decl., Exh. D.)  Because Plaintiff does not challenge Dr. Thorndyke’s qualifications, the court infers that the parties agree that Dr. Thorndyke may perform the mental IME.

 

 

Therefore, the court grants in part Defendants’ motion to compel the independent mental examination of plaintiff Rosa Buenrostro.

 

 

Plaintiff is ordered to submit to an independent mental examination within twenty (20) days of this order as follows:

 

1.  Dr. Earl Thorndyke, Ph.D. will conduct an oral mental examination on a date and at a time to be agreed upon by Plaintiff and Defendants.

 

2.  The examination will last no more than eight hours, including breaks.

 

3.  The examination will take place at 11835 W. Olympic Boulevard, Suite 1270E, Los Angeles, CA 90064.

 

4.  The Scope of the Examination is limited to a clinical interview and inquiry as follows:

 

•  Matters reasonably related to Plaintiff’s claimed mental injuries from the alleged incident that occurred on December 5, 2023:  TBI, post concussive syndrome, PTSD, Major Depressive Disorder, memory impairment, cognitive deficits, changes in mood and sleep/wake cycle changes, anxiety, mood swings,  stress intolerance;

•  Developmental, educational, employment, social, marital, legal, disability, family, medication, and substance-use; and

•  Physical health as it relates to the claimed mental injuries (e.g., decreased or increased appetite as a symptom of Major Depressive Disorder);

 

5.  Dr. Thorndyke will not perform any standardized tests.

 

6.  Dr. Thorndyke may not question Plaintiff about any of the following matters:  liability and the circumstances of the accident.

 

7.  Defendants must serve a revised and code-compliant deposition notice and “Description of the Neuropsychological Evaluation” that reflects the “Scope of Examination” limitations in item number 4.

 

8.  Dr. Thorndyke will submit his mental exam report to defense counsel within fifteen days after the exam.

 

 

            B.  Sealing Exhibits A, B, and C to the McGreevy Declaration

 

In Defendants’ reply and supplemental brief, Defendants asks the court to seal Exhibits A, B, and C, the reports on which this motion is based.

 

Unless confidentiality is required by law, court records are presumed to be open to the public, pursuant to a potent “open court” policy undergirded by the First Amendment and favoring the public nature of court proceedings.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(c); See NBC Subsidiary (KNBC-TV), Inc. v. Superior Court (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1178, 1199-10.)  Consequently, pleadings, motions, discovery documents, and other papers may not be filed under seal merely by stipulation of the parties; filing under seal requires a court order. (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 2.551(a); See H.B. Fuller Co. v. Doe (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 879, 888.) 

 

“A party requesting that a record be filed under seal must file a motion or an application for an order sealing the record.  The motion or application must be accompanied by a memorandum and a declaration containing facts sufficient to justify the sealing.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551(b)(1).)  The records cannot be sealed without the appropriate findings.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d).)

 

According to Defendants, the court may seal the three reports because (1) Plaintiff’s privacy rights are arguably an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) the overriding privacy interest supports sealing the record; (3) a substantial probability exists that Plaintiff’s right to privacy will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) the proposed sealing is narrowly tailored to only the three reports that might infringe upon Plaintiff’s right to privacy; and (5) no less restrictive means exist to protect Plaintiff’s right to privacy from public disclosure of these records.

 

The court agrees and grants Defendants’ request to seal Exhibits A, B, and C to the Declaration of Richard E. McGreevy, filed on May 6, 2026.

Request to seal Exhibits A, B, and C to the McGreevy Declaration is granted.

Case Number: 25CHCV03171    Hearing Date: June 24, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 06-24-26

Case # 25CHCV03171, Sherman, et al. v. Young, M.D., et al.

Trial Date: None set.

 

DEMURRER AND MOTION TO STRIKE

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendant Charles A. Young, M.D.

RESPONDING PARTIES:  Plaintiffs Jennifer Sherman and Anthony Sherman

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order sustaining demurrer the First Amended Complaint’s Second and Third Causes of Action.

 

Order striking punitive damages from the First Amended Complaint.

 

 

RULING:  Demurrer is sustained with leave to amend.  Motion to strike is granted.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

Plaintiffs Jennifer Sherman and Anthony Sherman (Plaintiffs) filed this medical malpractice action against defendants Charles A. Young, M.D. and Henry Mayo Newhall Memorial Hospital on September 5, 2025.  Plaintiffs allege that plaintiff Jennifer Sherman sustained an ankle fracture due to defendant Young’s negligent conduct while he performed a surgery.  Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint on April 22, 2026, asserting (1) medical malpractice against all defendants; (2) medical battery against defendant Young; (3) intentional concealment against all defendants; and (4) loss of consortium against all defendants.

 

Plaintiffs allege the following:  On February 15, 2025, plaintiff Jennifer Sherman presented to Henry Mayo’s emergency department after an ice-skating incident in which she sustained an acute comminuted, displaced proximal fibular fracture, mildly displaced oblique, distal tibia fracture, and possible non-displaced distal tibial fracture.  (FAC ¶ 12.)  Plaintiff Jennifer Sherman consented to a tibia-fibula ORIF procedure which defendant Young completed.  (Id. ¶ 13.)  Defendant Young’s post-operative notice states “a posterior malleolus fracture” was identified on the distal ankle and “fixated using 2.4 mm cannulated screws. 2 roughly parallel K wires were placed under lateral fluoroscopic imaging, avoiding joint penetration. Measurements were taken after wires were inserted. The near cortex was drilled, and a short threaded 2.4 mm cannulated screw was placed over each wire. This provided stabilization of the posterior malleolus fracture.”  (Id. ¶ 13.)  The ankle fracture was mentioned in no other document, and at no time after the procedure did Defendants disclose to Plaintiff that (1) an ankle fracture had occurred intraoperatively, or (2) surgical fixation of the ankle had been performed.  (Id. ¶ 14.)

 

Plaintiffs allege plaintiff Jennifer Sherman was not provided with complete medical records and radiographic imaging concerning the ankle-related fracture or diagnosis which prevented her from discovering the true nature and cause of her injury.  (FAC ¶ 15.)  The ankle injury required longer hospitalization and continues to require both surgical and non-surgical care and remains painful.  (Id. ¶ 18.)  Plaintiffs assert that plaintiff Jennifer Sherman did not arrive at Henry Mayo with an ankle fracture and that the fracture was caused by the negligent and unnecessarily forceful and violent conduct defendant Young performed during the ORIF procedure.  (Id. ¶ 16.)

 

On May 22, 2026, defendant Charles A. Young, M.D. (Defendant) filed the instant demurrer and motion to strike.  On June 8, 2026, Plaintiffs filed oppositions.  On June 12, 2026, Defendant filed a reply. 

 

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

Demurrers and motions to strike must be accompanied by a “meet and confer” declaration stating that the parties met and conferred “in person, by telephone, or by video conference” and stating that (1) the parties failed to reach an agreement resolving the issues raised in the demurrer and motion to strike;  or (2) that the party who filed the pleading subject to the demurrer or motion to strike failed to respond to the meet and confer request or failed to meet and confer in good faith.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a)(3), 435.5, subd. (a)(3).)  In Department F43, meet and confer means in person or via phone, not by letter or email.  (Department F43 Courtroom Information, p. 2.)

 

The parties never met and conferred directly.  (Declaration of Kevin P. Miller ¶ 3.)  However, the court exercises its authority to consider the merits of the moving papers.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENTS

 

Defendant contends Plaintiffs fail to allege sufficient facts demonstrating Defendant exceeded the scope of consent because there was no procedure performed on the distal fibula described in the complaint.  There is confusion concerning the tibia versus fibula.  Moreover, Plaintiffs fail to allege that plaintiff Jennifer relied on a concealed fact and suffered any harm as a result of a concealed fact, specifically that Defendant aggressively mishandled her ankle during surgery.  Even though the medical records may have failed to disclose any fact after the occurrence of the fracture, is irrelevant because Plaintiff could not reasonably have relied on a medical record prepared and obtained after the fact to her detriment.  Plaintiff’s alleged harm from the ankle fracture itself is a separate and distinct injury from the injury caused by the supposed concealment of the fracture.  Finally, Defendant contends the court should strike punitive damages because this is a professional negligence matter which requires leave of the court before making a claim for punitive damages.

 

Plaintiffs have attached a proposed Second Amended Complaint to correct the drafting error concerning the anatomical description of the fracture and eliminates any ambiguity concerning the fracture that was allegedly caused during the surgery.  As for the intentional concealment claim, Plaintiffs asserts the claim is based on the failure to disclose after surgery that the ankle fracture occurred during the ORIF procedure and required additional surgical procedures to repair it.  According to Plaintiffs, defendants concealed these material facts by omitting them from post-operative documentation and continuing to characterize plaintiff Jennifer Sherman’s condition as merely a tib-fib fracture.  This deprived plaintiff Jennifer Sherman of the ability to make informed medical and legal decisions concerning her care.  As for the motion to strike, Plaintiffs claim the allegations concerning punitive damages do not request punitive damages but states Plaintiffs’ intention to the request punitive damages upon a motion to the court.

 

 

REQUESTS FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

Defendant asks the court to take judicial notice of the verifiable scientific fact that “posterior malleolus” is part of the back of the tibia bone and not part of the fibula, pursuant to Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (g).  Defendant cites two sources to support the request:  (1) (IMAIOS e-Anatomy, “Posterior Malleolus, https://www.imaios.com/en/e-anatomy/anatomical-structures/posterior-malleolus-1537020496#);  and (2) (Bourne & Sinkler, Anatomy, Bony Pelvis and Lower Limb: Tibia (StatPearls, updated July 24, 2023) NCBI Bookshelf “https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK526053/.”

 

The court grants the request as follows:  The posterior malleolus is part of the back of the tibia bone and is not part of the fibula.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Demurrer

 

A party may respond to a pleading against it by demurrer based on one or more of eight enumerated grounds, including that “the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action” and is uncertain, meaning “ambiguous and unintelligible.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subds. (e), (f).)  The grounds for demurring must be apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

 

The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.”  (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.)  “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)  The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law[.]”  (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.)  In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated.  (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.)

 

            1.  Second Cause of Action:  Medical Battery

 

Medical battery arises when a physician performs a medical procedure without the patient’s consent or in deliberate deviation from the consent given.  To establish medical battery, plaintiff must allege (1) consent was conditional, (2) a doctor intentionally violated the condition while providing treatment, and (3) the patient suffered harm as a result of the doctor’s violation of the condition.  (Conte v. Girard Orthopaedic Surgeons Medcial Group, Inc. (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 1260, 1269.)  “[A] physician who performs a medical procedure without the patient’s consent commits a battery irrespective of the skill or care used.”  (Id. at pp. 1266-1267.)

 

Plaintiffs allege plaintiff Jennifer consented to a “tibia and fibula ORIF procedure,” a procedure that did not involve or require a fracture to her ankle.  (FAC ¶¶ 28-29.)  But Defendant’s violent mishandling of plaintiff Jennifer’s lower extremities during the ORIF procedure caused a fracture to her ankle and required Defendant to perform a more extensive surgical procedure to which Plaintiff never consented.  (Id. ¶ 29.)  Defendant performed a distinct and separate procedure involving the ankle (fixation of the posterior malleolus with screws and K-wires) but did not disclose this to plaintiff Jennifer and was not authorized to perform the surgery by plaintiff Jennifer.  (Id. ¶ 28.)  Plaintiff claims the ankle fracture and surgical repair to the ankle have caused her to suffer and continue to suffer pain and require both surgical and non-surgical treatment.

 

Defendant asserts the claim fails in part because the posterior malleolus is part of the distal tibia rather than the fibula.  Plaintiff claims she arrived at Henry Mayo with a distal tibia fracture and possible non-displaced distal tibial facture but that no radiographic or other evidence demonstrates she arrived at the hospital with a distal fibula—fractured ankle.  (FAC ¶ 12.)  After taking judicial notice of the posterior malleolus fact and reviewing paragraph 12 along with the allegations in the Second Cause of Action (Id. ¶¶ 28-33), the court finds that Plaintiffs’ allegations concerning the scope of consent are internally inconsistent, considering Plaintiffs also allege plaintiff Jennifer consented to a “tibia and fibula ORIF procedure” and that the “additional” posterior malleolus procedure would have involved the tibia bone rather than the fibula.

 

Thus, Plaintiffs do not clearly allege Defendant caused the ankle fracture during surgery and in turn went beyond the scope of informed consent to repair the ankle fracture.  Plaintiffs present a copy of a proposed second amended complaint which clarifies the bones that form the ankle and that Defendant’s operative note did not address certain bones (lateral malleolus/distal fibula) which were supposedly repaired during the “additional” surgery.

 

Therefore, the court sustains the demurrer to the First Amended Complaint’s Second Cause of Action with leave to amend.

 

 

            2.  Third Cause of Action:  Intentional Concealment

 

To establish fraudulent concealment, the complaint must plead “(1) concealment or suppression of a material fact; (2) by a defendant with a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff; (3) the defendant intended to defraud the plaintiff by intentionally concealing or suppressing the fact; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he or she did if he or she had known of the concealed or suppressed fact; and (5) plaintiff sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact.”  (Graham v. Bank of America, N.A. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 594, 606.)  Fraud claims must be pled with specificity; general and conclusory allegations do not suffice.  (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.)  The complaint must plead facts showing “how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.”  (Ibid.)  The facts must be clear enough to inform the opposing party what they must answer.  (See Scafidi v. Western Loan & Building Co. (1946) 72 Cal.App.2d 550, 558.)  “[C]onclusionary allegations that the omissions were intentional and for the purpose of defrauding and deceiving plaintiffs and bringing about the purchase . . . and that plaintiffs relied on the omissions in making such purchase are insufficient [to show fraud by concealment].”  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 347.)

 

“[A] duty to disclose arises in this context only where there is already a sufficient relationship or transaction between the parties.”  (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 312.)  A sufficient relationship arises when a transaction occurs between the parties such as that of a buyer and a seller, an employer and a prospective employee, or parties entering into any kind of contract.  (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 337.)  “[N]ondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud:  (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff;  (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff;  (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff;  and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.”  (LiMandri v. Judkins (1996) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336 [quoting Heliotis v. Schuman (1986) 181 Cal.App.3d 646, 651]; see also Warner Constr. Corp. v. City of Los Angeles (1970) 2 Cal.3d 285, 294.)

 

Here, a special relationship existed between Defendant, a medical professional, and Plaintiff, his patient.  (Alexander v. Scripps Memorial Hospital La Jolla (2018) 23 Cal.App.5th 206, 235 [Physician-patient relationship giving rise to duty of care exists where doctor affirmatively treats and advises patient.].)

 

Plaintiffs allege defendants concealed the material fact that plaintiff Jennifer sustained an ankle fracture during the ORIF procedure and that surgical fixation of the fracture was performed.  (FAC ¶ 41.)  Although Defendant’s operative note references and identifies fixation of a posterior malleolus fracture, no other medical records or post-operative notes disclose the existence of an ankle fracture, referring only to a tibia-fibular injury.  (Id. ¶ 41.)

 

As mentioned above, the First Amended Complaint is internally inconsistent concerning whether the fractures plaintiff Jennifer originally presented with were tibia fractures or fibula fractures.  This is important because the additional surgery addressed a fracture in the posterior malleolus which includes the tibia but not the fibula.  Yet, Plaintiffs allege plaintiff Jennifer consented to a “tibia and fibula ORIF procedure.”  (FAC ¶ 12.) 

 

Thus, it is unclear whether Defendant did conceal the fact of the ankle fracture and that the repair would occur as part of the procedure to which plaintiff Jennifer consented.  Furthermore, it is unclear how the material fact was concealed when Plaintiffs allege the “posterior malleolus” repair was revealed in the operative note and Plaintiffs do not state how they obtained the note.

 

Plaintiffs do adequately allege that plaintiff Jennifer suffered harm as a result of the surgery.  But the uncertainties in the First Amended Complaint make it difficult for the court to determine whether plaintiff Jennifer actually relied on Defendant’s silence and whether Defendant intentionally hid the “additional” surgery from her since it is mentioned in his operation note.

 

Therefore, the court sustains the demurrer to the First Amended Complaint’s Third Cause of Action with leave to amend, as to defendant Charles A. Young, M.D.

 

 

            B.  Motion to Strike

 

“Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435.)  A court may, upon motion or at any time, strike from the complaint “any irrelevant, false, or improper matter” or “all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 436, subds. (a)-(b), 187; Greshko v. County of Los Angeles (1987) 194 Cal.App.3d 822, 830 [noting the court’s inherent power to limit the focus of the proceedings to the issues framed by the remaining viable pleadings].)  An “irrelevant matter” is an allegation that “is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense,” “is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense,” or “a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint or cross-complaint.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subds. (b)-(c).)  The court must read the factual allegations in the complaint as a whole and assume their truth.  (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.) 

 

Defendant Young moves to strike punitive damages from the First Amended Complaint:

 

·         Paragraph 50 on Page 12:  “Upon motion to the court, and granting thereof, Plaintiff will seek exemplary damages in the within action in a sum according to proof.”

·         Paragraph 2 of the Prayer for Relief on Page 13: “Upon motion to the court, and the granting thereof, for punitive or exemplary damages where appropriate in an amount to be determined at trial.”

 

 

1.  Professional Negligence and Punitive Damages

 

A motion to strike punitive damages may lie where the alleged facts do not rise to the level of “malice, fraud, or oppression” required to support punitive damages.  (Turman v. Turning Point of Central California, Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.)  To state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a complaint must set forth specific facts demonstrating the elements stated in Civil Code section 3294.  (College Hospital, Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 721;  see also Brousseau v. Jarrett (1977) 73 Cal.App.3d 864, 872.)

 

“[A]ny claim for punitive damages in an action against a health care provider be subject to [Civil Code section 425.13] if the injury that is the basis for the claim was caused by conduct that was directly related to the rendition of professional services.”  (Central Pathology Service Medical Clinic, Inc. v. Superior Court (1992) 3 Cal.4th 181, 192-193 [“Plaintiffs’ cause of action for fraud in this case is directly related to the manner in which defendants provided professional services.  The claim emanates from the manner in which defendants performed and communicated the results of medical tests, a matter that is an ordinary and usual part of medical professional services.”].)

 

“In any action for damages arising out of the professional negligence of a health care provider, no claim for punitive damages shall be included in a complaint or other pleading unless the court enters an order allowing an amended pleading that includes a claim for punitive damages to be filed.  The court may allow the filing of an amended pleading claiming punitive damages on a motion by the party seeking the amended pleading and on the basis of the supporting and opposing affidavits presented that the plaintiff has established that there is a substantial probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim pursuant to Section 3294 of the Civil Code.  The court shall not grant a motion allowing the filing of an amended pleading that includes a claim for punitive damages if the motion for such an order is not filed within two years after the complaint or initial pleading is filed or not less than nine months before the date the matter is first set for trial, whichever is earlier.”  (Civ. Code, § 425.13, subd. (a).)

 

Here, Plaintiffs cite no statutory authority or case law which supports the proposition that a plaintiff in a professional medical negligence matter may include a conditional or anticipatory claim for punitive damages or placeholder allegations reserving the right to seek exemplary damages upon future motion in the operative pleading.

 

Absent a good faith basis for modifying or extending an existing law, litigants are generally prohibited from asserting a position in litigation without authority.  (See, e.g., In re Estate of Randall (1924) 194 Cal. 725, 728-729 [“Contentions supported neither by argument nor by citation of authority are deemed to be without foundation, and to have been abandoned.”];  see also Lafferty v. Wells Fargo Bank (2013) 213 Cal. App. 4th 545, 571-572 [“When a point is asserted without argument and authority for the proposition, ‘it is deemed to be without foundation and requires no discussion by the reviewing court.’”];  Atchley v. City of Fresno (1984) 151 Cal. App. 3d 635, 647 [“Where a point is merely asserted by appellant’s counsel without any argument of or authority for the proposition, it is deemed to be without foundation and requires no discussion by the reviewing court.”].)

 

Although Plaintiffs base their claims in allegations of professional medical negligence, no such motion for leave to include punitive damages has been filed.

 

Therefore, the court grants defendant Charles A. Young, M.D.’s motion to strike punitive damages from the First Amended Complaint.

 

 

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

Demurrer to the First Amended Complaint’s Second and Third Causes of Action is sustained with leave to amend.  This ruling applies only to defendant Charles A. Young, M.D.

 

Motion to strike is granted.  The court strikes the following from the First Amended Complaint:  Lines 17-18 on Page 12 and Lines 19-20 on Page 13.

 

Plaintiffs may file and serve an amended complaint within twenty (20) days of this order.

Case Number: 26CHCV01668    Hearing Date: June 24, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 06-24-26

Case # 26CHCV01668, Stevens v. Pelaez, et al.

Trial Date: None set.

 

APPLICATION FOR A WRIT OF POSSESSION

 

MOVING PARTY:  Plaintiff Dawn Stevens, in pro per

RESPONDING PARTY:  No response was filed.

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order granting plaintiff’s application for writs of possession for dogs known as Decker and Mallory.

 

RULING:  Application is denied without prejudice.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

Plaintiff Dawn Stevens (Plaintiff) alleges she is the lawful owner of two dogs:  Decker, her licensed service dog and mobility assistant; and Mallory, a Belgian Malinois puppy.  On October 28, 2025, Plaintiff suffered a medical emergency and was transported to West Hills Hospital due to severe respiratory distress and critically low oxygen levels.  Plaintiff temporarily entrusted her dogs to James De Jesus for the short-term during her hospitalization.  However, on October 29, 2025, defendant Pablo Pelaez obtained possession of the dogs without Plaintiff’s authorization and falsely represented to De Jesus that Plaintiff had authorized him to retrieve the dogs.  De Jesus relied on the assertion and surrendered possession of the dogs to defendant Pelaez.  Pelaez did not return the dogs.  Plaintiff believes defendants Ashley Castro, Dana Carrara, Redline Rescue, and Hector Ruiz Romo assisted defendant Pelaez in withholding, transferring, fostering, and detention of both dogs.  Defendant Carrara created a fundraiser and public representations concerning at least one of the dogs.  Redline Rescue through defendant Romo benefited from withholding the dogs without Plaintiff’s consent.  Plaintiff believes the dog Decker is currently in the possession of defendant Gina Quinones in Moorpark, California and that Mallory is currently in the possession of defendant Mi Kyoung Chaing, also known as Adrienne, in Las Vegas, Nevada.  Plaintiff has demanded the return of her dogs directly and through others but defendants have not returned them.  Plaintiff further alleges Decker’s microchip registration was altered or transferred without her knowledge.

 

On April 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed this action against the named defendants.  The First Amended Complaint, filed on April 29, 2026, asserts (1) conversion, (2) trespass to chattels, (3) claim and delivery, (4) declaratory relief, (5) intentional infliction of emotional distress, and (6) negligence.  Plaintiff is self-represented.

 

On May 29, 2026, defendant Gina Quinones filed an answer to the Complaint.

 

On May 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed an application for a writ of possession for the dogs Decker and Mallory.  The application is supported by the Declaration of Dawn Stevens, Declaration of Elizabeth Petito-Martinez, Declaration of Lamar Preston Hall, Declaration of James De Jesus, and Plaintiff’s supplemental declaration in support of claim and delivery.  It is unclear whether Plaintiff served the application on the defendants.

 

No opposition was filed.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Writs of Possession

 

Upon, or after, filing a complaint, the plaintiff may apply for a writ of possession by filing a written application for the writ with the court in which the action is brought.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 512.010, subd. (a).)

 

The application must be executed under oath and include:

 

(1) A showing of the basis of the plaintiff’s claim and that the plaintiff is entitled to possession of the property claimed. If the basis of the plaintiff’s claim is a written instrument, a copy of the Instrument shall be attached.

(2) A showing that the property is wrongfully detained by the defendant, of the manner in which the defendant came into possession of the property, and, according to the best knowledge, information, and belief of the plaintiff, of the reason for the detention.

(3) A particular description of the property and a statement of its value.

(4) A statement, according to the best knowledge, information, and belief of the plaintiff, of the location of the property and, if the property, or some part of it, is within a private place which may have to be entered to take possession, a showing that there is probable cause to believe that such property is located there.

(5) A statement that the property has not been taken for a tax, assessment, or fine, pursuant to a statute; or seized under an execution against the property of the plaintiff; or, if so seized, that it is by statute exempt from such seizure.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 512.010, subd. (b).)

 

Attached to the Dawn Stevens declaration are the following exhibits:  (1) a copy of a dog license issued to Plaintiff for Decker the dog by the City of Los Angeles, Department of Animal Services on December 15, 2025; (2) a bill of sale for a Belgian Malinois puppy provided to Plaintiff by the seller on September 17, 2025; (3) 24Petwatch microchip registry transfer/restoration documents concerning Decker the dog; (4) a copy of Plaintiff’s wristband from her stay at West Hills Hospital, dated October 28, 2025; (5) an email summary of a GoFundMe fundraiser created by defendant Dana Carrara for a dog named Mallory; and (7) screenshots of the GoFundMe fundraiser statements concerning the dog Mallary’s alleged condition and claimed rescue circumstances.  (Declaration of Dawn Stephens, Exh. A-F.)  To the best of Plaintiff’s knowledge, Decker and Mallory were not taken for any tax, assessment, or fine pursuant to the statute.

 

The evidence demonstrates that Plaintiff was issued a license for a dog named Decker on December 15, 2025.  The quality of the document presented to the court makes it difficult to decipher all the words on the document but the license appears to state that Decker’s “special purpose” was “disabled.”  This supports Plaintiff’s allegation that Decker is a service dog.  (Compl. ¶ 14.)  Plaintiff claims Decker has an estimated value of $5,000.  (Stevens Decl. ¶ 38(A).)  Furthermore, the bill of sale indicates that a female Belgian Malinois puppy, matching Plaintiff’s description of Mallory, was purchased by Plaintiff for $1,000 on September 17, 2025.  This also matches the allegations in Plaintiff’s complaint.  (Compl. ¶ 15.)  Although Plaintiff provides the 24Petwatch microchip registry transfer form identifying a dog named Decker, this does not demonstrate that Plaintiff was Decker’s original owner.  Additionally, the inclusion of the photos and information from the GoFundMe fundraisers as to Mallory do not assist the court with determining whether the dog in these photos is the dog that was purchased by Plaintiff on September 17, 2025.  If Plaintiff had provided the original 24Petwatch microchip registration and her own photos of Decker and Mallory, the court would be better positioned to determine whether the dogs are the same. 

 

Attached to the Declaration of Elizabeth Petito-Martinez is photographs taken by Petito-Martinez of Plaintiff with her dogs at the Freedom Center Church in Chatsworth, CA on October 25, 2025.  (Declaration of Elizabeth Petito-Martinez, Exh. A.)  Ms. Petito-Martinez volunteers weekly at the Freedom Center and knows Plaintiff through her regular attendance at church events and regularly observed Plaintiff with her dogs at weekly Saturday gatherings.  (Id. ¶ 4.)  Ms. Petito-Martinez presents photos she took of Plaintiff and her dogs at the Freedom Center on October 25, 2025, the Saturday before Plaintiff’s medical emergency.  (Id. ¶¶ 6-8.)  However, the document containing the photographs appears to be black and white photocopies of color photos which the court cannot properly view or decipher.  Without clear photos, the court cannot determine whether Ms. Petito-Martinez’s photos include the same dog that is shown in the GoFundMe photographs provided by the Plaintiff.

 

Lamar Preston Hall claims he has known Plaintiff for almost two years and was her immediate neighbor for more than a year.  (Declaration of Lamar Preston Hall ¶ 3.)  Mr. Hall observed Plaintiff with her dogs, mainly Decker, on many occasions and never observed anything that caused him to be concerned about the dogs’ safety, care or well-being.  (Id. ¶ 5.)  Mr. Hall claims he knows defendant Pablo Pelaez and saw him bring items to Plaintiff for Decker on several occasions.  (Id. ¶¶ 9, 12.)  On October 29, 2025, after Plaintiff had returned home from the hospital, Mr. Hall overheard Plaintiff speak with defendant Pelaez about letting the dogs stay with James De Jesus, that the dogs were gone, that her wallet and other valuables were gone, but that she did not believe De Jesus stole the items.  (Id. ¶¶ 19-20.)  According to Mr. Hall, Pelaez became visibility angry, yelled at Plaintiff, and told Plaintiff he was done with her, that this was her last chance, and that he was going to find the dogs and she would never see them again.  (Id. ¶ 21.)  Pelaez then drove away.  (Id. ¶ 26.)  This suggests that at some point Pelaez went looking for the dogs.

 

In his supporting declaration, James De Jesus confirms he took the dogs and Plaintiff’s wallet and valuables with him while running some errands on October 29, 2025.  (Declaration of James De Jesus ¶¶ 8-9.)  As De Jesus was completing his errands at the Trebek Center in Northridge, an individual named “Pablo” pulled up in his car and told De Jesus that Plaintiff was fine and sent him to retrieve the dogs.  (Id. ¶¶ 13-14.)  De Jesus had previously met Pablo at Plaintiff’s home.  Because De Jesus knew Plaintiff trusted Pablo and Pablo made assurances about taking the dogs and Plaintiff’s property back to her, De Jesus gave Pablo the dogs and Plaintiff’s wallet and valuables.  (Id. ¶¶ 16-17.)  A few days later, Plaintiff went to the Trebek Center looking for the dogs and her personal property.  (Id. ¶ 19.)

 

This confirms that defendant Pablo Pelaez took possession of Decker and Mallory at the Trebek Center in Northridge, CA on October 29, 2025.

 

Plaintiff requests writs of possession for Decker and Mallory.  In her supplemental declaration, Plaintiff claims that Decker is presently in defendant Gina Quinones’s possession at her residence: 6809 Auburn Circle, Moorpark, California 93021.  (Suppl. Decl. for Claim and Delivery ¶ 32;  Stevens Decl. ¶ 28.)  Based on an online database and skip-trace searches, Plaintiff believes Mallory is presently located at or associated with defendant Mi Kyoung Chaing at 7413 Cleghorn Canyon Way, Las Vegas, Nevada 89113.  (Suppl. Decl. for Claim and Delivery ¶ 34;  Stevens Decl. ¶ 29.)

 

However, the court cannot determine whether the dogs Plaintiff claims are Decker and Mallory are the dogs which Plaintiff claims are currently in possession of defendants Gina Quinones and Mi Kyoung Chaing.  As mentioned above, the documentary evidence provided by Plaintiff demonstrates she purchased a dog license for Decker, that she attempted to transfer ownership of Decker’s microchip to herself, that she purchased a female Belgian Malinois puppy on September 17, 2025, and that she paid $1,000 for the puppy.  The photos provided by Ms. Petito-Martinez are low in quality and make it difficult for the court to determine whether either of the dogs in those photos match the dog in the GoFundMe photos.  Furthermore, Plaintiff provides no photos of her own that were taken before her medical incident.  Nor has Plaintiff demonstrated through evidence how the dogs were transferred from defendant Pelaez transferred to defendants Gina Quinones and Mi Kyoung Chaing.

 

Finally, Plaintiff does not demonstrate that she served notice of her application on all defendants in this matter.  “Each party shall file with the court and serve upon the other party within the time prescribed by rule any affidavits and points and authorities intended to be relied upon at the hearing.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 512.050;  see also Code Civ. Proc., § 512.040 [requirements for notice of application].)  Failure to direct service to the correct party or their attorney of record therefore renders service defective under the statute.  A court may deny a motion without prejudice on the ground that service is defective and require the party to properly serve and refile the motion before considering the motion on the merits.  (Romero v. Los Angeles Rams (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 562, 564, fn. 1.)  The court has reviewed Plaintiff’s application and supporting papers and finds that she has failed to file proofs of service, indicating the defendants were served with her writ of possession application.

 

Therefore, the court denies Plaintiff’s application without prejudice.

 

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

Plaintiff’s application for a writ of possession is denied without prejudice.
