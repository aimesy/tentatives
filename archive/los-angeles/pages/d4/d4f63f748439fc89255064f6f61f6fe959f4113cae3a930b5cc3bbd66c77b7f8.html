Case Number: 25STCV12569    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 8 – Jessica Lange v. Ronen Zargarof, et al.

Defendant Abraham Michael Pessar’s Motions for Orders Deeming Requests for Admission Admitted and Compelling Responses to Form Interrogatories, Special Interrogatories, and Requests for Production

Defendant Abraham Michael Pessar moves for an order deeming that Plaintiff Jessica Lange has admitted the truth of the matters specified in Defendant’s requests for admission. Defendant also moves for orders compelling Plaintiff to respond to form interrogatories, special interrogatories, and requests for production.

As an initial matter, the Court notes that Defendant’s two motions to compel initial responses concern three different sets of discovery. Specifically, the motion to compel responses to form interrogatories also seeks to compel responses to special interrogatories. This constitutes an improper omnibus motion. Defendant is advised to file all motions separately and pay the required filing fee for each in the future. Defendant is ordered to pay the clerk $60 forthwith.

A party must respond to written discovery “[w]ithin 30 days after service.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.260(a) [interrogatories], 2031.260(a) [requests for production], 2033.250(a) [requests for admission].) When the responding party fails to serve a timely response to interrogatories or requests for production, the requesting party may move for an order compelling responses. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290(b) [interrogatories], 2031.300(b) [requests for production].) Failing to serve a timely response results in waiving any objections. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290(a), 2031.300(a).) When a party fails to serve a timely response to requests for admission, “[t]he requesting party may move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the requests be deemed admitted, as well as for a monetary sanction.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(b).) 

Plaintiff failed to serve timely responses to Defendant’s discovery requests. Defendant served his requests for admission, form interrogatories, special interrogatories, and requests for production on Plaintiff on February 18, 2026. (Bonakdar Decls., ¶ 4.) As of the date these motions were filed, Plaintiff had still not responded. (Id., ¶ 6.) However, Plaintiff’s counsel attests that he served discovery responses after these motions were filed, on June 25, 2026. (See Goodman Decls., at p. 2.)

First, Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s discovery motions are defective because they do not include a meet and confer declaration. This is not required. Meet and confer is not a prerequisite to file a motion to compel initial discovery responses.

Second, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s responses are not properly verified and, therefore, “are tantamount to no responses at all.” (Appleton v. Superior Court (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 632, 636.) The Court has reviewed the verification page in dispute and does not find that Plaintiff checked conflicting boxes. The responses are verified. Therefore, an order compelling initial responses to form interrogatories, special interrogatories, and requests for production is unnecessary.

Third, Defendant argues that the responses received are not code-compliant. The Court cannot address this in a motion to compel initial responses. If Defendant seeks further responses from Plaintiff, he must file a separate motion with an accompanying separate statement after meet and confer efforts have been exhausted.

Fourth, because neither Plaintiff nor Defendant attached a copy of Plaintiff’s discovery responses to requests for admission, the Court cannot find that the responses are in “substantial compliance” as to potentially avoid an order deeming requests for admission admitted. (See CCP § 2033.280(a)(2).) The Court also does not find that the Plaintiff’s failure to timely respond was a result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (See id., subd. (a)(1).) In opposition, Plaintiff argues that she was entitled to take Defendant’s deposition before responding to written discovery. However, Defense counsel never agreed to this arrangement. (See Goodman Decls., Ex. 4.) Discovery responses are due within 30 days of service, regardless of whether a deposition follows the 30-day timeframe, as it often does.

Defendant is therefore entitled to an order deeming admitted the matters specified in his requests for admission.

Sanctions

Defendant also requests monetary sanctions against Plaintiff. Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.300(c) provides, “[T]he court shall impose a monetary sanction … against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (See also Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290(c) [interrogatories].) Plaintiff failed to provide any responses by the deadline. There is no evidence that Plaintiff acted with substantial justification.

The Court finds sanctions are appropriate. Defendant did not, however, reasonably incur $3,030 in expenses in preparing these three motions. (Goodman Decls., ¶ 15.) They are almost identical to each other. The Court exercises its discretion to reduce Defense counsel’s hours incurred drafting these motions by one-third. [($2,850/3) * 2] = $1,900. The Court will award $180 in filing fees. Thus, Plaintiff reasonably incurred a total of $2,080 in total fees and costs.

Disposition

Defendant Abraham Michael Pessar’s motions to compel responses to form interrogatories, special interrogatories, and request for production are granted in part as to sanctions only.

Defendant Abraham Michael Pessar’s motion to deem matters admitted is granted. Defendant’s request for admissions is hereby deemed admitted.

Plaintiff Jessica Lange and her counsel of record are ordered to pay Defendant Abraham Michael Pessar $2,080 in sanctions within 21 days.

Defendant Abraham Michael Pessar is ordered to pay $60 to the clerk of the court as unpaid filing fees.


Case Number: 26STCV05222    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 6 - Panshi LLC v. The Madison Collection LLC

Defendant The Madison Collection LLC’s Motion to Set Aside Entry of Default

          Defendant The Madison Collection LLC moves to set aside a default that was entered against it on March 30, 2026. Plaintiff did not file an opposition.

Code of Civil Procedure section 473(b) provides, “The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” “Because the law favors disposing of cases on their merits, ‘any doubts in applying section 473 must be resolved in favor of the party seeking relief from default.’ ” (Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 980.)                  

Defense counsel shows the default resulted from mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Defense counsel states that, despite being retained by Defendant on March 4, 2026, he was unable to represent them because Defendant’s manager’s authority was being challenged by Plaintiff. (Lo Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) Plaintiff told Defense counsel that a special meeting of members was called, and the members had voted to terminate Defense counsel’s representation. (Id., ¶ 5.) While Defendant was seeking clarification on this issue, Plaintiff opportunistically entered this default against it. (Id., ¶¶ 6-7.) Vacating Defendant’s default serves the interests of justice.

Plaintiff will suffer no prejudice from vacating Defendant’s default. Plaintiff filed this action in February 2026. No default judgment has been entered against Defendant. The case is still young. 

Disposition

Defendant The Madison Collection LLC’s motion to set aside entry of default is granted. The Court hereby vacates Defendant The Madison Collection LLC’s default entered on March 30, 2026.  Defendant shall file its proposed answer (attached as Exhibit A to the declaration of Kelvin J. Lo) forthwith.


Case Number: 26STCV06749    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 9 - Philip Sanchez v. City National Bank, et al.

Defendants City National Bank, Royal Bank of Canada, and Paul Woodmansee’s Motion to Compel Arbitration

Defendants City National Bank, Royal Bank of Canada, and Paul Woodmansee (collectively “Defendants”) move to compel arbitration of this action brought against them by Plaintiff Philip Sanchez.

Request for Judicial Notice

Defendants request the Court take judicial notice of nine trial court rulings on other motions to compel arbitration. These documents are not relevant or necessary to the Court’s analysis and have no precedential value.

          Defendants’ request for judicial notice is denied.

Evidentiary Objections

          Plaintiff makes 19 objections to Defendants’ evidence. Objection nos. 17-18 are sustained. The remaining objections are overruled.

          Defendants make several evidentiary objections to the evidence submitted by Plaintiff. The objections are not numbered consecutively. As such, the objections are overruled.

Existence of Agreement

The party moving to compel arbitration must establish the existence of a written arbitration agreement between the parties. (CCP § 1281.2.) It can meet the “initial burden to show an agreement to arbitrate by attaching a copy of the arbitration agreement purportedly bearing the opposing party’s signature.” (Espejo v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1060.) “At this step, a movant need not ‘follow the normal procedures of document authentication’ and need only ‘allege the existence of an agreement and support the allegation as provided in’ ” California Rules of Court, rule 3.1330. (Iyere v. Wise Auto Group (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 755.)

Here, Defendants meet their initial burden by attaching a copy of the arbitration agreement. (Gutierrez, Jr., Decl., Ex. F.) While the agreement itself is not signed, the arbitration agreement was clearly identified on an acknowledgement page in a Code of Conduct training module. That acknowledgment states:

Read and complete the acknowledgment regarding (1) the RBC Code of Conduct; (2) the CNB Colleague Handbook; and (3) the Arbitration Agreement (Mutual Agreement for Arbitration of Disputes) below.

Agreement to Arbitrate Disputes. I hereby reaffirm that I have read and agree to CNB’s Mutual Agreement for Arbitration of Disputes and I will resolve any disputes concerning my employment pursuant to its terms. I understand that if I have questions about the Mutual Agreement for Arbitration of Disputes I should reach out to my Human Resources Business Partner.

…

Selecting “I Acknowledge” below constitutes my electronic signature, acknowledgment, and agreement to all of the above.

(Gutierrez, Jr., Decl., Ex. C.)

          Defendants provide evidence that Plaintiff completed this training five different times on September 25, 2020, October 8, 2021, June 21, 2022, August 18, 2023, and October 9, 2024. (Id., ¶ 6, Ex. A.) Defendant CNB’s Compliance Officer-Training, Alfredo Gutierrez, Jr. attests that “To complete the course, colleagues must electronically complete the acknowledgment by clicking an on screen ‘radio’ button.” (Id., ¶ 7.) Plaintiff himself admits that he completed these Code of Conduct trainings throughout his employment. (Sanchez Decl., ¶¶ 6-8.) Defendants meet their initial burden.

Plaintiff raises two primary arguments as to why no valid agreement was formed.

First, Plaintiff argues that his mere “click” on the “Acknowledge” button did not manifest assent to the agreement because the on-screen text was tied to a different purpose. Plaintiff also argues that no contract was formed because the one “click” on the “Acknowledge” button applied to four different agreements included on the same page. Plaintiff cites Herzog v. Superior Court (2024) 101 Cal.App.5th 1280 (Herzog), arguing that the case is dispositive of the issues presented here; Plaintiff contends that the instant record shows even less assent to contractual terms as compared to the assent at issue in Herzog.

The Court disagrees and finds Herzog distinguishable from the instant matter. In Herzog, a plaintiff clicked on a box labeled “I agree to Terms of Use” at the end of a “clickwrap” agreement when installing a mobile application that connected to a medical device. (Herzog, supra, 101 Cal.App.5th at p. 1285) The Terms of Use contained a hyperlink to a page with various contractual terms, including an arbitration agreement. (Id., at p. 1290.) The appellate court found that plaintiff did not manifest assent to arbitrate claims arising from the use of the medical device because the Terms of Use explicitly told the user that clicking the “I agree” box was an agreement to collect, use, and share the user’s information. (Id., at p. 1298-1299.) Further, the acknowledgment screen did not reference an arbitration agreement at all—instead, the user would need to click the Term of Use hyperlink and sift through 22 pages of terms to find it. (Id., at p. 1301.) Given these facts, the appellate court reasoned that there was no reasonably conspicuous notice of the arbitration agreement, and no unambiguous manifestation of assent. (Id., at p. 1305)

The record before the Court is markedly different. Here, Plaintiff was presented an acknowledgment screen that clearly indicated the existence of the arbitration agreement, directed the employee to read it, and made the employee confirm “that [they] have read and agree to CNB’s Mutual Agreement for Arbitration of Disputes and [they] will resolve any disputes concerning [their] employment pursuant to its terms.” The page contained a clear hyperlink titled “Mutual Agreement for Arbitration of Disputes” that led to a separate 5-page arbitration agreement. These circumstances show reasonably conspicuous notice. A reasonably prudent user, after reading this text, would be put on notice of the arbitration agreement. In addition, the Acknowledgment button was located beneath an unambiguous statement: “Selecting ‘I Acknowledge’ below constitutes my electronic signature, acknowledgment, and agreement to all of the above.” Plaintiff manifested assent to the agreement.

Second, Plaintiff argues that Defendants cannot authenticate Plaintiff’s electronic act. Plaintiff contends that Defendants’ proof—the Gutierrez declaration and the completion log—does not establish that Plaintiff himself clicked the button. The Court disagrees and finds that Defendants properly authenticated the agreement. “[A]n electronic signature is attributable to a person if it is the act of the person.” (Fabian v. Renovate America, Inc. (2019) 42 Cal.App.5th 1062, 1067.) “ ‘[T]he burden of authenticating an electronic signature is not great.’ ” (Ibid.) “The party seeking authentication may carry its burden ‘in any manner,’ including by presenting evidence of the contents of the contract in question and the circumstances surrounding the contract’s execution.” (Id. at p. 1068.) A defendant may establish the authenticity of an electronic signature “by explaining the process used to verify the signature” (ibid.) or showing the “security precautions regarding transmission and use of an applicant’s unique username and password, as well as the steps an applicant would have to take to place his or her name on the signature line.” (Espejo v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1062.)

Gutierrez attests in detail to his personal knowledge of “CNB’s policies and procedures with regard to scheduling of required colleague computer-based training, managing the content thereof, tracking of completion, and maintenance of business records documenting completion of all required compliance training.” (Gutierrez, Jr., Decl., ¶ 2.) He states he “personally downloaded [the CNB training history database] from CNB’s Learning Management System.” (Id., ¶ 6.) He further testifies that Plaintiff would need to access and complete the training through his unique identification and password, which is kept confidential and ensures secure access. (Id., ¶ 5.) This is sufficient to authenticate the agreement. The Court also notes that training records, which show Plaintiff’s completion of the training and acknowledgment form on five separate occasions, weigh in favor of a finding that the electronic act was the act of Plaintiff.

          Because the Court finds that Plaintiff’s electronic act constitutes valid assent to the arbitration agreement, the Court need not and does not analyze whether Plaintiff’s continued employment with CNB constitutes implied assent.

Unconscionability

          Plaintiff argues the agreement is unconscionable. “Both procedural and substantive unconscionability must be shown for the defense to be established, but ‘they need not be present in the same degree.’ ” (OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho (2019) 8 Cal.5th 111, 125 (OTO).) “The burden of proving unconscionability rests upon the party asserting it.” (Id. at p. 126.)

Plaintiff shows no substantive unconscionability. “Substantive unconscionability examines the fairness of a contract’s terms.” (OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho (2019) 8 Cal.5th 111, 129.) “ ‘[T]he unconscionability doctrine is concerned not with “a simple old-fashioned bad bargain” [citation], but with terms that are “unreasonably favorable to the more powerful party.” ’ ” (Id. at p. 130.)

Plaintiff argues that Defendant CNB’s misconduct in a prior arbitration with a different employee shows that the arbitration agreement itself is unconscionable. To support this “as-applied” argument, Plaintiff’s counsel describes at length CNB’s participation in a past arbitration in which he represented a different client, Maria Noone. Plaintiff’s counsel attaches upwards of 30 exhibits of various pleadings, orders, and communications from the Noone matter to demonstrate unconscionability in the proceedings. This argument is unavailing. The Court’s inquiry into substantive unconscionability examines the agreement’s terms, not Defendant’s compliance, or noncompliance, with said terms.

          Plaintiff also vaguely references a carve-out provision that allows an employer to bring injunctive claims, a confidentiality provision, and a provision requiring an employee to pay initiation costs as unconscionable features of the agreement.

          First, the Court cannot find any specific confidentiality provision in the arbitration agreement, nor does Plaintiff point to one. As such, the Court will not address it here.

Second, the injunctive relief provision is not unconscionable. It states: “Claims excluded are any claims by either party (meaning me or CNB) for temporary restraining orders and/or preliminary injunctions where such temporary equitable relief would otherwise be authorized by law.” (Gutierrez, Jr., Decl., Ex. F, at p. 1.) The agreement explicitly states that it does not cover “any claims by either party (meaning me or CNB).” (Ibid., emphasis added.) Allowing both parties to seek provisional injunctive relief in court is not substantively unconscionable. It is mutual.

Lastly, the cost provision included in the agreement is not unconscionable. “[W]hen an employer imposes mandatory arbitration as a condition of employment, the arbitration agreement or arbitration process cannot generally require the employee to bear any type of expense that the employee would not be required to bear if he or she were free to bring the action in court.” (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 110-111.) Plaintiff would incur, and did incur, a filing fee by bringing this action in state court. Under Armendariz, requiring this filing fee to initiate arbitration is proper.

Because Plaintiff does not meet his burden in showing the agreement is substantively unconscionable, the Court need not and does not address whether the agreement is procedurally unconscionable. (See Crippen v. Central Valley RV Outlet (2004) 124 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1167 [finding no procedural unconscionability and therefore not addressing substantive unconscionability].)

Disposition

Defendants City National Bank, Royal Bank of Canada, and Paul Woodmansee’s motion to compel arbitration is granted. 

Plaintiff is ordered to arbitrate this action against Defendants. The Court hereby stays the entire action pending resolution of the arbitration proceeding.

Defendants City National Bank, Royal Bank of Canada, and Paul Woodmansee’s motion for stay of proceedings set for hearing on August 7, 2026 is hereby advanced to this date and vacated.

Plaintiff Philip Sanchez’s four motions to compel discovery responses set for hearing on August 20, 2026, September 28, 2026, and September 29, 2026 are hereby advanced to this date and vacated.


Case Number: BC573118    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 7 - Mehrdad Setareh v. Kamran Setareh

Plaintiff Mehrdad Setareh and 3200 Imperial Highway Corporation’s Motion to Appoint Expert under Evidence Code Section 730

Judgment debtors Mehrdad Setareh and 3200 Imperial Highway Corporation (“Mehrdad”) move to appoint an expert under Evidence Code Section 730. Mehrdad seeks the appointment of a CPA who can determine whether his judgment against judgment creditor Kamran Setareh (“Kamran”) has been satisfied.

Evidence Code section 730 provides:

When it appears to the court, at any time before or during the trial of an action, that expert evidence is or may be required by the court or by any party to the action, the court on its own motion or on motion of any party may appoint one or more experts to investigate, to render a report as may be ordered by the court, and to testify as an expert at the trial of the action relative to the fact or matter as to which the expert evidence is or may be required.

In opposition, Kamran argues that this section only applies to appointment of experts before or during trial. Because Mehrdad and Kamran are in the post-judgment enforcement stage, this statute is inapplicable. The Court agrees. It cannot appoint an expert under this section. The motion is denied.

The Court has reviewed the briefing submitted by both parties and recognizes that several disputes remain outstanding, such as (1) when the $124,950 credit became effective; and (2) what Paragraph 7 of the judgment means.

The first issue was already briefed in full in the last motion brought by the parties on March 17, 2026. The Court expressed its decision on the issue in a tentative ruling[1], which stated:

Kamran also argues that Mehrdad improperly backdates a $124,950 settlement credit between the parties. The settlement agreement provides, “Upon clearance of the $4,000 check, Kamran shall acknowledge a $124,950 credit against the Judgment.” (Frandsen Decl., Att. E, ¶ 3.)

Kamran argues that this credit was not to be retroactively applied to dates in 2021; instead, the credit was to be effective towards the judgment on the date of the receipt of the settlement funds. Mehrdad argues the opposite: the settlement credit accounts for two cashier checks issued for $80,000 and $44,950 in 2021 and therefore should be applied to the judgment total on those specific dates.

While the Court certainly understands Mehrdad’s logic, the settlement agreement does not explicitly reiterate this same logic. Instead, it merely provides that the credit shall be acknowledged upon clearance of the check. There is no detail clarifying that the credit will be backdated. Given the plain terms of the agreement, the $124,950 credit is properly applied upon clearance of the check. There are no contradictory terms contained in the settlement agreement that provide otherwise. Mehrdad’s calculations are also erroneous for this reason.

The second issue identified has not yet been resolved by the Court. Given the circumstances, the Court will allow both parties to submit briefing on this discrete issue—i.e., whether payments toward the judgment received from 3200 Imperial Highway Corp. should be discounted by 35% because Kamran owns 35% of the corporation. An answer to this question requires interpretation of paragraph 7 of the judgment, which states “To the extent the Award is against IHC, any proceeds from IHC used to satisfy the Award or subsequent judgment amount owed to Kamran shall not be assessed or deducted from Kamran’s interest in IHC or any amounts owed to him for dividends or distributions of income from IHC.” 

The Court is optimistic that after it makes a ruling on this outstanding dispute, the parties will stipulate to an accounting and share costs equally. If the parties do not stipulate, the Court may appoint a referee on its own motion under CCP § 639(a)(2).

Disposition

          Judgment debtors Mehrdad Setareh and 3200 Imperial Highway Corporation’s motion to appoint an expert is denied.


[1] The Court recognizes that it did not formally adopt its tentative ruling on Mehrdad’s motion to compel acknowledgment of judgment. Instead, Mehrdad asked to continue the matter and later took it off calendar. The Court therefore adopts the same reasoning to respond to the issue set forth in the papers here, and incorporates it as part of this order.
