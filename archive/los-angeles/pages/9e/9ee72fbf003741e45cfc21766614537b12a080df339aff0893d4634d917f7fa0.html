Andrew Nickerson v. Carrier X, LLC, et al.

 

Tuesday, September 22, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 25STCV28822

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Plaintiff Andrew Nickerson’s Motion for Sanctions against Defendants Carrier X LLC, Free Conferencing Corporation, HD Carrier, LLC, Phoundry LLC, Wide Voice LLC, and David Erickson and their Counsel ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for retaliation and wrongful termination. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff Andrew Nickerson (“Plaintiff”) was the CEO of Defendant Carrier X, LLC, and charged with managing Defendants Carrier X LLC, Free Conferencing Corporation, HD Carrier, LLC, Phoundry LLC, and Wide Voice LLC, which were effectively an integrated business operation owned and managed by Defendant David Erickson. (Complaint ¶¶ 13–18.) Plaintiff was terminated from his position as CEO following repeated clashes with Erickson in which Plaintiff refused to participate in schemes to fraudulently transfer assets among the companies and attempt to circumvent federal sanctions on Russia. (Complaint ¶¶ 19–36.)

Procedural History: Plaintiff field the Complaint on October 2, 2026, alleging six causes of action:

1.      Lab. Code § 1102.5 Retaliation

2.      Breach of Fiduciary Duty

3.      Failure to Pay Wages

4.      Breach of Contract

5.      Wrongful Termination

6.      Accounting

On August 18, 2026, this court denied Defendants’ motion for protective order.

Plaintiff filed the present motion for sanctions on August 26, 2026.

Defendant filed an opposition on September 9, 2026.

Plaintiff filed a reply on September 16, 2026.

Analysis

 

I.                   MOTION FOR SANCTIONS

 

The present motion follows on the heels of this court’s denial of Defendants Carrier X LLC, Free Conferencing Corporation, HD Carrier, LLC, Phoundry LLC, Wide Voice LLC, and David Erickson’s (“Defendants”) motion for protective order on August 18, 2026, which sought to stay the deposition of nonparty Star Marcus and quash the relevant deposition notice. Plaintiff Andrew Nickerson (“Plaintiff”) seeks sanctions against Defendants on the grounds that the motion was not substantially justified under Code of Civil Procedure § 2025.420, which states with regard to motions for a protective order:

 

The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion for a protective order, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.420, subd. (h).)

 

Plaintiff bases the motion principally upon the fact that when the motion was served on August 4, 2026, the deposition it sought to stay had already been completed on July 16, 2026, and thus the motion was moot when brought. (Motion at p. 4.) The court indeed denied the motion on mootness grounds.

 

No sanctions are proper. Plaintiff could have sought sanctions in opposition to the motion for protective order, but did not do so. The request for sanctions now before the court includes not merely amounts incurred in opposing Defendants’ motion for protective order, but also those incurred in bringing the present unnecessary supplement. (Saldana Decl. ¶ 12.) The request for sanctions is thus belated and inflated.

 

The imposition of sanctions here would be unjust under these circumstances. Defendants’ motion for protective order was moot when brought on August 4, 2026. But as Defendants indicate, this was because of administrative difficulties that caused the rejection of an attempted filing on July 15, 2026, the day before the deposition occurred. (Hudson Decl. ¶¶ 2–4.) Plaintiff’s own motion presents ample grounds for its denial, demonstrating that the underlying facts raised questions of whether Plaintiff had made adequate provision for the availability of the other parties on the deposition date in question, whether the defective filing of a motion for protective order stayed the deposition under Code of Civil Procedure § 2025.410, subd. (c), and whether Defendants’ counsel in fact suspended the deposition under Code of Civil Procedure § 2025.470 when he appeared there to repeat his objections. (Motion at pp. 4–6.) Ordering sanctions on these facts would be unjust.

 

The motion is therefore DENIED.

Case Number: 26STCV09723    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: 732


Strategic Funding Source, Inc. v. Mac Print Co, LLC., et al.

 

Tuesday, September 22, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 26STCV09723

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Plaintiff Strategic Funding Source, Inc.’s Motions to Compel Further Responses to Requests for Admission and Form Interrogatories from Defendant Cory McDonald i___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for breach of contract. The Complaint alleges as follows. Defendants Mac Print Co LLC and Cory McDonald agreed to repay $112,500.00 to Plaintiff Strategic Funding Source, Inc. and personally guarantee repayment of the same amount. (Complaint ¶ 6.) Defendants have defaulted on this agreement. (Complaint ¶¶ 7–9.) 

 

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on March 26, 2026, alleging two causes of action:

 

1.      Breach of Contract

2.      Breach of Contract (Guarany)

 

Plaintiff filed the present motions to compel further responses to form interrogatories and requests for admission from Defendant Cory McDonald on August 14, 2026.

 

Defendant filed oppositions on September 8, 2026.

 

Plaintiff filed replies on September 14, 2026.

 

Analysis:

I.        MOTIONS TO COMPEL FURTHER

“Any party may obtain discovery . . . by propounding to any other party to the action written interrogatories to be answered under oath.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.010(a).) If a propounding party is not satisfied with the response served by a responding party, the former may move the court to compel further interrogatory responses.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300; Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 403.)  The propounding party must demonstrate that the responses were incomplete, inadequate or evasive, or that the responding party asserted objections that are either without merit or too general.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300(a)(1)–(3); Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc., supra, 148 Cal.App.4th at 403.)

A motion to compel further responses to requests for admissions may be made on the grounds that an answer is incomplete or evasive, or an objection is without merit. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2033.290, subd. (a)(1)–(2).)

Plaintiff Strategic Funding Source, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) seeks further responses to Form Interrogatories No. 15.1, 17.1, 50.1, and 50.6, and Requests for Admission No. 1–35 from Defendant Cory McDonald (“Defendant”).

As an initial matter, Defendant objects that the motion was not preceded by adequate informal conference under Code of Civil Procedure § 2016.040. (Opposition at pp. 3–4.) That statute requires a good faith attempt to resolve discovery issues “either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2016.040, subd. 9a).) Defendant argues that the motions here were only preceded by a letter. (Opposition at pp. 3–5.)

Plaintiff has shown adequate attempts to engage in meet-and-confer efforts before filing the motion. Plaintiff served a meet-and-confer letter describing problems with Defendant’s responses, and specifically requesting Defendant identify dates of availability for an informal discovery conference. (Gibbs Decl. Exh. C.) The reason no such conference occurred is because Defendant did not respond to the request. (Gibbs Decl. ¶¶ 5–6.)   Plaintiff has shown adequate efforts to “attempt” informal conference under Code of Civil Procedure § 2016.040, subd. (a), and the fault for the failure of these efforts lies with Defendant, not Plaintiff.

Defendant raises other procedural issues with the motion, such as the fact that electronic service was accomplished by service of a link to the documents, rather than transmission of the files themselves (Opposition at pp. 5–6), and a proposed lack of sufficient detail in Plaintiff’s justification for further responses in the separate statements. (Opposition at pp. 8–9.) Defendant does not explain the materiality of the difference between electronic transmission of files and electronic transmission of links to the files, and the defects identified in Plaintiff’s separate statements (to which Defendant provides no responsive separate statement) are not material.

Further responses are required for both sets of discovery at issue. The Form Interrogatories at issue sought facts and evidence supporting Defendant’s denials of the Complaint’s allegations, affirmative defenses, and denials of requests for admissions (Form Interrogatories No. 15.1 and 17.1), to which Defendant responded only with verbatim recitation of his affirmative defenses, identification of Plaintiff’s counsel as the only person who possessed relevant documents, and the mere statement — true, as discussed below — that he had not provided any admission to the requests for admission targeted by Interrogatory No. 17.1. (Separate Statement at pp. 3–7.) 

These responses are deficient. “Each answer in a response to interrogatories shall be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220, subd. (a).) Defendant’s responses are not straightforward. They fail to provide separate responses for each material denial or affirmative defense, do not state facts justifying each beyond their recitation, and evidence no attempt to identify the responsive evidence requested.

Defendant’s responses to Interrogatories No. 50.1 and 50.6 are also deficient. Interrogatory No. 50.1 asked Defendant to identify each person who has the agreements in question, to which Defendant responded only with identification of Plaintiff’s counsel. (Separate Statement at p. 5.) Interrogatory No. 50.6 asked Plaintiff to state whether and why any agreement alleged in the Complaint was ambiguous, to which Defendant responded, not with an explanation of any ambiguity, but another generalized invocation of Civil Code § 1633.5, addressing electronic communications, which is one of his affirmative defenses. (Separate Statement at p. 6.) These responses are non-responsive, and further response is required.

Further response is also required to Requests for Admission No. 1–35. Defendant’s responses here consisted only of objections, noting Plaintiff’s use of a prefatory instruction, and arguing that the requests’ reference to external facts and documents rendered them impermissible under 2033.060. (Separate Statement, passim.) But if the prefatory instruction is objectionable, that would not absolve Defendant of admitting or denying the requests: “If only a part of a request for admission is objectionable, the remainder of the request shall be answered.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2033.230, subd. (a).) And Defendant’s objections that the requests are not complete in themselves under Code of Civil Procedure § 2033.060, subd. (d), because they ask questions concerning the genuineness and legal effect of documents attached as exhibits is meritless. The statutory purpose of requests for admissions is to permit requests to “admit the genuineness of specified documents, or the truth of specified matters of fact, opinion relating to fact, or application of law to fact.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2033.010.) Nothing in the requests here exceeds these bounds.

And as a further response is to be given to the requests for admission, further response is also necessary for Form Interrogatory No. 17.1, which seeks facts and evidence justifying non-admitted responses.

The motions there therefore GRANTED. Further responses shall be served within 20 days of this order.

1.      Sanctions

Statute provides that the court shall impose sanctions upon a party who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further response to interrogatories, requests for production of documents, or requests for admission, absent substantial justification otherwise. (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 2030.300, subd. (d); 2031.310, subd. (h); 2033.290, subd. (d).)

 

Plaintiff seeks a total of $2,695.00 in sanctions against Defendant and his counsel in connection with these two motions, representing seven hours of attorney work at $385 per hour. (Webb Decl. ¶ 7.)

Defendant objects that the notice of motions does not identify the parties persons whom sanctions are sought. (Opposition at pp. 5–6.) This is untrue. The motion identifies “Defendant,” earlier defined specifically as Cory McDonald, and his counsel of record — i.e. John J. Hamilton, the attorney listed on his answer, who signed his discovery responses, and who signs the oppositions to the present motions.

Defendant argues that he has no counsel of record, evidently on the grounds that his answer lists Hamilton only as a “limited scope” attorney pursuant to CRC Rule 3.36. (Opposition at pp. 5–6.) But Hamilton’s identification as Defendant’s counsel on his answer is sufficient to “establish him or her as attorney of record. (Baker v. Boxx (1991) 226 Cal.App.3d 1303, 1310.) That a limited-scope attorney like Hamilton may nonetheless serve as counsel of record is reflected in the very rule pursuant to which he appears. (See CRC Rule 3.36, subd. (c) [“Notwithstanding rule 3.1362, an attorney who has completed the tasks specified in the Notice of Limited Scope Representation (form CIV-150) may use the procedures in this rule to request that he or she be relieved as attorney in cases in which the attorney has appeared before the court as an attorney of record and the client has not signed a Substitution of Attorney--Civil (form MC-050).”].)

Thus sanctions are properly awarded against both Defendant and his attorney of record John J. Hamilton  in the amount of $2,695.00.

Leslie Rubire Ibarra Lopez v. G I Security, Inc., et al.

 

Tuesday, September 22, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 26STCV28842

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Defendants Daniel Perez Jr. and Marcella Nada Perez’s Demurrer and Motion to Strike Portions of the Complaint

 ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for disability discrimination, wrongful termination, and wage-and-hour violations. The Complaint alleges as follows.  Plaintiff Leslie Rubire Ibarra Lopez (“Plaintiff”) worked for Defendant G I Security, Inc. which was owned and managed by Defendants Daniel Perez Jr. and Marcella Nada Perez. (Complaint ¶¶ 5–8, 19.) Plaintiff was terminated from her employment with Defendants after seeking medical time off prescribed by her doctor relating to her pregnancy. (Complaint ¶¶ 26–30.) Defendants also failed to pay required overtime and provide meal and rest breaks. (Complaint ¶¶ 20–25.)

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on October 2, 2025, alleging 13 causes of action:

1.      Pregnancy Discrimination

2.      Disability Discrimination

3.      Failure to Accommodate

4.      Interactive Process Violation

5.      Failure to Provide Pregnancy Leave

6.      FEHA Retaliation

7.      Failure to Prevent FEHA Violations

8.      Wrongful Termination

9.      Lab. Code §§ 510, 1194

10.  Lab. Code §§ 226.7, 512

11.  Lab. Code §§ 226

12.  Lab. Code §§ 201–203

13.  Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200

Defendants Daniel Perez and Marcella Nada Perez filed the present demurrer and motion to strike on January 5, 2026.

Plaintiff filed oppositions on August 18, 2026.

Defendant filed replies on September 15, 2026.

Analysis:

I.                   DEMURRER

A demurrer should be sustained only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (Code Civ. Pro., §§ 430.30, et seq.) In particular, as is relevant here, a court should sustain a demurrer if a complaint does not allege facts that are legally sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (See id. § 430.10, subd. (e).) As the Supreme Court held in Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311: “We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law. . . . Further, we give the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Id. at p. 318; see also Hahn. v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747 [“A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. [Citation.]”)

 

“In determining whether the complaint is sufficient as against the demurrer … if on consideration of all the facts stated it appears the plaintiff is entitled to any relief at the hands of the court against the defendants the complaint will be held good although the facts may not be clearly stated.”  (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 639.)

 

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of Cal., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Such demurrers “are disfavored, and are granted only if the pleading is so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (Mahan v. Charles W. Chan Insurance Agency, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 841, 848.)

 

A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment. (Schifando v. City of Los Angeles, supra, 31 Cal.4th at p. 1081.) The demurrer also may be sustained without leave to amend where the nature of the defects and previous unsuccessful attempts to plead render it probable plaintiff cannot state a cause of action. (Krawitz v. Rusch (1989) 209 Cal.App.3d 957, 967.)

 

Defendants Daniel Perez Jr. and Marcella Nada Perez (“Individual Defendants”) demurrer to the causes of action asserted by Plaintiff Leslie Rubire Ibarra Lopez (“Plaintiff”) on the grounds that no basis is alleged to impose individual liability upon them through the alter ego, agency, and joint employer theories. (Demurrer at pp. 6–12.)

 

Defendants scarcely address the principal means by which they are included in this lawsuit: Labor Code § 558.1. That statute provides:

 

(a) Any employer or other person acting on behalf of an employer, who violates, or causes to be violated, any provision regulating minimum wages or hours and days of work in any order of the Industrial Welfare Commission, or violates, or causes to be violated, Sections 203, 226, 226.7, 1193.6, 1194, or 2802, may be held liable as the employer for such violation.

 

(b) For purposes of this section, the term “other person acting on behalf of an employer” is limited to a natural person who is an owner, director, officer, or managing agent of the employer, and the term “managing agent” has the same meaning as in subdivision (b) of Section 3294 of the Civil Code.

 

(Lab. Code § 558.1, subd. (a), (b).)

 

The moving parties are included as defendants only  as to the ninth through thirteenth causes of action under the Labor Code and a derivative Unfair Competition claim, and in each case are alleged to be liable through Labor Code § 558.1. (Complaint ¶¶ 104, 116, 128, 135, 142.) Individual Defendants are alleged to be owners or managing agents of Defendant G I Security, Inc., Plaintiff’s employer, and to have caused the relevant violations at issue, such that liability for the violations alleged may be imputed to them. (Complaint ¶¶ 7–8, 17–18.) Plaintiff thus alleges adequate basis for liability against Individual Defendants.

 

The demurrer is accordingly OVERRULED.

 

II.                MOTION TO STRIKE

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof. (Code Civ. Proc., § 435(b)(1)). The notice of motion to strike a portion of a pleading shall quote in full the portions sought to be stricken except where the motion is to strike an entire paragraph, cause of action, count or defense. (California Rules of Court Rule 3.1322.)

 

The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or form any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437(a)). The court then may strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading and strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend. (Perlman v. Municipal Court (1979) 99 Cal.App.3d 568, 575.)

 

Individual Defendants move to strike the prayer for punitive damages against them on the grounds that insufficient facts are alleged to support the existence of malice or oppression. (Motion at pp. 6-8.)

 

Punitive damages are allowed in non-contract cases when a defendant is guilty of “oppression, fraud, or malice . . . .” (Civ. Code § 3294, subd. (a).) The terms are defined as:

 

  1. “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.
  2. “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights.
  3. “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.

 

(Civ. Code § 3294, subd. (c).)

 

Something more than the mere commission of a tort is always required for punitive damages. (Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890, 894.) Proof of negligence, gross negligence, or recklessness is insufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages. (Dawes v. Sup.Ct. (Mardian) (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 82, 88–89.) Punitive damages may be recovered in an action for negligence or other nonintentional torts if the plaintiff pleads and proves that the defendant acted with the state of mind described as “conscious disregard” of the potential dangers to others. (Pfeifer v. John Crane, Inc. (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 1270, 1299.) When malice is based on a defendant’s conscious disregard of Plaintiff’s rights, the conduct must be both despicable and willful. (College Hospital v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 794, 713 (“College Hospital”).)

 

The motion is unpersuasive. First, although the motion is brought solely by Individual Defendants, no prayer for punitive damages is made in any claim alleged against them, but rather only in the first through eighth causes of action against G I Security. Second, the prayer for punitive damages that is alleged is sufficiently stated, as it arises from an alleged intentional and retaliatory discharge of Plaintiff’s employment based on her disability, pregnancy, and request for medical leave. “When the plaintiff alleges an intentional wrong, a prayer for exemplary damage may be supported by pleading that the wrong was committed willfully or with a design to injure.” (G. D. Searle & Co. v. Superior Court (1975) 49 Cal.App.3d 22, 29.) Plaintiff makes such allegations here. (Complaint ¶¶ 33–34.)

 

The motion to strike is therefore DENIED.
