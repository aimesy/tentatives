RULING

 

            The Court denies the Motion for Summary Judgment.

 

BACKGROUND

           

            On September 4, 2024, Plaintiff Reva Mendez filed the Complaint against Defendants Walmart and Sam’s Club alleging causes of action for premises liability and negligence.

 

            On April 9, 2026, Defendant Sam’s Club filed the Motion for Summary Judgment.

 

            On August 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Opposition.

 

            On August 18, 2026, Defendant filed the Reply.

 

EVIDENTARY OBJECTIONS

 

            Plaintiff’s Objections to Defendant’s Evidence:

 

            Overruled: 1-2

 

            Sustained:

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437 subdivision (c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67, citing FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal. App. 3d 367, 381-382.)  

 

As to each cause of action, as framed by the complaint, the defendant moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to show “that one or more elements of the cause of action ... cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(p)(2); see also Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)  

 

“Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 162.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Id. at 163.)  

 

DISCUSSION

 

Plaintiff Reva Mendez alleges she slipped and fell on a blueberry on the floor while shopping at Defendant’s store located at 2601 Skypark Dr., Torrance, CA 90505.

 

Causes of action 1 and 2 – Negligence and Premises Liability.

 

The elements of a cause of action for premises liability are the same as those for negligence: duty, breach, causation, and damages. (Castellon v. U.S. Bancorp¿(2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 994, 998.)  Those who own, possess, or control property generally have a duty to exercise ordinary care in managing the property¿to¿avoid exposing others to an unreasonable risk of harm. (Annocki¿v. Peterson Enterprises, LLC¿(2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 32, 37.)  The existence and scope of duty are legal questions for the court. (Id. at p. 36.)  If there is a condition that poses a danger to customers and others on the premises, the property owner is “under a duty to exercise ordinary care either to make the condition reasonably safe for their use or to give a warning adequate to enable them to avoid the harm.” (Bridgman v. Safeway Stores, Inc.¿(1960) 53 Cal.2d 443, 446.)

 

Defendant argues that it cannot be held liable to Plaintiff because it had no notice of the alleged dangerous condition. Defendant reasons that a premises owner is liable only for those dangers of which it had actual or constructive notice, and it contends that the undisputed evidence establishes that it had neither.

 

As to actual notice, Defendant relies on both video and testimonial evidence to argue that no employee of Defendant was aware of the alleged dangerous condition before Plaintiff fell. Defendant states that an employee walked through the subject area at 11:13 a.m., the blueberry spill occurred at 11:28 a.m., roughly fifteen minutes later, and Plaintiff fell at 11:39 a.m., about eleven minutes after the spill. Defendant emphasizes that no employee passed through the area between the time the spill occurred and the time Plaintiff fell, and that Defendant was not otherwise alerted to the spill during that window.

 

As to constructive notice, Defendant acknowledges that a plaintiff may establish notice by showing that the condition existed long enough that the owner should have discovered it through the exercise of ordinary care, but it argues that no such showing can be made here. (Ortega v. Kmart (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1210.) Defendant argues that the Court has found a lack of constructive notice where the area had been inspected eight to nine minutes before the fall. (Moore v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. (2003) 111 Cal.App.4th 472.) Because the spill existed for only about eleven minutes before Plaintiff fell, and an employee had passed through fifteen minutes before it formed, Defendant contends that no constructive notice existed as a matter of law. (Gonzalez v. Interstate Cleaning Corp. (2024) 106 Cal.App.5th 1026.)

 

Defendant further argues that the surveillance video independently demonstrates the absence of notice. Reiterating that a premises owner is liable only for dangers of which it had actual or constructive notice, Defendant maintains that neither form of notice appears in the video. (Perbost v. San Marino Hall-School for Girls (1948) 88 Cal.App.2d 796, 803-804.) Defendant notes that when witness testimony is inconsistent with the video, the inconsistency is not treated as a disputed fact, and the video controls. (Swigart v. Bruno (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 529, 534, fn. 4.) Applying these authorities, Defendant argues that Plaintiff cannot create a triable issue by offering testimonial evidence that the surveillance video does not reflect. Defendant therefore concludes that, because it had neither actual nor constructive notice and the video confirms that absence of notice, Plaintiff cannot prevail on her claim.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the motion must be denied because Defendant fails to carry its initial burden of production. A moving defendant must show that the plaintiff has not established, and cannot reasonably expect to establish, a prima facie case on one or more elements, and where the defendant's own evidence does not support judgment, the court must deny the motion without reaching the opposition.

 

On actual notice, Plaintiff argues that the surveillance video shows a person entering the produce section wearing an apron, who Defendant’s PMK identified as a bakery employee. Plaintiff argues this creates a triable issue over whether Plaintiff had actual knowledge of the spill.

 

On constructive notice, Plaintiff argues that a failure to inspect within a reasonable time supports an inference that the condition existed long enough to be discovered, that the duty is heightened in a self-service store, and that no fixed time limit applies. Plaintiff points to evidence showing that Defendant assigned no inspector, set no inspection interval, kept no inspection or cleaning logs, and left the task to whichever associate happened to walk by.  The only interval Defendant establishes is roughly twenty-six minutes between an unidentified employee's walk-through and the fall, and whether that satisfies ordinary care is a question for the jury. Furthermore, Plaintiff's safety expert opines that an adequate program requires accountability, frequency, and verification, none of which Defendant had.

 

Constructive notice

 

“The plaintiff need not show actual knowledge where evidence suggests that the dangerous condition was present for a sufficient period of time to charge the owner with constructive knowledge of its existence. Knowledge may be shown by circumstantial evidence which is nothing more than one or more inferences which may be said to arise reasonably from a series of proven facts.” (Ortega, supra, 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1206-1207.)

 

Here, Defendant’s own characterization of the fall states that the spill was on the ground for 11 minutes prior to Plaintiff’s fall. (UMF 3.) Whether a dangerous condition existed long enough to create constructive notice is a question of fact. (Ortega, supra, 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1213.) In Girvetz v. Boys' Market (1949) 91 Cal.App.2d 827, 831, Defendants' own authority, the Court opined that a dangerous condition present “for as long as ten or fifteen minutes would in all probability be held to justify submitting the cause to the jury,” depending on the circumstances. (Id. at 831.) Thus, an eleven-minute duration creates a question of fact regarding constructive notice best left to a jury. Consequently, the Court finds that Defendant has not met its burden of showing that it lacked notice of the alleged dangerous condition.

 

Therefore, the Court denies the Motion for Summary Judgment.

DAWN LEWIS, an individual, NEW DAWN REAL ESTATE, a company of unknown form; REAL BROKERAGE LOS ANGELES, a California corporation; REAL BROKERAGE, LLC, a limited liability company of unknown origin; and DOES 1 through 30, inclusive,

Moving Parties:          Defendant Real Brokerage Technologies

 

Responding Party:      Plaintiff Flipside LLC

 

HEARING: Demurrer

 

            The Court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers.

 

RULING

 

            The Court sustains the Demurrer as to the first and third causes of action with leave to amend.

 

            The Court overrules the Demurrer as to the second cause of action.

           

BACKGROUND

 

            On April 18, 2025, Plaintiff Fliptside LLC filed the Complaint against Defendants Dawn Lewis, New Dawn Real Estate, Real Brokerage Los Angeles, and Real Brokerage LLC, alleging causes of action for breach of contract, breach of fiduciary duty, and negligence.

 

            On April 1, 2026, Defendant Real Brokerage Technologies filed the Demurrer.

 

            On April 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Opposition.

 

            On April 29, 2026, Defendant filed the Reply.

 

JUDICIAL NOTICE

           

The Court may take judicial notice of records of (1) any court of this state or (2) any court of record of the United States or of any state of the United States. (Evid. Code § 452 subd. (d).) The Court may also take judicial notice of “Official acts of the legislative, executive, and judicial departments of the United States and of any state of the United States.” (Evid. Code § 452 subd. (c).)

           

            Plaintiff requests that the Court take judicial notice of the Statement of Information for Real Brokerage Technologies filed May 29, 2024, with the California Secretary of State, the State of California Department of Real Estate license information for Dawn Marie Lewis, the State of California Department of Real Estate license information for Real Brokerage Technologies, the Fictitious Business Name Statement by registrant Real Brokerage Technologies, filed May 17, 2017, in the Official Records of San Diego County, and the Fictitious Business Name Statement by registrant Real Brokerage Technologies, Inc., filed May 16, 2022, in the Official Records of San Diego County.

 

            The Court grants Plaintiff’s request pursuant to Evidence Code section 452 subdivision (c).

 

LEGAL STANDARD

           

            A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 

53 Cal.4th 861, 872.) For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.) A demurrer “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.) 

 

A pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous or unintelligible. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) A demurrer for uncertainty may lie if the failure to label the parties and claims renders the complaint so confusing defendant cannot tell what he or she is supposed to respond to. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2.) However, “[a] demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) 

 

DISCUSSION

           

Meet and confer

 

Before filing a demurrer, “the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) 

 

            On March 27, 2026, the parties met telephonically regarding the Demurrer. Therefore, Defendant has satisfied the meet and confer requirements.

 

First Cause of Action – Breach of Contract

 

The elements of breach of contract are (1) the existence of a contract; (2) the plaintiff’s performance or a valid excuse for nonperformance; (3) the defendant’s breach or anticipatory breach of the contract; and (4) resulting damage to the plaintiff. (Wall Street Network, Ltd. v. N. Y. Times Co. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1171, 1178.)

 

Defendant argues that the breach of contract claim fails because Plaintiff does not allege facts sufficient to establish any of the required elements.  Defendant emphasizes that the Complaint contains only conclusory allegations, such as assertions that Defendant is liable or acted as an agent, which are not accepted as true on demurrer. There are no factual allegations showing that Defendant entered into any contract with Plaintiff, that Plaintiff performed under such a contract, or that Defendant breached any contractual obligation.

 

Defendant further argues that the governing purchase agreement expressly disclaims responsibility for investigating title or public records, which defeats any claim based on a failure to verify ownership. This limitation is consistent with Civil Code section 2079.3, which does not require agents to inspect public records or title matters. Finally, Defendant asserts that Plaintiff fails to allege any damages caused by Defendant.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues the demurrer should be overruled because the Complaint adequately pleads all elements of a breach of contract claim against Defendant. Plaintiff identifies the contract as a written Referral Fee Agreement under which Defendant, through its agent Dawn Lewis, agreed to receive one percent of the purchase price for referring the transaction. Plaintiff alleges it performed by paying the agreed referral fee. Plaintiff further alleges Defendant, through its agent, breached duties arising from the agreement by failing to exercise due diligence in verifying the true identity and ownership of the purported seller, instead presenting an impostor as the property owner and facilitating the transaction without proper investigation.

 

Plaintiff contends these failures constitute a breach of contractual and professional obligations, including duties to act competently, in good faith, and to protect the purchaser from fraud. Plaintiff alleges substantial damages resulting from the transaction, including over $1.36 million in purchase-related payments, additional post-closing costs and improvements, and the referral fee paid for services that were not properly performed. Based on these allegations, Plaintiff maintains the claim is sufficiently pled and should proceed.

 

As to the agency allegations, “generally, an allegation of agency is an allegation of ultimate fact and is, of itself, sufficient to avoid a demurrer.” (Garton v. Title Ins. & Trust Co. (1980) 106 Cal.App.3d 365, 376.) Plaintiff has provided Defendant Dawn Lewis’s Real Estate License, which lists Defendant Real Brokerage Technologies as her Responsible Broker. A salesperson is an agent of their listed broker as a matter of law. (Gipson v. Davis Realty Co. (1963) 215 Cal.App.2d 190, 206-207.) Thus, the Court finds that the Complaint sufficiently alleges that Defendant Lewis was an agent of Defendant Real Brokerage Technologies.

 

            Here, the Complaint alleges that “as the real estate agents, said Defendants owed implied and express contractual duties to FLIPTSIDE to act competently and in good faith towards FLIPTSIDE as purchaser of the Property. Said Defendants owed a contractual duty to locate and identify the true owner of the Property, to use due diligence to obtain reliable evidence that any entity or person claiming to be owner and seller of the Property was in fact that actual owner, and to act in accord with the best practices and procedures of real estate agents to protect the purchaser from fraud and other criminal activity.” (Complaint ¶ 48.) And that” Defendants breached the implied terms of the oral and written contract when they failed to confirm the person referred to FLIPTSIDE as a bona fide seller was the actual owner of the Property. Said Defendants failed to confirm true ownership of the Property, and failed to investigate the recording of documents purporting to change ownership at or about the same time as the opening of escrow. Transfers of title concurrent or immediately prior to the sale of a property, through the recording of grand deeds purportedly executed over 20 years before they were recorded, should have raised skepticism and caused heightened diligence on the part of said Defendants because owners do not typically wait 20 years to record grant deeds, nor do owners normally change title to property concurrent or immediately prior to executing purchase agreements and opening escrow.” (Complaint ¶ 49.)

 

            However, the Residential Purchase agreement reads “Buyer and Seller acknowledge and agree that Agent … shall not be responsible for inspecting public records or permits concerning the title or use of Properly… shall not be responsible for providing other advice or information that exceeds the knowledge, education, and experience required to perform real estate licensed activity.” (Complaint Exhibit 3 ¶ 18.) Thus, Defendant had no duty to investigate the property's title. Furthermore, the Complaint fails to allege any other contractual duty that Defendants breached.

 

            Therefore, the Court sustains the Demurrer as to the first cause of action with leave to amend.

 

Second Cause of Action – Breach of Fiduciary Duty

 

“To establish a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty, a plaintiff must demonstrate the existence of a fiduciary relationship, breach of that duty, and damages.” (Charnay v. Cobert (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 170, 182.)

           

            Here, Defendant argues that the Contract excluded any duties related to the investigation of title. However, the fiduciary duty between a real estate agent and their client cannot be contractually excluded, as the duty arises from the relationship, not the contract. (William L. Lyon & Associates, Inc. v. Superior Court (2012) 204 Cal.App.4th 1294, 1312.)

 

            Thus, the Court finds that the Complaint adequately alleges a breach of fiduciary duty claim against Defendant based on the actions of its agent.

 

Therefore, the Court overrules the Demurrer as to the second cause of action.

 

Third Cause of Action – Negligence

 

“The elements of a cause of action for negligence are well established. They are (a) a legal duty to use due care; (b) a breach of such legal duty; [and] (c) the breach as the proximate or legal cause of the resulting injury.” (Ladd v. County of San Mateo (1996) 12 Cal.4th 913, 917.)

 

Here, Defendant argues that the Contract excluded any duties related to the investigation of title. Unlike fiduciary duties, parties may exclude duties relating to negligence by contract. (See, Loughrin v. Superior Court (1993) 15 Cal.App.4th 1188, 1192-1194 [a residential purchase contract is a private arrangement that is not of public interest; thus, exculpatory clauses are allowed].) Thus, as noted above, the parties have agreed that Defendants were not responsible for title issues.

 

Therefore, the Court sustains the Demurrer as to the third cause of action with leave to amend.

REDONDO BEACH UNIFIED SCHOOL DISTRICT; and DOES 1 through 50, inclusive

Hearing Date:             August 31, 2026

 

Moving Parties:          Defendant Redondo Beach Unified School District

 

Responding Party:      Plaintiff Virginia Valdez Schalck

 

HEARING: Demurrer and Motion to Strike

 

            The Court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers.

 

RULING

           

            The Court sustains the Demurrer with leave to amend.

 

            The Court denies the Motion to Strike as moot.

           

BACKGROUND

 

            On April 28, 2026, Plaintiff Virginia Valdez Schalck filed the Complaint against Defendant Redondo Beach Unified School District.

 

            On June 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (FAC) against Defendant, alleging causes of action for negligence, negligent supervision/retention, failure to discharge mandatory duties, declaratory relief, and injunctive relief.

 

            On July 2, 2026, Defendant filed the Demurrer with Motion to Strike.

 

            On July 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Opposition.

 

            On August 3, 2026, Defendant filed the Reply.

           

LEGAL STANDARD

 

            A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 

53 Cal.4th 861, 872.) For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.) A demurrer “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.) 

 

A pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous or unintelligible. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) A demurrer for uncertainty may lie if the failure to label the parties and claims renders the complaint so confusing defendant cannot tell what he or she is supposed to respond to. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2.) However, “[a] demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) 

 

DISCUSSION

 

Meet and Confer

             

            Before filing a demurrer, “the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).)

 

            On May 27, 2026, the parties met via teleconference regarding the instant Demurrer. Therefore, the Court finds that Defendant has satisfied the meet and confer requirements.

 

            Defendant demurs to the FAC.

 

            Statute of Limitations

 

Defendant argues that the FAC is jurisdictionally barred because Plaintiff filed the original complaint one day late under the Government Claims Act. Defendant contends that the Court lacks jurisdiction over Plaintiff's claims because Government Code section 945.6 requires that any suit against a public entity be commenced within six months of the date written notice of rejection is personally delivered or deposited in the mail. (Gov. Code, § 945.6, subd. (a)(1).) Defendant maintains that the six-month period begins to run from the date the notice is deposited in the mail even where the claim was separately deemed rejected by operation of law. (Edgington v. County of San Diego (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 39, 46.)

 

Defendant points to Plaintiff's own allegation that written notice of rejection was provided by the district's counsel on October 27, 2025. (FAC ¶ 7.) Defendant states that six months from October 27, 2025 is April 27, 2026, and that Plaintiff filed the Complaint on April 28, 2026, one day after the deadline. Thus, Defendant concludes that the action is time-barred on its face and that filing the First Amended Complaint cannot cure the jurisdictional defect.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff responds that untimeliness does not clearly and affirmatively appear on the face of the FAC. Plaintiff argues that Government Code section 945.6 runs six months from the date written notice of rejection is personally delivered or deposited in the mail, and that the pleading alleges only that counsel provided written notice on October 27, 2025, which reflects the date of reception rather than the date of any mailing. Plaintiff contends that because a limitations demurrer lies only where the bar clearly and affirmatively appears on the face of the complaint, the demurrer fails on this ground alone.

 

“Except as provided in Sections 946.4 and 946.6 and subject to subdivision (b), any suit brought against a public entity on a cause of action for which a claim is required to be presented in accordance with Chapter 1 (commencing with Section 900) and Chapter 2 (commencing with Section 910) of Part 3 of this division must be commenced…If written notice is given in accordance with Section 913, not later than six months after the date such notice is personally delivered or deposited in the mail.” (Gov. Code, § 945.6, subd. (a)(1).) “In order for the bar ... to be raised by demurrer, the defect must clearly and affirmatively appear on the face of the complaint; it is not enough that the complaint shows that the action may be barred.” (Committee for Green Foothills v. Santa Clara County Bd. of Supervisors (2010) 48 Cal.4th 32, 42.)

 

Here, the Complaint reads “The claim was rejected by operation of law on October 26, 2025, and written notice of rejection was provided by Defendant’s counsel on October 27, 2025.” (FAC ¶ 7.) However, the statute of limitations begins to run when the notice of rejection is personally delivered or deposited in the mail. As the FAC makes no claims regarding when the notice of rejection was deposited in the mail, nor that it was personally delivered, the Court finds that the FAC does not show that the claim is untimely on its face. Thus, the FAC cannot be demurred to on statute of limitations grounds.

 

Standing

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff lacks standing because the substantive allegations of the FAC belong to Jacob Schalck rather than to Plaintiff. Although the pleading recites that Plaintiff sues solely in her individual capacity, Defendant contends that the alleged wrongs were done to Jacob and that the remedies sought vindicate his rights, and Plaintiff cannot establish standing merely by labeling the claims as her own when the underlying injuries, rights, and remedies belong to another. (Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 1004.) (Code Civ. Proc., § 367.)

 

Defendant maintains that every substantive grievance in the pleading is Jacob's, pointing to the denied MCJROTC promotions, ribbons, and leadership opportunities, the ADHD diagnosis in Jacob's record, the investigative notes concerning Jacob, the corrective actions ordered to remedy alleged violations of Jacob's educational rights, and the delayed service-academy pathway. Defendant argues that Plaintiff's claimed personal injuries, emotional distress from filing paperwork and anxiety about her son's situation, do not state a cognizable claim against a public entity, and that no common law duty is owed to a parent arising from a district's handling of a student's educational records or complaint procedures. Defendant concludes that because Jacob is an adult, his individual legal rights belong to him and only he may prosecute claims seeking to vindicate them. (Tepper v. Wilkins (2017) 10 Cal.App.5th 1198, 1206.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff responds that the Defendant misreads the FAC, because she expressly disclaims suing on her adult son's behalf and pleads categories of injury that are hers alone, which makes her the real party in interest as to those claims. (Code Civ. Proc., § 367.) She argues that the District failed to honor the mandatory records-amendment process of Education Code section 49070 after acknowledging in writing her son's July 14, 2025 authorization permitting her to act, having for months treated her as the proper party by mailing her the records and the amendment regulation, yet holding no meeting, issuing no determination, and giving no appeal-rights notice before invoking the age-of-majority rule for the first time in this litigation. (Ed. Code, § 49070.) Plaintiff contends that this denied her a statutory process in which she was the acknowledged participant and caused her own time, expense, and distress, and that she can at least amend to clarify the capacity in which she participated and the personal injuries she seeks to recover.

 

Plaintiff further argues that the written promises of compliance and personal notification were made to her by Superintendent Wesley on July 9, 2025 and confirmed by Assistant Superintendent Garland on July 17, 2025, so the damages from her reliance when the December 31, 2025 deadline passed are hers and not derivative of anyone else's injury. She adds that her physical harm, emotional distress, lost time, and out-of-pocket costs are documented personal injuries distinct from any claim belonging to her son, that these allegations must be accepted as true at the pleading stage, and that she requests leave to amend to the extent greater specificity is required.

 

“A complaint filed by a party who lacks standing is subject to demurrer.” (Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 1004.) “Every action must be prosecuted in the name of the real party in interest, except as otherwise provided by statute.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 367.)

 

Here, every wrong alleged in the FAC was directed at Plaintiff's son Jacob. The negligence causes of action revolve around alleged discrimination and failure to investigate Jacob’s MCJROTC program, and the mandatory duty allegations are based on Jacob’s records and time in MCJROTC. While the Complaint alleges damages to Plaintiff arising from actions taken against Jacob, Plaintiff provides no duty that Defendant owed to her, as she was not a pupil, nor are any wrongs alleged to have been committed against her. Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiff is not the real party in interest.

 

Therefore, the Court sustains the Demurrer with leave to amend.

 

Motion to Strike

 

Defendant requests that the Court strike the allegations regarding Jacob’s individual rights and damages regarding the “Kentucky Detour Year.” However, as the demurrer to the FAC was sustained, the Court finds that the instant Motion to Strike is moot.

 

Therefore, the Court denies the Motion to Strike as moot.
