Tentative Ruling

Halee Dion v. General Motors, LLC, Case No. 24SMCV03549

Plaintiff Halee Dion’s Motion for Attorney Fees

Hearing Date: August 17, 2026

Background

This is a lemon law case. Plaintiff Halee Dion (“Plaintiff”) brought suit against Defendant General Motors, LLC (“Defendant”) alleging Defendant’s failure to repair or re-purchase Dion’s 2023 Cadillac CT5’s defects, including transmission and engine issues. (Complaint, ¶ 12.)

On July 24, 2024, Plaintiff filed the complaint. On August 28, 2024, Defendant answered.

In October 2025, both parties filed pre-trial motions in limine.

On October 30, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Settlement of Entire Case.

On March 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion for attorneys’ fees. On August 4, 2026, Defendant filed its opposition. August 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

Motion for Attorney’s Fees

 

A prevailing buyer in an action for damages and other legal remedies should receive full compensation of reasonable sums expended in pursuit of their claims against manufacturers who failed to comply with the Song-Beverly Act. (Civ. Code § 1794(d).) 

 

“A trial court assessing attorney fees begins with a touchstone or lodestar figure, based on the ‘careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney ... involved in the presentation of the case.” (Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1321.) “The reasonableness of attorney fees is within the discretion of the trial court, to be determined from a consideration of such factors as the nature of the litigation, the complexity of the issues, the experience and expertise of counsel and the amount of time involved. The court may also consider whether the amount requested is based upon unnecessary or duplicative work.” (Wilkerson v. Sullivan (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.)¿

 

Prevailing Party

 

The parties do not contest that Plaintiff is the prevailing party.

 

Reasonable Rate

 

Plaintiff claims fees for four attorneys on this case, Lara Rogers at $430.00 per hour, Iraida Gonzalez at $480.00 per hour, Carey B. Wood at $560.00 per hour, and Bobby C. Walker at $440.00 per hour. As well as $215.00 per hour for Paralegals Destiny Olvera, Robert Aguilar, Silvia Martinez, Susana Alatorre, Cesar Utuy, Isable Garcia, and Maria Chavarin. Lara Rogers performed the vast majority of work on this case with 35.8 hours. Carey Wood performed 7.2 hours, Bobby C. Walker performed 2.2 hours, and Iraida Gonzalez performed 0.8 hours. In light of this, the Court finds the number of attorneys on this case reasonable. Furthermore, all attorneys billed between $430 and $560.00 per hour. The Court finds this rate reasonable given the attorneys’ qualifications and experience. (Declaration of Carey B. Wood, ¶¶ 12-15.) Plaintiff’s counsel has also shown that courts have previously approved their rates varying from $415.00 per hour to $540.00 per hour. (Wood Decl., ¶¶ 17-20.)

 

Reasonable Hours

 

Plaintiff claims 69 hours litigating this case. Plaintiff also claims approximately an additional 5 hours responding to and hearing this motion. The Court finds 5 hours is excessive for Plaintiff’s reply and appearance on this motion and thus reduces these hours to 3 hours or $1,680.00. Plaintiff claims approximately an additional 5 hours in connection with reviewing Defendant’s motion to tax costs, preparing the opposition brief, reviewing Defendant’s reply and attending the hearing. The Court finds 5 hours is excessive for Defendant’s opposition and appearance and thus reduces these hours by 3 hours or $1,680.00.

 

Defendant argues that the Court should reduce the hours billed by 5.1 for the entries on September 25, September 26, October 1, and October 13 in 2025 regarding a Motion to Compel Further Responses to Requests for Production of Documents, Set One and an IDC statement which were never filed because the parties settled on October 30, 2025. The Court agrees and reduces the hours billed by 5.1 hours or $2,193.00.

 

Defendant argues that Counsel should not recover an of the 0.7 hours counsel billed before the Complaint was drafted and filed such as calls to the client explaining the prelitigation demand. The Court finds that the 0.7 hours billed for communication with the client and reviewing the case for litigation are reasonable. Thus, the Court denies Defendant’s request to decrease the hours billed by 0.7 hours.

 

Defendant argues that excessive communication was billed and there were duplicative intra-office conferences. The Court does not agree that these communications were excessive or duplicative, and communication is necessary during litigation. Similarly, Defendant challenges the hours billed by paralegals. However, tasks such as serving, filing, drafting proofs of service, and scheduling the vehicle surrendered are all necessary and reasonably incurred.

 

Defendant also argues that time for templated motions including the memorandum of costs, motions in limine and trial documents should be reduced. The Court agrees that the motions in limine were template motions and reduces the hours by 2.8 or $1,568.00.

 

10% Reduction

 

Additionally, Defendant requests a 10% reduction of counsel’s time because they had eleven timekeepers billing on this matter. As discussed above, the Court does not find the number of individuals working on this matter because the majority of the work performed in this matter was by Lara Rogers who billed 35.8 hours. Thus, the Court denies Defendant’s request for a 10% reduction.

 

 

Conclusion

Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorneys’ Fees is GRANTED in the amount of $23,539.00[1].


[1] Plaintiff claimed $31,323.00 for 79 hours of work (69 hours billed, 5 hours estimated for the reply and appearance on this motion and 5 hours estimated for the opposition to the motion to tax and appearance on that motion). The Court reduced the 69 hours by 10.9 hours ($6,104.00), the estimated hours for the instant motion by 2 hours ($1,120.00), and the estimated hours for the motion to tax by 1 hour ($560.00). With those reductions, Plaintiff is awarded $23,539.00 in fees.


Case Number: SC129602    Hearing Date: August 17, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Kenneth Kaplan v. Baker Equity, et al., Case No. SC129602

Defendants’ Motion for Relief from Order Deeming Requests for Admission Admitted

Hearing Date: August 17, 2026

Background

Plaintiff Kenneth Kaplan brought this action for fraudulent conveyance and to quiet title to 268 S. Almont Drive, Beverly Hills, California against Defendants Baker Equity, LLC; Eagle Nasher, LLC; Shoor Temple, LLC, A Delaware Corporation; Shoor Temple, LLC, A Nevada Corporation;  Joseph Youshaei; Jila Youshaei; and Farbod Youshaei. Kaplan alleges that all Defendant corporations are owned by the Joseph, Jila, and Farbod Youshaei. The companies own three properties: two car washes and the Beverly Hills house, the subject of this action. (First Amended Complaint (“FAC”), ¶ 25.) In 2017, Kaplan obtained a $486,094.07[1] judgment against Defendants in Massachusetts. After entering judgment, the Massachusetts court issued an injunction prohibiting the Youshaeis from selling their assets. (FAC, ¶ 29.) Kaplan domesticated the Massachusetts judgment in June 2018.

Kaplan filed this action in June 2018 because the Youshaeis allegedly violated the Massachusetts injunction by transferring the Beverly Hills property and the car washes to their companies. (FAC, ¶ 46.)  Kaplan filed the operative First Amended Complaint (FAC) in August 2018.

Following the Youshaeis’ failure to respond to discovery, the Court granted Kaplan’s Motion to deem RFAs admitted on May 28, 2019.

In August 2019, the Court consolidated this case with Case No. SC 12960, filed by Eagle Nasher, Shoor Temple LLC, and Farbod Youshei against the Youshaeis. The parties in that case stipulated to a judgment that the Youshaeis appealed in 2021.

Kaplan dismissed the two Shoor Temple defendants, Eagle Nasher, LLC, and Farbod Youshaei from this action following the settlement and resolution of the appeal.

In January 2023, this case was stayed pending Joseph Youshaei’s bankruptcy proceedings. Joseph Youshaei’s bankruptcy proceedings were resolved on August 2, 2024. On October 10, 2024, Baker Equity filed for bankruptcy. As of September 2025, bankruptcy was no longer an issue.

On March 18, 2026, the Court denied Kaplan’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings.

On July 13, 2026, Kaplan filed a motion in limine to preclude Defendants from introducing evidence contradicting the requests for admission that the Court deemed admitted in 2019.

On July 29, 2026, Defendants filed this motion for relief from the 2019 order deeming Plaintiffs’ Requests for Admission admitted.

Defendants move under Code Civ. Proc. § 2033.300 and § 473(b). Defendants argue that they were self-represented until recently and were unable to respond to discovery requests or realize the importance of the discovery requests because Defendant Joseph Youshaei has been ill and Defendant Jilla Youshaei has been his primary caretaker. (Motion, p.4.) Defendants also argue that Plaintiffs cannot show that granting this motion would cause substantial prejudice. (Id. at p. 5.)

Trial in this matter is set for August 17, 2026.

Motion for Relief from Order Deeming Requests for Admission Admitted

CCP section 2033.300 provides that “[a] party may withdraw or amend an admission made in response to a request for admission only on leave of court granted after notice to all parties.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.300(a).)  “The court may permit withdrawal or amendment of an admission only if it determines that the admission was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect, and that the party who obtained the admission will not be substantially prejudiced in maintaining that party’s action or defense on the merits.”  (Id., § 2033.300(b).)  “The court may impose conditions on the granting of the motion that are just, including, but not limited to, the following: [¶] (1) [a]n order that the party who obtained the admission be permitted to pursue additional discovery related to the matter involved in the withdrawn or amended admission[;] [¶] (2) [a]n order that the costs of any additional discovery be borne in whole or in part by the party withdrawing or amending the admission.”  (Id., § 2033.300(c).) 

“Section 2033.300 eliminates undeserved windfalls obtained through requests for admission and furthers the policy favoring the resolution of lawsuits on the merits.”  (New Albertsons, Inc. v. Superior Court (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1418.)  “Because the law strongly favors trial and disposition on the merits, any doubts in applying section 2033.300 must be resolved in favor of the party seeking relief.”  (Id. at 1420.)  “Accordingly, the court’s discretion to deny a motion under the statute is limited to circumstances where it is clear that the mistake, inadvertence, or neglect was inexcusable, or where it is clear that the withdrawal or amendment would substantially prejudice the party who obtained the admission in maintaining that party’s action or defense on the merits.”  (Id. at 1420-21.) 

The statutory language in section 2033.300, “mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect”, is identical to some of the language used in CCP section 473(b).  (Id. at 1418-19.)  “The use of identical terms in two different statutes serving similar purposes suggests that the Legislature intended those terms to have the same meaning in both statutes.”  (Id.)  “Moreover, the legislative history of section 2033, subdivision (m), the predecessor of section 2033.300, suggests that the Legislature intended ‘mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect’ to have the same meaning in the statute as those terms have in section 473, subdivision (b).”  (Id. at 1419.) 

Here, Defendants’ only evidence of mistake is their declarations that they did not realize the importance of the RFAs at the time Plaintiffs moved to deem the RFAs admitted, more than seven years ago, and that they may have been represented at the time and believed their attorney would handle it. (Joseph Youshaei Decl., ¶¶ 4-6.) Defendants also attest that Joseph Youshaei has been ill at various points throughout this litigation, but do not provide any evidence that illness prevented them from responding to the RFAs at issue. (see generally, Joseph Youshaei Decl.) Pro per litigants are held to the same standard as attorneys. (Kobayashi v. Superior Court (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 536, 543.) Failure to recognize the importance of requests for admission would not constitute excusable neglect for an attorney. (Henderson v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co. (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 215, 229 [“Conduct falling below the professional standard of care, such as failure to timely object or properly advance an argument, is therefore not excusable.”].)  Recognizing this type of error as excusable mistake or neglect in Defendants’ case would constitute exceptional treatment of a pro per litigant because this Court could not set aside an order deeming RFAs admitted if an attorney made the same error.

Additionally, Kaplan would be prejudiced if the Court set aside the 2019 order deeming the requests for admission admitted because trial is set to start the same day this motion is heard, August 17, 2026. If the Court granted Defendants’ motion, Kaplan would need to re-structure his entire case on the eve of trial and would likely need additional discovery, requiring a trial continuance. This case is already more than eight years old, so the Court cannot delay trial any further.

Defendants have not shown excusable neglect or mistake.  

Conclusion

Defendants’ Motion for relief from order deeming requests for admission admitted is DENIED.
