I.       INTRODUCTION

         On August 19, 2025, plaintiff Wolfgang Starke (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against defendants Golden Tiger Life Corp. (“Golden Tiger Life”), Ajay Dhawan, and Suparna Dhawan. As Ajay Dhawan and Suparna Dhawan share the same last name, the Court refers to them by their first names for purposes of clarity and intends no disrespect.

          Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) on March 26, 2026, asserting causes of action for: (1) breach of contract, (2) breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (3) negligent misrepresentation, (4) breach of fiduciary duty, (5) intentional misrepresentation, (6) violation of California Business and Professions Code § 17200 (“UCL”), (7) unjust enrichment, (8) conversion, (9) concealment, and (10) money had and received.

          Defendants Ajay and Suparna (collectively, “the Dhawans”) Defendants”) filed a demurrer to all causes of action on May 29, 2026. The Dhawans then filed a motion to stay the action pending the resolution of bankruptcy proceedings involving co-defendant Golden Tiger.

          Plaintiff filed opposition briefs to the demurrer, motion to strike, and motion to stay on July 28, 2026.

          The Dhawans filed a combined reply brief on August 3, 2026.

II.     DEMURRER

A.   Legal Standard

A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the pleadings and will be sustained only where the pleading is defective on its face. (City of Atascadero v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 445, 459.) “We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law. We accept the factual allegations of the complaint as true and also consider matters which may be judicially noticed. [Citation.]” (Mitchell v. California Department of Public Health (2016) 1 Cal.App.5th 1000, 1007; Del E. Webb Corp. v. Structural Materials Co. (1981) 123 Cal.App.3d 593, 604 [“the facts alleged in the pleading are deemed to be true, however improbable they may be”].) Allegations are to be liberally construed. (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) In construing the allegations, the court is to give effect to specific factual allegations that may modify or limit inconsistent general or conclusory allegations. (Financial Corporation of America v. Wilburn (1987) 189 Cal.App.3rd 764, 769.)

B.   Factual Allegations

Plaintiff alleges that he invested $1.6 million in a “turmeric-based drink venture and related business lines” initiated by Ajay and his wife, Suparna, first through their private company, non-party Shiv International Corporation (“Shiv International”), and later on, through Golden Tiger Life. (FAC, ¶ 1.) Beginning in September 2018, Ajay solicited investments from Plaintiff, promising substantial equity and returns based on alleged business opportunities with major corporations. (FAC, ¶ 1.) Plaintiff made numerous investments between September 2018 and July 2024, which he claims were diverted by the Dhawans for their own personal benefit and to Shiv International. (FAC, ¶ 4.)

In July 2020, the parties entered into a Shareholder’s Agreement memorializing Plaintiff’s 20% equity stake in Golden Tiger for $500,000. The agreement required Individual Defendants to assign all trademarks and intellectual property related to Golden Tiger Life’s product lines from Shiv International to Golden Tiger Life, that directors manage the corporation in accordance with sound business practices, and that annual reports be provided to shareholders. (FAC, ¶ 5.) However, the intellectual property was not timely transferred, Plaintiff was excluded from financial decision making, and annual reports and financial records were withheld for years. (FAC, ¶ 6.)

Plaintiff further alleges that the parties agreed (verbally and in writing) that his shareholding would be increased to 28%, but that Ajay continued to list Plaintiff as only a 20% shareholder on Golden Tiger Life’s tax returns, and never executed the amended Shareholder’s Agreement. (FAC, ¶ 7.)

C.   Plaintiff’s First and Second Causes of Action for Breach of Contract and Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

The Dhawans demur to Plaintiff’s First and Second Causes of Action for breach of contract and breach of the implied covenant.

With respect to Plaintiff’s breach of contract claim, the Dhawans argue that Plaintiff refers to both a written July 24, 2020 Shareholder’s Agreement and a later oral February 17, 2022 understanding to increase his shares, but cannot tell which obligations are written and which are oral. The Dhawans also contend that the FAC fails to plead a concrete breach of the “written governance covenants” which are conditional and qualified duties that are based on the directors’ discretion, and that any breach did not cause Plaintiff to lose his entire investment. (Demurrer, p. 6.) The Dhawans additionally argue that any purported failure to maintain and provide financial reports is not the cause of Plaintiff’s alleged damages because Plaintiff does not merely claim damages amounting to the investment made pursuant to the shareholder agreements entered into on July 24, 2020 and February 17, 2022, but his entire investment of $1.6 million. Furthermore, with respect to Plaintiff’s claim for breach of the implied covenant, the Dhawans argue that it is duplicative of Plaintiff’s breach of contract claim and, to the extent that Plaintiff alleges they mismanaged Golden Tiger Life, Plaintiff’s allegations of mismanagement would impose duties beyond the Shareholder Agreement.

In opposition, Plaintiff solely argues that these arguments should not be considered by the Court because they could have been raised in response to Plaintiff’s initial Complaint and the Court already overruled their demurrer to his First and Third Causes of Action. However, in Carlton v. Dr. Pepper Snapple Group, Inc. (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 1200,1211, the court of appeal allowed the defendant to file a renewed demurrer to the entire complaint despite previously overruling a demurrer to the same cause of action. Also, “[t]he interests of all parties are advanced by avoiding a trial and reversal for defect in pleadings.” (Pacific States Enterprises, Inc. v. City of Coachella (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 1414, 1420.) The Court additionally observes that even if the First and Third Cause of Actions survived the previous demurrer, these specific arguments were not raised for the Court’s consideration.

Nevertheless, despite Plaintiff’s failure to address the demurrer’s arguments on a substantive level, the Court OVERRULES the demurrer to the First and Second Causes of Action because the Dhawans’ noticeably fail to include any authority supporting their arguments regarding the sufficiency of Plaintiff’s allegations as a matter of law. “A legal proposition asserted without apposite authority necessarily fails.” (People v. Taylor (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 628, 643). Here, the demurrer only cites to case law for general propositions of law, which do not support any of the arguments that the Dhawans assert.

D.  Plaintiff’s Fraud Claims

The Dhawans demur to Plaintiff’s causes of action for negligent misrepresentation (Third Cause of Action), intentional misrepresentation (Fifth Cause of Action) and concealment (Ninth Cause of Action) on the grounds that they are not specifically pleaded and that the alleged misrepresentations are not actionable because they are “promises or predictions of future events.” (Demurrer, p. 9.)

With regard to fraud claims, pleadings must allege facts as to “‘how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.’” (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 73.) The Court previously noted that there were no specific allegations with respect to any misrepresentations or concealment by Suparna and sustained her demurrer to these claims. Plaintiff’s FAC still fails to allege any affirmative misrepresentations by Suparna, but adds sufficient allegations to support his Ninth Cause of Action for fraudulent concealment since Plaintiff alleges that the Dhawans both concealed information about the salaries they were paying themselves and their family members through Golden Tiger Life as well as the payment of their personal credit card balances through Golden Tiger Life’s bank accounts. (FAC, ¶ 196.) Accordingly, the demurrer to the Third and Fifth Causes of Action are SUSTAINED and the demurrer to the Ninth Cause of Action is OVERRULED as to Suparna.

As for Plaintiffs fraud claims against Ajay, Ajay argues that any statements made were not actionable. “The law is well established that actionable misrepresentations must pertain to past or existing material facts. [Citation.] Statements or predictions regarding future events are deemed to be mere opinions which are not actionable. [Citations.]” (Cansino v. Bank of America (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1462, 1469.) However, Plaintiff specifically alleges that Ajay misrepresented being “in discussions” with certain individuals and businesses or submitting products for market testing; Ajay also allegedly misrepresented how the business was being managed. (FAC, ¶¶ 160-164; 178-181.)

Therefore, the demurrer to the Third, Sixth, and Ninth Causes of Action is OVERRULED as to Ajay.

E.   Plaintiff’s Claim for Breach of Fiduciary Duty

Plaintiff alleges that Ajay and Suparna owed and breached fiduciary duties by failing to maintain proper books and records, misappropriating and mismanaging funds, including Plaintiff’s substantial capital contributions, causing his individual interest in Golden Tiger Life to be harmed. (FAC, ¶ 172.)

The Dhawans demur to this cause of action on the grounds that Plaintiff is merely pleading a derivative claim and does not allege any direct harm to Plaintiff. (Demurrer, pp. 9-10.) Defendants also argue that Plaintiff fails to allege a derivative action because no pre-suit demand to the board has been made. (Demurrer, p. 10; Corp. Code, § 800.)

To establish a breach of fiduciary duty claim, a minority shareholder must prove: (1) the defendants were majority or controlling shareholders who owed fiduciary duties to the minority; (2) the defendants breached those duties by engaging in self-dealing, unfair transactions, or oppressive conduct that benefited themselves disproportionately; (3) the breach caused injury specifically to the minority shareholder's individual interest rather than to the corporation generally; and (4) damages (Lynch v. Cook (1983) 148 Cal.App.3d 1072.)

Here, Plaintiff alleges that the Dhawans breached their fiduciary duties by fraudulently inducing him to make different investments in Golden Tiger Life. These are specific injuries sustained only by Plaintiff as an individual and directed towards him individually. (See Vega v. Jones, Day, Reavis & Pogue (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 282, 297 [plaintiff stated individual, not derivative, claim when alleging defendant deceived him into exchanging valuable stock for worthless stock].)

Accordingly, the demurrer to the Fourth Cause of Action is OVERRULED.

F.   Plaintiff’s Seventh Cause of Action for Unjust Enrichment and Tenth Cause of Action for Money Had and Received

The Dhawans argue that Plaintiff’s claims for unjust enrichment is inadequately pleaded because Plaintiff does not allege that they personally received any sums or that it would be inequitable for them to retain those benefits. As for Plaintiff’s Tenth Cause of Action for money had and received, the Dhawans argue that Plaintiff does not allege that any money was received for Plaintiff’s use. However, the Court OVERRULES the demurrer to the common counts on the grounds that the Dhawans fail to cite to any legal authority supporting their assertions.

G.  Plaintiff’s Claim for Conversion

The Dhawans argue that Plaintiff’s conversion claim fails, in part, because Plaintiff does not allege a possessory interest to a specific identifiable sum. (Demurrer, p. 15; Kim v. Westmoore Partners, Inc. (2011) 201 Cal.App.4th 267.) They claim that Plaintiff “does not plausibly plead that he still owned the same dollars in the corporate accounts”, but this argument ignores Plaintiff’s allegations that he “solely funded” Golden Tiger Life and was “the only known shareholder to have contributed any capital to the company.” (FAC, ¶¶ 1, 3.) The Dhawans also claim that Plaintiff refers to undefined amounts of money, but the cited portions of the FAC do not pertain to Plaintiff’s conversion claim at all.

Accordingly, the demurrer to the Eighth Cause of Action for Conversion is OVERRULED.

H.  Plaintiff’s Claim for Violation of the UCL

Last, the Dhawans demur to Plaintiff’s Sixth Cause of Action for violation of the UCL on the grounds that Plaintiff cannot obtain nonrestitutionary disgorgement or damages. However, Plaintiff only prays for restitution and disgorgement of all amounts by which Defendants were unjustly enriched. Therefore, Plaintiff is not seeking “nonrestitutionary disgorgement or damages” as the Dhawans claim and the demurrer to the Sixth Cause of Action is OVERRULED.

III.    MOTION TO STRIKE

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof. (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1).) The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].)

         The Dhawans move to strike paragraphs 175, 185, 190, and 201 of Plaintiff’s FAC, and Plaintiff’s prayer for attorneys’ fees and punitive damages. The specific paragraphs identified are allegations supporting the prayer for punitive damages.

A.   Punitive Damages

The Dhawans move to strike Plaintiff’s allegations in support of and request for punitive damages but only argues that no despicable conduct has been alleged or that punitive damages are unavailable in connection with Plaintiff’s contractual or quasicontract claims.

The motion to strike Plaintiff’s punitive damages request is DENIED because the FAC is clear that punitive damages are only being requested with respect to any tortious conduct premised on fraud and the Court has overruled the Dhawans’ demurrer to the Third, Fourth, Fifth, Seventh, and Ninth Causes of Action.

B.   Attorneys’ Fees

The Dhawans move to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for attorneys’ fees on the grounds that an “irrelevant matter” is an allegation in a pleading is a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint and Plaintiff failed to identify any statutory or contractual basis for attorneys’ fees. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 431.10, subds. (b)(3), (c); 1021.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that there is no need to strike this prayer because there is no need to plead a right to attorneys’ fees at all. Plaintiff relies on Snatchko v. Westfield LLC (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 469, 497, which is inapposite because it involved a party’s entitlement to attorneys’ fees under Code of Civil Procedure section 1021.5 and does not address a generalized prayer for attorneys’ fees. Plaintiff does not identify any agreement or statute which provides for attorneys’ fees. Accordingly, the motion to strike is GRANTED with respect to Plaintiff’s prayer for attorneys’ fees.

IV.    MOTION TO STAY

          As an initial matter, the Court disregards the voluminous exhibits attached to the Dhawans’ declarations and requests for judicial notice because they are not relevant or necessary to determine their demurrer, motion to strike, or motion to stay. Moreover, many of the documents are not appropriate for judicial notice and seem to be an improper attempt to litigate the merits of the case.

         The Dhawans argue that this action should be stayed until the resolution, discharge, or dismissal of the Chapter 7 bankruptcy proceeding of Golden Tiger Life. The Dhawans argue that allowing this action to proceed against them personally would “bypass the federal claims process, violate the automatic stay, and waste state court resources.” (Motion, p. 5.) The Dhawans claim that this action would result in a “parallel, duplicative trial regarding corporate financial records, transactions, intellectual property development, and executive compensation that are currently under the active and exclusive investigation of a federal Chapter 7 Trustee and her appointed national counsel.” (Motion, p. 5.) The Dhawans also argue, without any explanation, that Plaintiff’s claims against them personally are “inextricably intertwined” with Golden Tiger Life’s bankruptcy because their alleged liability arises from their conduct as the officers, directors, and shareholders of Golden Tiger Life. There is no explanation how Golden Tiger Life’s bankruptcy proceedings would be affected or what kinds of “inconsistent rulings” would be made.

V.      CONCLUSION

The demurrer to the FAC is OVERRULED as to all causes of action asserted against Ajay Dhawan.

The demurrer to the FAC is SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to the Third and Fifth Causes of Action against Suparna Dhawan and OVERRULED as to the First, Second, Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, Eighth, Ninth, and Tenth Causes of Action. Plaintiff is given 20 days’ leave to amend.

The motion to strike is DENIED as to Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages and GRANTED as to Plaintiff’s prayer for attorneys’ fees. Leave to amend is not granted at this time.

The motion to stay proceedings is DENIED.

Moving parties to give notice.

On June 23, 2026, defendant FJKA Living Trust (“Defendant”) filed this motion to compel further responses to Request for Admissions – Set 1 from plaintiff Diane D’Angelo (“Plaintiff”). Defendant also requested that the Court impose sanctions on Plaintiff and her attorneys, Law Offices of Sharona Eslamboly Hakim, to pay sanctions in the amount of $2,280, consisting of $2,200 in attorney’s fees and $60 for the filing fee of this motion.

On July 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed a “combined” opposition brief stating that she served substantive verified responses and the motion was moot. Plaintiff also argues that sanctions should not be imposed because Defendant did not adequately meet and confer with Plaintiff before filing this motion and the amount of sanctions requested is excessive.

On August 5, 2026, Defendant filed a reply brief acknowledging that Plaintiff served further responses on July 30, 2026. Defendant maintains its request for sanctions and refers back to the meet and confer letter sent on May 13, 2026, to which Plaintiff never responded.

Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.290(d) makes monetary sanctions mandatory unless the Court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. Sanctions may be awarded even after responses have been provided to the moving party after the motion was filed. (CRC 3.1348.) Plaintiff does not show that imposing sanctions would be unjust. First, Defendant provided Plaintiff with a meet and confer letter and failed to receive any response to it. Second, Defendant waited over a month after sending this letter to file the instant motion.

Accordingly, while the motion is denied as moot, the Court imposes sanctions on Plaintiff and counsel of record, jointly and severally, in the amount of $2,200, consisting of 3.6 hours at an hourly rate of $600 and a $60 filing fee, to be paid within 20 days of the date of this Order.

I.      INTRODUCTION

         Plaintiff Arianna Griffin (“Plaintiff”) filed this action on October 1, 2025, against defendant Specialty Restaurants Corporation (“Defendant”) arising from a slip and fall that occurred on or about September 29, 2023. Plaintiff asserts causes of action for negligence and premises liability.

          On March 19, 2026, Defendant filed a demurrer to the Complaint on the grounds that the action was time-barred because it was filed more than 2 years after the slip-and-fall incident. (Code Civ. Proc., § 335.1.)

On August 4, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition brief and an application for leave to file the late opposition.

Defendant filed a reply brief on August 5, 2026.

II.     LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer may be brought if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).) A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the pleadings and will be sustained only where the pleading is defective on its face. (City of Atascadero v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 445, 459.)

III.    DISCUSSION

          As stated above, Defendant demurs to the Complaint on the grounds that it shows, on its face, that the action is time-barred. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant negligently maintained its property which allowed a slip hazard to exist and caused her to sustain injuries on September 29, 2023. (Comp., ¶¶ 5, 7-10.) The statute of limitations period for a personal injury action is 2 years, meaning that Plaintiff should have filed her Complaint by September 29, 2025, at the latest. (Code Civ. Proc., § 335.1.)

          In a late-filed opposition brief, Plaintiff argues that the Complaint was electronically filed on September 29, 2025, but rejected. The notice of rejection, generated on October 1, 2025, at 1:56 p.m., stated: “Email for service on Complaint must match the data.” (Aziz Decl., Ex. B.) When an electronically submitted complaint is rejected for noncompliance with filing requirements or nonpayment of a required fee, the limitations period is tolled from the date the court received the document through the later rejection-notice date, plus one additional day, if the complaint is resubmitted in a form that corrects the identified errors. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1010.6. subd. (e)(4)(E).) This statute is consistent with case law which provides that “a paper is deemed filed when it is deposited with the clerk with directions to file the paper.”¿ (Rojas v. Cutsforth (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 774, 778 (Rojas).)¿When a filing has an insubstantial defect, “the clerk should file the complaint and notify the attorney or party that the perceived defect should be corrected at the earliest opportunity.” (Id., at p. 777.) Examples of such defects include failing to submit a form required by local rules and listing the wrong courthouse or division. (Id., at p. 777-778.)

Here, Plaintiff submitted the complaint on September 29, 2025, and the clerk did not file the document because conflicting email addresses for service were provided. Plaintiff argues that the Complaint remains timely because counsel corrected the error and re-submitted the Complaint within 11 minutes at 2:07 p.m., tolling the statute of limitations from September 29, 2025, to October 2, 2025.

With regard to the untimeliness of the brief, Plaintiff’s counsel argues in an “application” for leave to file a late opposition that the failure to serve an opposition brief by the deadline of July 30, 2026, was due to a staffing issue. Plaintiff’s counsel declares that this matter was originally assigned to an unidentified attorney who is no longer employed by the firm. Plaintiff’s counsel also states that the “Director of Litigation”, whose responsibilities included overseeing litigation calendaring, resigned on June 4, 2026, and the deadline to oppose this demurrer was not reflected on the calendar. Plaintiff’s counsel did not realize that the deadline had passed until August 4, 2026, at which point he directed that the opposition be filed and served.

The Court has discretion to disregard untimely-filed papers and in this case, the Court chooses to exercise such discretion. (CRC 3.1300(d).) Plaintiff’s proffered reason for the calendaring issue is not credible because this demurrer was filed on March 19, 2026, long before the departure of the litigation director in June of 2026. The timeline provided by Plaintiff’s counsel also means that for two months, no review of the case file and calendar had been conducted, even though a case management conference was held on May 6, 2026, and a case management statement was prepared and filed on May 1, 2026.

Therefore, Defendant’s demurrer is SUSTAINED. Since Plaintiff’s Complaint cannot be successfully amended to overcome the statute of limitations, the demurrer is sustained without leave to amend.

Plaintiff may file a motion for relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473(b) for failing to timely oppose the demurrer and request a nunc pro tunc order correcting the filing date of the Complaint. In opposing such motion, Defendant may list its costs and expenses incurred in attempting to meet and confer for this demurrer, drafting the demurrer, reviewing the opposition brief, and drafting the reply brief.

IV.    CONCLUSION

Defendant’s demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.
